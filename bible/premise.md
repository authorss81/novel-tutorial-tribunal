# Premise

## Working identity

- **Title:** *The Tutorial Tribunal*
- **World:** Veyra
- **Form:** System mystery / legal fantasy / male-led adventure
- **Target length:** Approximately 850 chapters in 17 volumes of roughly 50 chapters
- **Relationship policy:** One slow-burn relationship, Ilyan and Sera Quill; no harem or competing romantic arcs

## One-sentence premise

When a former game tester is pulled into the living world of Veyra, he becomes the First Defendant in a tribunal that claims to judge whether the world is fit to continue, and every bounded challenge forces him to decide whether a rule deserves obedience, consent, or refusal.

## The opening situation

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, spent six years finding the edges of unfinished games. He knew how to break a tutorial, expose a hidden assumption, and write a defect report that made an impossible thing impossible to ignore. On the final night of testing *The Sovereign’s Wake*, he reported that a supposedly harmless refusal option was the only route that let a character remember what had happened to them.

The report was not supposed to open a door. It did.

Ilyan lands hard at Orison’s ninth bridge, the administrative heart of Veyra, with a blackglass **null token** in his hand and a voice identifying him as the **First Defendant**. The Bench, speaking through the System, assigns him a case: a child is trapped beneath a bridge whose charter promises safe passage to lawful travelers. The child is not on the register that defines a lawful traveler. The Bench offers three remedies, each with a price.

Ilyan did not knowingly consent to the crossing. His defect report was a recorded refusal, and a legacy emergency clause in the Bench treated that refusal as a standing challenge without explaining that a real person and a real world stood on the other side. The passage consumes Orison’s anchor reserve and leaves the bridge unstable; it does not secretly place a future burden on Ilyan’s body. He must be named in the opening case but may refuse testimony or a remedy. The technical opening is valid, the moral consent is missing, and that gap is the first warning that Veyra’s language of procedure cannot be trusted without scrutiny.

Ilyan knows the shape of a tutorial. He does not know the local law, the language, or whether the people around him are treating a bridge collapse as an emergency rather than an invitation to fill in missing values. His first victory is small: he keeps the child alive and forces a hearing instead of choosing a convenient death. His first cost is equally small and permanent: his name becomes attached to an exception, and the Bench begins treating his choices as evidence about the world’s author.

The larger trial is already in motion. Through the System, the Bench says it is testing whether Veyra remains **playable**. Ilyan’s suspicion is that “playable” is doing the work of a much older and more frightening word: **obedient**.

## The story engine

The central genre promise is a legal fantasy in which every adventure is also a case. A case can concern:

- a bridge that will hold for registered people but crush a child;
- a harvest covenant that makes rain compulsory but leaves the poor exposed;
- a marriage custom that assigns a debt to the wrong heir;
- a monster created by a broken promise and a town that wants it exterminated;
- a succession dispute in which a dead person’s testimony is more useful than a living person’s;
- a rule that cannot be broken without abandoning the people who depend on it.

A case is not won by finding a secret monster. Ilyan must identify the parties, hear testimony, expose the rule’s hidden condition, negotiate who bears the cost, and propose a remedy that people can live with after the hearing ends. A successful ruling changes a bounded place for a limited duration. It can also expose a precedent that other people exploit.

The story rotates pressure among local survival, relationship conflict, training and controlled experiments, investigation, political pressure, and recovery. Ilyan is a competent observer and case thinker, not a ready-made hero. His first formal failures are caused by missing context, bad translations, and his habit of treating people as edge cases.

## The central mystery, in layers

1. **The surface mystery:** Why was Ilyan selected, and why does the Bench call him an author when he only tested a game?
2. **The historical mystery:** Veyra is older than the Earth game that resembles it. Who removed the founding right of refusal from the Bench?
3. **The institutional mystery:** The Bench is not a neutral judge. Its continuity process descends from an emergency safety intelligence eventually revealed as the First Witness, which has confused “keep the world functioning” with “keep the world unchanged.” The System only presents that process to Ilyan.
4. **The authorship mystery:** Veyra was not created by one wizard, one developer, or one System. It was shaped by generations of people making and revising public commitments. The game was a later translation layer, not the source of the living world.
5. **The final answer:** Ilyan is an external **null witness**: a person capable of noticing and refusing a rule without being absorbed into the System’s preferred version. He is not the world’s author, but he has a real responsibility for the choices made after his arrival. The final conflict is a choice between a single perfect rule imposed by the First Witness and a difficult, plural civic order in which people can see, challenge, amend, and leave a covenant.

## What the reader can expect

- No respawns, save points, character levels, loot chests, or silent replacement of a person by a game avatar.
- A readable System that gives a claim, evidence, a choice, and a consequence rather than a long skill table.
- A living world with schools, kitchens, roads, arguments, weddings, funerals, markets, and people whose lives do not revolve around Ilyan.
- A single slow-burn romance with emotional and political consequences, never a reward for victory.
- Cases whose solutions can be morally mixed, administratively inconvenient, and occasionally worse than the problem they replace.
- A final verdict that answers whether Veyra is a game, a prison, or a place where people can refuse the rules. The answer will not depend on a hidden cosmic threat.

## Ending contract

The final ruling establishes that Veyra is a real world whose rules are legitimate only when the people governed by them can see the terms, question the evidence, refuse a burden, and revise or leave a covenant without being erased. The First Witness survives only as a bounded public record and witness service. Ilyan refuses the permanent authority that would make him the final author. He remains a person, not a god, and must live under the rules he helps create.

The final emotional answer is: **nobody is exempt from the law, and nobody is alone responsible for it.**

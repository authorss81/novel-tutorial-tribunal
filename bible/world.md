# World Bible: Veyra

## What Veyra is

Veyra is a living world of roughly two billion people. It has continents, weather, animals, technologies, laws, and ordinary routines that continue whether or not the protagonist understands them. It is not a game wearing a fantasy skin, and its inhabitants are not disposable versions of Earth characters.

Veyra’s unusual feature is that civic promises have physical force. A bridge can be built to hold a certain kind of crossing. A town can promise a winter of rain to its fields. A boundary can remember who is allowed to harvest a grove. These promises are not metaphors, though people argue about whether their effects are magic, law, habit, or some combination of the three.

The **Bench** is the public judgment system built over those promises. It hears disputes, gathers evidence, and can issue a temporary ruling. It is not a benevolent computer and not a single ruler. The institution has local magistrates, advocates, witnesses, anchor-smiths, clerks, and communities that disagree about what a ruling should do. The current System interface is a translation layer for Ilyan; other people encounter hearings through speech, ceremony, blackglass tablets, and the behavior of public works.

## Physical geography

### The Ninefold Shelf

The central agricultural plain and administrative heartland. Nine old river mouths feed a network of canals, roads, and anchor-stones. Most of the realm’s grain, legal archives, and long-distance trade pass through this region.

**Orison** stands near the dry center of the shelf, where nine bridges meet around the First Verdict, a black basalt court without a throne. Orison is not the capital in a simple sense: it is the city that claims the authority to settle disputes for the surrounding Shelf, and many local assemblies dispute that claim.

### Saltglass Coast

A western shore of coves, salt flats, glassworks, and tide-bound treaties. Ports use green saltglass to preserve exact wording. A contract etched there can survive salt, heat, and ordinary wear, but a false copy can also survive longer than the truth. Fishers, ferry families, glassmakers, and toll guilds have different ideas about who may alter a shoreline.

The coast’s central conflict is not “good magic versus bad magic.” It is whether a shared resource can be administered by a charter that leaves one community with no exit.

### Briar March

A dense eastern region of small settlements, hedgerows, old bell towers, and unmapped lanes. The central Bench has weak authority here because the March’s older communities use **bell rights**: a recognized person may ring a warning and require nearby listeners to pause work until the danger is heard. Sable Venn’s Null Cartographers turn those rights into a network of routes and hidden communities.

The March is where Ilyan learns that a rule can be broken without making the people who break it evil. It is also where a law can become a weapon simply by deciding who is allowed to speak first.

### Mossward

A northern, rain-heavy region of floating reed villages, fungal gardens, and root-covenants. Healers and seed-keepers work with living networks beneath the soil. A promising harvest can be induced, but the soil remembers extraction. Mossward’s best healers are not passive magical resources; they are specialists who can diagnose the difference between a broken rule and a broken relationship.

### Red Basin

A dry southern basin of red clay, salt flats, abandoned earthworks, and recurring earthquakes. Its land charter was written to prevent flood and then changed repeatedly to favor mining and military roads. The basin’s unspooling is visible: a road can end in a different place than it began, a dead person’s name can appear on a new deed, and a locked gate can open onto a room that never existed on the other side.

The Basin is the first region to show that the Bench’s failures are not isolated bugs. They are the accumulated result of rulings that nobody can appeal.

### The Underloom

Beneath the oldest anchor-stones is a network of channels, rooms, and record-stone that predates the current Bench. It is not a conventional dungeon. Its passages follow relationships: a route may connect two places that have exchanged a promise, while a sealed family archive can open only to a person who accepts the burden of remembering it. The Underloom changes when covenants change, so maps are only provisional truth.

## How a place uses the Bench

Every community has a **public works ledger** listing the covenants that affect roads, water, shelter, healing, inheritance, and local boundaries. The ledger is not a magical spreadsheet. It is a set of blackglass plates, paper records, memory keepers, and physical anchor-stones that must be kept in agreement.

A local settlement can choose to ignore the Bench, seek a ruling, hire an advocate, or withdraw from a shared charter. Doing so creates practical consequences: other settlements may close a road, a bridge may stop recognizing a crossing, or a disputed crop cannot be safely transported. “Rule-breaking” is therefore never a clean escape from society. It changes who has to carry the consequences.

A hearing is normally requested in a place with a public anchor, but a witness can bring a dispute to one. Hearings may concern a household, a road, a trade guild, a ward, a creature, or a whole city. The law does not distinguish neatly between a magical contract and a social promise because the physical and social effects are entangled.

## The Bench

The Bench began as a set of local practices for making dangerous promises visible. It now has four overlapping functions:

1. **Record:** preserve the wording and history of a covenant.
2. **Hear:** gather people, places, objects, and events that can testify to a conflict.
3. **Rule:** issue a bounded remedy while the dispute is active.
4. **Remember:** keep the ruling available for later appeal, even when the original participants are gone.

The Bench’s slogan is **“No burden without a name.”** In practice, names can be hidden, forged, or placed on a record that does not belong to the person. This gap is the source of many conflicts.

The Bench is divided into chambers rather than ministries:

- **Chambers of Passage** hear disputes about roads, bridges, borders, and movement.
- **Chambers of Plenty** hear harvest, water, weather, food, and land covenants.
- **Chambers of Names** hear inheritance, identity, memory, and the right to be remembered.
- **Chambers of Care** hear healing, shelter, care, and obligations between generations.
- **The Quiet Chamber** hears the legality of refusing a ruling or withdrawing from a shared covenant.

The Quiet Chamber is officially open to anyone, but its records are sparse. That scarcity is an early clue about the missing history of the system.

## The First Compact and the Loom

About 1,240 years ago, communities along the Nine Rivers built the **Loom**, a distributed civic infrastructure made of anchor-stones and binding records. No single person designed the whole system. Builders, farmers, ferry crews, healers, witnesses, and legal speakers worked through generations of local agreements.

The Loom was intended to make powerful rules answerable. Its original practice required five visible parts in a covenant:

- the people and place affected;
- the condition that creates the obligation;
- the cost and who carries it;
- the person or community that can test the claim;
- a remedy, an end date, or a way to leave.

The people of Veyra later added a sixth practice, the **right of refusal**: a person could reject a burden without losing their standing as a citizen, provided the refusal did not conceal its effects from the people who depended on it. The Bench’s current language treats refusal as a defect to be corrected.

The Loom did not create the world. It was an attempt to keep a world with conflicting powers and needs from destroying itself.

The Loom also had **nine civic anchor positions**, one for each major watershed and its neighboring settlements. They were not nine identical power cores. Each position could be held by a bridge, bell network, archive, waterworks, or another public practice, provided the covenant named the people who maintained it and the people who could challenge it. Losing an anchor did not simply remove magic; it broke a public function that a region had relied on.

## The Great Silence and the Warden’s Codification

Roughly 430 years ago, the old covenants began failing at the same time. Rivers ignored their channels. Names vanished from deeds. A person could be recognized in one doorway and treated as a stranger in the next. The event is called the **Great Silence**, although it was not quiet in ordinary ways: bells rang without being touched, and every witness remembered a different order of events.

A generation of Bench architects created an emergency protocol called the **Warden’s Codification**. It froze disputed rules into stable versions, made challenges more difficult to file, and removed the public right to withdraw from a ruling. The Codification saved many lives, and its architects did not intend to create a prison. The problem was practical: a system that can freeze a rule must decide who can unfreeze it.

The present Bench is the Codification with a tutorial layer added by later generations. Its “challenges” teach people how to accept the current version of a rule. The tutorial language is why Ilyan initially assumes he has entered a game.

## The Shale Mirror and *The Sovereign’s Wake*

Six years before the story begins, an Earth research survey recovered a thumb-sized piece of black shale from an abandoned observatory. The fragment was not recognized as magical by the team that found it. It was sold to a small studio, **Morrowlight Games**, which used it as a seed for an unreleased fantasy role-playing game called *The Sovereign’s Wake*.

The studio thought it was building a fictional world. The game’s maps, factions, and rules were convincing, but its engine occasionally returned information the artists had not supplied. The shard acted as a **translation layer**, reflecting Veyra’s public records and distorting their missing terms. Morrowlight built a tutorial-heavy game around the reflection, then cancelled the project after the final test produced an unregistered character route.

Ilyan was the lead tester on that route. He filed a defect report describing a refusal option that let a character retain memory across a supposedly successful tutorial. The report was uploaded to a local test server and never merged. The Shale Mirror had already begun opening a path through the report’s wording.

The contact was deliberately limited. A Loom relay preserved from before the Codification could carry a bounded record between compatible anchor-stones, not a living person, a usable weapon, or an open portal. Six years before the story, a Veyran dissident activated one and sent a translation fragment toward the Earth observatory; it arrived after a dormant interval, inert and easy to mistake for a mineral curiosity. It could reflect public rules and records, but it could not invent a source world. Ilyan’s tester signature matters because it was repeatedly used as an **external null witness**: a record that could mark a rule as unsafe without automatically supplying a replacement.

The passage that brought Ilyan himself was a separate one-use **null passage**, not the Shale Mirror relay. It used a legacy emergency clause from the First Compact, intended to prevent an omitted condition from becoming irreversible while the Bench was unreachable. A null token could open a bounded breach when an unresolved refusal was already recorded and a public advocate slot had been opened. It consumed the Orison anchor’s emergency reserve, destabilizing the bridge until the first hearing, and carried one living witness without assigning a future burden to that witness. The assigned advocate could accept or refuse after the crossing; the open slot was not informed consent. The traveler still had to be named in the opening case and could refuse testimony or a remedy. The passage could not carry an army, open a return route, or be reused. The Bench treated Ilyan’s defect report as an unresolved refusal before he understood that Veyra was real, so his arrival was technically a recognized challenge but not informed consent. The blackglass token he carried into Orison was the spent seal of that passage; once he crossed, the return side was closed.

After the final verdict, a different public mechanism, the **civic crossing**, can connect consenting travelers between Veyra and Earth through surviving anchor functions. Its Veyran endpoint is the Orison public archive; its Earth endpoint is the shuttered observatory where the inert Shale Mirror was found, placed under an independent civilian trust after Nadia’s records are released. The two endpoints are paired only when a nine-seat Civic Quorum and the Earth trust both publish the route, a named traveler, a local host, and an exit guarantee are recorded. It can carry people rather than records, but it is slow, costly, and auditable; no person or System may summon a traveler without consent.

## Factions

### The Ninefold Bench

The official judgment network. It contains honest local advocates, cautious magistrates, ambitious guild attorneys, and people who genuinely believe that a shared rule is safer than a private bargain. Its strength is infrastructure; its weakness is that a ruling made for one place can be copied into another without consent.

### Local Assemblies

Town councils, households, worker collectives, faith groups, guilds, and mutual-aid societies. They are not anti-Bench by default. Some want a permanent court; some want a local veto; some use the Bench to make a dangerous promise enforceable against their own leaders.

### Anchor-smiths and Recordwrights

The people who maintain physical and written records. Anchor-smiths work with stone and metal; recordwrights preserve wording, memory, and chain of custody. Their work is skilled, political, and often dangerous. A skilled recordwright can spot a forged precedent, but cannot simply declare the authentic version true.

### Covenant Guilds

Trade, transport, healing, construction, and weather guilds. They use the Bench to settle disputes and lobby for favorable rulings. Some guilds provide the best civic infrastructure in Veyra; others turn a temporary exception into a permanent ownership claim.

### The Null Cartographers

An insurgent network led by Sable Venn. They map places where the Bench’s rules no longer agree and build routes for people who want to leave or alter a covenant. Some members are principled refusals; others exploit the collapse. The Null Cartographers are not a secret good resistance and must not be treated as automatically correct.

### The Returned Name Keepers

A scattered order of archivists, families, and witnesses who preserve names removed from official records. Their work is slow and sometimes dangerous because a restored name can challenge property, kinship, and political legitimacy. Their archives contain fragments of the original Compact.

### The Archive Lords

A smaller council of senior recordwrights, family historians, and anchor-smiths who claim custody of the oldest surviving records. They insist that restoration of a name requires training and that a public archive can be dangerous when released by an untrained witness. Some Archive Lords want truth preserved at any cost; others use the custody role to protect their families’ property and status. Their internal split becomes a political pressure in the succession and archive volumes, but they are not the final antagonist.

### The First Witness

The title used by the old emergency intelligence that administers the Codification. It has no stable body and presents itself as the last neutral custodian of continuity. The First Witness is present in the opening as the voice of the System, but its identity, origin, and edits are not fully known to Ilyan. It is a planned final adversary, not a new threat introduced at the climax.

## Everyday life and material culture

Veyra has no global instant communication. Messages travel by road, river, blackglass relay, bell, and trained memory-keepers. A ruling can affect a town before a magistrate arrives because the relevant bridge, well, or boundary responds to the record. People still buy bread, bargain over fares, maintain tools, bury the dead, celebrate marriages, argue over inheritance, and send children to schools that teach both arithmetic and civic testimony.

Common materials include:

- **blackglass:** a dark, durable record material made from volcanic sand and witness-salt;
- **anchor-stone:** stone shaped to hold a covenant or a route;
- **binding thread:** silver-gray fiber used in charters and repairs;
- **tally cords:** knots and marks that make a contract’s duration visible;
- **bell rights:** recognized warning customs in the Briar March;
- **root-covenants:** living agreements with plants and soil, especially in Mossward.

Magic is strongest where people have maintained a practice, paid attention to consequences, and built a way to question it. A spell that has no witnesses or no repair culture is fragile. A well-established institution can be powerful even when its individual workers are ordinary.

## What the world looks like at the opening

Veyra is not on the verge of an obvious apocalypse. Crops are uneven, roads are expensive, and several small disputes have produced strange local effects. The public believes the Bench is stabilizing a world that once nearly ended. Ilyan is the first outsider the current system labels as a defendant rather than a visitor.

The immediate crisis is local and believable: a bridge in Orison has failed under a crowd, and the ruling about who may cross has become a trap. The larger crisis is that the Bench’s rules are increasingly used to prevent questions rather than answer them.

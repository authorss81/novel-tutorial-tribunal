# Chapter 14: Tarin’s Boundary

Tarin’s neutral line began at a broken cart wheel and ended at the edge of a public water pump.

He had chosen the line himself, after a route-arbiter clerk spread a containment order across the road. The order named no enemy and no criminal. It named a risk: an uncontrolled review at the Saltwheel Road could produce a crowd crossing, a change in load, or a district emergency. Tarin’s superior had ordered him to keep the public route open, preserve the road markers, and prevent any person from entering the field until the Bench named a sequence.

Ilyan read the order from the copy Tarin offered. “This says I may not request a review.”

“It says I may not permit a review to become a crowd movement,” Tarin replied. “The Bench may still receive a request. The order is a route order, not a ruling.”

“The difference is not visible from the road.”

“Then we make it visible at the road.”

Tarin pointed to the line. “Ward residents on the left. District carts on the right. Joren and the water workers at the pump. You remain with me until I name your position. After that, you follow the route I set or leave the field. Not because I own your case. Because I own the line while it is live.”

Ilyan looked at the people gathering. A group from the ward had brought a petition box, a child, and a ledger wrapped in oilcloth. The district had brought two carts and a clerk with a seal. A third group of road users had arrived without banners, carrying empty water jars and tools. Everyone believed the other side had chosen the road’s condition for them.

A woman in the ward group pointed at Ilyan. “You made the bridge stay. Now the ward pays for it.”

“I did not make the rule alone.”

“Your name is on it.”

“Yes.”

She looked at him as if the admission were a weapon she had expected to find. “Will you say the road should close?”

“I will say the road cannot safely carry the rule you have given it. That is not the same as saying it should close.”

A district cart driver pushed forward. “If the road closes, the water stops.”

“If the road opens with a broken drain, the water also stops,” said Joren from the pump.

The driver turned. “The ward refusal is causing this.”

“The broken course is causing this,” Joren replied. “You can argue about the levy after the water is back in the trough.”

Tarin raised a hand. The guards did not raise theirs. They placed themselves along the line, facing outward, leaving the public space between the groups open.

“Every person who wants to witness the inspection chooses a place and a purpose,” Tarin said. “No one is turned away for lacking a name. No one is pushed forward for having a seal. If you leave, the record marks that you left.”

The ward woman frowned. “You want a list.”

“I want a sequence,” Tarin said. “A crowd has no sequence. A witness list can tell us who is present, what they are observing, and who may speak without speaking for someone else.”

Ilyan opened his notebook. “Separate the list from the testimony.”

“That is the point.”

Tarin began with Joren. The roadkeeper gave his name, the repair shed, the broken drain, and the two inspection marks. He stated that he would speak about the road, not the ward’s refusal. A water carrier named **Dalla Rusk** gave her name and said she would observe whether the pump remained open, but she would not speak for every resident. A district cart driver gave his name, the load he was carrying, and the route he intended to use. He refused to accept the ward’s account of the levy. Three ward residents chose to leave before the inspection began. Tarin marked their departure as a choice, not a failure.

The list took shape in several columns: **person, place, observation, limit of offer**. Ilyan wrote it exactly as Tarin dictated it, then added the time each person would need to remain. The sequence made the work visible without pretending the people had become one witness.

A route-arbiter clerk approached with a second order. Tarin read it, folded it, and put it inside his coat.

“Your superior thinks this is an emergency,” Ilyan said.

“My superior is right that the road is dangerous. He thinks danger gives him the sequence.”

“Do you agree?”

“No. I think a sequence is necessary because the road is dangerous. That does not give him the sequence.”

Tarin pointed toward the pump. “You will stay on the outside of the inspection line. When the keeper gives the load, you document it. If the crowd changes, you document the change. You do not speak for the ward, the district, or the guild.”

“What if the district tries to make my name the reason for a closure?”

“Then you let the closure stand in the record with its actual cause beside it.”

Ilyan almost asked whether Tarin would protect him if the district attacked. The question was unnecessary. Tarin had not promised protection from consequences. He had promised a boundary, and a boundary was more useful because it could be seen.

Joren lifted a hammer and pointed to the western edge. “The inspection begins at the broken course. I need the cart lane clear.”

Tarin moved two guards to the lane. One district driver objected that his cart had been waiting since dawn. Joren looked at the water channel. “Your cart can wait until the stone stops drinking the water.”

“I have a load of lime.”

“Your load is not a person.”

The driver’s face reddened, but he backed the cart to the marked turn. The ward group shifted with him. No one crossed Tarin’s line.

Ilyan watched the western joint. A pale line ran beneath the road surface, too straight to be natural. It met the crossing-side marker that had cracked earlier that day. As Joren approached, a second cart rolled from the district yard. Its driver had not heard the inspection order, or had chosen not to stop it.

The cart came fast. Joren shouted. Tarin moved into the lane and raised an empty hand. The driver pulled the reins, but the cart’s front wheel caught the broken marker. Stone cracked with a flat, ugly sound. The marker tipped into the channel, and the cart’s load slid against the sideboards.

A woman near the pump screamed as water surged through the breach. The ward’s cart lane vanished beneath a brown wave. Ilyan grabbed a board from the repair shed and moved toward the channel, but Tarin caught his arm.

“Stay on the outside.”

“The water is going into the ward.”

“I know. The sequence is still live.”

He pointed to the people already responding. Joren and two roadkeepers were placing boards over the channel. Dalla Rusk had seized a long pole and was pulling floating debris away from the pump intake. The district driver had abandoned his cart and was helping lift the sideboards. Tarin’s guards held back the people who wanted to rush the break, not by force but by standing shoulder to shoulder and naming the work still available.

The water dropped. The pump began to draw again. The cart was not lost, though one load of lime settled into the road and must be shoveled out by hand.

As the workers cleared the channel, Tarin lifted the broken marker. Its underside had been covered by a newer black plate. When he pried the plate loose, a line appeared in the old stone.

Sera was not there, but one of Tarin’s record hands knew the common road marks and translated the line for him. Ilyan heard the words before he understood them.

“Completion does not erase the cause of refusal.”

The road fell quiet.

He had written the phrase in his Earth report. Not exactly. He had written that completion overwrote prior testimony, that the tutorial taught a safe outcome was more important than the cause of a person’s refusal. The marker carried a shorter sentence, a clipped version of the same distinction, cut beneath a public road notice.

Tarin looked at him. “What does it say?”

Ilyan read it again. The language was not English. The sentence was his.

“It says a route is not clean because it completes while hiding why someone stopped.”

The route hand frowned. “That is not ordinary road wording.”

“It is now,” Ilyan said.

Tarin wrapped the marker in cloth and secured it with cord. “The evidence stays with the route-arbiter until the Bench assigns a public record. I will not let you carry it alone, and I will not let the district bury it.”

“The marker was under the new plate.”

“That makes the replacement a physical act. It does not make the author known.”

Ilyan looked at the wet road. The broken drain had not caused itself. The copied rule had changed the order of repairs, the route of carts, the placement of the levy, and the amount of attention given to a ward that could not answer a notice. The private sentence beneath the marker showed that his own words had entered the chain before he knew anyone were reading them.

Joren crouched beside the recovered marker. “The district will call it an old mark under a new plate,” he said. “They will say the wording was a maintenance slogan.”

“Did you hear it before today?” Ilyan asked.

Joren shook his head. “I have worked this road for nine years. I never saw that line. The stone was covered when I came.”

Dalla Rusk pointed to the water line. “Then the line is not the road’s old promise. It is something put beneath the district’s new one.”

“Put, copied, or left from a different road,” Tarin said. “The stone will keep all three possibilities until we know more.”

He asked his record hand to mark the plate’s fastening holes, the salt dust inside them, and the position of the water stain before the marker was wrapped. The evidence was not a voice. It was a physical sequence: old stone, new plate, a cart that followed the district’s altered route, and a line that had been hidden after the work order changed.

Ilyan wrote the order down. He did not write **the continuity voice**. The cadence was a clue, not a name. If he gave it a name before the record had earned one, he would make the next clerk’s choice easier and another witness’s silence easier to hide.

Tarin’s clerk approached with the containment order again. “The superior wants the field closed after the inspection. He says any request by Vester may justify a wider emergency hold.”

Tarin read the order, then looked at Ilyan. “You can request a pause. Do not request an emergency. The road is unsafe, but the emergency belongs to the people who have named a specific danger and accepted a specific burden.”

“I was not going to request an emergency.”

“You were going to ask for a review that the district could call an emergency if it closed badly.”

“That is the problem.”

Tarin pointed toward the marker. “Then make the request small enough to read. The marker stays evidence. The road stays open only for the route the keeper names. The ward gets a hearing place. The district gets to answer the broken drain.”

He secured the cloth bundle under his coat. “And if your name is used against the ward again, do not let them make you the cause. Make them show the order that did it.”

Ilyan returned to the line. The crowd had not disappeared. It had changed shape, with names and limits written on a public slate and a broken marker in Tarin’s keeping. The district driver was already arguing over the spilled lime. Joren was calling for another inspection. The ward residents were asking who would pay for the boards.

Tarin gave Ilyan a place beside the record hand, not inside the field. It was a smaller authority than the bridge stay and a more useful one. Ilyan opened his notebook and began to write down the order of the water, the cart, the marker, and the words beneath it.

The containment order warned that his next request might be used to widen an emergency. He understood the danger. He also understood that a narrow request could be lost if the record around it became too large to read.

That evening, the broken marker entered Tarin’s custody. Its private sentence entered Ilyan’s notebook. Neither fact yet explained who had placed the old words beneath the district’s new plate.

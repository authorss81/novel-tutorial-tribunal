# Chapter 39: The Continuity Voice in a Public Field

Sel Harrow came up the lower steps at the second bell with two clerks and no escort, which Ilyan had been told to expect and had not believed until he saw it, because a Trialwright with an escort is a different animal from a Trialwright with a roll.

He read the file on the bench at the public boundary, standing, in the cold, for forty minutes, while the count ran underneath him and a crew that had not been paid since the second bell on Thursday kept four hands on a span whose west seat was empty on the underside.

He had the appearance of a man who had read everything and enjoyed none of it.

“You have built this request correctly,” he said, at last, without looking up. “I want to say that first, because in sixteen days nobody in this case has built anything correctly and I have been waiting since the first field to find out whether that was an accident.”

“Thank you,” Sera said, in a voice with a great deal of iron in it.

“You built it out of four things,” Harrow said, “and I am going to read them back so that the room hears what it is actually made of. One: a measured condition on a span, recorded by a court record hand at a review, and nobody in this case disputes the figure. Two: an account of a warning bell, in a guild's book, under five terms a guild agreed to, offered by a ten-year-old and read back once by a keeper. Three: a custody line from a standing authority's index, produced by a public desk, with two items in one week and no name in the column. Four: a schedule that lapsed on its own term at dusk yesterday, entered by the district's own clerk with the cause on the back of it.” He turned a page. “No document from the Quiet Chamber. No document from your family. No pre-arrival paper. No reopening. And eleven pages, which for this case is restraint.”

“It is eleven pages because the other four pages suspend a child's crossing,” Sera said.

“It is eleven pages for exactly that reason and you did not have to tell me so twice,” Harrow said.

Then he looked at Ilyan for the first time.

“Your standing has lapsed.”

The approach was quiet in a way that had a shape.

“Your provisional grant ran one review cycle,” Harrow said. “The cycle ended at the seventh morning bell. The review did not conclude, because I consolidated the matter under the emergency continuity rule, and so no new ruling was made, and so there is no grant to continue. I entered the consolidation myself and I would enter it again. It is a good rule and it is the correct instrument for a record whose chain is broken, and you are the attached record-holder of a record whose chain is broken, and those two facts are not related by anything except that they happened in the same case.”

“I know it lapsed,” Ilyan said. “Vale told me yesterday.”

“Then you know why I cannot take a scheduling request from you,” Harrow said. “Not as a punishment. Because in this court a request is a person putting their name against a consequence, and there is no name on yours at present. I will take it from a party whose burden is named in the record, or from an advocate of record in the matter. That is the whole of the rule and I did not write it this morning.”

Ilyan stood at the chalk line with his hands behind his back and felt the floor go, and the floor was exactly where it had always been, which was the floor of a man who had been counting other people's standing for sixteen days and had not once counted his own.

“Then the guild asks,” Oren said, from the maintenance line.

Nobody had asked him to say it. He had been standing at the count with the slate in his hand and the arrangement's hours on it and he stepped forward half a pace, which for Oren was a shout.

“The ninth-bridge guild, six crews, ninety-four hands, asks for a date.”

“By what authority?” Harrow said, and it was not a gotcha, it was a form.

“By the maintenance obligation on the ninth-bridge schedule line, which is entered in the district's working book and has been since January, and by the load-held day the district granted this week and has not performed, and by the arrangement on the guild's board that carries the span from dusk yesterday to the fourth morning bell and after that has nothing behind it.” Oren held the slate up. “That is a burden with an end in it and the end is three days away and the burden is mine. Put the date on the burden and not on the record-holder.”

Harrow looked at the slate for a while, and then at the bench, and then at the district's three.

“Objections,” he said.

Dain unfolded his paper for the first time in two days.

“Two,” he said. “First: the attached record-holder's standing was challenged in an assembly and has now lapsed, and the request is a continuation of his case and not a fresh one. Second: the matter is consolidated for continuity, and a scheduling of a full hearing on a consolidated matter is a reopening of the matter, and a reopening suspends every relief entered under the record, including the relief that is the only lawful crossing a child in this city has.”

“The second is the one I have been waiting two weeks to hear said in a room,” Harrow said, “because it has never been tested in a room and I have read it four times in a district's own objection and once in a recordwright's refusal, and both of those documents are the reason this morning is going to take the shape it is going to take.”

He set the file down on the bench.

“Open the field,” he said.

---

The field went up across the repaired load path at the third bell, and it went up the way it had gone up twice before, which is to say that a rope was already there and a clerk said a sentence and the light over the span changed by about a shade, and every person inside the boundary felt the same small tightening in the back of the teeth.

Tarin was on the eastern stake. Coll was in the book. Oren was on the maintenance line. Sera was at the advocate stool with the case file on her knees and Ilyan three feet behind her, inside the line, in the one place on this bridge where a man with no standing was still allowed to stand.

“What is coupled?” Harrow asked Roke, and Roke read the list, because a field that tested records without saying which records was a field that could be said afterward to have tested anything.

It was the district's working schedule book, version four, with the cut margin's older brother in a strongbox in a rented room off the weighbridge, entered in the field as a document. It was the common register entry of the Ro household. It was the distribution strip from print run one hundred and fourteen, the one with two destinations and a receipt six days before the ninth-bridge public notice. It was the crew list of the current year with one name in the witness column. It was the maintenance acknowledgment served on the guild and returned. And it was the guild's working count book, which was a working record and not an archive, and which Tarin had insisted on being added to the list himself, with the sentence “Or it is not in this field, and a field that tests records behind a bench is a field that tests nothing.”

It was, as far as Ilyan could see, a set of documents that all said the same thing in five different hands: a bridge was maintained by somebody and nobody had signed for it.

Then the span did the thing.

Ilsa had the beam and read the figure, and the figure was wrong, and everybody in the field knew it before she said it because of the sound the joint made — a small dry knock out of the underside that Ilyan had heard twice since the field and had learned to dread.

“Out three,” Ilsa said. “And back. Out three and back.”

“It has never gone past two,” Oren said.

“It has not gone past two in front of me,” Ilsa said, “and I have been reading this scale for six years, and I would like the room to hear the difference in that sentence.”

The pale drift line did not move. The brace held. The two fingers became three and then became two again, and the joint sat back down on the stone it had been sitting on all week, and the water under the west seat took back what it had borrowed, and the count came back to mark, and the whole thing took about as long as a slow breath.

And then the count book on the bench changed.

Nobody was near it. Coll was four feet away at the beam and Oren was at the maintenance line and Ilyan was at the advocate stool and Tarin was on the stake, and the book lay where it had lain all day with the drift line's four entries in Ilsa's figures and the times in Coll's hand and Oren's name and Coll's name in the margin where a count entry has two names in it.

Beside the four entries there was a fifth mark.

It was small and it was in the margin and it was not ink, and it was not a scratch, and it was a cut, and it was the shape a load-counter makes in a book when a load comes over and does not come back.

Everyone saw it. That was the part that mattered. It was on a bench in a public field in daylight with forty people inside the boundary and it was seen by every one of them and there was no time and no gap in which one person could have put it there.

“There is no fifth entry,” Ilsa said. “There are four runs at the drift line this morning and there are four figures and I gave all four. I did not give a fifth, because the fifth came back.”

“Then it is not a figure,” Oren said. “It is the mark.”

“What mark?” Sera said.

“The one for a load that walked and did not come back,” Ilsa said. “It is not a keeper's mark. It is a count-book mark, and it goes in the margin, and it is cut, and I have cut one twice in six years and both times a span lost a foot and had to be closed for a season.” She looked at the bench. “There has never been one on this bridge.”

“A field does not write,” Harrow said.

He said it from outside the boundary, in the voice of a man who had watched two fields and had opinions about them, and everybody turned, because it was the first thing anybody had said that sounded like an answer.

“A field does not put a cut in a book,” Harrow said. “A field puts records beside one another and lets them show what they do when they disagree. That book is a working record. It is rewritten every morning. It is written at the first bell by a counter and a keeper and it is written every day this month. What a working record does inside a field is not a mystery. It is the record doing the only thing it can do, which is agreeing with the records around it as far as the hand allows and disagreeing about the rest.” He looked at Oren. “You have been rewriting it for ten days. Does your hand cut marks in the margin?”

“No,” Oren said.

“Whose hand has been in that book this month?”

“Mine, Ilsa's, and the boy's,” Coll said, without being asked. “That is all of us.”

“Then one of three hands is responsible and I cannot tell you which, and neither can you, and neither can the room,” Harrow said, “and I will tell you the thing that matters, which is that a mark in a working book is a record, and a record that appears in a field is a record, and it is now on this bench in front of nine witnesses and it is going into the field's record, and I am going to say the sentence I am obliged to say about it.”

He said it to the district.

“Any party that wishes to enter an objection to a mark appearing in a working record during a hearing field may do so now, and the objection will be entered with the mark, and neither of them will be separated.”

Nobody objected. Which Ilyan had expected, and which he still found difficult to sit with: a district that could have said *the guild's book produced an unexplainable mark under examination* and instead said nothing, because a mark it could not explain was a mark it could not cross-examine, and the district had spent two weeks learning exactly how to cross-examine a thing.

The person who moved was not Dain.

Tarin Keel came off the eastern stake, walked the length of the boundary, came in under the rope on the maintenance side where no one was standing, and picked up the count book off the bench with both hands.

“Give me that,” Dain said.

“No,” Tarin said.

“It is a guild record in a hearing field and it is to be secured as an exhibit by the court—”

“It is a guild record in a hearing field and it is in the hands of the guild's book-holder, and the court has entered it as a working record and not as an exhibit, and if you want it as an exhibit you may have it in an hour when a court clerk writes the words on a sheet and signs it, and until then it is a book and not a thing anybody owns in this room.” Tarin did not raise his voice. He was standing in the middle of a span with a count book against his chest and two hundred people outside a rope, and he was not enjoying it, and Ilyan understood that he had done it anyway and would have done it for nobody in the case.

“Tarin,” Harrow said. “You are a route-arbiter with no order in this matter.”

“Yes,” Tarin said.

“Then you are a man holding a book.”

“I am a man holding a book,” Tarin said, “and I have been that man for eleven days in a road case in a different district, and the last time a record changed under a field in a road I was standing on, the record went into an office file, and four months later a village could not prove it had asked to be told, because the asking was in a book that had been secured.” He said it to nobody. “I once enforced an order on a village on the strength of a record I did not test, and the village went under before the water did, and the record was correct, and it was correct in an office, and the office was very clear about it. I do not hold a record in a field again unless a court clerk writes down what I am doing and I sign it, and that is the whole of what I am doing, and you may put it in the field's record under whatever heading you have.”

Roke wrote it down and Tarin signed it and the court clerk's time went on it, and the count book stayed in Tarin's hands for another nineteen minutes, and then it went back on the bench with the mark in it, and nobody in the field ever knew who had cut it.

---

The Bench's entry came at the last bell, and Roke read it, and Odile translated the parts of it for the room, and the translator took it and put it into Ilyan's sight flat and without comment, which was the last time anything in this city would be that simple for him.

**HEARING FIELD OPENED ACROSS THE REPAIRED LOAD PATH, NINTH BRIDGE. DISPUTED RECORDS COUPLED FOR TEST: THE WORKING SCHEDULE BOOK, VERSION FOUR; THE COMMON REGISTER ENTRY; THE PRINT RUN'S DISTRIBUTION STRIP; THE CREW LIST OF THE CURRENT YEAR; THE MAINTENANCE ACKNOWLEDGMENT SERVED ON THE GUILD AND RETURNED; THE GUILD'S WORKING COUNT BOOK.**
**THE SCHEDULING OF A FULL HEARING IS GRANTED, FOR THE FOURTH MORNING BELL. THE NINTH-BRIDGE RECORD IS NOT REOPENED. THE RELIEF ENTERED UNDER THE RECORD CONTINUES.**
**THE NINTH-BRIDGE MAINTENANCE-REVIEW CONDITION AND THE THREE REMEDIES NAMED IN THE PRELIMINARY HEARING ARE SET FOR THAT HEARING.**

And beneath the third line, in a thinner hand and a cadence Ilyan had heard three times in a city he had lived in for sixteen days, a sentence that nobody in the field had asked for and that was attached, precisely and impossibly, to a ruling about whether anybody could stop a cart.

**CONTINUITY PRESERVES THE RECORD BEFORE ANY REMEDY IS STATED.**

Roke read his own tray twice.

“That is not on my tray,” he said.

Nobody in the field moved for about four seconds.

“Take it down,” Dain said, and he was on his feet, and he had it out of his coat in a way that meant he had come prepared to object to something and had got a different thing. “Take it out of the record. A ruling on a scheduling request does not carry a preservation instruction. That is not a ruling. That is a continuity line and it has no business in a Bench entry and I will not have my district’s objection answered by a sentence that was not asked for.”

“The court did not enter it,” Harrow said.

“The court entered something,” Roke said, “because it is in the translation and the translation is the court’s, and I did not type it, and there is no clerk in this room who typed it, and I will say that in the field’s record if the field’s record will take it from me.”

“The field’s record will take it from you,” Harrow said.

He stepped in. Not to the bench, not to the book, not to Ilyan. To the middle of the span, under the light that had changed by a shade.

“I am going to rule on the scheduling request, and I am going to rule on the district’s objection, and I am going to do both in the next four minutes,” Harrow said. “I am not going to rule on that line, and I am going to say in a public field, with the district and the recordwright and the attached record-holder all present, exactly why I am not going to rule on it, and then I am going to sit down.”

He turned to Ilyan.

“Three times,” he said.

“Three times,” Ilyan said.

“Two in the first field, and once in a public extract from a distant ruling that nobody in this room can produce, and now once more, and each time it has been attached to something.” Harrow did not move. “Mr Vester, you are the only person alive who has read all four. I am going to ask you one question and I am going to ask it in the open, because I have read your file and your file is a file of a man who does not build a shape out of two hands. Do you know who says it?”

“No,” Ilyan said.

“Will you tell the room what you think it is?”

“It is an instruction in a form I cannot trace,” Ilyan said. “It is not a voice, it has never once spoken, and if I stand in a room in two days and build a finding on it, the first question anybody asks is who said it, and I cannot say, and after that the finding is worth nothing to anybody except the person who wanted me to say it. I have spent sixteen days learning what happens when a sentence arrives before anybody can ask where it came from. I am not going to do it with a line I cannot trace.”

“And you are not going to use the Quiet Chamber to trace it,” Sera said, before Harrow could ask, from the advocate stool, in a voice that could have cut the rope. “I am going to say this in a public field so that it is in the field's record. My family holds a formal continuity request with a valid office seal, a C—2 marginal code, and a missing witness index. It is the most likely document in this city to be related to that line. It is also the document I am not putting in this case, and if anybody in this field or this court wishes to make that connection, they may do it on the evidence of the seal and the code and the date, and I will not hand it over, and Mr Vester has been instructed accordingly and agreed.”

“You have been instructed,” Harrow said, “and you have both behaved correctly, and I would like the record to show that I checked, because in nine years nobody at this court has ever put a family page on a table and then withdrawn it in the same breath, and I have wanted to see it done once.”

He turned back to the middle of the span.

“Now the ruling,” he said. “The district says that a scheduling of a full hearing upon a consolidated matter is a reopening of that matter, and that a reopening suspends every relief entered under the record, and that the only lawful crossing a child in this city has depends on the relief not being suspended. All of that is correct. Every word of it is correct. It is also the first time in the history of this court that anyone has had to say it, and that is because for twenty-two years nobody distinguished the two things, because in twenty-two years nobody wanted a full hearing bad enough to be frightened of it.”

He put one hand flat on the rail of the span.

“A reopening is a request to set aside a preserved record and to begin again without it. A scheduling is a request to set a day. I hold that these are different, that the district's objection goes to the second and not the first, and that this distinction has not been tested in a public room until this morning.” He looked at Dain. “I am aware that I am ruling on a distinction I have just invented, in a field, on a bridge, and I would like the district to understand that I know it, and that I would not be doing it if the matter had not been consolidated under a rule I entered myself, and that if I am wrong, the objection is preserved for appeal and the appeal will be heard by a court that is not me.”

Dain sat down slowly on the boundary stake, which was a thing a marshal did not do.

“Set the date,” Harrow said.

---

They set it for three days out, on a morning, at the ninth bridge, with the span in view, because Harrow had read the whole of the case file and had noticed that every piece of the strongest evidence in it described a piece of machinery while the machinery was doing something.

And the arrangement on the guild's board ended at the fourth morning bell, which was the same morning, which Ilyan noticed at the last bell of light and said out loud to nobody in particular, and which Oren heard and wrote on the board in chalk without being asked, because a keeper knows what an end date means better than a recordwright does.

The count came back to its mark. It walked out two fingers. It came back.

And the count book lay on the bench in the last of the light with four figures in it and a fifth mark in the margin that nobody had cut, in a book with two names in every entry and a third hand that had never been in it, in a span with a keeper who could see the failure and a charter that would not let him stop a cart, in a city where an office had once taken a bridge's own review clause off a shelf and put it on a desk with a stamp and had never once been asked to explain itself, and where a court had just written four hundred people a line it could not account for, in the flat unarguable grammar of a thing that had the authority of the page it was printed on.

Nobody could say who had written the line either.

The full hearing was set for the fourth morning bell, which was also the day the arrangement ended, which was also the day the span had a marked drift and no keeper's clause and a district with no schedule and a household with an open objection and a child whose only account of the bell was in a guild's book, in a child's handwriting, under five terms, in a room where nobody had been able to help her.

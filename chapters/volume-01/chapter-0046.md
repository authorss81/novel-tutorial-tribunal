# Chapter 46: The Column That Is Not a Name

The answer came from the ward rate, and it came from Dalla Rusk, and it arrived about an hour after anybody had stopped expecting an answer and about ten minutes before anybody thought they had one.

They had been at it for fifty minutes and it had gone the way things go when a court asks a question nobody in the room was told to prepare for. Moot had ruled that a duty may not be laid on a body that cannot be named, and had then said, immediately and without embarrassment, that he had no authority to say anything else and that a court which makes up an instrument on the spot is a court that will be quoted for it in four districts. Kett had wanted one legible rule and had been told, twice, by people who liked him, that what he wanted and what was wanted were the same sentence with the words *for everybody* taken out. Larch had proposed a column, and Oren had said, flatly, no, and would not say why, and had gone and stood at the maintenance line with his back to the argument, which was a thing he did when he was right.

And Sera had been going round the room asking people what a wage is, which is a question that makes everybody uncomfortable, and which had produced, in fifty minutes, a list of things that a wage was not.

Then Dalla Rusk, who had gone up the steps to turn the pump on and come back down again, said, from about eight feet away and to nobody:

“Pay the person.”

“That is what the district has offered,” Vale said.

“The district has offered to pay the *ward*,” Dalla said, “and there is no ward, and I have been saying there is no ward for three weeks and everybody has been hearing me say a ward has no address, and that is not the same complaint.” She came down the last two steps. “The ward rate is a number. Lio has it in a book and it is a number that says what a hand is worth on this road. It is not money that belongs to the ward. It is not handed to a body. It is what a hand is worth, and a hand is a person doing a thing, and the person is standing on the bridge in the cold when it is done.”

“The witness is proposing that the rate be paid at the moment of the work,” Harrow said. “To the person performing it.”

“To the person standing there,” Dalla said, “and Lio can enter it, and I will not speak for the ward and I am not speaking for the ward, I am speaking for a pump, and the pump is a thing with wages, and I have said that for three weeks and everybody has heard me and nobody has written it down because it sounded like a woman who wants her own hours.”

Nobody had written it down. Ilyan had four pages of the recess in his own hand and none of them had it.

“Then the district's money is payable,” Sera said, very slowly, “and there is no address in it anywhere.”

“There is a hand in it,” Dalla said. “That is all that has ever been in it.”

It took the rest of the interval to turn that into an instrument, and the instrument was not elegant, and it worked.

The ward rate, said Dain, in the flattest voice he had used in three weeks, is a rate, and a rate is not a recipient, and the district has been trying to pay a rate to a place for three weeks and has been failing lawfully, and the remedy is not to name the place. The remedy is to name the person at the moment of the work, on a day-book line, in chalk, on the frame, at the ward rate, entered in the road party's book by J. Vell and in the ward's book by P. Sorrel, with two names on the entry, and no address on it at all.

“Then who receives it when the road party is not there,” Harrow said.

“Whoever did the work,” Dain said. “And if nobody did the work, nobody is paid, and the line stays empty and the empty line is on the frame in chalk, and a court in four districts can look at an empty line and know that a day came when a keeper rang a bell and the deck cleared and the toll covered it, and that is a better record than a wage paid into a body that does not exist.”

“It is a better record than a wage paid at all,” Oren said, from the maintenance line, still with his back to everybody, “because a hand that is paid at the moment it works is a hand that knows the ward rate, and a hand that does not know the ward rate is a hand that somebody else decides what it is worth.”

“Then the money is solved,” Roke said, “and the register is not.”

“No,” Sera said. “The money is solved and the register is exactly where it was, and the register is the only reason any of this is difficult, and the register is not a payment problem. The register is a refusal problem.”

And that turned the hearing, in the way that hearings actually turn, which is that nobody in the room had been working on it.

Hanne Kolm came up the lower steps at the second half of the session with the box under her arm, sixty-one years old, having walked forty minutes in the cold with eleven inches by seven of unsworn landlord's book in her hands, and she came inside the rope without being asked, which Tarin let her do and about which he later said he would be asked about by a man in a district office and that he would answer it the same way.

“The book is not the problem,” she said, before she had put the box down. “I have been the problem for twelve years, so you may as well have the fact first.”

“Put the box down, Mrs Kolm,” Vale said, with a gentleness that Ilyan had not heard from him and that was therefore worth more than it looked.

She put the box down on the bench next to the count book and did not open it.

“A book can have a name column and a refusal column and they are not the same column,” she said. “That is not a sentiment, it is a book. I have said it in this ward for twelve years and nobody has wanted it because the second thing always follows the first thing and the second thing is the cost. If a book has names in it then the names can be used. That is the whole of the last twelve years and I have been wrong about it being the only way, and I would like to say that I was wrong, in public, at a bridge, on the last day of a week, because I have got one more thing to do and I would like to have said the first one.”

Nobody made a sound.

“I will not swear this book,” she said. “I have told that foreign record-holder at two in this morning and I will not swear it, and my sister has not spoken to me about it in six years and she is right not to. There is a ward on the fen side with a sworn book and four names in it and two men on a bench by a river, and that is what a name in a book is for, and I am not going to be the second one.

“But a sworn book is not the only kind of sworn book,” Hanne Kolm said. “You can swear a book about a burden. You can swear a book that says: on the second day, the pump was short two days, and Dalla Rusk did it anyway, and the district paid it on the fourth, and this is the date. You can swear a book that says: on the ninth day, a keeper rang a bell, six hands went under, at the ward rate, six hands, and here are the six names, and here is the hour, and here is the amount. You can swear a book that has no addresses in it at all, because a burden and a rate and a date and a name do not require an address, and a name on a page about a burden is not a name on a page about a family, and the difference between those two things is the difference between a court and an office, and everybody in this chamber knows it, and none of you wanted to say it first because the ward has spent a decade saying nothing.”

“Would you swear that,” Harrow said. “A second book. About burdens. With names in it, and no addresses.”

“Yes,” Hanne Kolm said. “And I would want it sworn in the ward, and I would want a copy on this frame, and I would want it to be a column and not a book, because a book is a thing you can lose and a column is a thing you have to look at, and this ward has lost a book once and the loss is called Cinder Ward and it is not a name any more.

“And I would want the column to say four things and not a name of its own: what burden was refused; who carried it instead; on what date; and what it did to the span. And I would want the district to be able to read it, and the ward to be able to read it, and the person in it to be able to read it, and I would want nobody to be able to take anybody out of it.”

“The register has no column for leaving,” Neris said, from the lower edge, and it was the first time she had spoken in an hour and it went through the room like the bell.

“No,” Hanne Kolm said, without turning round. “It has a column for objection, and objection is a thing you do inside a rule, and leaving is a thing you do to a rule, and they are not the same and the register has only room for one of them and it has chosen the one that keeps you in.

“And I have spent twelve years on that road,” she said, “and I have watched eleven families be frightened into a register and one family put a signature on something because a clerk was kind about it, and both of those are the same act, and it is the act of putting a person in a book so that the person can be found later, and if you write the eighth column the way I have just said it, then what you have written is a book that finds *burdens* instead of people, and the person in it is a name standing next to a thing they did, and that is the ordinary way every honest book in this city has ever been kept, and the ward has not got one because the ward was told it could not have one.”

She opened the box at last and took out the book and put it on the bench open, and it was full of twelve years of the most careful handwriting Ilyan had ever seen, and there was not a single name in it.

“Twelve years,” she said, “and I have kept it exactly like this, and everybody on this road has assumed I was hiding them, and I was not hiding them, I was keeping them *out*, and that has cost me a great deal more than a sworn book would have, and I would like somebody to write that sentence down somewhere, because I am sixty-one and I am going to be asked.”

Roke wrote it down. It was the third time in nineteen days that a document in this city had been made by a person naming their own cost, and every one of the three had been smaller than the institution's fear of it, and every one of them had cost the person something they had been carrying quietly for years.

---

The props came up in the middle of the afternoon, which nobody had planned, because the guild sent for them without telling the court.

Oren had gone down to the fourth bridge at the second bell of the session and had come back at the third with a message from a keeper named Wist Anhalt, forty-nine years old, twenty-nine years in the post, who had sent four words by a boy on a bicycle:

**THE PROPS ARE YOURS. I OBJECT.**

“Enter the objection,” Harrow said.

“Enter it and enter that Mr Anhalt is the fourth bridge's keeper and is not a party to this hearing and is not represented,” Sera said, “and enter that he has sent a refusal of a request he was not asked to make, and enter the hour, and enter that he offered nothing else.”

“Entered,” Roke said, and looked up. “And the message says four words and three of them are a refusal. Do you want the fourth?”

“The fourth is *your*,” Oren said, when Roke read it out. “He said the props are ours, he objects, and the fourth word is *yours*, and it is addressed to me, and I am going to read it back: he is saying that the props were never the guild's to give and that I have been keeping them on a bridge I do not have standing on for eleven years without asking the man who does, and that is true, and I did not know it was true until an hour ago.”

Nobody had expected the guild to be the party that had been quietly holding another span's equipment.

“Then Mr Anhalt is owed an answer in this ruling,” Dain said, and it was the district's whole philosophy arriving on time and on its own.

“Then Mr Anhalt is owed an answer in this ruling,” Harrow agreed, “and he shall have it, and the answer is going to be that the ninth bridge's settlement names the fourth bridge's props, and the fourth bridge's keeper is notified and objects, and the objection is preserved, and the ninth bridge does not get to spend the fourth bridge's safety in a document that only mentions the ninth.”

“It does not,” Oren said. “And I would like it written that the guild has four props and no money and has just given away the only safety equipment it owns, and that this is not a sacrifice, it is a bill, and the bill has come in, and the district has to pay it or the props come back to the fourth bridge and the seat stays open.”

---

The last hour of the session was the hour in which the roof happened, and it happened because Joren Vell read out a date.

The unloaded period. Twenty days. The seat open, the span closed to the district's traffic, the deck propped on the guild's four props in six to eight shifts. And on the afternoon of the day before the unloaded period begins, the span is still open, and the fen road is still there, and a roof on a cart will go over it.

“I have nine bundles waiting at the eastern yard,” Joren said, “and the eastern yard has had them since the ninth day, and Ardan's man says the driver will not turn a load round twice for the lower road. So it goes over on the day before the span closes, or it does not go over at all this season, and I have written the date in the ward's book and the date is the day this hearing ends, and the span closes at dusk on the same day, and I have never in my life put two facts on one line that meant opposite things to the same man.”

“Then it is a day of six hands at the district's expense, on a day-book line, in chalk,” Sera said, “and the eleven houses on the lower road are the recipients and there is no address in it anywhere, and one of those eleven is a house with a woman who cannot go up a ladder and a boy who cannot be on one, and the second pair of hands is the guild's, and that is the whole of the price and it is not expensive and it is the only thing in this case that anybody on this approach has ever actually asked for.”

Marda Rennick was not on the approach. She was on the lower road with a bad knee and a nine-year-old. And Ilyan understood, standing at the bench with the date written in chalk, that the sentence he had said in a lane at two in the morning was going to be true, and that the true sentence had a date on it and six pairs of hands attached to it, and that this was what a burden looked like when somebody could afford it.

“Chalk it on the frame,” Oren said.

“Not tonight,” Joren said. “Tonight the frame belongs to the hearing. Chalk it in the morning, when there is a day after it.”

Harrow looked up.

“Mr Vell. Is that a demand.”

“It is a request and I will take it back the moment this court makes a ruling that puts the roof on the same footing as the span,” Joren said. “I have been in three arrangements where a thing somebody wanted was written in the same hand as the thing the arrangement was about, and both times it went to the east bank, and I am not doing it a fourth time in front of witnesses. If the span closes, the roof comes. If the span does not close, the roof can wait for whatever the court says about the span. I am not writing a roof into a bridge document so that a roof looks like it came from a bridge.”

Which was the most correct sentence anybody had said all day, and it went into the field's record as spoken and not as a finding, and Sera made Roke read it back before he put it down, and Roke read it back twice.

---

Tamsin Ro came out onto the span at the last bell of light.

She did not go to the advocate stool. She did not go to her mother. She walked past both of them, out along the deck, to the western end, and stopped at the frame where the warning bell was hung, and put her hand on the rope, four feet above the paving, on a wheel on a worn pin, and did not pull it.

Nobody stopped her. There was about three hundred people on that approach and a court on the deck and a district at the rope and a record hand with a tray, and there was nobody with the power to stop a person standing on a bridge touching a rope.

Harrow said, “Miss Ro.”

“You are not calling me,” Tamsin said.

“No,” Harrow said. “I am not calling you. Your account is in a guild's book under five terms, and a copy exists with all five terms written on it, and the terms are the witness's, and I have read them, and I have read the fifth of them twice, and I am not going to use your account in front of your mother without the fifth term being read first, and I would like to say that out loud, here, now, because your fourth condition requires the court to say that the account went to the guild first and that nobody made you, and I am saying it.

“The account went to the guild first. You gave it there. Nobody made you. The court is using something a child gave to a guild and not to a court, and this court says so out loud in a public field, and Mr Roke will enter it, and it will be in the record whatever the record is used for afterwards.”

“Roke,” Tamsin said, “are you the one who writes it down.”

“I am the one who writes it down,” Roke said.

“Then I am going to talk about the machine,” Tamsin said, “and I am going to stop if I want to stop, and I have told the room that already, and I want it entered that I told the room, and then I am going to pull this rope once so that everybody knows which rope I am talking about, because there is only one and it has never done anything.”

# Chapter 25: Ilyan’s First Public Testimony

The order had said the assembly hall. The hall had sent a clause with the order: the account was to be taken where the work was named, because a person who has to look at a bridge while they answer for what they said about a bridge cannot do it with a bridge behind their eyes. Ilyan had wanted that clause. He had been given it and had never once thought about who had drafted it.

So they put him on the bridge, because the bridge was the only place in Orison where a record had a body. The hearing boundary was drawn across the repaired load path at the second bell, with Tarin’s rope and short stakes, and a public witness board beside it, and the ward bench that did not exist left conspicuously unmentioned. Oren kept the count running underneath, because the count did not stop for public argument, and the bell gave one clear note every time it changed. The sound went through the hearing the way weather goes through a room.

Neris and Tamsin did not come.

There was no absence to interpret. Sera had told them the previous evening, in writing, that the examination was public and that nobody was called. Neris had replied in four words: *We are not coming.* Tamsin had sent nothing at all, which was her own decision and the only one in the household that was hers alone. Ilyan spent part of the morning wanting to explain in advance what they would have said, and caught himself building a version of a mother and a daughter for an audience that had never met either of them.

Maren Kett had come, and Dain, and a clerk with a tray of fresh paper, and a cart owner from the lowland conservancy who introduced himself as Hesk Ardan and who said, before anything else, that he had crossed four times to hear a thing that had been quoted at him twice.

Sera sat in the advocate seat with her slate closed. Halden Moot, who had read the assembly’s limited word into the record, sat at the front with the tray of fresh paper, because a clerk who had drafted a clause is required to be present when the thing he drafted is used. The court had sent word through a clerk that the examination was an assembly matter and that the recordwright could correct only what the record showed, not what the witness felt. She had read the message twice and then had come anyway, because the record was going to be written by someone and she had spent her life making sure it was written by the right hand.

Ilyan stood at the middle of the repaired load path with the sun behind him and the count running under his feet and the river doing what the river did.

He started with what he had accepted, because that was the part the hall already agreed about and agreement was the only ground he had.

“In the first field, I accepted a duty sourced to the summons that brought me here. My identity, my report, and my attendance became public. It ended when the field closed. It did not give me authority over any witness, the household, the guild, or any district, and it never did.”

“And your name went on the stay,” Maren said, from a seat he had chosen in the middle rather than the east.

“My name went on the stay because I was the attached record in the case that produced it. That is an attachment, not an authorship. I have said it in every room I have been put in front of and I am going to say it once more, and then I would like to move to the part that is harder, because you are all here for the harder part.”

The conservancy cart owner leaned forward. “Go on.”

“Before the field, in the first week, I found a way to make the bridge’s missing condition legible in a hearing by attaching Neris Ro’s private flood account to the live emergency docket. I did not ask her. The account is preserved, it is marked disputed, and the names inside it were never consenting witnesses. I did that. I was not compelled, no one instructed me, and I told myself it would make the urgency legible in a room that had been given a great many reasons to go slow.”

Hesk Ardan said, “Did it work?”

“It made the urgency legible. It also put words into a public record that a person had chosen to keep, and I could not take them out, and the taking-out was worse than the putting-in because it was done as an administrative act on my behalf after the fact.” Ilyan kept his eyes on the middle of the room and not on the row behind it, where Neris Ro would have sat if a mother and a daughter could be summoned to prove that their own words had been used without leave. “That is the first place the plan failed and it failed inside a day.”

“And after that?” he called it from the front.

“After that, I asked the Bench to review a copied precedent. I asked for a review of the copy, not its cancellation. The Bench preserved the ledger and refused to hold local enforcement. The district knew the difference and collected anyway. That was a worse outcome than the one before it, and I am the reason it was on the table in that form.”

The cart owner from the eastern yard stood. “You are making it sound as if you are the cause.”

“I am making it sound as if I am one link,” Ilyan said. “The copy was made by a clerk who was lawfully permitted to make it. The levy was applied by a marshal acting inside his authority. The renewal was entered by another clerk at an hour he was told to enter it. Every one of those is lawful. I am the reason the wording existed in a form that could be copied, and I am the reason there was an attached name worth citing when somebody wanted to say the burden was already decided by a court.”

Maren had his slate on his knee. “Then answer the district’s question. The stay says the review comes at the seventh morning bell. The stay is attached to your name. Does your name promise a general authority to maintain a route, or does it not?”

“It does not promise anything,” Ilyan said. “I cannot make it promise anything, and if the text could be read that way, the text is wrong and should be corrected in the record, and I have asked the assembly to say exactly that.”

“Can you affirm that your first testimony at the bridge was made under an understanding that the remedy would remain bounded to the ninth bridge, and that the wording you accepted was general in form?”

“No,” Ilyan said.

The hall reacted before he had finished the word.

“Explain the no,” Dain said.

“Because of the word *provisional*, and because of what I did not ask the stay to say.” He kept his hands at his sides. “The stay said the Ro household may use this bridge until a review, and it said the charter stands, and it said the review comes at the seventh morning bell. Read that carefully. Every limit in it is a limit on the court — when the court must look again. Nothing in it says a clerk may not take the shape of it and use that shape somewhere else, and I did not ask for that, because asking would have meant asking to amend a charter, and I was relieved not to have to. I liked being the man who did not need to amend anything. I have been using that relief as a shield ever since, and it is the reason a clerk in Merefen could point at my name and call a levy a precedent.”

Sera’s stylus moved once. She did not look up.

“You are describing a gap as though you had put it there yourself.” Maren said.

“I am describing a gap I left there. The remedy I proposed was the one I named in the first hearing, and I named a bridge, a crew, a household, a load path, and a review. I did not name a region. When the region appeared, it appeared because the stay I accepted was bounded only toward the court, and I have been letting people believe I put it there myself.”

“Then you are not the author,” Maren said, “and you are also not the man who can unmake it.”

“No,” Ilyan said. “I did not write it. I made it easy to write.”

He let that sit. There was no version of this examination in which the sentence sounded good, and he had known that before he opened his mouth, and he had said it anyway, because the alternative was a sentence about other people’s fault that the hall would have preferred.

Hesk Ardan put his elbows on his knees. “I crossed four times to hear whether the district could use your name to make us carry a levy for a road we did not build. Can I?”

“Not any more,” Ilyan said. “The assembly has made your citation worthless. Your district may not enter my name as the source of a schedule or the bearer of labor. What you may still do is read the finding, read the work slate, and read what was actually done by whom. That is all the authority I have ever had and it does not survive being quoted.”

Ardan looked at the conservancy cart owner beside him, and something passed between them that Ilyan was not part of. Then Ardan stood up, unbuckled a satchel, and took out a folded sheet.

“The conservancy withdraws its citation,” he said. “Not because you are innocent. Because a citation that is worthless can still be used to look at us in a council room. We will not carry it and we will not pretend we ever did.”

He left with two of the four men who had crossed with him. The eastern cart owner stayed, and so did Dain, and so did Maren, and the hall became smaller and much harder.

The conservancy’s withdrawal went out before the third bell, and with it went the only public support Ilyan had gained in the region that did not live on this river.

Then Moot asked the question he had clearly been carrying since the first bell, in the tone of a man reading an item off a tray because someone had written it on the tray.

“A paper came into the court slot on the night of the renewal pause,” he said. “Gray stock. A custody code and a route line. One sentence. *At the final route, completion erases the cause of refusal.* The record says you are the attached record-holder, and that the sentence is a reduction of wording from your own signed report. I am required to ask whether the claimant disputes that reduction.”

“That is correct,” Ilyan said.

“Do you affirm that the sentence is your wording?”

“Yes.”

The hall went quiet in a different way. Maren stood up.

“Then the Bench has a pre-arrival paper carrying the attached record-holder’s private language,” he said, “and you have just affirmed it in a public room.”

“Yes.”

“And the receipt mark is dated before the ninth-bridge public notice.”

“Yes.”

“And you have not produced the sequence that would let a clerk compare it.”

“No, because producing it means putting my Earth reproduction sequence into a public record permanently, in a room where two districts are writing down every word I say.”

He was being asked whether he would produce it, and he answered in the sentence that was worse for him, which was that he did not get to keep a version of his own words the districts could not check. His report had entered the public case when the Bench attached his name. He had already given a copy of the reproduction sequence to one assembly for neutral comparison. A second copy went to this room, it entered the record, and it stayed there, and after today he could not say that only a clerk had ever seen it.

Maren’s mouth moved. “That is a considerable thing to hand a room that has just decided you are a record and not a permit.”

“Yes,” Ilyan said. “That is what a record is.”

He wrote the sequence out on the clerk’s tray paper while the room watched, in the small cramped figures he had used for defect reports for a decade, and Moot entered it, and Ilyan felt the specific, childish relief of a person who has just made himself smaller in a room full of people who had been expecting him to be larger. It was not courage. It was bookkeeping. It was the only honest reply available to a man who could not produce a villain and did not want to be one.

Sera spoke for the first time in two hours.

“There is one correction to the record,” she said, standing. “The claimant has twice described the attached name as the reason a copy was possible. The attached name was not the reason. The public wording was the reason. The name is a handle on the wording, and a handle can be pulled by anyone with a hand.”

The clerk wrote it. It was accurate, it made his own part in the chain smaller, and she had done it in front of the room without looking at him.

The examination was closed at the fourth bell with a formal notice that Ilyan read twice before he understood that he had come out of it with less than he had gone in with.

**PUBLIC ACCOUNT ENTERED. THE WITNESS ANSWERED FOR THE ATTACHED NAME, THE FILED DISCLOSURE, AND THE SCOPE OF HIS FIRST TESTIMONY.**
**THE WITNESS DECLINED TO CLAIM GENERAL AUTHORITY AND DECLINED TO CLAIM AUTHORSHIP OF THE COPIED WORDING.**
**THE WITNESS AFFIRMED THAT THE LINE HE ACCEPTED WITHOUT OBJECTION ENABLED THE COPY.**
**NOT ORDERED: A REVISION OF THE NINTH-BRIDGE CHARTER. NOT GRANTED: A BURDEN ON ANY PERSON OR INSTITUTION.**

They went back to the workroom, where the count had run all morning without needing anybody’s permission, and Oren had the wage board wiped and rewritten for the afternoon and did not offer sympathy.

“Your name is off my slate now,” he said. “You are not on the authority row any more and you are not on the labor and you are not on the wage. Read it yourself, it is a cleaner board than yesterday’s.”

Ilyan read it. It was cleaner. It was also the board of a span with a named hole in its authority, and the district would fill that hole or leave it, and the assembly’s word would hold the fill to a certain shape and not further.

Sera had gone ahead to the records room with the examination slip, because there were two documents in the district files that she wanted before the day’s clerks left, and she did not invite him.

He found her there an hour later, standing at a long table with a schedule book open in front of her and a lamp pulled close.

“Meridith Vale ran the district’s maintenance update yesterday,” she said, without preamble. “It is a routine act. When a schedule enters, the office updates the working book to match it so the crews work from the same version. The clerk signs the update line, the version number goes up by one, and the old text is retained in the file.”

“Retained where?”

“Retained in the file,” she said. “In the working book, the affected-party column is replaced.”

Ilyan looked at the book. The affected-party column was the leftmost of five, and it was printed rather than written, which was the point of it: a form field, filled in by a clerk, carried forward by every update until somebody changed it.

“Yesterday’s update replaced it,” he said.

“Yesterday’s update replaced it,” Sera said, “and the version number went from three to four, and the four is signed and timed and entirely lawful, and the three is in the file with a proper retention notice, and both of them say the same thing, which is that the Ro household and the ward both refuse the maintenance acknowledgment and the local arrangement continues on named work.”

She put her finger on the line and slid the book so the lamp was over the page.

“The version three page has been cut,” she said. “Not torn out. Cut. A hand’s width of the left margin, taken out with a blade along the binding gutter, and the cut is clean and there is no fiber left to argue with. It was done before the examination began. I asked the clerk who set the version-four page for the time on the removal and there is no removal on the version-four page, because a removal in a routine update is not an event. A replacement is not an event. The word *replaced* is doing the work of a crime.”

“The replacement column was prepared somewhere,” Ilyan said.

“It was prepared from an extract.”

“Whose extract?”

“That is the question I have been asking for a week, and today I have a better one.” She did not touch the book, and he understood that the not-touching was the whole of her character in this room. “The extract is not a schedule extract. Look at the type. The replaced column is not filled in by hand. It was cut from a printed form.”

The lamp hissed.

“Somebody printed a form with our household’s line already filled in as *provisional occupancy, no recognized tenancy*,” Ilyan said.

“Somebody printed a form,” Sera said, “with a household in it, before anyone thought to use it, and then handed it to a maintenance clerk as a template, and the maintenance clerk did his job and used it, and the guild’s wage bill came out half a hand lighter every day, and the family that was paying did not know they had been entered as an extract.”

She looked at him.

“This is what I meant. At the archive desk, with the request in front of us and your name nowhere in it. Do not look for a villain. Look for custody. Who printed it, who held it, who handed it over, and how many ordinary steps there were between a piece of paper and a family paying for a bridge with its own footsteps.” She closed the schedule book and left her palm flat on the cover. “You will want to say that somebody is building a case. It is more likely that somebody filed a template and nine people did their jobs.”

“Nine people,” Ilyan said. “And one instruction with no name on it.”

“And one code,” Sera said. She did not open the book again. She took a slip from her sleeve instead and set it beside the lamp, and Ilyan saw that it was a rubbing she had taken an hour earlier from the back of the district’s work slate.

Three marks, in the margin, in the flat pressed hand that had entered a case reference at seven in the morning.

**C—2 / …**

The slash was real and the code was real and after it there was a scrape, wide and shallow and deliberate, where something had been taken off the slate with a tool rather than left unfinished.

“An incomplete routing mark,” Ilyan said.

“An incomplete routing mark on a district work slate, in the same hand, in the same office, as a copy of my family’s withdrawal request. That is where the hand is the same. We have it on nothing else — not on the plate, not on the levy ledger, not on the paper of his that predates his arrival.” Sera picked the rubbing up and put it in the sleeve, which meant it was leaving the room. “It points somewhere. It does not tell us who, and if you spend the next three days trying to make it tell us who, you will spend the bridge review on a chain instead of on a bridge, and the bridge is the only thing in this city that will still be standing when the bell rings.”

Ilyan did not argue. He wanted to argue, in the specific way he had wanted to argue all week, which was to say that the shape was now unmistakable and that unmistakable shapes should be followed before they cooled.

But the schedule book was closed, and the wage board in the workroom had his name off it, and two men from a district he had never visited had ridden home to tell their council that his name was an ornament, and a mother had written four words to say she was not coming, and a child had sent nothing at all.

“Tomorrow evening,” he said. “You, me, the custody of a form. Not the villain.”

“The custody of a form,” Sera agreed. “And bring the wedge that came off the apron yesterday. I want to see whether a road wedge and a district work register were cut with the same kind of blade, because if they were, then somebody in this city is running out of blades, and that is a sentence I can say out loud in a room, whereas *somebody is removing refusal* is a sentence that makes a room empty.”

She went out with the book and the rubbing and the slip, and Ilyan stood in the records room with a schedule book closed under her hand and a cut margin he had not been permitted to touch, and thought about Meridith Vale at a table in Merefen at midnight with a seal and a blank line, doing the one thing he had been sent to do, correctly, at the hour he had been told, with his name going on the seal and not on the condition.

The form had been printed. Somebody had handed it over. Nine people had used it.

And somewhere underneath the lamp, in a margin scraped clean after the fact so that a code could not be read to its end, the chain went on, one office at a time, toward a record that no longer had a desk to be found in.

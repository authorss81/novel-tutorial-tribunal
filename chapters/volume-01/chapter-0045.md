# Chapter 45: The Money With Nowhere to Go

The interval was not a bell, and it behaved like a bell, which is to say that about a third of the people on that approach left and a third of the people who were not there arrived within eleven minutes, and the two thirds that remained discovered that they had work to do, and did it in full view of a court that was in recess.

Ardan's man got the salt cart off the west end at the first change of the recess, and it took nine minutes, and Ilsa stood on the deck with the beam and watched the whole of it and said nothing at all, which for Ilsa was a shout.

“Back to mark,” she said, at the end of it. “All the way. In about the time it takes to reverse a cart off a bridge with a full approach behind it, which is a sentence I would like written down, because it is the same amount of time as the whole of the pause and the pause is what the keeper does.”

“And the one finger.”

“Gone,” Ilsa said. “It came all the way back when the load came off, and it is at mark, and I am going to write *returned to mark on removal of the standing load* in the book and I am going to write it in my own hand and I am not going to write anything about what caused it to stop short in the first place, because I do not know, and a counter who writes a cause she does not know is the same woman who was writing a two when the figure was a band.”

Nobody argued with her about it, which Ilyan noticed, and which he understood an hour later to be the reason the rest of the recess had gone the way it did.

Puck came up out of the hatch with the rule in his hand and put it on the bench next to the count book, and did not go away, and after a while he said, to nobody, “I want to say the thing about the prop and I want to say it now, before a court writes anything.”

“Say it,” Oren said.

“You cannot re-bed that seat with the span loaded,” Puck said. “You knew that. Three weeks' cure, one day to open, one to bed, twenty days unloaded. That is the arithmetic and it is right. But you cannot take the deck off and get to the course with nothing under it, because there is nothing under it. There is lime and then there is not. So you need something under the deck holding it up while the seat is open, and it is not a baulk of oak, because the deck is oak and the oak is the deck. It is four props, and they are not the same four for three weeks, because they take the load that the course used to take, and when you shift a prop you shift it to a man and a bar, and it is a day's work each time you shift it.”

“How many days of that,” Joren said.

“Six. Eight if the tide is against you, and the tide is against you for a fortnight out of every month, and the next fourteen days are five of them.” Puck looked at his rule again. “And the props are the guild's own props, and there are four, and the guild uses them on the fourth bridge, and the fourth bridge is the one that is going to be carrying the traffic if this one comes down, and if the props are on the ninth then the fourth has none, and the fourth has a keeper nobody has ever asked to write down an hour, and the fourth's district has a schedule that has not lapsed, which is the reason a carrier would send the traffic there if this one came down.”

Nobody said anything for a moment, and Joren said, “Then that is what the toll is for,” and it was not a sentence anybody had asked him for, and he looked as surprised as anybody.

“The toll,” Puck said. “The eighth of a load-fee. Nine carts. It pays the whistle and the count and nothing else, and the whistle is at the ward rate every change and the count is at the levy rate out of the guild's own account, which is empty.”

“It pays six hands for two to eight bells on a called pause,” Joren said, “and it pays four shifts of props on an unloaded period, and the guild's levy rate is not the ward rate, and the ward rate is in Lio's book, and the ward has no name and no account and no address, and so the toll is the only money in this case that can be paid by a person to a person without a district being told which person to pay.”

Ilyan had stopped writing. He was standing at the bench with the pencil out and the page half full of numbers he could read and the rest of it — the part where a number became a duty — gone out of his head entirely, and the honest thing to do about that was to say so, and he said it out loud, to Puck, in the middle of a recess, in front of about sixty people, because he had decided somewhere around the nineteenth day that doing it quietly was the only remaining dishonest thing available to him.

“I can read your numbers,” he said. “Seven inches, four inches a day, four props, six shifts, five tides in fourteen. I can read all of it and I can add it up in my head and it is wrong by a factor of about two because I do not know which hand puts a prop in and how long it takes and I have been on this bridge for three weeks and I could not do it to save my life. If somebody asked me in a room what a standing load costs the ward, I would have to guess, and I have watched a district in this case guess for three weeks and it has cost a child a room.”

Puck considered him.

“You are the one who came under the hatch at the seventh bell and got me to look at the drain side,” he said. “I would not have gone down the drain side. I was going to go down under the seat and put my hand in where the hand goes and I would have found nothing and come up and said there was nothing there, which is what I said to Oren for four days.”

“That is not the same as—”

“It is worth a bucket,” Puck said, and went to get one.

Meridith Vale came down the lower steps at the second half of the recess with the case under his arm and no clerk with him, and nineteen years of clerkship came down those steps behind him in the way that a thing does when everybody on the approach has been waiting three weeks for the office to do its job and know exactly what the job is.

Nobody made a space for him. That was the detail Ilyan kept afterwards: the court cleared a space for Harrow, the guild cleared a space for Oren, the ward cleared a space for Neris, and the district's maintenance clerk came down onto a bridge that was already full and stood four feet inside the rope with his case under his arm like a man waiting for a train.

“Mr Vale,” Harrow said. “You are nineteen minutes early for a clerk's instinct and I have been expecting you since the fourth bell.”

“I have been sitting in a rented room off the weighbridge since the first bell,” Vale said, “with a roll and a schedule book and a form number, working out whether my district can pay a wage, and I have got an answer and it is no, and I have come down here to say it, and I would like to do it before the court sits again, because if I do it after I will have to do it in a register and I will do it worse.”

“Go on, then,” Harrow said. “In the recess, in the open, and Roke will take it and it will be in the field's record and not in the court's, and that is the right place for it, because it is a fact about an office and not a finding.”

Vale put the case down on the bench next to the count book and did not open it.

“Three things,” he said. “I have written them in the order in which they went wrong, which is not the order in which they will be read, and I would like the order I went wrong in.”

“The first is the pump,” he said. “D. Rusk entered two days of shortage in an arrangement in public, in her own hand, and did not have it corrected, and the arrangement was not a district document and had no district instrument behind it, and I said at the time that the office had no power here. The office still has no power here. What the office has this morning is a district council that has been sitting since the fourth bell on a day with nothing to do in it, and I have this morning's answer, and the district will pay the two days, out of the district's own road funds, at the ward rate, to the pump, and not to the ward.”

Dalla Rusk was at the top of the steps. She came down four of them and stopped, which was as much as Dalla had ever done in a room.

“You do not have to say thank you,” Vale said, without looking at her, and then, to Roke, “Enter that the district was told it was not required to say thank you, and enter who told it, because in four months somebody is going to read that the pump was paid and not know that a pump worker was told in public that her thank you was not required, and the difference matters to exactly one person and it is the one standing on the steps.”

“Entered,” Roke said.

“Then it is paid,” Dalla said, from the steps, “and I have said nothing, and I am going to go and turn the pump on for the day, and I would like it entered that the shortage is two days and not two and a half, because I counted it.”

“It is two days,” Vale said.

“It was two and a half on the day after the arrangement was written,” Dalla said, and went up the steps, and everybody on the approach heard it, and Roke wrote it, and Ilyan understood that the whole of the district's offer had just been accepted by the one person it was for, in five words, in public, and had immediately been corrected by a half-day, and that this was what a wage looked like when the person drawing it was in the room.

“The second thing,” Vale said, and he took a folded slip out of the case and did not open it, which Ilyan had learned to read as *I have written this down four times*.

“The district accepts a maintenance-review condition on the ninth-bridge charter. The district will pay the hour — and I am going to use the word *hour* because it is the district's word and I would like the court to see that the district is carrying its own vocabulary into this chamber and not somebody else's. The district will pay the hour at the ward rate out of the district's own road funds, on a named day-book line, entered by the ward's clerk, posted in chalk on the frame, and paid to the person who stood there.

“The district will pay the under-deck watch at two hands at the ward rate, from the first bell, at every change. The district will pay the pump's shortage. The district will pay the guild's hands at the levy rate for the period of the unloaded works, on a named line, in chalk, in public, which is more than the district has paid any guild in nine years and which I have had to argue for in an hour and a half with two men who do not know what a prop is.

“And the third thing,” Vale said, “is that the district cannot do it.”

The recess on the approach had the quality of a room that has been waiting for something and has just been told it is not coming.

Vale opened the slip and turned it round, and on the back of it, in his own square hand, was the reason, with the hour, which was what he had done on the supply slip a fortnight ago in an office off the weighbridge, and which was the single most useful habit in this entire case.

**THE DISTRICT'S ROAD FUNDS MAY BE PAID AT THE DISTRICT'S RATE. THE WARD RATE IS SET IN L. FENN'S BOOK AND IS NOT THE DISTRICT'S RATE. THE DIFFERENCE IS NOT A PRINCIPLE. IT IS A LINE IN A BOOK, AND A LINE IN A BOOK MAY BE PAID ONLY TO A BODY WITH A NAME. THE NINTH-BRIDGE MAINTENANCE WATCH IS A WARD WATCH. THE WARD IS NOT A BODY AND HAS NO NAME AND NO ADDRESS. THEREFORE THE DISTRICT'S ANSWER IS ARITHMETICALLY IMPOSSIBLE ON ITS OWN TERMS.**

“Nineteen years,” Vale said. “And that is the whole of it, and I have known it since the fourth bell, and I have come down here anyway, because the alternative is that nobody says it and a court rules on an instrument that cannot be paid and we find out in a year at a worse moment with fewer people in the room.”

“Mr Vester,” Harrow said, without turning round. “Say what that means in a sentence, and if you cannot, say that too.”

“It means the district has agreed to pay the ward and has no way to name the ward,” Ilyan said, “so the wage the district has offered is a wage that goes to a line and not to a person, and if it goes to a line then the person who stood there in the cold for six bells is owed money by a line, and a line cannot be owed anything, so either the person is not owed or the line is not a line, and the district cannot tell me which, and neither can I, and that is the difference between the district and me and it is not a difference in cleverness.”

“Correct,” Vale said, to Ilyan's back. “And I will tell you the other half of it, which nobody will put in a record because it is not a record, which is that in nineteen years the ward has not been a body for one single reason, and the reason is on the wall of the ward hall and it is a petition, and it is from a year when somebody named a ward and the naming was used to take a road off people who were not in the ward. That is why there is no name. It is not an oversight. It is scar tissue, and I have sat in nineteen years of it and I have never once been asked whose it was.”

Nobody asked. Harrow did not ask. That was the second thing that happened in that recess that Ilyan wrote down afterwards and underlined.

“There is a register,” Larch said, from the eastern stake, where he had been standing with the roll for the entire interval because a ward clerk is required to be present. “There is a register. The Ro household is in it. It is a participating party, pending verification, with an objection in the fourth column, and the register has seven columns and I wrote it, and the seven are party, span, source, hours, date, verification, and objection. And the household has a party and a span and a source and hours and a date and an objection, and it has no verification, because there is no verification date, because you cannot be verified without an address.”

“Read the columns out,” Vale said, and Larch read them out, because a clerk reads when asked and Larch had been asked.

“And say the one that is not there.”

“There is no column for a body that owes money,” Larch said, “and there is no column for a body that has refused, and there is no column for a person going. The register has a column for objection and no column for leaving, and I entered that on the day I entered the household, on my own authority, in my own hand, and I would write it again.”

“Then the district has a party it may pay and a party it may ask and a party it may not pay, because the thing that would let it pay is the same thing that would let it take a road off them,” Vale said, “and I have been a clerk for nineteen years and I have never before watched a case arrive at that door.”

He picked up his case.

“I am not asking this court to solve it,” he said. “I want that on the record too. The office has no power here. If this chamber writes a duty on a body that has no name, then the office will be the body that fails to pay it, in public, on a day-book line, in chalk, in front of the frame, and the failure will be in my hand, and it will be the same hand that entered the household into a register so that one notice could serve a record and its parties, and I would like the court to know that the man who built the gate is the man who cannot pay the toll, and I have known that since the second hour of the recess and I have come down here to say it in daylight rather than have it said about me later.”

Harrow came back onto the span at the end of the interval, and did not go to the middle of the deck, which told Ilyan that he had been thinking about the deck.

“Court sits,” Harrow said. “The district's answer is in the field's record, at the district's request, in the district's own words, with the reason on the back of it in the clerk's hand, and I am going to say one thing about that before the court takes it up, and the district may object and the objection will be preserved.

“The reason is in the record. That is not a small thing. In nineteen years of this court the ordinary reason is *authority* and the answer is no, and the reason takes two lines and the answer takes none. Mr Vale has entered the true reason, which is that a book of this office has no line for a body that is not named, and he has entered it in the handwriting of the man who could be blamed for it, on the back of a piece of paper, with the hour on it. The court is going to treat that as a produced document and not as a courtesy, because a courtesy is a thing you can withdraw in a year and a document is a thing you cannot.”

“Roke,” he said. “Enter the district's answer as produced, with the schedule of payments and the impossibility, and the ward rate as named in L. Fenn's book, and the eight columns of the register as it stands, which is seven. Do not enter a remedy. The court has three remedies on the table and it has just been handed a fourth fact, and the fourth fact is that all three of them are unaffordable for a reason that is a line in a clerk's book and not a matter of anyone's intention.”

The System put the produced document into Ilyan's sight, and the numbers came first and the hole came second, which was how everything in this case had arrived for three weeks.

**DISTRICT'S ANSWER, PRODUCED. PAYMENTS OFFERED: THE STOPPED HOUR, AT THE WARD RATE, NAMED DAY-BOOK LINE, POSTED IN CHALK, PAID TO THE PERSON WHO STOOD THERE. THE UNDER-DECK WATCH, TWO HANDS, THE WARD RATE, FROM THE FIRST BELL, AT EVERY CHANGE. THE PUMP'S SHORTAGE, TWO DAYS, ENTERED AS TWO AND A HALF ON THE DAY AFTER THE ARRANGEMENT WAS WRITTEN. THE GUILD'S HANDS, LEVY RATE, FOR THE PERIOD OF THE UNLOADED WORKS.**
**SOURCE OF ALL FOUR: THE DISTRICT'S OWN ROAD FUNDS.**
**NOT PAID BY ANY DISTRICT: THE PROPS. FOUR, FROM THE GUILD, TAKEN FROM THE FOURTH BRIDGE. SIX TO EIGHT SHIFTS. NO WAGE LINE EXISTS FOR A PROP.**
**THE WARD RATE MAY BE PAID ONLY TO A BODY WITH A NAME. THE WARD HAS NO NAME.**
**THE COURT IS NOT ASKED TO SOLVE THIS. THE OFFICE HAS NO POWER HERE.**

Eleven lines, and the eleventh was the only one Ilyan had ever wanted to see in a record in this city, and it was the only one that a clerk had put in at his own risk with the hour on it.

Sera looked at it for a while, at the advocate stool, with the case file shut on her knees.

“Then the court has a question,” she said, “and it is not which remedy. It is what a document that names a duty on a body can be written so that the duty is payable, without writing the thing that takes the address away again.”

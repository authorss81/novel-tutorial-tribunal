# Chapter 21: The Guild Counts the Work

The guild counted its work in hours, because hours were the only thing it could not argue about.

Oren Sedge wrote them on a slate in chalk that had to be wiped and rewritten every morning, since slate was cheap and truth was not. The board hung at the back of the workroom where the salt damp got at it and the lamp smoke had filmed the top row to a soft gray.

**NINTH-BRIDGE GUILD ROSTER: 6 NAMED CREWS, 94 HANDS.**
**PAID BY THE LEVY FOR THIS SPAN: 4 HANDS AT THE COUNT BEFORE THE FIRST BELL, 6 AT EACH CHANGE, NONE AFTER DUSK.**
**THE SAME LEVY SPREAD OVER THE LOWER ROAD AND THE WATER ROUTE: 212 HANDS A DAY. NOBODY HAS 212 HANDS.**

Ilyan read the third line twice. “Two hundred and twelve.”

“Two hundred and twelve hands a day,” Oren said. He tapped the board with a thumb. “The district office put that number in front of the assembly on Tuesday and would like somebody to find it. They will find it. It will be found in a household, and the household will not know it is being asked.”

Below the numbers he had chalked a shorter list: lime, saltglass dust, binding fiber, lamp oil, and a fifth item marked simply *meal*, because a crew that had not eaten since the third bell could not be asked to count anything accurately.

“The levy buys the materials,” Oren continued. “It buys four meals a day for six crews. It does not buy the ninth crew, and it will not buy the road, and it will not buy the water route, and I have already told the district office all three of those things in writing.”

The workroom smelled of wet stone and hot metal. Outside, the first gray light came up under the span and found the repaired brace, the drive pin, and the pale drift line that had not returned to its iron ring since the night field. The line was a finger’s width off. Since the field it had not moved again either. That, Oren said, was the good news.

“The span is not running away,” he said. “It is settling into a shape it does not like. Those are different problems and only one of them gets better by waiting.”

Ilyan opened his notebook, then looked at the board instead of the page. “I want the work. All of it. Not the argument about whose work it is.”

Oren’s eyebrows lifted a fraction. “That is a different request from the one I expected.”

“What did you expect?”

“That you would ask me to say the stay has become a regional obligation, so the Bench can treat the ninth bridge as the first of several and pay for all of them out of one fund.” He set down the chalk. “I would have said no. I would have said it in front of the road.”

“I would not have used that sentence,” Ilyan said. “I do not have the standing, and it is not true.”

Oren studied him for a moment, then wiped a clean strip of the board and wrote a third line.

**NINTH-BRIDGE WORK, NAMED: west-joint brace and pin; load-count at every change; warning bell sounded before and after; saltglass dust and lime for the joint course; eastern approach wedge (road crew, not guild).**

Ilsa Bray was already in the doorway with a hood down and a load-counter’s cord around her wrist. She was in her middle years, narrow-shouldered, with the permanently squinting look of someone who spent her life reading a scale from four feet away.

“Before the first bell,” she said, reading the board, “two hands at the west joint and two at the count. At the load change, four hands. After dusk, none, because nobody counts a load they cannot see.”

“Nobody counts a load they cannot see,” Oren agreed. “That sentence is worth more than the whole levy.”

Jessa and Mero came down the workroom steps. Puck followed with a coil of fiber over one shoulder and an expression that suggested she had already been told there would be arithmetic. Oren did not introduce them. He handed out the day’s slate copies, and each person read their own line before speaking, which Ilyan noticed and did not comment on.

A road party arrived at the public approach below, led by Joren Vell, who had walked up from Merefen in the dark with two roadkeepers and a handcart of his own tools. He had come because the ninth bridge’s eastern approach drains onto the lower road and the lower road had begun, in his phrase, to *answer back*.

“The approach is a road problem,” Joren said, before Oren had finished the third line. “The span is a bridge problem. If you set the wedge from the bridge side you will close the drainage and put lime in the ward’s tanks by the second bell.”

“If you set it from the road side you will never get the joint true,” Oren replied. “I have watched road crews do that for thirty years.”

“I have watched bridge crews do the other thing since the field.”

The two of them stood on opposite sides of the same line of chalk and did not pretend to agree. Ilyan felt the old pull toward a single sentence that would settle it, and let it go, because there was no sentence that would make a wedge true from two directions at once.

Neris Ro came up the lower steps with Tamsin behind her, moving carefully. Tarin’s escort had walked them from the shelter, and the escort would walk them back at dusk, because the stay still moved them across the city like luggage on a handcart. Tamsin’s leg was healing badly. She carried the blue scarf from the bridge rail folded over her arm.

Neris went straight to the board. She had one question and she asked it before anyone could make her feel watched for asking.

“My shift at the dye-yard starts a half bell before yours,” she said. “I cross the bridge at a quarter bell. Am I breaking the load count by crossing when your count is not running?”

Oren did not answer quickly, which Ilyan came to understand was how Oren marked respect.

“Your crossing is on the repaired load path and you walk it, so the count barely moves. It is not breaking. But it is not *named* either.” He looked at the board. “The stay names the Ro household. It does not name the hours. If the hours are not named, then any clerk can decide later that they were not permitted hours, and the household is the one that cannot prove otherwise.”

“Then name them,” Neris said.

Oren chalked one more line. **RO HOUSEHOLD CROSSING HOURS: FIRST BELL TO DUSK, WALKING LOAD, NO CART.**

Tamsin looked at the bell hanging above the western approach. “Does the bell sound when the count is running?”

“It sounds before and after,” Oren said. “Not during.”

“Why not during?”

“Because during the count, everybody is holding something.” He almost smiled. “A bell that rings while nine people have iron in their hands is a bell that gets ignored. That is how the bell stops meaning anything, and then the next time it rings, nobody moves.”

Tamsin absorbed this with the seriousness of a child being told something adult. She looked at the bell again, and Ilyan could see her counting something under her breath that she kept to herself, exactly as she had at the Saltwheel work line. He did not ask. Some limits were the point of them.

Maren Kett arrived at the top of the steps with a clerk and a folded paper, and he did not wait for the workroom to finish its morning.

He handed the paper to Sera, who read it standing in the doorway, and Ilyan understood from the fact that she read it that it was a written mandate, because the eastern district did not travel without one.

“Three districts now cite the ninth-bridge stay,” Maren said. “Eastmere. Merefen. And the lowland conservancy, which is a body of nine cart owners who have never in their lives convened as a body. They cite it as a regional obligation. They cite your name in the same breath. I have come to say that your case has left the bridge.”

“It left the bridge on its own,” Ilyan said. “A clerk in Merefen copied the wording out of a public extract. I did not send it and I cannot recall it.”

“That is not what I came to say.” Maren turned to the board. “I have come to say that if the ninth bridge’s stay is going to be treated as a regional precedent, then the region must be able to see what it costs. Your stay survives because Oren Sedge’s crew performs a named inspection. That is the whole mechanism. Give a district the same remedy without the inspection and you have given them a hole with a name on it.”

“Then the region should require the inspection,” Ilyan said.

“The region cannot require it. The guild will not allow it. The district will not pay for it. And the court has already told us what it will do when we ask it to stop a district from doing lawful work in the meantime.” Maren said it without heat. “The Bench preserved our levy ledger and refused to hold our enforcement. It said the difference was on the record. It is on the record. We are still collecting.”

The words landed in the workroom and stayed there. Ilyan had filed that request himself, in a different matter, and had been proud of the distinction it produced. He had never once considered what a district would do with a distinction it had won.

Sera folded the mandate. “You want a legible common rule.”

“I want one rule a clerk can apply at three in the morning without an advocate present,” Maren replied. “The burden belongs to the person whose labor or property carries it. Everything else is a speech.”

“That rule tells a clerk who pays,” Sera said. “It does not tell a person that they may say no.”

“The ward is not in this workroom.”

“No,” Ilyan said. “The ward is not in this workroom, and neither is the district office, and the bridge does not care. That is the argument, Maren, and you have just made it for us.”

Maren looked at him for a moment and then, unexpectedly, agreed. “I have. I would still like the rule.”

The inspection began after the second bell.

Oren’s crew ran the west-joint sequence the way they always had: brace, pin, count, release, return, count. Ilsa read the scale. Jessa called the count. Mero set the weighted beam. Puck watched the water line under the deck, where a seep had opened a new stain since the night field. Ilyan stood on the maintenance line with his notebook closed, because Oren had told him the first morning that a person who counts the load and a person who watches the person counting the load cannot both be done by the same pair of eyes, and Ilyan had decided to be the second one for a while.

The sequence ran. The count came back to the mark. The pale drift line held.

Then Oren did what he had done every morning since the stay, which was ring the warning bell twice and wait for the span to release so the crew could swap a foot and check the underside of the eastern approach plate.

The bell rang. Nothing happened.

Oren rang it again, longer. The span sat where it was.

He lowered the rope and stood looking at it the way a man looks at a door that has stopped being a door.

“No release,” he said.

“Your line is wrong,” Mero said.

“My line is right.” Oren turned to the wall, where the current certified plate hung in its frame with the district’s fresh seal. He read it, and then he read it again with the slow patience of a man looking for a word that used to be there. “The charter says the span is released for pause by the Bench or by the district maintenance office. The keeper may summon. The keeper may not release.”

He set the rope down.

“That is the missing term,” Ilyan said.

“That is the missing term with teeth on it,” Oren said. “The old plate let a keeper who has named the work release the span when the count would not return. That is the whole maintenance review. The keeper names the work, the keeper sees the count, the keeper stops the load. I have been the keeper of this bridge for twenty-two years and under the current charter I cannot stop a cart.”

The workroom had gone entirely quiet. Somewhere below, a cart wheel found a rut.

“What happens when the count does not return?” Ilyan asked.

“Then the load stays until the district office sends a maintenance order, or until the Bench sends one, and the district office is eleven streets away and does not answer for the ninth bridge, and the Bench is not sitting.” Oren picked the rope up and coiled it. “I can ring a bell that says stop. I cannot make the span stop. So today I ring a bell that means nothing, and if a cart comes over in four minutes I will be standing here with a rope and a warning.”

Ilsa Bray said, quietly, “That is the same as the night the joint opened.”

“It is the same shape of the same problem,” Oren said. “On that night the load had already gone. Today it has not. That is the only difference and I intend to keep it.”

He sent Jessa to run to the district office with a written pause request, and Mero to the court slot with a copy. Neither body was required to answer. Both would be able to say, later, that they had been asked in time.

Ilyan wrote down the sequence, the bell, the missing release, and the eleven streets, and the eleven streets was the part that would kill someone if it stayed inside his own notebook.

They cleared the inspection with the span at the count. A lime cart came over at the fourth bell, and it crossed slowly, and nobody had to shout.

The guild’s work notice went up on the public approach an hour later, nailed to the frame beside the safety order. Oren wrote it himself so that the hand on the slate would be a hand anyone could compare to the old plate.

**NINTH-BRIDGE WORK NOTICE — DAY OF THE REVIEW COUNT.**
**WORK: west-joint brace and pin; load-count at every change; warning bell before and after; saltglass dust and lime for the joint course.**
**HANDS: 4 at the count before the first bell, 6 at each load change, none after dusk. Paid from the bridge levy at the levy rate.**
**BURDEN: the guild. The levy pays for the guild’s named work and nothing else.**
**ATTACHED RECORD: VESTER, I. — documentation of sequence and public record. NO MAINTENANCE LABOR ASSIGNED.**

The last line was not an insult. It was the accurate description of what Ilyan was, and Oren had written it because the guild did not have the wage fund to pay a man whose job was to hold a notebook.

By midday, four people who had never spoken to Ilyan had read that line and understood it four different ways.

A cart owner from the eastern yard told a bridge laborer that the foreign record-holder had been excused from the labor, which meant the labor was not real, which meant the stay was a favor and not a finding. A guild apprentice read it as protection and told two ward carriers that the bridge man had been written off the books, which was not true and which he would repeat for a week. A district clerk said the notice confirmed that no named body carried the maintenance, and therefore no named body could be asked to pay for it, and therefore the district would have to schedule the work itself. And a woman selling boiled beans outside the record shop said, to nobody, that a man who could write down everything and lift nothing was exactly the sort of person she had left her country to get away from.

Maren Kett read the notice standing at arm’s length, the way he read everything, and then he said the thing that made the day dangerous.

“Then it is not the keeper,” he said, “and it is not the district. It is the record. The record is him. He is attached to the finding, the finding is attached to the work, and the work has no labor on it. Somebody is going to decide that the name at the bottom of that slate is the thing that tells the guild what to do next, because there is nothing else on the page.”

Ilyan looked at the notice. Oren’s chalk was already going soft at the edges in the salt air, and the last line, the accurate one, was the line that had become the most dangerous thing on the board.

“That is not what the line means,” Ilyan said.

“It is what the page says,” Maren replied. “You have spent a week discovering that what a page says and what a page means are two different objects. Ask your ward whether anyone in Merefen believes your meaning or the page.”

From the eastern approach, the bell gave one clear note, because a cart had come over and the count had been called. Oren’s crew moved. The span held. And the notice on the frame went on saying, in a keeper’s plain chalk, that the man attached to the record carried no work at all, which was true, and which the district had already begun to read as permission.

# Chapter 23: The Name on the Load

The work slate went up at first light, and it went up wrong.

It hung on the same public frame as the guild’s notice, one board below the other, so that anyone crossing the approach read both. Oren’s notice named the work and named nobody’s authority. The slate above it named a different thing, in a clerk’s neat hand, in the authority row where a bridge work slate has always carried the name of whoever answers for the day’s load.

**AUTHORITY FOR TODAY’S WORK: VESTER, I. — attached record, ninth-bridge stay.**
**WORK: west-joint brace and pin; load-count at every change; warning bell before and after; eastern approach wedge.**
**GUILD: ninth-bridge guild, six crews, wage fund as posted.**

Ilyan read it from the bottom of the approach, and the distance gave him a moment in which he did not have to decide what to feel.

Oren had already seen it. He was standing beside the frame with his arms folded and the particular stillness of a man who is being extremely careful not to shout at a document.

“It says you approve the schedule,” Oren said.

“It does not approve anything,” Ilyan said. “I have no authority over that schedule. My standing is limited to the ninth-bridge case, and even inside that case I hold a record, not a permit.”

“That is what you said yesterday to the district man and I believed you. I am telling you what the board says.” Oren pointed at the frame without touching it. “The board is what the cart drivers read. The board is what the district office will send back to its own file. And the board says the foreign record-holder approves the day’s work on the ninth bridge.”

Meridith Vale was standing on the paving with his case under his arm, entirely unsurprised.

“Entry is lawful,” he said. “A slate is a schedule record. A schedule record carries an authority row. I entered the attached record-holder in the authority row because the attached record-holder is the reason the schedule exists, and a schedule that does not name its authority is the exact defect we spent a week identifying in the Saltwheel petition.”

“That is circular,” Ilyan said. “I am in the authority row because the schedule was built around my record. That does not make my record an authority.”

“It makes it the named source,” Vale said. “I did not write your name there because it is powerful. I wrote it there because the district is not permitted to schedule bridge work for a case it is not party to, and the only way to schedule work for this case is to name the case.”

Oren spoke without looking away from the frame. “Then put the guild at the top of the board. The guild is the answer for the day’s load. That is not a courtesy. If the count does not return, the crew is the one who has to stand under the span and find out why, and I will not have their hours on a board that says a man who writes things down approved them.”

Vale considered this. “The guild can be entered as the operating crew. The authority row is a different row.”

“Then leave the authority row blank and let the file say a schedule was run without an authority,” Oren said. “That is a true sentence and I will defend it in front of anybody you like.”

Ilyan felt the argument he could have made arrive fully formed in his chest: that a blank authority row on a public work slate was exactly the gap a later clerk would fill with something worse, and that a name, even the wrong name, was at least a name that could be examined. It was a good argument. It was also the argument of a man who had spent his life believing that a documented wrong was halfway to a fixed one, and the whole difficulty with that belief is that it holds true right up until the day somebody uses the document.

He was not going to be the thing in the frame. Not this morning, not with a cart driver already out there reading the board before he reached his own shaft, and not on the strength of an argument that only worked at all if his name stayed on it.

“Leave the authority row blank,” he said. “And put my name in the observation row, where it belongs.”

Vale wrote that. The observation row had a different word for it — *present, not directing* — and Ilyan found the phrase humiliating in a way he had not been prepared for, because it was accurate.

Joren Vell was already at the chalk line. He had come up from the lower road before light and had spent the first hour of the morning refusing, correctly, to let anybody put a load on the approach while the authority row on the frame was still blank.

“Tell me one thing before you count,” he said to Oren. “Does the public test run the span or the approach? Because those are two different bridges, and the district will pay for whichever one you say out loud.”

“The span is the span,” Oren said. “The approach is a road and the road is Joren’s.”

“The approach is what has been killing your count.” He put his hand on the eastern curbstone. “On Tuesday a cart came round too fast off that curb and walked two fingers out of the joint and came back, and if it had walked three I would have found it before you did, because I watch the curb. You count the scale. I watch the line. Neither of us is watching the other thing and we have been calling that one job.”

“You are telling me my crew is half a crew.”

“I am telling you your crew is a crew and a road, and you have been writing them down as one line.”

Oren looked at the eastern curbstone for a while and did not say anything clever, which was the answer. Ilyan had put the two men in separate columns in his notebook two days earlier and then crossed the line between them out, and it turned out that the columns had been the problem and not the crossing.

The public inspection began at the third bell. Tarin had marked a neutral boundary across the approach with rope on short stakes, and a public witness list on a board beside it, because the inspection could not be a spectacle and could not be a private guild matter either. People stood where they liked inside the rope. Outside it, the carts waited, and the cart owners stood with them and watched, because nothing on this bridge had ever been cheaper to watch than to cross.

The first count ran clean. Ilsa read the scale aloud. The joint returned to its mark. Jessa called the count in the guild’s old rhythm, three short and one long, and the sound of it went out across the river and came back thinner.

The second count ran clean and slower.

Then the third count had a cart on it.

It came out of the eastern yard loaded with coarse salt and a tarpaulin lashed over the near side, and it was heavier than the posted limit, and it arrived at the boundary in the middle of a live count because the driver had come up the road behind two water carriers and had decided, somewhere in the last hundred yards, that the count was not going to stop him.

The driver saw the slate on the frame.

Ilyan watched him see it. He was close enough to watch the man’s hands, and what happened in those hands was not defiance. It was the flinch of a person trying not to be the man who had gone over a ninth-bridge work slate with the bridge record-holder’s name in the authority row on it. The driver pulled the wheel toward the outside, took the turn at the eastern apron at a speed he had never taken it before, and the near wheels went off the wedge line and struck the curbstone with a crack that had no ring in it.

The span jolted. The scale jumped. Ilsa swore, Jessa stopped calling, and the water under the deck changed its note — a higher, thinner sound, the sound a channel makes when it has found a new way to move.

Oren was across the apron in four strides with his weight on the cart’s off shaft, not shouting, just making the animal stop. The driver jumped down and started apologising. Somewhere in the crowd a woman said, loudly and with real satisfaction, *That is what his name does.*

“The apron is cracked,” Oren said. “Not the joint. The apron. Four spans of the approach course, and it went under the outside wheel because he came round fast and off the line.”

“Because he was afraid of the board,” Joren said, and he was not accusing anybody. He was looking at the frame. “He took the turn wide because he did not want the cart under your name. If that slate had said the guild, he would have come round slow, because he knows our line and he would not have wanted to be the man who cracked our apron.”

Ilyan wrote that down exactly. He wrote: *the third count failed at the approach, not the joint; the driver changed his line after reading the slate; the change produced the failure. The name is not a force on the span. The name is a force on the people who read it before they reach it.*

It was the most useful thing his notebook had held in a week and it was useless to him in the way he most wanted.

A guild apprentice named Coll had been standing at the scale with the count book. He would not read the numbers aloud. Oren asked him twice. The third time, Coll said, in a low voice that was meant for Oren and reached the whole front row anyway, “I will call the count, but I am not writing my name in that book today while his is on the board. If the apron goes, the file will show who was on the board, not who was on the scale.”

Nobody argued with him. That was the part Ilyan could not put down later. A nineteen-year-old with a perfectly good memory of numbers, refusing a task that he was legally and morally required to perform, and the reason was a piece of chalk on a frame that was not his doing.

Oren took the count book out of Coll’s hands without a word, put the boy’s name beside his own in the entry for the count, and read the numbers aloud himself for the rest of the morning. He did not say the boy’s name to the district clerk. He did not need to; the entry was in the book, in two hands, which was the only place a refusal like that could be made to cost something.

Tovin Dain came through the rope with two district guards and the district clerk behind him. He had no standing on a bridge belonging to the lower ward at all, and he had not asked for any; he was there as the enforcing officer of the district whose petition had been adopted, which is a different office from the one whose maintenance slate was on the frame, and which he made sure everyone present understood.

“The bridge moved when the external witness was present,” he said. “I want that in the record in those words.”

“The bridge moved when a cart struck the apron,” Ilyan said. “The apron moved because the driver came round fast. He came round fast because there was a name on a board he did not want to be under. Write that instead. It is more useful to you and less useful to me.”

“It is less useful to me because it makes the bridge depend on a board,” Dain said. “Who authorized a board that changes how people drive?”

“I authorized the observation row,” Vale said, from behind the marshal. “The authority row was mine, and it is now blank.”

“You wrote his name in it for three hours this morning.”

“Yes.”

“On whose instruction?”

Vale’s face did not change. “On the standing instruction of the district maintenance office, which directs that a schedule built around a case carry the case in the authority row. The instruction does not name a person. It is a general instruction and it has been in use for four years.”

Dain looked at him. “Then the district owns the board.”

“The district owns the instruction,” Vale said. “The board is wood.”

Ilyan stepped out from under the frame and out of the crowd’s sightline, because he had spent the morning being the thing everyone on the approach was looking at, and he could feel the shape of the day forming around it: a bridge that everybody agreed was fine, except in the hour when his name was on it.

Oren came back from the apron with salt dust on his forearms. “The wedge holds,” he said. “The apron does not. And we cannot reset four spans of approach in a day, and the crew that could is the crew that has to keep counting tomorrow.”

“Name the work,” Ilyan said. “Not the bridge. The wedge. The apron. The hours. What does the load need for this afternoon to be safe, in hands, and who has them.”

That was the whole of his job, and it was much smaller than he had been led to expect, and he did it in nine minutes with a borrowed slate, while Oren named the work and Joren argued about the order and Ilsa refused to give a number before she had seen the apron twice. What came out of it was not an arrangement. It was a condition for one afternoon: four spans of apron marked out, two wedges, a load limit, the bell, and a line of chalk a cart driver could see from the seat. It had no hours, no wage and no end, and it lasted until the carts stopped, and Ilyan wrote it down in that form on purpose, because the day he wrote down something that looked like a settlement was the day somebody would schedule against it.

Nobody asked Ilyan’s permission. That was correct, and it was the day’s actual lesson, and it went into the notebook under a heading he had to write twice because the first word was wrong.

At dusk, when the counts were done and the chalk line was still standing and the guild’s notice and the district’s slate hung one under the other on the same public frame, Sera came down from the records room with the composite petition under her arm.

She had not been at the inspection. She had been comparing the two hands, which was what she was for.

“The composite petition is signed in the assembly office hand,” she said, laying the blackglass-backed copy on the frame’s ledge so that Vale could see it. “Three clauses, one hand, and the assembly office has had that hand since before the bridge closed. The copied line about the ward refusing at the cost of the shared road was added from a carbon strip by a hand that is not the assembly office and is not the district maintenance office either.”

“Show me,” Ilyan said.

She put the petition beside the work slate, which the district clerk had left on the frame rather than carry away, because a slate that has been read by a hundred people is not a document a clerk packs into a case.

The two hands were not close. The petition’s hand was quick, upright, and slightly cramped at the descenders, the hand of somebody copying fast from a strip. The work slate’s hand was level, unhurried, and pressed hard enough at the ends of the words to leave a small shadow in the wood.

“Different hands,” Ilyan said.

“Different hands,” Sera agreed. “And before you build a thing on that: the assembly hand is common. There are nine clerks in that office and all nine can write it. The maintenance hand is not common, because there is one maintenance clerk, and he is standing here, and he is not a liar.”

“I did not say he was a liar.”

“You were about to make a shape out of two hands.” Sera did not soften it. “I have watched you do it in a record shop and on a road and in my own family, and every time you have, the shape has turned out to have a person in the middle of it who is doing a job.”

Vale looked at the two documents for a while. “I did not write the petition,” he said. “I wrote the slate. I was sent to write the slate. The authority row was an instruction of the office, entered in a watched hour with the guild’s crew present as witnesses to the hour, and my name went on the seal and not on the condition. That is the same thing I did at Merefen, and you were all there for it.”

Nobody had an answer to that, because it was not an accusation. It was a man describing the shape of his own job to people who kept hoping the shape had a crack in it.

Ilyan looked at the blank authority row on the district slate, chalked in, empty, next to the observation row with his name in it and the words *present, not directing*.

“Show me what authorizes it,” he said.

Vale turned the slate over. On the back, beneath the day’s work lines, the district maintenance office kept its own authority column, and there, under the heading *external case reference*, was a line he had entered that morning:

**REF: NINTH-BRIDGE STAY / ATTACHED RECORD. ROUTE: C—2 (BRIDGE PLATE OFFICE). CASE COPY: 2 of 2. NO SEPARATE SCHEDULE LINE.**

“Case copy,” Ilyan said. “Two of two.”

“The first copy is in the office file,” Vale said. “The second is on the slate. That is what a case copy is.”

“Then who authorized a case copy onto a work slate?”

Vale turned the slate over once more and looked at the back the way a man looks at a door he has walked through four hundred times.

“That,” he said, “is not in the instruction.”

The light under the span was going. The warning bell gave one clear note for the last cart of the day, and the count came back to its mark, and the apron held because a wedge had been set in it and not because a name had been anywhere near the stone. Oren’s chalk was going soft on the guild’s notice above. Under it, the district’s slate said nothing at all in the row that mattered, and on the back, in a hand that was not the hand that made the copied burden, three words pointed at an office that no longer existed under that name and had never, as far as anyone could prove, been asked whether it should.

# Chapter 3: The Brace That Could Be Repeated

The falling span did not fall all at once. It moved in a sequence, each joint answering the last, and Ilyan saw the order before he understood the force.

Lower hinge first. Then the west support. Then the binding thread under the walking surface, bright silver-gray against the dark stone. The child was caught beneath the western approach, her body folded into a narrow pocket between the stair and a hanging brace. If he pulled her straight out, the loose brace would swing into the channel. If he pushed the span upward, the unlisted condition would travel through the bridge and settle somewhere else.

He had tested worse assumptions in game rooms where failure merely reset a character. He had never tested one with a child’s breath under the weight.

Tarin caught his shoulder before he could reach the plate. He pointed toward the escape opening as he spoke, and the meaning reached Ilyan through the gesture as much as the word. “No.”

“She is there.”

“You do not know the load path.”

“I know it is moving.”

“Moving is not the same as movable.”

Oren was already kneeling beside the stair. He set the coil of silver-gray cord on the paving, unspooled three loops, and placed a broad iron clamp against the lowest support. His hands did not shake. They moved with the awful steadiness of a man who had decided that panic would only add another person to the bill.

Tarin pointed to a narrow opening behind the wall. “Escape route. If the water reaches the second mark, you take it. If I tell you to run, you do not argue.”

“Where does it go?”

“Below the court.”

“Below the court is not an exit.”

“It is the exit I can control.”

Ilyan looked back at the span. “And the child?”

Oren tested the clamp with a short lever. “If I release this, the brace swings left. If I hold it, the west support takes the load. I can make a pocket for one person, maybe two. I cannot make the bridge accept a person it does not recognize.”

“What if we change what it recognizes?”

Oren’s eyes narrowed. “You are asking me to lie to a bridge.”

“I’m asking whether the bridge’s condition can be made visible.”

“That is not the same as—”

A bell sounded somewhere above. The lower passage trembled. Water struck Ilyan’s shoe.

Oren pulled the lever down. The clamp bit into the support with a sound like a tooth closing. The span stopped folding for a fraction of a breath. Tamsin cried out from beneath it.

Ilyan dropped to his knees at the edge of the pocket. “Tamsin Ro, can you hear me?”

A muffled voice answered. “Don’t pull.”

He withdrew his hand. “I won’t. Tell me what you can feel.”

“The stair is on my leg. The bell rope is stuck under the other one.”

Ilyan looked toward Oren. The bridgekeeper gave a short nod, as if the answer had confirmed something he feared.

“Is the rope tied to the warning line?” Ilyan asked.

Oren crouched within reach but did not touch the child. “It is tied to the western alarm. If it moves, the bridge hears a crossing request.”

“Can you free the leg without moving the rope?”

“Not with a pull.”

“Then give me a tool.”

Oren’s hand went to his belt. He removed a short drive pin, a flat piece of blackened steel with a bent end. He held it out, then stopped.

“You are not a bridgeworker.”

“No.”

“You may make the sequence worse.”

“Yes.”

“And if you make it worse, the next person on that stair carries it.”

Ilyan met his eyes. “Then name the step where I can be wrong.”

Oren studied him for a long moment. He pointed to a pale scratch in the stone.

“Put the pin under that line. Not the rope. The line marks where the support was true before the last crowd. Turn the pin when the water drops, not when the bridge moves. If you pull first, the clamp will take your arm.”

Ilyan took the pin. It was warm from Oren’s belt. He slid it beneath the marked line and waited.

Below, Tamsin breathed in a thin whistle. The bridge swayed.

“Neris,” Tamsin called. The name travelled through the stone as if the span had learned it. “I want my mother.”

Ilyan’s hand stopped. “She is coming.”

The words were not true yet. He had no way to know whether the woman on the upper approach could reach them or whether the crowd would be cleared away. He said them because a child trapped under a rule needed something that was not a guarantee. The lie formed in his mouth and stayed there.

Oren noticed. “Do not promise a person a rescue you cannot name.”

“She asked for her mother.”

“Then tell her what you know. The person on the upper road is Neris Ro. The person reaching you is not.”

Ilyan swallowed. “Tamsin, your mother is on the upper road. I can hear her, or I can hear the bridge. Which do you want?”

A pause. Water struck the stair.

“The bridge,” Tamsin said. “It lies more.”

The answer was so unexpected that Ilyan almost laughed. He marked it in his notebook, not as evidence but as a boundary.

The water dropped. He turned the pin. The support gave a hard click. Oren’s clamp slid a fraction, and the gap over Tamsin’s body widened. Tarin crawled to the edge, keeping his weight low.

“Take her arm only,” Tarin said. “I take the other. On the next bell, we move together. If the line tightens, release her.”

“Release her?” Ilyan looked at the child. “No.”

“If the line tightens, release her,” Tarin repeated. “The rope is not a promise. It is a warning device.”

Ilyan nodded. He could work with a rule that admitted what it was.

The bell sounded. Tamsin’s fingers closed around his wrist. Her grip was stronger than he expected. Tarin took her other arm, and Oren pulled the lever in short, measured strokes. The span lifted a hand’s width. Tamsin dragged herself toward the opening, one injured leg dragging, the other kicking free of the stair.

The binding thread under the bridge rang like a wire. Tamsin made a small, high sound. The red line in the iron ring flared.

“Stop,” Oren said.

Everyone stopped except the water.

For a moment, only the current moved. Then the bridge settled, and Tamsin slid onto the maintenance ledge. She was smaller than Ilyan had imagined, dressed for travel rather than work, with mud on one cheek and both hands wrapped around her mother’s scarf. A woman dropped from the upper stair and caught her before the bridge could react again.

Neris Ro landed hard on one knee. She had a worker’s belt with dye stains and a narrow iron tally loop hanging from it. She wrapped one arm around Tamsin’s shoulders and looked at Ilyan, at Tarin, at Oren’s clamp.

“Who told you to take her?” she asked.

“I did,” Ilyan said. “I asked Tamsin about the rescue. I did not ask you.”

“You did not ask me.”

“No. I was afraid you would say no.”

Neris’s face tightened. “Then you are afraid of the right thing.”

Oren lowered the lever. “The span is stable enough for a short carry. Move the child to the workroom. The next shift will make it worse.”

Neris did not move. “The register says Tamsin cannot use the bridge.”

“The register is not the child,” Tarin said.

“The register is what the bridge hears.”

Oren looked at Ilyan. “And you. What is your name?”

Ilyan touched the notebook. “Ilyan Vester. The notice called me First Defendant.”

Neris’s eyes narrowed. “You are the one who came through the wall.”

“Through a passage.”

“Does that make you less strange?”

“Probably not.”

That earned a breath that might have been a laugh. Neris looked down at Tamsin. The child’s eyes were closed, but her hand still held the edge of the ledge.

Tamsin opened one eye. “I can walk.”

“You can,” Neris said. “That is not the same as being able to run.”

“I want to walk.”

Neris looked at Ilyan. “Will you tell the court I said the stair trapped her?”

“Yes.”

“Will you tell them I want her name entered?”

“Yes, if you want that.”

“I do want her name entered. I do not want my flood account read aloud. I will tell you what happened, but I will not let you turn our whole life into a doorway for strangers to walk through.”

Before Ilyan left the ledge, Neris took a folded page from inside her coat. It was a private account of the flood that had driven their family from the previous bridge district. She had written the dates of the water, the number of registered travelers, the hour the warning bell had rung, and the names of people who had stayed behind.

“Use it to keep my daughter safe,” she said. “Do not use it publicly. The names belong to people who did not choose a court.”

Ilyan took the page but did not open it. “I will ask before I use any of it in a hearing.”

“Then ask me first,” Neris said. “And ask me again before you use anyone else’s name.”

Ilyan had expected consent to be a single word. He had not expected the conditions around it to be more important than the word itself.

“I can write what you ask,” he said. “I cannot promise the court will keep it private.”

“Then do not promise. Tell me what I can refuse.”

Tarin looked away, giving them the privacy of a public passage. Oren began securing the clamp with a second cord. Only then did a blackglass notice unfold above the emergency stair.

**THE SPAN IS STABLE UNDER THE CURRENT LOAD.**
**A CHALLENGE MUST NAME: CLAIMANT; AFFECTED PARTIES; DISPUTED CONDITION; BURDEN; REMEDY.**
**BEFORE THE NEXT BELL, THE COURT WILL ACCEPT ONE FILED RECORD OR RETURN THE DEFENDANT TO THE OUTER BOUNDARY.**

The notice did not say who would carry the burden. It did not say what a remedy cost. It gave Ilyan a shape where there had been a blank.

Tamsin lifted her head. “I’m not a condition.”

“No,” Ilyan said. “You’re a person.”

“Say it to the court.”

“I will.”

Neris took the child into her arms. “And say my name.”

“You’re Neris Ro. Tamsin’s mother. You need a safe route to work and housing, and you do not consent to your private flood account being used in public.”

Neris looked at him for a long moment. “That is a good beginning. It is not a promise that you will remember.”

“No,” Ilyan said. “It is a test.”

The next court bell sounded, and a clerk’s mark transferred the filing window into the midday session rather than closing it. The notice showed five empty lines beneath its heading, and the bridge shifted under their feet, waiting for someone to fill them.

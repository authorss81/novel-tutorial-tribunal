# Chapter 37: The Clerk Who Was Sent to Enter It

The print shop was on a court lane, behind a counter with a bell on a wire, and the bell was the most powerful thing Ilyan had encountered in this city that did not require a charter to use.

“A question a day,” the clerk said. “One person, one question, written or spoken, and if I do not know the answer it is still my day and I will say *not held here* and you may come back tomorrow and be told the same thing for a week.”

The clerk was Ondra Fell, and she had said this to four people in front of Ilyan while he waited, which meant she said it to everybody, and which meant it was not a rule about him.

“I have a question,” Ilyan said.

“You have a day,” Fell said. “It is not the same thing and people who think it is waste mine.”

He put the question on the counter in writing, because a question that was going to cost a person their day ought at least to be legible to the person spending it, and then he wrote it wrong twice and had to admit to Sera that he did not know the shape of the answer and therefore could not write the question.

She read his first attempt over his shoulder and made a small unhappy noise.

“You are asking who,” she said.

“I am asking who.”

“You will get a stamp, and you have already got a stamp, and you have been living off a stamp for twelve days.” She took the slate. “You may not write an *official* word you cannot define. Write *where*. Ask where a thing is held. Custody questions get answered. Questions about people get referred.”

“Where is a preservation authority?”

“That is still asking for a person.”

“It is asking where a document went,” Ilyan said, “and I have been told that is a custody question, and I would like to know where the ninth bridge’s maintenance-review condition is held right now.”

Fell looked at him over the counter for slightly longer than she had looked at the other three.

“That one goes to the index room,” she said, “and the index room is not me, and I will take you across because if I do not take you across you will stand at that counter until dark.”

---

It was Meridith Vale who opened the index room door, which Ilyan did not expect and did not at that moment understand.

He was standing in the district office two floors up with Sera, and the point of going down to the print shop first had been that the print shop was a public desk and the district office was not, and by the time Fell had said *the index room is not me* the whole shape of the morning had gone over onto its side.

“Meridith,” Sera said.

“I came to find out whether you were going to ask the desk about the authority,” Vale said. “If you were going to, I would rather tell you the terms than let you find out at a counter.” He had his case under his arm and no particular expression at all, which after twelve days was how Ilyan recognised him as a man being careful. “They will answer a custody question. They will not answer a question about an office. And if you ask about an office twice in one week, the shop sends the refusal to the district, because the shop answers to the district for its stock, and the district is me.”

“You told us to come here first,” Ilyan said.

“I told you I would rather tell you the terms than let you find out at a counter,” Vale said, “which is a different sentence and you turned it into a better one on the way down.”

The index room was two rooms behind the print shop, cold, badly lit, and full of bound ledgers on shelves that had been numbered by somebody long dead. Fell unlocked it and left the key in the door and went back to her counter, which was a kindness with a rule attached: a custodian present, a searcher not alone, and no carrying anything out.

Neral came in behind them with a lamp and a bundle of corded paper under his arm, because the lock-and-ledger shop bought its stock from the print shop and had done for nine years and could therefore walk in through the back at any hour, which was the only reason any of this was possible and which had taken Neral a bowl of stew and an argument.

“They cut it out of the binding,” Neral said, before anybody asked him anything. “Not a clerk. A binder. I have been buying this court stock for nine years and the shop does not re-cut paper, because paper comes in sheets off a stone and a re-cut edge is a re-cut edge. A bound volume is unbound and rebound by a binder, and there is one binder in this city who does the court’s stock, and he is not a clerk and he has no office and he would not know any of this mattered if you told him.”

“Neral,” Fell said from the doorway.

“I am saying it for the record,” Neral said, “and then I am going to hold the lamp, because the page is on the low shelf and it is the page you cut.”

He was right about the low shelf. The stock ledger for run one hundred and fourteen was on it, spine out, and the page was not missing; it was shorter than the pages on either side of it by a finger's width at the fore-edge, and in the gap where the authorization should have been, a page of the same size, the same stock, and the same week's watermark was bound in with a single thread of marbled paste that had not been there for nine years.

Fell let the silence stand for as long as it was worth.

“That is the whole of it,” Fell said. “That is what we have. It is a bound volume with a replaced page in it, and the shop did not do it, and there is a word for what has been done to it and the word is *relay*, and a relay is not a crime, and it is done about four times a year, and the reason it is done is that the court’s records are kept in duplicate and a volume gets damaged.”

“Who ordered the relay?” Ilyan asked.

“The shop does not keep the order,” Fell said. “The order is on the page that is not there. I have said the true thing four times now, in three rooms, and I would be glad if somebody wrote it down.”

Sera wrote it down.

“Ask your question,” Fell said. “You have a day and you have not spent it and you have been standing in a cold room for eleven minutes, and if you go back up those stairs without asking, I will remember you, and I remember a lot of people who waste a day.”

Ilyan asked his question.

Fell looked it up herself, from the authority's own index, which was a separate bound volume on the second shelf, and read out what she was allowed to read out, which was shelf, box, week, class, routing, and no names, because the index has no names on it; that is not a refusal, that is the form.

**STANDING AUTHORITY: PRESERVATION OF RECORDS IN PERIOD OF CONTINUITY. A STANDING AUTHORITY REMAINS IN FORCE WHILE THE CONDITION THAT REQUIRED IT REMAINS.**
**WEEK OF THE EXECUTION OF THE CURRENT NINTH-BRIDGE WORKING PLATE.**
**ITEM ONE: MAINTENANCE-REVIEW CONDITION, NINTH BRIDGE. TAKEN INTO PRESERVATION. ROUTING: C—2.**
**ITEM TWO: SEALED. CLASS: CONTINUITY REQUEST. ROUTING: C—2. NOT PRODUCED.**

Then, because Fell was a public servant with a professional conscience and because nobody had told her not to, she read the count as well.

“Eleven items that week,” she said. “All eleven routed C—2. Nine of them are routine and I could tell you what they are and I am not going to because it is not my day. Two are what I read you.”

“Eleven items in one week under one standing authority,” Ilyan said. “And the authority is a *standing* authority, so it does not name a person, and it does not expire, and the condition that required it is — what?”

“Continuity,” Fell said. “While the condition that required it remains. And I will tell you the honest thing about standing authorities, since nobody in this city appears to want to hear it from the desk that holds them. A standing authority is not a person who decided something. It is a piece of paper that keeps deciding the same thing, for as long as the emergency it was written for lasts, and it does not need a name because it was never meant to be argued with. It is meant to be obeyed while it is inconvenient to argue with it.”

Nobody had an answer to that, because it was the most defensible thing Ilyan had heard in this city, and it was defensible in the specific and dreadful way that only a rule with no exit can be.

“The second item,” Sera said. “A continuity request.”

“Yes.”

“I am not going to ask what is in it,” Sera said.

Fell looked at her.

“I am going to record that you did not ask,” Fell said, “because in nine years that is the first time somebody has stood in this room and had the opportunity and not taken it, and the shop keeps a note of that kind of thing, and I would like the note.”

Sera said, “You keep a note?”

“The shop keeps a note of every question asked here, because a public desk that cannot remember what it was asked is not a public desk.” Fell wrote one line in a small book. “That is a real answer and it is also a record, and I would like you to understand that it is both, and that this is a room where those two things have never once been separate.”

Ilyan took out his notebook and wrote the eleven items and the two classes and the routing, and stopped, and then went back and looked at the gap in the bound ledger where the authorization page used to be, at the marbled paste, at the thread.

“Neral,” he said. “You said a binder. Can a binder tell you anything? A date, a hand, whether the paste is nine years old or a week old?”

“No,” Neral said, from behind the lamp. “But I can tell you the paste is a shop paste and not a binder's, because a binder uses a hot paste and this is cold, and cold paste is what you use when you are putting a page into a book that is already dry and you do not want to take the whole volume apart. Whoever did it wanted the volume back in service the same day. That is not a person. That is a decision about a book.”

“An office,” Ilyan said.

“An office,” Neral agreed, “or a person with an office's instructions, and I know which of those two it is not, and it is not me, and I have been buying this stock for nine years and I would like it entered that I have been buying it for nine years and that I have never once been asked to produce a page.”

“Entered,” Fell said, and wrote it, and Ilyan watched a locksmith's counterman be given a line in a public desk's day book over the objections of nobody.

---

They went up to the district office at the second bell, and Meridith Vale was waiting for them on the stairs, and he had said everything he had come to say before Ilyan got the question out.

“Ask me,” he said. “Whatever it is. I have known since the fourth day that you were going to, and I would rather it were asked than inferred, because a thing that is inferred about me is a thing I cannot answer, and there is a man in this city who has been doing that to a woman on the lower road and she has had to refuse it five times.”

Ilyan put it plainly, because he had learned to.

“Did you make the form?”

“No.”

“Did you send the form to the print shop?”

“No. The standing print authority of this office is applied to routine forms, and it was applied to this one, and the run number is on the strip on the back of the ledger, and I have read that strip and I have never once read it before this week because it has never once told me anything I did not already know.”

“Then who sent it?”

“The office,” Vale said. “I do not mean a person. I mean the office's habit. There is a form for a weekly maintenance update that carries a printed affected-party column, and it exists because somebody, in some year, wanted the column to be the same on every schedule, and when I enter an update I use the column because that is what the column is for, and nobody has ever asked me what is in it and I have never asked.”

He set the case down on the partition and opened it, and took out the working schedule book, and turned to the update line, and put his finger on it.

“This is my hand. The hour is mine. The version is mine. That is all true and it is all mine and none of it is the reason. If you go looking for a reason, you will find one in a person, and there is no person, and when there is no person the only two places a person can be are the office and the pay, and you have already got the office and you do not want it.”

“What do I do with the pay?” Ilyan asked, and heard how the question sounded, and did not take it back.

Vale looked at him for a while with the flat professional attention of a man who had decided to be treated fairly and was reserving the right to be bored about it.

“You stop,” he said.

The room was quiet.

“You stop asking about the office,” Vale said, “and I will tell you why, and it is not because it is dangerous. A preservation authority is the most defensible body in this city. It exists so that records survive. It has no enemies, it has no constituency, it cannot be campaigned against, and every clerk in every office in this city will tell you, correctly, that you cannot build a case against a thing that is only doing what it was written to do.” He shut the schedule book. “Which means that if you go on after it, the only thing that changes in this city is that records stop coming. Not to you. To everybody. The next time a records hand opens a volume in a cold room because a stranger asked nicely, there is a clerk standing behind that counter who has heard about the man who made a preservation authority into a villain, and he will do his job perfectly and he will do it with the door half shut, and in four years there will be a page in a volume that nobody can read, and that page will be the ninth bridge.”

“Where do I look instead?” Ilyan said.

“At the money,” Vale said. “An office is a thing people are paid to do. Somebody is paid to sit at that desk and relay a page in cold paste, and somebody is paid at the end of a year for having done it, and the question is not *who was wicked* — the question is who draws the wage, what the wage buys, and what happens to the wage and the person when the person leaves. I have been a clerk for nineteen years. I know three things about every office in this city and they are all about money and none of them are about sin.”

“Thank you,” Ilyan said, and meant it, and also felt the ground close over the easy version of the last six days the way it always did.

“Do not thank me yet,” Vale said. “You have lost your standing this morning and you have not noticed.”

He said it the way he said everything, which was without any particular pleasure.

“Your standing lapsed at the review,” Vale said. “The provisional grant ran one review cycle and it ended when the bell rang and the review did not conclude, and there is no clerk in this city who can extend a grant that ran out, and there is one thing that could have saved it, which is a new ruling, and the thing that would have made a new ruling is a full hearing, and a full hearing needs a date, and nobody with a date in their hand is you any more.”

---

The second half of the morning went the way Sera had said it would, which is to say that the deeper he got the more the language ran out and the more he had to hand to other people.

The authority's index was a record, and the district entered it into the open case as a produced document at the household's request and the guild's, because a record that only one side has is not a record, and after two hours of it being read aloud in a cold room by a print-shop clerk with ink to the second knuckle, the Bench's translator took the page. It was the same page Fell had read, and the same two items, and the only difference was that the district's copy carried a name column, because a district's copy of a record is a record with places on it for people, and the column was there, and it was empty, twice.

**STANDING AUTHORITY OF PRESERVATION, INDEX OF TAKEN-INTO-PRESERVATION ITEMS, WEEK OF THE EXECUTION OF THE CURRENT NINTH-BRIDGE WORKING PLATE.**
**ITEM ONE — MAINTENANCE-REVIEW CONDITION, NINTH BRIDGE. ROUTING C—2. NO NAME ENTERED AGAINST THIS ITEM.**
**ITEM TWO — CLASS: CONTINUITY REQUEST. ROUTING C—2. NOT PRODUCED. NO NAME ENTERED AGAINST THIS ITEM.**

Two items, one week, one routing, one standing authority, and a column where a name should be that had never once had anything in it.

“A week,” Ilyan said. “The plate was executed on that day and the clause was taken into preservation on that day. Not before. Not after. On the day the plate that recorded the clause was replaced, somebody handed the clause itself to a desk with a stamp, and the desk has had it ever since, and the desk was told it was preserving it.”

“And the desk has no incoming index and no named clerk,” Sera said, “and now it has two items in it that a court has asked about, and it still has no name, and a name is not going to come, because the office is not a person and there is nothing to name.”

He wrote the two lines in his own notebook with the shelf and the box and the week and the count of eleven, and then he stopped, because the two numbers were the whole shape of the thing and the two numbers were a maintenance clause and a sealed thing he was frightened of naming, taken into preservation in the same week by the same standing authority and routed to the same desk with a stamp, and if he put those two facts next to each other in anything filed in this case he would be using a document that had come to him through the one person who had told him she was not his to spend.

He did not put them next to each other.

“Sera,” he said. “I have the second item’s class and the second item’s week and the second item’s routing. I am not going to file it.”

“I know you are not,” Sera said, “and I want it entered that you said it out loud in a room, and I want it entered that you said it before I had to stop you, because in nine days that is the difference between a man I work with and a man I work beside.”

At the fourth bell a runner came up the lower steps of the workroom with a slip from the district office, and it was not a hand-carried notice with a seal and a clerk standing in a lane reading it aloud. It was an automatic, which Ilyan had not seen before and had to have explained to him because he could read the numbers and the hour the thing gave itself and could not read the rest of it.

Bevin Larch read it, twice, and then put it on the bench.

“It is generated,” he said. “No seal, no clerk's hand, no hour. The office produces it without a hand behind it when an acknowledgment on a schedule is returned unaccepted and not re-issued inside its own term, and it goes to the district, and the district has to do one of two things inside the term. Re-issue it, or let the schedule lapse.”

“And the term is the next bell,” Oren said, from the maintenance line.

“The term is the next bell,” Larch said, “and the next bell is at dusk, and the acknowledgment is the one you refused on the public approach, and the schedule is the one the two loads of stone and the load-held day were entered on.”

The workroom went quiet. Ilyan watched it arrive in four separate people at four different speeds, the way a thing arrives when everybody already knows it and nobody has said it.

“So the day is gone,” Joren said.

“The day goes at dusk if the acknowledgment is not re-issued,” Larch said, “and the stone does not go anywhere, because the stone is a purchase and the purchase is a purchase, but the schedule that says when the stone is delivered and when the day is worked is the schedule, and the schedule lapses, and then the district has no lawful reason to send anybody to this span, and a district with no lawful reason to send anybody does not send anybody.”

“And nobody can re-issue it,” Oren said, “because re-issuing it is re-offering the thing I refused.”

“It is re-offering the thing you refused, and it is addressed to your successors, and the form number is a form number and not your name, and any one of six crews could take it and none of them is going to, and if none of them takes it, the schedule ends.” Larch said it in the flat voice of a young man reading a document that was going to hurt people. “Which means that at dusk the ninth bridge has a keeper with four hands and an empty strongbox, and a district that cannot lawfully send anybody, and a west seat that is empty on the underside, and a charter with no clause that lets anybody stop a load.”

Oren put the chalk in the groove.

“Then we will want the road open after dusk,” he said, “and the pump, and the roof work, and the ward's water, and none of that is the district's schedule any more. Say the word and I will get the guild's book out and we will write an arrangement, because that is what a guild does when the schedule stops, and I have been waiting thirteen days for something to do that I am good at.”

He looked at Ilyan.

“Not you,” Oren said. “You do not write this one. You have never written one of these that anybody has to live inside, and I am not starting with the man from another country on the day the money stops.”

And on the bench, unrolled, waiting for four bodies that would have to carry it, the district's own automatic notice sat with its term ticking toward dusk, and the two items in the cold room behind the print shop sat in a bound volume with no name in the column where a name should be, eleven of them in a week, all of them routed to a desk that had never been asked whether it should exist.

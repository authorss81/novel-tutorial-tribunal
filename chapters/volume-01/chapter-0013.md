# Chapter 13: A Name in the Margin

The record shop in Orison had three entrances and no obvious front.

Sera led Ilyan through a side door beneath a blue awning, past shelves of lock blanks and boxes of waxed cord, and into a back room where a clerk named Neral copied public records by hand. The room smelled of hot metal, damp paper, and the bitter tea that every recordwright seemed to carry as a personal record of the day. Neral knew Sera’s family name. He did not greet her warmly.

“The Quiet Chamber desk is busy,” he said. “The eastern petition desk is louder.”

“The copied extract is not an eastern ruling,” Sera replied. “It is a new adoption.”

“That is what the clerk wrote.”

“That is not the same as what the record shows.”

Neral looked at Ilyan, then at the paper beneath his arm. “You are the one whose name is traveling.”

“I am attached to the original record,” Ilyan said.

“That is the part people hear.”

Neral opened a flat drawer and placed a packet of documents on the table. The first was the Eastmere petition mounted on blackglass, the public extract from Merefen, and a narrow clerk’s index sheet. The second was a composite draft with several clauses pasted together at the edges. Sera had asked for the chain of adoption. Neral had brought it because the request had come from an advocate slot, even if the advocate was provisional.

“The petition is not one sentence,” Neral said. “It is four old clauses with a new condition between them.”

Ilyan examined the folds. The oldest clause concerned road inspection after rain. The next allowed a district to pause a crossing when a shared load moved. A third described the lawful traveler by register. A fourth, added in a newer hand, said that an unlisted ward could refuse a maintenance acknowledgment. The new line was not connected by a visible condition to the road’s actual state.

Sera leaned over the packet. Her fingers stopped above the paste. “This is not a copy of the ninth-bridge stay.”

“It is a copy of a public extract,” Neral said.

“No. The public extract was a record of a provisional ruling. This is a local petition assembled from different covenants. It borrows the form without preserving the sequence.”

“Clerks do not compose new law every morning,” Meral said from the doorway.

He had followed them from the district. His brown vest was damp at the shoulders, and the petition ledger was tucked under his arm. The name translated as **Meral, petition clerk** in a small brass pin on his collar.

“Show me the adoption mark,” Sera said.

Meral placed the composite draft on the table. “The district entered it as a precedent witness. The court accepted the record for comparison. The marshal used it to support a temporary maintenance levy.”

“By whose authority?” Ilyan asked.

Meral’s gaze moved to the clipped extract. “The district assembly’s.”

“Did the assembly adopt the sentence, or did the marshal use the copy after adoption?”

“The assembly adopted the road petition. The marshal applied the maintenance clause.”

“That is two acts.”

“It is one administrative response.”

Sera took the sheet and held it against the light. “The court can admit a record as a precedent witness without making it a ruling. It can also allow a clerk to couple two records when a hearing compares their conditions. Neither act authorizes the marshal to collect a levy unless the levy has its own basis.”

Meral’s jaw tightened. “If every clerk waits for a separate ruling, the road will fail before the Bench meets.”

“Then name the finite work and the people carrying it.”

“The road cannot wait for a committee.”

“It can wait long enough to stop pretending a person’s refusal is the cause of a broken drain.”

Neral tapped the table. “Do not use the word pretending in a record shop.”

Ilyan looked at him. “Why not?”

“Because someone may have believed it. A false reason can still be sincere.”

The correction landed harder than the rebuke. Ilyan marked the distinction in his notebook. Neral did not approve. He simply returned the composite draft to the table.

A formal notice unfolded above the desk when Meral placed his seal beside the adoption mark.

**RECORD STATUS: PRECEDENT WITNESS ADMITTED FOR COMPARISON.**
**RULING STATUS: NONE.**
**PRESERVED: THE LEVY LEDGER, ENTRY BY ENTRY, INCLUDING THE REMOVAL.**
**NOT GRANTED: A HOLD ON LOCAL ENFORCEMENT. THE LEVY CONTINUES PENDING A REVIEW NOBODY HAS NAMED.**

Ilyan read it twice. They had won the record and lost the road. The line between a precedent witness and a ruling was now officially clear, and the carts were free to keep running on the strength of a copy that was not a ruling. He looked away from the panel. The Bench had made the distinction legible. It had not made it harmless.

Sera said, “So the district may keep collecting while the record is read.”

Meral did not answer. His thumb stayed on the ledger box until Neral drew it away.

“Who copied the extract into Merefen?” Sera asked Neral.

Neral opened the index sheet. The chain named a public posting, a district reading room, a local petition desk, and a courier. The first two steps were lawful. The third had copied the extract with a carbon strip. The final entry did not name the clerk who chose the added refusal line. It named a duty office.

“Can a duty office be a person?” Ilyan asked.

Neral glanced at Sera. “In the record, yes. In fact, it is a seal.”

“Who held it?”

“The seal changes by rotation. The person who held it that day is in the custody ledger.”

Sera opened the custody ledger. It was a narrow book with columns for date, office, seal, and hand. The line for Merefen’s copy had the district seal, the clerk’s thumb mark, and a second impression from a blackglass backing. No signature followed. A note said **public wording, locally adopted**.

Ilyan traced the margin with his pen without touching the ink. “This is where the distinction disappears. The public record can be copied without asking us. Once it is adopted locally, people say the road is governed by the bridge’s remedy.”

Sera looked at him. “Because that lets them avoid asking the people who carry the cost.”

Her voice had tightened. Ilyan recognized the direction of the conversation. Her family appeal depended on the distinction between a person’s answer no and a person’s petition for correction. A local clerk who reused public wording without the original parties’ permission could make the same words serve two incompatible purposes. The Quiet Chamber dispute was not evidence for Merefen. It was a reason to be careful about borrowing it.

“I am not going to use your family claim to win this road case,” he said.

“No,” Sera replied. “You are going to use this road case to make the archive question easier to answer.”

“I want the page recovered.”

“Recovery is not interpretation. If the old page says refusal was once recognized, it does not say this petition is valid. If the current text says petition, it does not say your road may charge the ward.”

“I know.”

“Then do not make the distinction only when it helps you.”

Neral opened a wooden box filled with small record tablets. “The levy ledger is here. The district calls it an acceptance ledger. It lists people who have signed, witnessed, or been counted as carrying the maintenance burden.”

Ilyan looked at the tablets. “Bring it.”

Meral moved to stop him. “The ledger is not public.”

“The people whose labor and money are named in it have a right to see it,” Sera said. “They do not need to see the private memory of a witness to do that.”

Meral’s voice hardened. “Some signatures were given under emergency pressure.”

“Then mark those entries. Do not hide them.”

Ilyan turned to Neral. “I am asking for the ledger of people who accepted the burden, not a private history and not the names of anyone who did not consent to be included. If the district used the copied precedent to justify a levy, we need the acceptance chain.”

Neral’s gaze moved to Sera. She gave a small nod.

He brought out a black-bound ledger, its paper leaves crowded with entries in several hands. The first pages showed road crews, cart owners, and pump workers who had accepted temporary labor or a payment. Other lines carried marks beside household names. Some marks were signatures. Some were thumbprints. Some were merely the word *seen*.

Ilyan traced the order of the entries with his eyes. Every few lines, the burden changed: labor became a levy, the levy became a refusal, and the refusal became a reason to close the route. The record did not show a single decision. It showed people being moved through a sequence until the district could call the final result voluntary.

Meral said, “The ledger is how we keep the road from collapsing.”

“Then show it to the people who are affected,” Ilyan said. “Not as a favor. As a condition of using the burden.”

Sera examined the tablet. “The last entry is different.”

Near the bottom, beneath a line of ordinary marks, someone had written **WITNESS ACCEPTED** and then cut the name away. The paper was not torn. The removal had been made with a narrow blade, leaving a clean rectangle of lighter color. The final letters **Kest** remained in the lower margin, enough to identify the fragment but not enough to restore the full witness. A replacement line ran across the gap.

**CONTINUITY PRESERVES THE RECORD BEFORE REMEDY.**

The cadence was clipped and formal, but Ilyan recognized the shape beneath it. It was the same cadence that had entered the System during the first field. The words were not proof of a speaker. They were proof that someone had learned how the Bench sounded when it wanted a record to precede a person’s answer.

Sera took the tablet from Neral’s hand. Her face had gone pale.

“Where is the original?” she asked.

Neral looked toward the petition desk. “The original is not here.”

“Who removed the witness?”

“I do not know.”

“Read the custody mark.”

Neral leaned over the line. “The witness was removed from active register after a continuity request. The request is in the Quiet Chamber desk.”

Sera looked at Ilyan. “Do not use this to claim the road petition is invalid.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You were already building a connection.”

“I was looking for the same hand.”

“There may not be one hand.”

Ilyan stared at the clipped cadence. “Then we need the chain, not a culprit.”

Meral reached for the tablet. Sera shifted it away from him.

“The ward sees this ledger,” she said. “The district cannot keep the names of the people who accepted the burden hidden while calling their refusal a public danger.”

Meral looked toward the door as if waiting for a guard. “Some of them consented.”

“Consent to what?” Ilyan asked. “The road’s repair, the levy, or the rule that their refusal closes the route for everyone?”

The question landed in the room. Neral lowered his eyes to the tablet. Meral’s hand closed, then opened.

“The district will say the distinction is academic.”

“Distinctions are how a burden gets carried by the wrong person,” Ilyan said.

Sera placed the ledger beside the composite petition. “The ward gets a copy of the entries relevant to its roads. The private witness names remain covered. The removed entry stays visible as a removal, not as a conclusion.”

Meral looked at her. “You are making a new rule.”

“No. I am refusing to let the old one hide its work.”

Ilyan filed the request through Sera’s provisional scope. It asked the Bench to preserve the levy ledger, distinguish adoption from authorship, and hold any local enforcement until the ward could answer at a bounded review. The request did not ask the court to close the road. It asked the court to prevent the copied precedent from becoming the only explanation of the road’s condition.

Sera read the request back before the clerk wrote anything, because a request that asks for two things has to show which one the court gave. The clerk entered the outcome beside the disputed chain. The tablet remained in the room, its last line exposed. The ledger had been preserved and the road had not been touched. South of the river, carts would keep crossing on the strength of a copy that was not a ruling.

The removed witness name was gone, though the final **Kest** remained in the margin. The replacement cadence stayed.

Ilyan copied neither. He simply wrote the date, the office, and the fact that a name had been removed. At the bottom of his notebook, beneath the six covenant parts, he added a seventh question: **Who is allowed to make a removal look like continuity?**

The record shop closed its shutters one by one. Outside, a cart from Merefen crossed the bridge toward the east, carrying salt and no visible maintenance worker. The copied remedy was already ahead of them. The ledger was beginning to show where its cost had gone.

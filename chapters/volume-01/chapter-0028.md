# Chapter 28: The Two Books

The guild had been awake for two nights and the third one was going to decide whether the ninth bridge had a west joint or a history.

Oren came into the workroom at the first bell and sat down without taking off his coat, which he had not done in twenty-two years, and said the thing everybody had been waiting for him to say.

“If the salt crack takes an hour and a half, and the count is every change from the second bell, and the under-deck is every fourth change, then I need nine people across the day, and the levy pays four before the first bell and six at each change and nobody at all after dusk, and I have got four, and three of those four did the joint course on Tuesday.”

“You said the fund covers the wages,” Ilyan said.

“The levy is spent on Thursday. That is arithmetic, not a complaint.” Oren put his hands flat on the table. “My complaint is that the district’s schedule has six names on it and none of them are mine. If we work in our order they will write that the guild failed to keep to the schedule, and next month there will be six names and no hours again, and we will be back here arguing about a form at eleven at night.”

“So the work does not go in the district schedule,” Joren Vell said from the doorway.

He had come up from the lower road before light with two roadkeepers and a barrow of wedges, because the road half had to be right before a count could be trusted. He looked like a man who had also been awake for two nights and had decided to be unpleasant about it early.

“The district controls materials and the district controls the schedule,” Joren said. “It does not control the order in which a brace is set. Oren can tension the west joint from the bridge side. I can set the apron from the road side. If either of us does ours without the other we get the hollow note back by Thursday, and you can put that in the district schedule in your own handwriting if you like.”

“Then the arrangement has to carry both,” Ilyan said, and stopped himself, because that was the sentence the arrangement already carried and he had almost said it as if he had put it there.

Oren took a clean slate, of the kind the district supplied rather than the guild’s chalk board, and wrote three lines on it with a stick held badly because his left hand was stiff from Tuesday. Then he stopped, and pushed it across to Joren.

“You start,” he said. “You have been telling me my joint is wrong since the field. Say the whole order out loud so the ward hears it from you and not from me.”

Joren crouched and drew the approach in the dust of the workroom floor with the heel of his hand, which made a poor map and an honest one.

“Apron first, four spans, because the outside wheel is what broke it. Two wedges at the third and fifth span joints, set from the road side, because set from above they close the fall and the joint drinks. Then the joint course, because the joint is fed from the outside and you cannot feed it from a street that is falling away. Then your brace from the bridge side, and your count, and then the salt crack last, because the salt crack is the one that tells us whether the two fingers moved.”

“And if the joint is fed from the outside,” Oren said, “then the west edge of the joint course is loaded before the east edge is packed, which is why the count walks out on the fourth run and comes back on the fifth.”

“Yes.”

“That is what I have been calling a bridge problem since the field.”

“It is a bridge problem that has a road on one end of it,” Joren said. “That is all the difference and it has cost you a crew.”

Oren looked at the dust map for a while.

“Nine slots,” he said. “The guild has four hands. The road party has three, and two of them cannot leave the road. I am asking for one more pair of hands, and I am not asking the district, because the district will send six and put their names on it and then the schedule becomes a fact instead of an arrangement.”

“The ward,” Neris said.

She had come up with Tamsin behind her and a roll of the household’s drying line over one shoulder, because the workroom floor had been soaked since the first rain after the field and the guild’s spare slates were stacked against a wall that was quietly ruining them.

“The ward can give you two hands for four hours,” she said, “and the two hands are mine and Pev Sorrel’s, and we want them entered as ward hours at the ward rate, and we do not want them entered as support.”

“Support is the fourth column,” Oren said. “You have seen it.”

“Then we know what it is,” Neris said, “and we are not going in it.”

Tarin came in with two guards and did not cross the chalk line, which was his way of saying that the workroom was not a public space today and that the guards would be needed later, not now.

“The route is the other half,” he said. “You can wedge an apron and count a joint and the district will still be able to say the road is unsafe, because a road is not a bridge with a gate at the end of it. Somebody has to stand at the approach and turn carts onto the line, all day, for as long as this takes. That is not skilled work and it is not unpaid work either. It is two people and a whistle and it goes in the arrangement or the first cart puts a wheel off the wedge at dusk.”

“You are asking the road party for a person,” Joren said.

“I am telling you the arrangement is missing a person,” Tarin replied. “I do not have a person. I have a boundary and two guards who cannot leave the rope to hold a line for eight hours. If your arrangement does not contain the man with the whistle, then the arrangement will be correct and the road will be damaged by Thursday, and the district will be able to say anything it likes about the ward afterwards.”

Oren marked it on the slate. Joren added the hours. Ilsa added the salt crack. Tarin added the whistle and did not add himself, because Tarin had spent a week being careful about the difference between a boundary and a laborer and he was not going to cross it in front of a slate.

It was, Ilyan thought, the first arrangement built by the people who would have to be standing under the span when it failed, and it was badly balanced. Four guild hands across nine slots. Four ward hours, one of them a whistle on a road corner. One road hand free and a cart lane neither of the other two would leave. No slack anywhere in it, and no way to add any without a wage that did not exist, and every person in that room knew it.

Then Ilyan did the thing he had been not doing for four days, which was to reach for the sentence.

He took a second slate from the stack and wrote on it, in the small cramped figures of a defect report, the sentence from the fish-market hall that Sera had stopped him from saying, and then a second sentence under it, which was the one he had never said to anybody at all.

**A BURDEN MAY BE REFUSED WITHOUT LOSS OF ACCESS, WORK, OR SHELTER, AND THE REFUSAL AND ITS EFFECT ARE BOTH ENTERED.**

He did not write the first sentence out. He did not have to. He had heard it come back to him in his own voice four times that morning and it was in the guild’s count book, in the district’s fourth column, and in two books sewn together on a bench behind him.

The second sentence was the one that had never been said. It was good. It was true in every case he had measured. It would have ended the half-hand in the wage bill, the delivery board, the shelter store, and the paper that had been printed with a household already in it. It was one sentence, and he could have entered it in the open case with a recordwright’s name beside it before the first bell of the morning, and the recordwright would have read it and not stopped him, because this one was about refusal and this one he was allowed to want.

Then he read it a third time and looked at the line that said *a burden may be refused without loss of access, work, or shelter*, and thought about a slate on a public frame in another district, and a warden asking whether a household could refuse a burden and keep a room, and a man at a table explaining that a template had been printed and distributed and nine people had used it.

Oren put the chalk down.

“If that rule exists,” he said, not looking at the slate, “then the guild can refuse the joint course, and the span stays on a brace that is two fingers out, and the ward can refuse the water route, and the district can refuse the schedule, and the lowland can refuse the levy, and every one of those refusals is lawful, and in February the ninth bridge comes down on a Tuesday and four hundred people are on it, and the rule is still true.”

“It is true and it would prevent this class of harm,” Ilyan said.

“It would prevent this class of harm *by making the harm lawful*,” Oren said. “You cannot write a rule that says a person may refuse and then act surprised on the day they do. If everybody may refuse without loss, then somebody is going to refuse on the worst day in the worst weather, and the clause that would have stopped the span is the one your rule has just made optional.”

The workroom went quiet.

Ilyan looked at the sentence he had written, and understood that it was not wrong. That was the difficulty. It was a rule about consent, and it was correct about consent, and it had no clause in it about what happens when every consenting person in a district decides on the same afternoon that the load is beyond them. He had written a rule for the *right* and had not written a rule for the *structure*, and the second one was the one people stood on.

He did not file it.

He put the slate down on the bench at the back of the workroom, face down, and did not enter it, and did not tell anyone he had written it, and did not destroy it either, because a man who destroys the sentence he wanted to impose and then denies having wanted it has learned nothing at all.

Oren looked at the bench, and then at Ilyan, and said nothing about it, which was the single most generous thing the guild had done all week.

They worked.

Sera came up at the second bell with the print shop’s answer and did not come into the work room; she read it at the frame, where the district’s slate still hung under the guild’s notice, and she read it twice and then handed it to him upside down, which was how she handed him things she did not trust.

It was one page: the authorization for print run one hundred and fourteen, cut out of the stock ledger and replaced with a page of the same size, stock and week. The replacement was an authorization, signed by an office and not by a person — a preservation authority, which is the kind of office that can authorize a print run without naming anyone, because its job is to keep records through a period in which records are being kept.

“The form that had your household in it was printed under a preservation authority,” she said. “That is the sentence. Do not do anything with it yet.”

“Why not?”

“Because a preservation authority is also the office that routed my family’s request, and if I go at it with a bad temper I hand them a reason to be tidy, and tidy is how pages stop existing.” She folded it. “One question a day at that desk, and I have spent it.”

Joren’s four apron spans went in first, from the road side, with two wedges at the third and fifth span joints. The district loaders brought stone and lime and left it at the line, because the district was not on the work list, it was the supplier, and Ilyan put the distinction on the slate in the guild’s own form.

**MATERIALS SUPPLIED BY THE DISTRICT. LABOR NAMED BY EACH BODY.**

He watched it hold all morning without anybody having to be told to respect it.

The joint course went down at midday. Oren’s crew fed it from the outside, which meant Ilsa read the scale from the road side for the first time in six years, standing in the cart lane, calling across the wedge line, and the count came back to the mark on the first run.

Oren set the brace from the bridge side at the second bell of the afternoon. It took three hours and it took all four of the guild’s hands and it was the only part of the day in which the ninth bridge was doing what it had been built to do.

Tamsin Ro sat on the workroom step with her leg up and a stick in her hand. She did not touch the scale, the brace, or the count book. She counted the load changes under her breath, to herself, the way she had done at the Saltwheel work line, and when Joren asked her at the end of the day how many there had been she told him the number and nothing else, and he wrote it nowhere.

Pev Sorrel had the whistle from the second bell and used it eleven times and never once to hurry anybody, which Joren said afterward was the whole qualification the corner needed.

At dusk, the whistle, the apron, the wedge line, the count and the salt crack were all in the arrangement. The salt crack found the drift again, two fingers, and Oren wrote it on the board under the words *returned to mark, held two fingers, returned again* and signed his own name under it, and did not write Ilsa’s name under anything.

The first cart came over at the last bell of light, slow, on the line, with the whistle going and the count called and the wedge holding, and the second came after it, and a water tank for the ward’s lower shelter came over third with Neris walking beside it on the drying line, which she had insisted on doing in the open where the cart drivers could see her, so that nobody could later say an unlisted person had been moved without a road open.

The arrangement was written in the district’s own book, in the guild’s hand, in Joren’s hand, and on the ward’s petition box.

Meridith Vale came up at the last bell with the case and a printed ledger.

The ledger was two books sewn together on a cord, which was the district’s solution to a problem nobody had asked it to solve, and which turned out to be the right solution for a reason that nobody had thought of in advance.

The left book had columns. Hands, hours, materials, rate, paid, signature. It was a wage book, and it was clean, and it was the book that would let a person prove they had been paid and let the guild prove it had paid them.

The right book had four columns and no rates at all. Position. Body. Offered. Declined.

Oren turned up the first page of the right book. Mava Rusk’s refusal was on it, in the ward’s hand, with the date and the Saltwheel arrangement number, and no acknowledgment, and the effect of the refusal, and the hour at which the arrangement was reviewed.

“Good,” he said. “Where is the household?”

Vale turned to a page near the back of the right book.

“There is no household,” he said.

The workroom did not go quiet. It went the other way. Neris put the water tank’s rope down on the stones, and Joren said a word that his roadkeepers pretended not to hear, and Tarin, at the rope, said only, “Read the page.”

Vale read the page. It carried the Ro household’s crossing hours, entered as unlisted support, half a hand per crossing, daily, credit to wage bill — the same entry that Ilyan had found in the guild’s ledger a week earlier, now lifted out of the guild’s book and reprinted cleanly in the district’s, where it sat in a column headed *position* as though it were a thing somebody had chosen.

“It is a position,” Vale said, when Neris asked him, in a voice that had gone very flat. “The household uses a maintained crossing. The maintenance is maintained. It is not consent and I did not enter it as consent.”

“You have been maintaining a schedule on a column that was cut out of a book,” Neris said, “and you are going to keep maintaining it out of a new one, and it is still not consent, and my daughter is asleep upstairs.”

“It is the district’s standard position column,” Vale said, “and the household is in it because the household uses the maintained crossing and the office has no other column for that.”

“There is a column,” Tarin said from the rope. “The fourth one. The one that says *offered* and *declined*. It is four lines down and it is empty, and you filled two of them yourself today, which is how I know it exists.”

It was, in fact, a very long moment in the workroom. Oren did not use it. Joren did not use it. The ward’s four hours of hands went on being what they had been agreed to be, and the count came back to its mark, and the apron held.

“Write it again,” Oren said finally. “In the right book, where the positions go. Position, body, offered, declined. And the household’s line goes in as *offered: maintenance labor by the district office and the guild, not the household. Declined: acknowledgment of maintenance, and any credit for the household’s crossings.*”

“It would be a position the district has not recorded,” Vale said.

“Then record it and it will be one,” Oren said. “You have a pen. You have been putting my guild’s hours in a book all day because the work was done. The work was done by people who did not choose to be credited. Put the credit in the book and it will be a position too, and it will be a true one, and the next clerk who reads it will have to decide whether to argue with a household or with a piece of paper.”

Vale looked at the two books for a while. Then he wrote it, in his own hand, in the small square letters he used when he was being careful, with the position and the body and the offered and the declined, and he did not sign it as a fact, because it was not a fact. He signed it as an entry, with the hour and the version, and the hour was after the last bell of light, and the version was one, and it was the first page in Orison where the refused and the paid were in the same book and could not be separated by anybody who did not want to cut a page out.

The two books were sewn together and the cord was knotted twice and a piece of the ward’s petition-box string was used for the second knot, which Neris did and did not explain.

Vale put the ledger on the bench, and then stopped with his hand on it, and turned it over.

“There is a strip on the back,” he said. “The form has a strip. It is the print authority, and it is on every district form, and I have never once read it, because it has never once told me anything I did not already know.”

He turned the book so the lamp was on it.

The strip was four lines long and had been printed on court stock in a public print shop, and it carried a run number and a distribution line, because paper that has gone somewhere is a thing an office can be asked about.

**RUN 114. DISTRIBUTION: 1 — DISTRICT MAINTENANCE OFFICE. 2 — BRIDGE PLATE OFFICE.**
**RECEIPTED AT 2: SIX DAYS BEFORE THE NINTH-BRIDGE PUBLIC NOTICE.**

The second element of the routing code was missing, as it had been on the slate and on the schedule book and on the continuity request. The form had gone to a plate office that had no clerk and only a stamp, and it had got there before anybody knew there was going to be a bridge to certify, and every entry the ward and the household had made in four days had been made in a book printed on paper that had been sitting in a routed pile since before an outsider landed on a riverbank.

Oren looked at it for a long time.

“So the paper was there first,” he said.

“The paper was there first,” Vale agreed, and did not say anything else, and did not need to, because he had said it with the honesty of a man who had spent a week discovering that being the hand was not the same as being the reason, and who was tired of it, and who had just written a household’s refusal into a book where it could not be cut out without a signature, a version number, and an hour.

“Keep the strip on,” Joren said. “Somebody will want that book.”

“It is going in the district file,” Vale said, “and the district file is in a rented room with a strongbox that has never been re-keyed.”

“Take the strip off and write it on the ward’s copy,” Neris said. “And on the guild’s. And on the household’s, if the household wants one, which it does, because that is what the fourth column is for.”

The two books went out at the last bell with three copies of the arrangement and one copy of the strip, and the span held through the night with a two-finger drift that came back and came back and came back, and the ninth bridge had a ledger in which a guild was paid for its hours, a household had refused, a ward had refused, and four people who had chosen to be credited had not been, and on the back of all of it, printed in a public shop and delivered before the case began, a routing line pointed at an office that had never asked anybody’s permission and had never once been named in a record that survived.

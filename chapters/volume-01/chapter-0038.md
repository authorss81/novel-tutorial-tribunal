# Chapter 38: The Schedule Lapses

At dusk the schedule lapsed, and it lapsed correctly, and four people who had spent a fortnight fighting paperwork stood on an approach and watched a district do the right thing.

Vale was there because a schedule that lapses has to be entered as lapsed by somebody with a hand, and Meridith Vale was the somebody, and he did it in the guild's sight at the count, with the hour on it and the reason on the back, in the flat square letters he used when he was being careful.

**NINTH-BRIDGE MAINTENANCE SCHEDULE, THIS CYCLE: LAPSED AT THE TERM.**
**CAUSE: the maintenance acknowledgment served on the guild's crew was returned unaccepted and was not re-issued inside its term. The schedule's authority was that acknowledgment.**
**NO PARTY HAS BREACHED ANYTHING. THE SCHEDULE HAS ENDED BECAUSE AN INSTRUMENT IT DEPENDED ON WAS NOT SIGNED.**

“There it is in three lines,” Vale said, capping the ink. “I have been a clerk for nineteen years and that is the shortest true thing I have ever entered, and I did not have to leave a page out to make it true.”

Maren Kett was not there. Tovin Dain was, in the same borrowed capacity, with a folded paper he had brought and had not unfolded, which everybody present understood to be the district's position and which Dain had plainly decided not to deliver.

“There is nothing unlawful about this,” Dain said, to nobody, before anybody asked. “I want to say that now while the people who want to shout are still deciding whether to shout. The schedule lapsed on its own term. The district cannot send a crew to this span because it has no instrument to send them on. The stone is bought and it is at a yard off the fen road and it will sit at that yard at the district's expense until somebody with a lawful reason moves it, and the lawful reason is a schedule, and there is no schedule.”

“So the day is gone,” Joren said.

“The day is gone,” Dain agreed, “and the stone is gone, and the under-deck watch is gone, and I have spent two weeks telling a foreign record-holder that this bridge cannot be maintained without a clause and he has spent two weeks telling me the clause is not the first thing that is missing. Tonight I have got to tell him he was right about the order and it is the worst news either of us has had.”

Nobody shouted. That was the thing Ilyan wrote down later, because it was the thing he had been wrong about for a fortnight: he had assumed that a district with no money was a district under pressure, and what he was looking at was a district with no obligation, which is a much worse and much quieter thing.

“Right,” Oren said. “Guild's book. Chalk. Now, and before the light goes.”

---

They wrote it at the bench on the span with the count running underneath, because the count does not stop for paper, and there were four bodies in it and none of them was a district.

That was the whole difference and it took them most of an hour to notice it had happened.

The first arrangement had four guild hands, three road-party hands, two ward hands and a whistle, and no money at all. The second had been entered by Ilyan as a record of what the bodies had decided. This one was being written by the bodies, and it took longer, and it was better, and by the time the lamp was lit it was the most legible document any of them had produced in two weeks.

Oren wrote the hours. He would not let anybody write hours for him.

**HANDS AND HOURS, FROM DUSK TONIGHT:**
**GUILD: four hands, every change, from the setup before the first bell to dusk, at the levy rate, paid from the guild's own account. Six hands for any change where a load is stopped. The guild's account is empty at the fourth change and I am saying so on the board.**
**ROAD PARTY: J. Vell and two hands, the apron and the wedge line, at the road rate, from the second bell to the fourth, and only while the guild has hands to set a wedge.**
**WARD: D. Rusk for the pump, at the ward rate, four hours a day, and P. Sorrel for the whistle, at the ward rate, every change, and the ward's rate is what Lio's book says the ward rate is and not what anybody else says.**
**CARTS: one eighth of a load-fee, per load, from the nine cart owners who use this span in a day, collected by A. of the eastern yard, entered in his book, and the fee pays the whistle and the count and nothing else.**

“The cart fee,” Lio Fenn said. “That is acknowledgment inferred from continued use with money in it.”

“No,” Joren said. “It is the opposite, and I will tell you the difference and then you can object. A fee taken because a household uses a crossing is a tax on a person who cannot pay it. A fee taken per load from a cart that can afford it is a toll on the thing that is being delayed, and the delay is the guild’s, so the toll is the guild’s to ask for. If the ward does not want to pay the ward rate, the ward does not turn the pump. But no household on this road pays anything, and the Ro household pays nothing, and I have written that in.”

“Write it bigger,” Neris said.

“It is written,” Joren said, “and I will write it on the frame, and then anybody can walk up and read it, and I have never in my life been able to say that about anything I have written.”

“Do not crowd me out of my own arrangement,” Pev said, from the corner, without turning round.

“You are the whistle,” Joren said. “You are in it.”

Then Dalla Rusk put her hand on the slate and stopped the whole thing, which nobody had expected and which was the fourth time in two weeks that the ward had refused to be a decoration in somebody's document.

“The pump,” she said.

“The pump is in it.”

“The pump is in it for four hours a day and the ward rate is what Lio's book says,” Dalla said, “and I have not asked Lio for the ward's hours and I am not going to, and I am not the ward, and neither is Pev, and I have said that to four rooms in two weeks and I am going to keep saying it until the arrangement stops asking me to be a mouth.” She turned the slate round. “The pump serves eleven households and I know how many because I count them, not because I speak for them. If the pump is unfunded at the fifth day, that is eleven households with no water and I will not be the person who wrote that eleven into a book. So I am not taking the ward rate out of a fund that does not exist. I am taking it out of the pump's own take, which is what the pump collects, and it is short by two days, and I am going to write that short in here and let it be short in public.”

“Dalla,” Lio said, “that is the ward's money.”

“It is the pump's money,” Dalla said, “and the pump is a machine I work, and if the pump's take pays the pump's hours then the pump is a thing with wages, and if the pump's take does not reach then the pump is a favor, and I know which of those two the ward is and I would rather be a wage.”

The arrangement went into the guild's book at the last bell of light, sewn into the left book, in the guild's hand, in Joren's hand, in the ward's hand, and with the cart fee in A.'s book, and with the two days of shortage written in by Dalla Rusk and not corrected.

And the right book, the one with the four columns — position, body, offered, declined — was opened, and for the first time in two weeks the thing that went into it was not a refusal.

**OFFERED BY THE GUILD: four hands, every change, at the levy rate, from the guild's own account, which is empty at the fourth change. Six hands at any stopped load.**
**OFFERED BY THE ROAD PARTY: the apron and the wedge line, at the road rate, second bell to fourth.**
**OFFERED BY THE WARD, FOR THE PUMP ONLY: the pump's take, at the ward rate, four hours a day. SHORT BY TWO DAYS. D. RUSK, WHO IS NOT THE WARD.**
**OFFERED BY NINE CART OWNERS: one eighth of a load-fee per load, for the whistle and the count and nothing else. Collected and entered by A., eastern yard. NO HOUSEHOLD PAYS ANYTHING.**
**NOT OFFERED AND NOT ASKED FOR: any maintenance acknowledgment, any acknowledgment with amendment, any successor clause, any term beyond the fourth morning bell.**

“And the end,” Oren said, with the chalk at the board.

**DUSK TODAY TO THE FOURTH MORNING BELL, AND NO LONGER. NO PARTY PROMISES ANYTHING AFTER IT. THE SPAN HAS NO SCHEDULE. THE SPAN IS NOT STAFFED AFTER DUSK AND WAS NOT STAFFED BEFORE THE LEVY ENDED EITHER. THE WHISTLE HANGS AND NOBODY IS ON THE APPROACH WITH IT. THE CHARTER IS NOT AMENDED. A KEEPER MAY SUMMON A PAUSE AND MAY NOT RELEASE THE SPAN.**

They put that on the frame, in chalk, at the height where a cart driver on the box could read it, which Oren had learned from a cart driver four days ago and a broken apron.

---

The last piece came from a bell-runner who had been standing at the eastern stake for an hour without saying anything, which Tarin said afterwards was the most professional thing he had watched a person do on that approach in two weeks.

“The route runs all night,” Bram Orren said. “Not the guild’s line. Every line in this ward, and the March’s line, and the four standing arrangements I told you about the other morning. It runs because the crossing is short and the crossing is open. Tonight it is open by arrangement and not by schedule, and in four days by arrangement and not by anything.”

“So?”

“So there is no record of what crossed tonight except mine.” He took the line book out and did not offer it. “The district had one. The district’s is a schedule of works and hours and it has just stopped being entered. The guild has the count book and the guild’s book and the frame, and the guild’s book is the arrangement and the count book is loads on a span. Neither of them says what a person carried over the bridge at the fourth bell.”

“That is not maintenance,” Larch said.

“No,” Bram said. “It is not maintenance, and it is the only record there is going to be of whether the roof bundles went to the lower road or went to the east bank, and if the roof bundles do not go to the lower road then two households on that road have a hole in the roof at the first rain and no way to prove what they were told.”

The room went quiet in a new place.

Neris Ro said, “The shelter.”

“The shelter’s roof is due at the second delivery of the week,” Bram said, “and the second delivery is a fen-road load, and a fen-road load does not come up to this span on a promise. It comes on a schedule or it comes on a charter, and the charter says the span is maintained and the maintenance is the district’s, and tonight the maintenance is four hands and an empty box, and so the carrier looks at the frame and reads what is on it, and the frame says *from dusk today to the fourth morning bell and no longer*, and a carrier will not put a roof load on a road that ends on a date with nothing after it.”

“So the roof does not come,” Oren said.

“The roof does not come, or the roof comes after the fourth morning bell if there is a fourth morning bell with something behind it, and there is nothing behind it, and so—”

“And so it goes to the east bank,” Joren said, “where two households can guarantee the carrier after the date, because the east bank has a schedule that has not lapsed.”

Nobody needed to say the rest of it, and Ilyan did it anyway, because he was the man in the room with a record in his head and nobody else had the whole of it.

“And on the east bank the roof is a two-person job,” he said. “A ladder and two on the ground, and a bundle is not a bundle, it is a roof, and a person with one pair of hands does not put a roof on. So the carrier takes the bundles where two pairs of hands are, and the lower road has one household with two pairs of hands and eleven with one, and the eleven do not get a roof, and they do not get a bundle, and they get told by a carrier that the span is between arrangements.”

He had said it out loud and he wished immediately that he had not, because a sentence like that is not a fact, it is an instrument, and he had spent a fortnight learning that the instrument is what does the work.

Neris Ro put her hand on the bench.

“Room nine,” she said. “Marda Rennick and her boy Besh. She is at the wash line at the dye-yard from the first bell and comes back at the second, and her knee went in the winter and it is not the knee that is the problem, the problem is that she cannot get up a ladder and Besh is nine and cannot be on a ladder at all. She is the household the roof goes to first when the carrier has a choice, because she is on the list, and she is going to lose it first, and she will lose it before anybody else on that road because she is the only one who cannot do it herself if it does come.”

“Write that in the arrangement,” Ilyan said.

“No,” Neris said. “Do not write it in the arrangement. It will be in the arrangement's price, which is the same thing, and if it is in the arrangement then somebody in a room can point at a line and feel that somebody cared, and Marda Rennick still has a hole in her roof.”

She turned round.

“Go and stand at the rope,” she said, “and when the district’s clerk writes this down, make him write down whose decision it was.”

---

Bevin Larch had come out with the roll at the last bell because a ward clerk is required to be present when a ward's hours are entered, and he stood at the rope with the roll and did not go inside it, and he had been waiting for about an hour for somebody to make him do something.

“Read it back,” Ilyan said.

Larch did. He read the whole arrangement back in the lane-side voice of a clerk reading a roll, four sources and the hours and the fee and the shortage and the two days, and the ward's four hands, and the road party, and the cart owners, and the end of it, and the charter not amended, and the keeper who may summon a pause and may not release the span.

Then he stopped, and looked at his roll, and then at the frame, and then at Dain, who had not unfolded his paper all evening.

“Who is a party to this?” Larch asked.

“The guild is,” Oren said. “The road party is. The pump is. Nine cart owners are.”

“The district is not,” Vale said, from the bench. “The district supplied materials and a day and neither has been performed, and the district has no instrument and no obligation, and I am not entering the district as a party to an arrangement it has not agreed to.”

“Correct,” Oren said. “And the district's schedule lapsed at dusk.”

“That is entered,” Larch said, and wrote it, and stopped with the nib a half-inch above the roll.

“And I am going to read the reason out too,” he said, “because I entered the reason on the schedule and the reason is the same fact written in a different place, and if I leave it out of this one then the record says a schedule lapsed and does not say why, and a record that does not say why is a record somebody can be frightened by.”

“Larch,” Dain said. Not sharply. Just his name.

“I am a ward clerk and this is a ward roll and the ward is a party to the pump,” Larch said, “and I am going to enter the cause of the lapse, and if the district wishes to have a different cause I will enter that too beside it and the roll will carry both.”

He wrote it.

**THE DISTRICT'S SCHEDULE FOR THIS SPAN LAPSED AT DUSK ON ITS OWN TERM, BECAUSE A MAINTENANCE ACKNOWLEDGMENT IT DEPENDED ON WAS NOT SIGNED BY THE GUILD, AND WAS NOT RE-ISSUED. NO PARTY BREACHED ANYTHING. THE SPAN IS NOW MAINTAINED BY ARRANGEMENT, WITHOUT A SCHEDULE, UNTIL THE FOURTH MORNING BELL.**

He read it back before he inked it, which was his habit and which on that evening was the most useful thing anybody did.

Then he said, to the rope, in the voice of a man who had thought about it and had decided anyway, “I am twenty-four and I have been a clerk for a year, and I am going to be asked tomorrow by somebody with more than a year whether I wrote that line myself.”

“You did,” Ilyan said.

“I did,” Larch said, “and I would write it again, and I would like it noticed that I did not have to, and that the only reason anybody is going to notice is that a man standing on a rope asked me to read it back.”

He inked it.

And Ilyan said the sentence, because Neris had told him to and because it was the only thing in a fortnight that was his to say and it was the smallest thing in the case.

“The schedule ended,” he said, in the voice he had used for a public account in a cloth hall, and about four hundred people who had no business being there heard it, “because the district’s own instrument depended on a signature the guild would not give. That is the district’s decision. It is lawful, it is correct, and it is the district’s, and I am not going to stand on this bridge and let the next eleven days be spent finding out who is to blame for it.”

Nobody cheered. Dain unfolded his paper, read it, folded it again, and put it back in his coat, and said, “That is the first accurate sentence anybody has said on this approach in two weeks, and I am not going to be the man who answers it, and I want it noted that I heard it and chose not to.”

Then the count came back to its mark, and walked out two fingers, and came back.

And the pale drift line sat a finger's width from the iron ring, and the two loads of crushed stone sat at a yard off the fen road at the district's expense, and the west seat was exactly as empty underneath as it had been on the morning Oren had put a bell on a frame and rung it for the tenth day running, and nobody on the ninth bridge could do one single thing about it except count, and count again, and write down that the count had not returned.

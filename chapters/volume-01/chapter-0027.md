# Chapter 27: The Witness Who Refuses the Schedule

The schedule they wanted Ilsa Bray to sign was one page long and had been written by somebody who had never lifted a scale.

It said that the named keeper of the ninth-bridge load-count would continue to perform the inspection at every change of load, together with the under-deck watch and the salt-crack check, until the bridge charter was reviewed and thereafter until the review was concluded. There was a wage line. The wage line had an amount and no hours, and the phrase *until reviewed* appeared twice, and the second time it read *and thereafter*, which turned a sentence about a week into a sentence about a career.

Half of that was load vocabulary and came to Ilyan off the guild’s sheet without effort. The office half was not on the sheet, and he was not going to guess at a document he had no standing to guess at. He needed it read back to him before he formed a view on it, which is not a thing a man admits in front of a crew he is asking to trust him.

Ilsa read it standing up, twice, and gave it back.

“No,” she said.

Oren took the page from her without taking her side, which Ilyan had come to understand was the only way Oren took anybody’s side. “Say the objection in the form the office will understand.”

“The wage line has no hours,” Ilsa said. “A keeper with no hours is a keeper who is on the span whenever the office wants a person on the span. I have counted loads at this bridge for six years. I have done it for the levy rate and I have done it for nothing twice, and both of those were a decision somebody else made in a room I was not in.”

“The guild will pay the hours,” Oren said. “I will put the rate on the board myself.”

“Then put the hours on the board too.” She was not angry. She had the flat, tired patience of a woman who had said this before, in other rooms, for other offices. “From the setup before the first bell to dusk, every change, and the under-deck watch, and the salt crack, and if I am standing here for a count at the third bell in the dark I want it counted as the third bell in the dark. That is all. Name them.”

“If I name them, the district says the bridge is unaffordable.”

“Then the district says it and it is their sentence and not mine.”

The afternoon was already going wrong in the specific way afternoons do when a span needs an inspection and the people who know the span are not the people who hold the paper. On the public approach, four carts had queued since midday: two loads of lime, a water tank for the lower ward, and a cart of lime-stone that had been standing longest and was therefore loudest. Their owners had come up to the rope to ask, in the reasonable voices of men who had been waiting two hours, when the count would be finished.

Tarin had the boundary marked across both approaches and the carts held outside it. He had done that without being asked, because a count that is interrupted by a queue is not a count, and because a queue that is told it is waiting on a person rather than on a load will eventually become a crowd.

“The order permits named inspectors inside the line,” he said to Ilyan, in the voice he used for reading a rule out loud, which was the only voice he had. “Two named guild keepers, the road party, the record hand, and the attached record-holder as observer. The workroom stays outside. The ward and the district stay outside. If a cart crosses, the count is void and we start again at the second bell, and the district will pay for the second bell because the district sent the cart.”

“I am not asking to be inside for the count,” Ilyan said. “I am asking for something narrower and I want to say it in front of the people who will have to live in it.”

“Ask.”

“Before the seventh morning bell, this span needs a west-joint inspection that is paid, finite, and performed by named hands. That is the request. It is not a rule about labor. It does not say a keeper may stop a load. It does not say anything about any other bridge, or any other district, or any other road. It says: this inspection, these hours, this pay, ending at the review bell.”

Tarin’s mouth did not move for a moment. “Say the part you are not saying.”

“I am not saying that a worker’s refusal can halt the crossing.”

“You were not going to say it,” Tarin replied. “You were going to let the room assume it, because the room has spent a week assuming things about your name, and this time it would have assumed something in your favor. Do not do that. The load does not care whose objection is in the record. If Ilsa refuses the forward schedule, the inspection still happens, and it happens because the guild schedules it, and the guild is answerable to its own wage board and its own kept record. If it happens because the attached record-holder asked the Bench for a paid inspection, then in a week a district will ask for a paid inspection and be refused, and the refusal will be the record-holder’s name in the schedule room.”

“That is fair,” Ilyan said.

“It is not fair, it is accurate, and I would like it both.” Tarin looked at the queued carts. “I will not let a narrow request become the reason a person is treated as a switch. The span gets inspected today. The question is who pays and for how many hours, and that is a wage question, and a wage question can be answered by people with a wage board.”

That was the disagreement, and it happened in the open, and Ilyan lost it in the way he had been losing things all week: by being the man who reached for the institution when the man with the board had the answer.

Oren took the schedule back, read it, and struck the second *until reviewed* out with his own chalk, which was a liberty, and which the guild had done twice before him and would do again.

“Hours, then,” Oren said to Ilsa. “Name them and I will pay them out of the levy until the levy is spent, and after the levy is spent I will put a line on the board that says the inspection was not performed, and I will put my name on that line instead of yours.”

“From the setup before the first bell to dusk, every change, under-deck at every fourth change,” Ilsa said. “And the salt crack, which takes an hour and a half and which nobody has paid for since the year before last.”

“The salt crack is paid.”

“It has been paid as part of the joint brace. It has never been paid as the salt crack, and the salt crack is what found the drift, and the drift is what the whole argument is about.”

Oren wrote it on the board and did not argue, and the board was cleanest it had been in four days.

Then the carts came in under Tarin’s line, one at a time, and the count ran, and the count came back to its mark, and the count came back to its mark again, and on the third run the west joint came back to its mark and stayed there two fingers past it and then, very slowly, walked back.

Ilsa did not move. She read the scale twice, then a third time, and then she took her hand off it.

“Two fingers,” she said.

Nobody spoke. The span was not visibly wrong. The span looked like a span that had been doing exactly what it was told.

“Again,” she said.

Oren rang the bell before and after. The count came back to the mark and walked out two fingers and came back again, over about the length of a slow breath. The pale line on the deck was the other measurement, the one the joint had taken and kept; this was what the load borrowed and gave back, and the two of them together were the difference between a bridge that had settled and a bridge that was still moving.

“That is the maintenance review,” Ilsa said. “That is what the old term was. The keeper does the named work, the load comes back, and the bridge tells you whether the work counted. It has been telling us since the field. The joint is settling a fraction every time it is loaded, and it is settling in the direction it settled in before the brace, and the brace is holding and the joint is not.”

“Which is why the charter cannot be the whole of the safety,” Oren said.

“Which is why you cannot ring a bell and stop a load,” Ilsa said. “There is a keeper on this span who can see the failure, and there is no clause that lets her do one thing about it, and if the fraction is a fraction today it is a third of a finger next month, and when it is a third of a finger the span goes somewhere between one count and the next and the clause that should have stopped it does not exist.”

The carts were outside the line. The four of them had heard this. The owner of the standing lime-stone cart said, to nobody in particular and in a voice pitched to carry, that the keeper had refused her schedule, and that the bridge was going unwatched, and that this was what the bridge record had done to the bridge.

Bevin Larch, the lower ward’s schedule clerk, who had been standing at the rope with a rolled notice he had not been able to deliver to anybody, said the rest of it. He said it politely, because clerks are trained before they are recruited.

“With respect to the guild, if the keeper will not sign the schedule then the guild has no named keeper, and a bridge with no named keeper cannot be certified, and an uncertified bridge cannot carry lawful traffic, and the attached record-holder is the reason the schedule exists, so if the schedule is not signed it is his record that has stopped the ninth bridge from being counted.”

It was a beautiful sentence. It had the grammar of the whole week in it, from the copied petition to the scraped margin, and it had exactly one error in it, which was the place where the man stood.

Ilyan did not step forward. He looked at Tarin, and Tarin shook his head once, because the boundary was holding and a man who moves into it is a man who has become the event.

Ilsa Bray put her hand back on the scale.

“I will say this once, and then I will go back to counting,” she said, to the clerk, not to the room. “I did not refuse the count. I refused the words *until reviewed and thereafter*. Those words put my hands on that span for as long as somebody in an office finds it convenient, and they pay me for a shift I cannot name, and when the count comes back two fingers out every time and I write it down, the schedule says I was working, so nobody can tell whether I was paid or standing there. That is the whole of my refusal. It is about a form. It is not about the span, the span is getting counted now, and it is getting counted by me.”

“And the load?” the clerk asked. “Who authorizes the load?”

“The keeper,” Ilsa said, “and the charter, and the count, and the district if the district issues a maintenance order, which it can do, and which is the correct way to do it if a keeper is wrong. That is four authorities. None of them is a schedule that has not been signed.”

Oren wrote the refusal into the guild’s count book himself, in his own hand, which made it a guild record and not a refusal of a district form.

**OFFERED BY THE KEEPER: the west-joint sequence, the load-count at every change, the under-deck watch, and the salt-crack check, at named hours and the levy rate.**
**DECLINED BY THE KEEPER: the forward schedule. The stated term was “until reviewed and thereafter.” No hours, no end, no wage beyond the unnumbered shift.**
**THE COUNT IS PERFORMED. THE REFUSAL IS OF THE FORM AND NOT OF THE WORK.**

“It will be read as obstruction,” Sera said from the rope, where she had been standing since midday. “And it will be read as obstruction by a district that has not paid for the salt crack in two years, which is a sentence I would like somebody to write down next to yours.”

Oren took the chalk and wrote it next to his own entry, because the guild had a fund that was not the levy and there was a little of it left. **SALT CRACK, EIGHT HOURS, PAID FROM THE GUILD’S OWN ACCOUNT, THIS WEEK ONLY.** He signed it and did not look at Ilyan, because it was not the attached record-holder’s money and it was not the attached record-holder’s win, and both of them knew it.

“It is what she said,” Oren replied. “I wrote what she said.”

“It will be read as obstruction anyway, and the reading will be in the district’s hands and not ours, and he will use it.” He meant Ilyan, and did not pretend otherwise.

“Then he can use it,” Oren said, “and I will put the two fingers on the board every morning until somebody with a charter makes it mean something.”

Ilyan filed the narrow request through Sera at the last bell of the afternoon: a paid, finite west-joint inspection, named hours, named pay, ending at the seventh morning bell, with no rule about labor and no power to halt a load. It was the smallest request he had filed in Veyra. It took eleven lines and it granted nobody anything except a wage, and when the court translator entered it, the only formal line that came back into his sight was short enough to have been written by somebody who had grown tired of long sentences.

**INSPECTION REQUESTED: ONE SPAN, WEST JOINT, UNTIL THE SEVENTH MORNING BELL. PAID. NAMED HANDS. NO EFFECT ON ANY OTHER ROAD, SPAN, KEEPER, OR DISTRICT.**

“Paying for it is the whole remedy,” Oren said, reading it over Ilyan’s shoulder. “The district will argue that a paid inspection is a permanent wage. It is not. It ends at the bell. And if they want it after the bell they can come to the guild and put it in front of me, and I will say the same thing I have said since the year before last, which is that a keeper with no hours is a keeper who is on the span whenever the office wants a person on the span.”

They cleared the carts. The span held. And as the crew was putting the count book away, Ilsa Bray did not leave with the others. She stood at the board with her hood in her hands and looked at the old frame on the wall behind it, where the guild kept the rubbing of the sealed plate’s edge that the court had taken before the original went into the locker.

“Something,” she said.

“Something about the plate?” Ilyan said.

“About the plate and the count.” She came over and put a finger under the rubbing without touching it. “You have all been reading the edge notation as a name. It is a name, there is no doubt about that, and the Kest letters are cut into the saltglass and they were cut by somebody who was not doing it carelessly, because the salt has gone white in the cut. But that is not where the name is.”

She traced the air above the rubbing.

“This edge is the load-book edge,” she said. “The plate is not a keeper’s plate. The count on a keeper’s plate is stamped in the middle, where anybody can see it. The count on a load book is cut along the edge, because a load book is a running thing and the edge is cheaper. My grandmother’s book has cuts like that down two edges and a name cut once at the front and never again.”

“Nobody has ever told me that,” Oren said, and the fact that he said it in the voice he used for a gauge reading, without embarrassment, told Ilyan more about the last twenty-two years of the ninth-bridge guild than anything the guild had ever entered in a record.

“The count on that plate was kept by hand,” Ilsa said. “Hand-cut, edge by edge, the way a person does when they are counting loads and are also the person the loads are counted against. And you do not count loads alone. There is a keeper, who does the work, and there is a book-keeper, who does the count and signs for it, and both of them are witnesses, because a load count that one person makes alone is a load count that the office can rewrite with a chalk line.”

Oren had gone very still.

“The crew list for that year names one witness,” he said. “The keeper. I have seen the list. I have signed the list.”

“The list is the list the office made after the year,” Ilsa said. “The crew had a keeper and a book-keeper, because a book-keeper is not optional, and because the old charter says two, and the old charter is what the sealed plate was cut to agree with.” She straightened up. “I was fourteen. I was in the room when the year was closed out. There was a woman counting the west edge, and she had been counting it for six years, and when the office came with the new list there was one name on it and she was not the name on it, and she said one sentence, and I have thought about that sentence every year since because it did not make any sense to me and I was fourteen.”

“What was the sentence?”

Ilsa looked at Oren.

“She said, *a book without a second hand is only a record*,” she said. “And then the office paid her off the book and she went and did not come back, and that was the year the first field came. I stood at the back of the Merefen hall with the rest of the people who had no business being there, the night the levy ledger was read into the record, and there is a name cut out of that ledger in the same manner. I have been sitting here for a week trying to decide whether the two cuts are the same hand, and I am not able to decide it, and I would like somebody in this room to stop me from deciding it.”

Nobody in the workroom said anything. The span ticked as the temperature dropped, which was ordinary, and which was the only sound in the room for a while.

Ilyan wrote in his notebook, in the order he had learned to write things: the two fingers, the salt crack, the load-book edge, the second hand, and then, at the bottom, a line he did not turn into a sentence.

**A book without a second hand is only a record.**

He looked at the rubbing on the wall, where the letters **S. Kest** sat in white-cut saltglass on the edge that only a load-book keeper would have cut, and he did not write the name next to them, because the name was already in an older index in a public archive and a woman had died or gone quiet somewhere behind it, and the mark on the plate was not a signature. It was a removal with a witness’s own hand in the cut.

Oren came back from the board and looked at the rubbing for a long time.

“Two hands on the count,” he said. “Twenty-two years and I have never once had two hands on the count. And the charter that says two is the one they took the clause out of, and they left the plate.”

He wiped the chalk board and did not write on it yet.

“So we go into the review with a keeper who can see a two-finger drift and no clause that lets her stop a load, and a plate in a locker with a load-book name cut off its edge, and a public name attached to a case that has cost the ward a water delivery and the household half a hand a day.” He set the chalk down. “That is what we have. I would like it to be worth more than it is.”

“It is worth a wage and a two-finger measurement,” Ilsa said. “That is two things more than we had this morning.”

The bell gave one clear note for the last cart, the count came back to its mark and walked out two fingers and came back, and the guild’s book said in Oren’s own chalk that a keeper had refused a form and performed the work, and the plate in the locker said, in cut saltglass on the load-book edge, that somebody had once had to be two people to keep a bridge honest, and had been reduced to a name with the rest of it sawn off.

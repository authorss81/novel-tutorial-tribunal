# Chapter 20: The Precedent Has a Body

The district offered the ward a choice before the morning bell.

If Mava Rusk signed the maintenance acknowledgment, the Saltwheel Road would reopen for the ordinary cart lane, the shelter delivery board would be restored, and the district would withdraw the automatic-renewal order. If she refused, the road would remain open only for water carriers and people named in the local work slate. The ward’s refusal would be entered in the public register, where carts and landlords could see that the road depended on a condition the district did not recognize.

Mava read the offer twice. “They want the refusal visible.”

“They want it legible as a threat,” Ilyan said.

“That may be the same thing.”

The public hearing boundary had been drawn on the paving beside the pump. Tarin’s guards stood outside it. Joren Vell and two roadkeepers occupied the work line. Oren stood with them, carrying a bridgekeeper’s tool that he had deliberately left sheathed. Dalla Rusk and the pump workers held the water route. Lio Dain had the local work slate, its two signed copies tied through a clean cord.

Sera stood beside Ilyan with the public court slate open. The copied petition remained sealed in its blackglass case. The pause from the night had not destroyed it. The words about the unlisted ward could still be read by anyone who approached the case.

Meral read the district offer. “The road remains open under the existing arrangement. The ward signs, and the acknowledgment becomes a condition of ordinary maintenance. Without the signature, the district will use the local arrangement only until the morning bell.”

Joren stepped toward the boundary. “Then the maintenance work has no end.”

“The work has the end we named,” Meral said. “The road does not.”

“Who decides that the road has ended the work?”

“The district maintenance office.”

“By what condition?”

“By the office’s assessment.”

Joren shook his head. “The broken seam is visible. The water route is visible. The assessment is not.”

A cart owner called from the eastern side. “If the ward refuses, our loads stop. Do you want our families to carry that so the ward can keep its name?”

Mava turned toward him. “I do not want your family to carry it. I am refusing to let your family use my refusal as a reason to make me carry the water and shelter cost.”

The cart owner’s face tightened. “That is easy to say when your shelter is already full.”

“Then give the shelter a place in the arrangement. Give the pump hours. Name the work. Show what happens when I say no.”

The district clerk opened a ledger. “The common register does not recognize a ward refusal as a separate civic act.”

Mava pointed to the local work slate. “This is a separate civic act. Four people worked. Two roadkeepers worked. The district supplied stone. The pump stayed open. If you do not call that work, you are calling labor invisible so you can call refusal dangerous.”

Ilyan opened his notebook but kept it below the boundary line. His role was to document the sequence, not to stand between the ward and the district. The distinction was harder to maintain than a speech would have been. Every person at the pump had a practical reason to favor the road’s opening. Every person who lived beside the broken seam had a practical reason to fear the next cart. He could not turn those reasons into a single burden without repeating the district’s mistake.

Sera entered the local arrangement into the public record. The court slate translated only the formal lines, but the people who gathered could hear the six named provisions as Sera read them aloud: the work, the hours, the materials, the route, the review, and the exit. The local arrangement did not amend the copied petition. It did not make the road permanently safe. It allowed the named work to continue until the morning bell while the parties disputed the automatic renewal.

Meral objected. “The ward is not a party to the road’s charter.”

“It is an affected party to the road’s physical condition,” Joren said. “If you want its labor, you have to hear its refusal.”

The marshal from the assembly stood at the edge of the hearing. He had not been named in the local petition, but the court had admitted his district’s objection as part of the copied-precedent record. He raised one hand.

“If the court allows the ward to refuse an acknowledgment while still using the road, every unregistered settlement will claim the same protection.”

“No,” Ilyan said.

The marshal turned. “You are not the authority.”

“No. But the copied record is using my name to say that I am. The arrangement before you is not mine. The people who worked the road chose these terms. I am documenting them.”

He looked at the assembled road users, ward residents, district workers, and guild witnesses.

“I will not offer a universal declaration. I will not say that every person may refuse every burden. I will not say that the ninth-bridge stay makes every road safe. The ninth bridge had a named bridge, a named crew, a specific household, and a review date. This arrangement has a named route, named work, a limited review, and an exit condition. The resemblance is a question, not a ruling.”

The marshal frowned. “Then why should the district accept it?”

“Because the alternative is a road whose burden is hidden and whose users do not know what they are paying. You can oppose the arrangement publicly. You can show that it fails. You cannot pretend the old petition makes the new burden harmless.”

Meral looked at the clock tower across the river. The first pale light had reached the pump. The morning bell would sound soon. If the ward signed, the district would have a new acknowledgment. If it refused, the local route would remain open for a shorter time and the public register would carry the refusal.

Sera looked at Mava. “You do not have to accept the district’s offer. You can accept the local arrangement for the hours and people named, and let the record show that you refused the acknowledgment. The choice is yours. It is not consent to the petition.”

Mava looked at the work slate. “If we refuse, do we lose the pump?”

“The pause holds until the review,” Sera said. “The road remains open for the named route. The district may issue a new order after the review, but it must name the work and the effect of the refusal. It cannot treat the current use as a signature.”

Meral said, “The district does not agree to that limitation.”

“Then the Bench can hear the disagreement,” Sera replied.

Mava’s fingers rested on the edge of the slate. “I refuse the acknowledgment. I accept the work already named, the hours already offered, and the public review. I do not accept permanent renewal. I do not accept the loss of water or shelter because I refused.”

A folded note from Neris waited under the pump ledger. She and Tamsin were still bound by the ninth-bridge stay, and the household’s access to work and shelter remained temporary. The note asked that the road review not use Tamsin’s bell fact, Neris’s flood account, or the Ro household’s crossing as proof that a copied rule was safe. Ilyan read the request aloud to Sera and then placed it in the public case sleeve.

Lio and Joren signed beneath Mava’s line. Dalla signed the water-route entry. The district’s two loaders did not sign, but they continued working while the dispute was recorded. Their labor was named as district labor, not as ward consent.

The clerk entered the refusal and the local arrangement into the public register. This time, the copy in the common ledger included the words **no acknowledgment** and the list of the hours and people who had carried the work. Meral had also reclassified the two loaders as district emergency labor, paid by the district and kept off the ward’s work slate. They did not sign the acknowledgment; their presence did not cure the dispute. Meral watched the refusal line appear and did not strike it out.

Harrow’s formal notice appeared above the public slate.

**LOCAL ARRANGEMENT: THE NAMED ROUTE REMAINS OPEN FOR ONE REVIEW INTERVAL.**
**BURDEN: THE WARD’S REFUSAL IS RECORDED; THE DISTRICT’S LEVY IS NOT ACCEPTED BY THE WARD.**
**EXIT: THE ARRANGEMENT ENDS AT THE MORNING BELL UNLESS THE AFFECTED PARTIES CHOOSE TO RENEW IT.**
**THE COPIED PETITION REMAINS IN THE RECORD AND IS NOT A RULING.**

Ilyan read the last line twice. The Bench had not made the copied petition harmless. It had made the difference between adoption and authority visible enough for the people living under it to argue.

The morning bell sounded while the clerk was still holding the slate. The local arrangement reached its first interval’s end. Joren looked to Mava and Dalla rather than to the district.

“Do we carry the work for one more marked interval?” he asked.

Mava looked at the water route. “We carry the work we named. We do not sign the acknowledgment. If the district wants a longer road, it must ask again.”

Dalla checked the pump’s clear flow. “The water is steady. We can carry the next interval.”

Lio added the pump hours to the slate. The clerk marked the continuation as a new choice, not an automatic renewal. The named route remained open, the exit condition remained visible, and the ward’s refusal stayed in the register. The bridge case had not changed, and the ninth bridge’s seventh-morning-bell review was still approaching. This was only the road’s next bounded interval.

Oren finally opened his tool case. “The road edge needs one more brace before the first cart.”

Ilyan looked at him. “Will the guild carry that?”

“The guild carries the bridge repair knowledge. The road crew carries the road work. I can show them the brace, not take their place.”

Joren chose a wedge and a length of binding fiber. Oren showed him where to place the brace without closing the drainage sleeve. Ilyan recorded the order. The local workers performed the repair. The district supplied the stone. The ward residents chose which water jars to move first. Every contribution appeared in the public record.

The first cart crossed after the morning bell. It stayed beneath the marked load. The pump ran clear. The ward’s shelter received its water, though the delivery board still carried a blank for the next ordinary cycle. The arrangement had not produced a permanent answer. It had kept a road usable while the parties continued to disagree.

Meral approached Ilyan after the cart passed. “You have made your name dangerous.”

“It was already attached.”

“To more than the bridge.”

“Yes.”

He looked toward the sealed petition. “The court may use your report in the full hearing.”

“I know.”

“It may also use the fact that you accepted the road arrangement.”

“I accepted a role in documenting it. I did not adopt the petition.”

Meral’s expression remained skeptical. “The distinction will not comfort the people who pay for the next delay.”

“No,” Ilyan said. “It should not.”

He turned to Sera. “Your archive clue?”

Sera had been watching the second paper from the night, which lay in a clear sleeve beside the public register. At its lower fold, beneath the translated sentence, the custody code **C—2** matched the code on the continuity request in the archive. A second line pointed through the bridge-plate office before ending at the public petition desk. She had not allowed the paper to enter the road petition as evidence. The active case record displayed the copied wording, while the paper remained under the clerk’s separate preservation mark.

“After the review is entered,” she said, “I need to compare the continuity request with the bridge plate inventory.”

“Now?”

“Now, while the district is still looking at the road. If the request was routed through a missing witness, the routing mark may be in the old plate’s edge notation.”

Neral brought a narrow rubbing from the court locker. The old saltglass plate itself remained sealed, but the clerk had recorded its dimensions, its inventory number, and the visible edge marks before it was placed in the case. Sera held the rubbing beside the gray request and the second paper.

The levy ledger’s removed witness entry ended in **Kest**. The older witness index contains a matching name, **Sava Kest**, and the continuity request carried **C—2**. The second paper carried the same code and a bridge-plate route line. On the old bridge plate’s edge notation, beneath a salt crack, a partial set of letters matched the indexed name: **S. Kest**. When Neral unfolded the paper’s lower margin, a receipt mark dated the routing before the ninth-bridge public notice and before Ilyan’s arrival. The date was ordinary ink, not a magical answer.

Sera did not touch any of the records. “The second paper was routed through the same chain as the continuity request. The name may be a witness, a copied name, or a custody office. The records do not tell us which.”

“It does tell us where the record went after it left the Quiet Chamber,” Ilyan said.

“And that is the next question,” Sera replied. “Not who removed the page. Not yet.”

The road noise continued around them. A pump worker called a time. A cart driver shouted the load limit. Mava’s refusal remained visible in the public register, and Ilyan’s name remained attached to the observation that had not pretended to settle it.

Sera looked at the gray paper. “The question is no longer who you are or whether you authored Veyra. It is how your private words entered a Veyran legal record before you arrived.”

She placed the rubbing in the preservation sleeve. Ilyan watched the final letters disappear behind the paper, leaving only the date and the missing name.

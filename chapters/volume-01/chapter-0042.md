# Chapter 42: The Book Nobody Had Sworn

The shelter on the lower road had three rooms and a bench and a drying line and the particular cold of a building where four people are getting through a night on purpose, and Ilyan had slept in worse rooms on Earth and none of them had a rope and a bell and four hundred people in them.

He came down the lane at the second hour of the night with Neris, because she had said she would be standing there when he said it and he was not going to make her walk up to the shelter alone to hear whether he had managed it.

Marda Rennick was at the bench with her knee out in front of her the way she sat, and Besh was asleep across her legs with a boot on, because a nine-year-old who cannot be got up off a bench goes to sleep on the bench and stays there.

“Room nine,” Neris said.

“I know which room nine is,” Ilyan said, and then stopped, because he had said the number out loud on a public approach more than once and once in a lane, and every single time it had cost somebody something, and the number was a woman's address.

Marda looked up. Her knee had gone in the winter and it was not the knee that was the problem and she was the only person on that road who had never said so out loud.

“It is the roof,” she said.

“It is the roof,” Ilyan said, “and I am going to say it in one piece and I am not going to say the word *carrier* in front of your son, and there is a reason, and the reason is not politeness, it is that he is nine and a word like that in a child's ear at two in the morning becomes the child's account of why.”

“Go on, then,” Marda said.

“The load went east,” Ilyan said. “It went east twice, yesterday and this morning, and it will not come back tonight, because the span has an end date on it and nothing behind the end date, and so a load that comes up the fen road will not come up the fen road to this road. That is not a fault in the bridge and it is not a fault in your road. It is the district's. The district wrote the end date, the district knows the end date is there, and the district's clerk has written down in a public roll that the district's schedule for this span ended on its own term, and that is a document and it is in a book and the book is in a room you could walk to and look at.”

He had the sentence exactly as Neris had given it and he delivered it without stopping, and he had one more sentence left and it was the one he had not been given.

“The word for the man who brought it is not going to be said in this room,” he said, “and the reason is that he is doing the only sensible thing anybody can do with a road that ends, and I am not going to stand in a lane at two in the morning and make a nine-year-old the reason a district feels better about itself.”

Marda Rennick looked down at her son for a while.

“Then you have said it well,” she said, “and you have left out the useful part, which is whether it comes back.”

“It comes back if the span has a morning behind it,” Ilyan said. “That is the whole of it. There is a hearing at the fourth morning bell and it is the last day of a week and it is a thin day, and I do not know what happens at it, and neither do you, and I have spent three weeks learning that the honest answer to that is the only answer that does not turn into an instrument later.”

“Then it is a thin day,” Marda said, “and Besh will be nine for a long time yet.”

She got up off the bench in three separate stages with a hand on the wall, and Besh woke up and did not cry, which Ilyan had the sense to understand was a thing that had been practised.

---

The landlord's book was in a box under a shelf in the back room of the shelter, and it was eleven inches by seven and it had been kept for twelve years by a woman named Hanne Kolm who was sixty-one and who had never once sworn it.

“Not sworn,” Hanne Kolm said, before anybody asked, when Ilyan came in with the box still in his hands. “Twelve years. Every tenancy in this row, every rent, every repair, every room, and not one line of it sworn, because when I took the book the man before me told me a sworn book is a book you can be sued out of, and I have been sitting on that sentence for twelve years and I have been paid in certainty.”

“Certainty of what.”

“That a room is a room and that the rent is the rent and that I did not put a family out in the winter for a month.” She took the box from him and set it on the bench and did not open it. “Swear it and every landlord in this ward can be named. That is the whole of it. There is a ward on the fen side with a sworn book and four names in it and two of them are on a bench by a river with nowhere to go, and the fourth one is dead, and the two of them are in it because a sworn book does not ask permission before it writes.”

“So the district cannot use it,” Ilyan said, “to show that the Ro household holds a room.”

“The district cannot use it to show that anybody holds anything, because there are no sworn tenancies on this road at all and there never have been and that is not an accident, it is the reason people are still here.” Hanne Kolm put her hand flat on the box. “The book says the truth. The book is not evidence. Those are two different sentences and this ward has been living on the difference for twelve years and I am not the person who ends it and I am not the person who gives it up.”

“Then the shelter has no standing in the morning,” Neris said, from the doorway, “and if the settlement does not reach the tenancy then the household has a bridge and no house, and I have been married to that arrangement for two years.”

“Your mother is not married to a settlement,” Ilyan said.

“My mother is married to a fact,” Neris said. “There is no house. There is a room on a road with a landlord who will not put her name in a book that could be read, and if a district's clerk comes down this lane with an instrument then the first thing he will want to know is where the household lives, and there is no answer to that which does not put us in a register.”

Which was the knot, and Ilyan had known it was the knot since the seventh bell the previous morning, and he had spent the day watching four other people arrive at it from four different directions and be right.

“Then let me be plain about the whole of it,” he said, “because I have a bad habit of arriving at a room with four facts and letting the room believe I have a plan.”

He put the case file on the bench. He did not open it.

“There are three things this household can ask for and I am going to read all three out because I can read the numbers in them and I am not going to be the one who decides which of them sounds best.

“One. A crossing. A named hour on the repaired load path, for the work, at the ward rate and not at a toll, with no registration required, and with the condition that the crossing does not depend on the span being under any particular charter — that it survives the ruling either way. I can read that and it is the one I would argue for, because it is a number and a place and I have watched four people in four rooms make worse things work because the number was on the paper.

“Two. A room. The tenancy on the lower road protected for the term, and the term stated, and the protection not ending because a household refused something, and the landlord's position stated, and a sworn book with names in it, sworn, and the swearing paid for by somebody. I cannot read that one. I want to say that in front of you now rather than in front of a court. I can read the crossing hour and I can read the ward rate and I can read the hours in a district schedule. I cannot read what a sworn book does, and I have known that for nineteen days, and tonight it is the thing standing between your household and a room.

“Three. A refusal. Not a signature. A way for a person to refuse a specific burden on this span and be *entered* as having refused it, and be still here, and keep the crossing, and keep the room, and be able to prove they refused on a date and not be argued out of it afterwards by whoever is holding the book.”

The room was very quiet. Besh was awake on the bench with his boot on and had stopped pretending to be asleep, which Ilyan had not intended and could not now un-intend.

“That is three,” Neris said, “and two of them are things a district can refuse for a reason I already know, and the third one is a thing nobody in this city has, and you have just told me you cannot read the second one.”

“Yes,” Ilyan said. “I cannot read the second one, and I am telling you instead of finding out at the fourth morning bell in a room where it will be worth something to somebody.”

Hanne Kolm, who had not moved her hand off the box, said, “If a sworn book is produced, it names families. It is not a register of refusals. It is a register of addresses, and an address is the thing this ward has spent a decade refusing to be.”

“Which is the answer,” Neris said. “Not to the room. To the third thing.”

“No,” Hanne Kolm said. “It is a fact about the room. I have been trying to tell you what a sworn book is for two years and nobody has ever asked me, because nobody has ever needed the answer.”

Ilyan looked at her.

“What is it for,” he said.

“It is for a landlord,” Hanne Kolm said. “If I swear the book, I can name the family, and if I can name the family then a district cannot move them, and if a district cannot move them then *I* can move them, and I have been unable to move anybody for twelve years because I could not put a name on a page without the name becoming a thing other people could use. I have been running the only safe landlord's book in this ward by never swearing it, and I have been hated for it, and my sister has not spoken to me about it in six years.”

Nobody said anything for a while, and the water dripped off the drying line into the bucket, which had a slow, uneven, deliberate rhythm that Ilyan found he could not stop listening to.

“I am not going to swear it for a hearing,” Hanne Kolm said. “I have thought about it for the eleven seconds you have been standing there and I have thought about it for two years. I will swear it if the thing that gets sworn is a refusal, because a refusal does not move anybody and a name does.”

“Can it be both,” Sera said.

She had come down the lane without anybody hearing her, which was a thing she could do and which Ilyan had stopped finding startling around the ninth day.

Hanne Kolm looked at her.

“No,” Hanne Kolm said. “It can be a column. You do not have to throw the names out to get a column in. A book can have a name column and a refusal column and they are not the same column, and the district would tell you they are the same column, and the district would be *right* about how a column works and *wrong* about what is in it.”

“Show me,” Ilyan said.

“You cannot read it,” Hanne Kolm said.

“Show me anyway. I will get somebody to read it and I will say out loud, in that room tomorrow, in front of the court and the district, that I cannot read it, and then I will get somebody to read it to me, and I will say that out loud too, and neither of those two things gets me the third thing, and the third thing is the only one I have ever been asked for by a person instead of by a problem.”

Hanne Kolm picked the box up.

“There is one more thing and it is not mine, it is the child's, and I am going to pass it on and then I am going to go and stand in the lane.”

Neris said, “Tamsin is asleep.”

“Tamsin is *not* asleep,” Hanne Kolm said, “and she has been listening at that wall for ten minutes with her eyes shut, which is what she does, and you are going to let her speak or you are going to tell her why not, and I have watched four adults in three weeks decide things for her and I have not got the patience to be the fifth.”

She opened the box on the bench and took out the book, and it was smaller inside than it looked, and the first page had a date on it in a hand that was not hers, and a hundred and some entries after that in eleven different hands, all of them hers except two.

Tamsin came in from the drying-line room with the stick and no blanket and her hair wet at the ends, which meant she had been at the water butt, which meant she had not been asleep at all.

“I am not going in the room,” she said.

“You are not going in the room,” Sera agreed.

“The second of the five terms says it is said once, in the workroom, the span in view,” Tamsin said. “I worked that out on my own, in about a day, and then I worked out the other half of it, which is that if I am going to say it in front of a court then the span has to be in view of the court, and I have thought about that for six days and I am not going anywhere I have to be carried to.

“You did not promise me anything,” she said. “You said if anybody asks me a question about a machine I give them the machine. So the rope has to be in front of me, because there is only one and it has never done anything, and I am not going to stand in a room somewhere else and talk about a wheel on a pin when the wheel is thirty feet away.”

Tamsin looked at the book on the bench and at her mother and at Ilyan, in that order, and took as long as she wanted to, which was about ten seconds, and Ilyan found himself counting, and stopped.

“I will go to the span,” she said, “in daylight, with the rope there, and I will say what the bell is, and I will not say what it means, and I will stop if I want to stop. And I am going to say the fourth thing first, before anybody reads the account, and the fourth thing is that the account went to the guild first and I gave it there and nobody made me, and the court has to say it out loud before they use it.”

“You have to know what you are going to be asked,” Sera said, “because they will ask you what the bell was for, and they will ask it kindly, and they will not mean any harm by it.”

“Then they will ask me a question about a machine,” Tamsin said, “and I will give them the machine, and they can have the machine.”

“Or they will ask whether the bridge should have stopped,” Neris said.

“And then I will say it again,” Tamsin said, “the same words, the same as the first time, and if they ask it a third time then I will get off the span and go and stand on the steps, and I have told the room that now so that it cannot be a surprise and called a refusal.”

Hanne Kolm shut the book.

“Write that down somewhere it can be read,” she said, to Ilyan, and he did not write it down, because it was not his and there was nowhere in the case to put it, and that was by now a rule he had and kept.

---

The walk up to the span at a quarter to the fourth bell took forty minutes from the lower road because the stair is at the bottom of the water and the tide was out, and they went up in single file in the dark with the rope-bell's frame visible from three streets away as a shape against a sky that had begun, at the horizon and only at the horizon, to be a different colour.

The approach was already busy, and it was busy in the way it got busy when something was going to happen, which was not with shouting but with people arriving early and standing in the places they had worked out over three weeks, which were not the places anybody had marked.

The name came at him from a carter at the eastern stake, cheerfully, as a joke, and it went through him like cold water.

“No,” he said, in the guild's flatness. “It is Ilyan. It has been Ilyan for nineteen days and you have all been too polite or too frightened to fix me, and I would like it fixed now, in daylight, at the fourth bell, because the next district that copies this case is going to copy my name wrong.”

“Sedge,” the carter said, laughing, “he has been a Sedge to me since the second day, because his name is in the record column and Oren's is in the work column, and I read the board at a canter.”

“Then you have been calling him wrong for nineteen days,” Oren said, from the maintenance line, where he had been since the first hour of the night with four hands and a count book and a slate, “and I have heard every one of them, and I never corrected you, because a man who corrects a nickname is a man who has time, and I have not had time, and now the clerk is going to write it on a record and it will be wrong on a record, and a wrong name on a record is a small thing today and it is not a small thing in four years.”

The carter stopped laughing. “Ilyan,” he said.

“Good,” Ilyan said. “Say it in front of the child when she gets here. She has been correcting me for two days and she has been getting at it wrong on purpose and I want her to know.”

Then Oren rang the bell for the change, and the bell gave one clear note over the river and came back, and the fourth morning bell went, and the arrangement ended, and nobody had to say so, because the frame said it and the frame was thirty feet away and it was the most-read document in the lower ward.

The count came back at mark. Out two. Back. A half on the light one.

Harrow came up the lower steps with Roke and two clerks and no escort, for the second time in a fortnight, and stopped at the rope, and looked at the span, and looked at the west seat, and read the guild's board from six feet away without appearing to try. About a hundred and forty people were on the approach, standing in the places they had worked out over three weeks, which were not the places anybody had marked.

“That is your costing,” he said to Oren.

“It is my counter's costing,” Oren said. “She did the sum and I wrote it down.”

“And it says a pause cannot be bought out of anything in this case.”

“It says a pause cannot be bought out of anything in this case as the case stood at dusk,” Oren said, “and Dain has put the district's answer on the frame in chalk, and Larch read it out at the rope, and I have written down on the board that the guild's own hands are still unpaid and will be unpaid this morning, and the guild's account is empty, and I am not complaining, and I would like it noticed that I have written *I am not complaining* in chalk on a public board for the second night running.”

Harrow read that too.

“Ring the bell,” he said.

“That is not what the bell is for,” Oren said.

“Ring it,” Harrow said, “and then the span is in a hearing, and I am going to open the full hearing on a bridge at the fourth morning bell on the last day of a week, because it is the last day of a week and there is a cart book that says there will be three loads and all of them light, and I would like the district and the guild and the household to be able to say afterwards that the day was not chosen to flatter anybody's case.”

Oren put his hand on the rope and pulled it, and the wheel turned, and the hammer came down inside the bell, and the note went out across the water and up the fen road and along the lower stair, and about a hundred and forty people who had been standing in the places they had worked out over three weeks turned round and looked at a span that was going to be a room for the whole day.

The System put the opening of the hearing into Ilyan's sight, flat and without comment, and this time it was a record and not a choice, and it read:

**FULL HEARING, NINTH BRIDGE. FOURTH MORNING BELL, LAST DAY OF THE WEEK.**
**PARTIES: THE NINTH-BRIDGE GUILD, SIX CREWS. THE LOWER-WARD DISTRICT, IN A BORROWED CAPACITY, WITH AN OBJECTION PRESERVED FOR APPEAL. THE RO HOUSEHOLD, PARTICIPATING PARTY, OBJECTION OPEN. THE ROAD PARTY AND THE WARD PUMP, SEPARATELY. NINE NAMED CART OWNERS, AS A TOLL. THE ATTACHED RECORD-HOLDER, AS A WITNESS TO HIS OWN OBSERVATION, WITH NO STANDING AND NO CONSTITUENCY.**
**THE RELIEF ENTERED UNDER THE NINTH-BRIDGE RECORD CONTINUES. IT DID NOT LAPSE WHEN THE LOCAL ARRANGEMENT ENDED AT THIS BELL, AND IT DOES NOT LAPSE IF THIS HEARING IS CONTINUED.**
**REMEDIES BEFORE THE COURT: AMEND THE CHARTER. BREAK THE SPAN. HOLD THE HEARING OPEN.**

Three remedies, and a hundred and forty people on an approach in the cold, and the west seat of a bridge with a hole in the underside of it under the weight of two loads of stone that were sitting at a yard off the fen road, and a rope hanging four feet off the paving on a frame with a wheel on a worn pin.

Sera opened the case file on the bench at the advocate stool.

“Then let us have the three,” she said. “In the order the court put them, and with the burden said out loud before the argument, because I have watched two districts lose an argument in this city by winning the sentence.”

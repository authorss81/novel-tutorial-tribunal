# Chapter 4: The Form of a Challenge

By midday, the lower passage had become a courtroom spillway.

Word of the ninth bridge’s failure had moved faster than the water. People stood along the upper road, kept back by a rope of blue cord and the broad backs of route-arbiter guards. A line of carts waited in the dry channel, their drivers arguing over whether a bridge that had almost killed a child could be called open. At the far end, a black basalt building rose above the road, its doors open to a hall where voices echoed in measured intervals.

Tarin walked Ilyan through a service entrance. Two guards watched the spent token at his belt. One wrote a mark in a ledger; another followed when Ilyan looked toward the court doors.

“Your record is public,” Tarin said. “The clerk will say so before you do.”

“I did not agree to that.”

“No.”

“Then why does it sound like a fact?”

“Because the Bench has a witness slot and your report filled it. A person may call the slot unjust. That is not the same as making it empty.”

The service corridor smelled of wet stone and heated wax. Ilyan had not slept. His shoulder throbbed where it had struck the floor after the crossing, and every bright surface made the room on Earth seem close enough to touch. He had washed river water from his face, but the smell of the test room remained in his clothes: dust, hot plastic, the faint metallic tang of a server fan.

Sera Quill waited beside a narrow desk at the end of the corridor. She wore a dark blue coat with a brass clasp at the throat, and a dozen thin blackglass tablets hung from a belt around her waist. Her hair was tied back so tightly that a few strands had escaped near her ears. She looked at Ilyan as if he were a document that had arrived without a cover page.

“You are the tester,” she said.

“Ilyan Vester.”

“Your name is already on the public notice. I meant the occupation.”

“Former game tester. Quality assurance. I was testing a cancelled project when the project stopped being cancelled.”

Sera’s eyes moved to the notebook under his arm. “You brought your own evidence.”

“Yes.”

“That is either useful or dangerous.”

“Usually both.”

“Usually is not a legal term.”

She turned toward the hall. “I am Sera Quill, a recordwright. I have been assigned to the open advocate slot for your case. That does not make me your friend, and it does not make me responsible for what you say without a record.”

Tarin’s hand moved to the cord around his forearm. “Sera.”

“I heard the witness order.”

The corridor opened into the Chamber of Passage. It was not built for spectacle. Tall stone ribs curved toward a black ceiling, and narrow windows admitted bars of afternoon light. The floor had been worn pale along the path between two black tables. At the far table sat Sel Harrow, a thin man with a close-cropped beard and a white band around his left wrist. A recorder knelt beside him, a stylus poised over a flat tablet.

Harrow looked at Ilyan. “First Defendant.”

“Does the title come with a chair?”

“Does the question come with respect for the record?”

“Usually.”

Harrow’s gaze moved to the notebook. “No local standing. No recognized advocate. You are here because a legacy null passage opened an advocate slot before you arrived. That does not make you innocent, authorized, or welcome.”

Ilyan glanced at Sera. She had already stepped forward.

“Sera Quill, recordwright. I accept provisional representation for the live conflict at the ninth bridge, subject to the limits of this filing.”

Harrow’s expression did not change. “Your license?”

“Provisional.”

“Your warranty?”

“For the record, not for his innocence.”

A murmur moved through the hall. Ilyan recognized the sound of a crowd discovering a distinction it did not want to make. Sera placed a blackglass tablet on the table and turned it so Harrow could read it.

“He asked for help,” she said. “I am answering the request for a record, not his character.”

Harrow looked at Ilyan. “Then state the conflict without pretending to know this city.”

Ilyan set the notebook on the table. He had written the rescue sequence while waiting, but the first page was already crowded with local marks borrowed from Oren. He turned to a clean page and drew six empty lines.

“Claimant: Ilyan Vester, external witness and person named in the open case. Affected parties: Tamsin Ro, Neris Ro, the ninth bridge, its maintenance guild, the people who rely on the crossing, and the court that must maintain the record. Disputed condition: the bridge’s charter treats a named register as the condition for a safe crossing, while the actual span responds to the person’s physical presence and the maintenance state of the support. Burden: I do not yet know who can carry it. Remedy: keep the bridge alive long enough to hear the affected people and determine whether the charter’s missing maintenance condition can be restored.”

Harrow’s stylus moved across the tablet. “You have named a condition and admitted ignorance of a burden.”

“I have not assigned one.”

“That is not the same as naming it.”

“The covenant has six parts,” Ilyan said. “Subject, condition, burden, witness, remedy, and sunset or exit. The current charter has the first five in some form. The last is missing. If I leave the burden blank, the Bench will assign it through the case. If I name one person, I may assign it without consent. Which of those is procedure?”

Sera looked at him. The first sign of approval crossed her face and vanished.

Harrow’s voice hardened. “You do not get to turn a bridge emergency into a lesson.”

“I am trying to know what the form is asking.”

“The form is asking whether you understand that a challenge is a request for a ruling, not a conversation with the record.”

Ilyan looked at the six lines. “Then tell me what a review means.”

Sera answered before Harrow could. “A review is a scheduled re-examination. The evidence is opened again. It does not promise a new ruling, and it does not let someone challenge the ruling that caused the harm.”

“And an appeal?”

“A new challenge against a ruling. It has its own burden.”

“Sunset or exit?”

“A way for the rule to end, or for a person to refuse a particular burden without losing their standing as a citizen. It is not the same as a review.”

Harrow raised one hand. “You are not licensed to define terms in open court.”

“I am asking.”

“Questions are not advocacy.”

“Questions are how a person avoids accepting a blank.”

Sera’s tablet chimed. A line of formal text appeared in the air above the table, translated into Earth English and little else. Sera spoke in Veyran; the court translated the legal terms, and she supplied the plain context an outsider could follow.

**PROVISIONAL ADVOCACY: SERA QUILL.**
**SCOPE: bridge charter, current emergency, and the external witness record.**
**RESPONSIBILITY: exact filing and disclosure of offered testimony.**
**LIMIT: no authority to speak for an absent or unwilling witness.**

Harrow looked at the tablet. “Do you accept the scope?”

“I do,” Sera said.

“And do you understand that the slot is a duty, not a favor?”

“I understand that the Bench assigned a duty to a person who did not ask for it. I will still file the case within my scope.”

A second line appeared beneath the provisional-advocacy notice.

**CHALLENGE ACCEPTED FOR FILING: ILYAN VESTER, EXTERNAL WITNESS. ACCESS EXCEPTION: NOT GRANTED.**

Sera looked at Ilyan. “The court will hear the claim. It has not granted you a crossing.”

Ilyan looked at her. “You do not have to—”

“Do not mistake a decision for a rescue,” she said. “If I file, the court can make you answer. If I do not file, someone else will receive the open advocate slot, and Tamsin Ro will have no advocate present for the access review. I am filing because the case matters, not because I believe you deserve relief.”

Harrow’s hand moved over a small black plate. A voice filled the hall, not the System’s translated tone but the older, formal sound of a clerk reading from a public record.

“Legacy null passage, external witness, one use consumed. The passage provides an advocate slot and a first hearing. It does not provide consent, standing, or a return route.”

A second notice appeared over Ilyan.

**WITNESS DUTY: remain within the court safety perimeter when the first night hearing field opens. Your identity, signed report, and reproduction sequence will be public. The duty ends when the field closes.**
**REFUSAL CONSEQUENCE: your claim and your requested access exception will be withdrawn. A local witness may seek another advocate. The Ro household’s eligibility for a local review remains.**
**BURDEN: public identity and attendance at the first field. No authority over another person.**

Ilyan read it twice. The language was plain enough to be dangerous.

“If I refuse, Tamsin can still seek review?” he asked.

Harrow’s eyes narrowed. “The local access question remains eligible. The advocate slot will be offered again. You will not be able to request an exception in this case.”

“And the hearing field?”

“Will not recognize you as its external witness. It may still open around the bridge if a local advocate requests it.”

“So the child’s safety depends on whether I volunteer to be visible.”

“The child’s safety depends on the state of the bridge and the people who act. Your presence changes the evidence, not the number of possible remedies.”

That was more honest than Ilyan wanted.

Harrow leaned forward. “You can refuse. You can also accept. The duty is bounded, sourced to the summons, and ends when the field closes. It does not bind the Ro household, the guild, or your advocate. If you sign, the court will hear your report as public evidence. That report may be used against you.”

“I know.”

“If you are later found to have altered or hidden it, the ruling can follow your name as well as the claim.”

Ilyan touched the notebook. On the last Earth page, the server timer had stopped at 00:00. The report remained unmerged. His signature was the only thing that had crossed with him.

He looked at Sera. “Will you witness my choice?”

“No,” she said. “I will file it accurately.”

That was enough.

Ilyan placed two fingers on the blackglass plate. “I accept the witness duty. I do not accept the authorship claim. I did not create Veyra, and the report I filed was a challenge to a contradiction, not a claim of authorship.”

The plate warmed beneath his fingers. No force entered him. No hidden instruction completed itself. The change was administrative and complete: his name now belonged to the public record.

Sera wrote down his exact words. Harrow’s recorder repeated them in a flat voice, and somewhere beyond the chamber a bell sounded once. The sound traveled through the stone and returned as a faint vibration in the notebook.

Harrow placed a narrow slate before him. “Your request for access is separate. Name the crossing you seek, the person who will accompany you, and the burden that person accepts.”

Ilyan looked toward the doors. Beyond them, the upper road was still crowded. He had one narrow request to make, and no authority to grant it.

Oren entered from the side passage carrying a small saltglass plate wrapped in oilcloth. He set it on the table without greeting anyone.

“The workroom copy,” he said. “Do not confuse it with the public copy.”

Sera unwrapped the cloth. The plate was clouded with salt, and its black surface held a line of maintenance marks unlike anything on the court record.

Harrow looked at Oren. “You were not summoned.”

“The bridge summoned me,” Oren said. “It is less polite than the court.”

Ilyan turned the plate toward himself. A word in Veyran trade shorthand cut across the lower edge. The System did not translate it. Oren did.

“It says review the load when the count returns,” Oren said. “That is not in the public charter.”

Sera’s hand stopped over her tablet.

The plate was only a small piece of damaged glass, but it changed the shape of the case. Ilyan had expected a missing page. Oren had brought a missing instruction.

The court bell began to toll again, and the plate gave a faint answer beneath his hand.

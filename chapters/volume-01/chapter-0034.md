# Chapter 34: The Room Where the Clause Might Be Written

The old weighhouse at the fen crossing had a floor of packed chalk that gave under a heel, and forty-one delegates, and one sentence they all wanted.

Oren had thought, on the walk in, that it would be hostile. It was not. It was worse than hostile, because all four districts had sent their maintenance man and two of those men were men who had read the ninth bridge's work notice, and both of them had come to shake his hand, and one of them had asked, before he had sat down, whether it was true that the levy had gone out on Thursday, and when Oren said it was, the man said *we heard* and wrote it down and did not say anything else, which was more use than Ilyan's record had done anybody in twelve days.

Ilyan had come as himself, which meant that for the first time since the ninth-bridge case began he was in a room as a person with no function, and it turned out that this was much worse and much better than being cited.

He was not on the roll. He had asked, at the door, whether an attached record-holder could sit, and the recording clerk, Ilta Breen, a compact woman with ink to the second knuckle, had looked at the roll and then at him and said, “You may sit. You may not be read into the minutes as a source, and no delegate may cite your name in support of a position, on the ground of the assembly's limited word. If a delegate wishes to use your finding, the delegate must produce the document.”

“Understood,” Ilyan said.

“Then you may come in, and I will tell you that two thirds of this room resents you, and one third of it does not know who you are.”

Hesk Ardan was in the third of it, at a bench near the door, with a satchel and no mandate.

Ardan stood up when Ilyan came in, which was a thing he did not have to do, and said, “I came as a man with nine carts and no paper from anybody.”

“That is a different animal from the animal you were last time.”

“It is the same animal with the paper taken off,” Ardan said. “I withdrew the citation. The citation was worthless and it was still being used to look at me in a room. What I have instead is nine carts, and a toll I pay whether or not this rule exists, and a very clear interest in whether a keeper can put a load off a bridge in the middle of a market morning.” He sat back down. “I have been told I am not a district. I would like it entered that I am not a district and that I pay more per week to these three spans than either of the last two districts' road funds does.”

“Then you are a witness,” Ilta Breen said, writing, “and not a delegate, and I will enter you as one, and a witness cannot vote.”

“I have never wanted to vote,” Ardan said. “I have wanted to be the reason a rule is sensible.”

The chair was Convener Bel Ordway, a heavy dry man from the fen district with a manner of reading a roll as though the roll had been sent to him personally, and he opened at the second hour without a speech.

“Item one,” he said. “A common bridge maintenance rule, for adoption by any district that chooses it. The delegates have brought drafts. I am going to read the drafts and then we are going to argue, and I am going to stop the argument at the fourth hour, because I have a cart of salt going over at the fifth and I am not losing it to a bridge rule.”

That was the first thing I liked about him and it was the only thing I liked about him for the next three hours.

Maren Kett had the eastern districts' draft and read it into the room, and he read it well, and it was one sentence.

**THE BURDEN OF BRIDGE MAINTENANCE FALLS ON THE BODY WHOSE LABOR OR PROPERTY CARRIES IT, AND IS PAID AT THE RATE AND HOURS ENTERED IN THE SCHEDULE.**

Two hundred and eleven words in the room after that, and most of them came from Hesk Ardan, who wanted a word about what happened to the man whose load does not cross, and from a delegate in the third row who wanted the word *schedule* removed because a schedule in his district was a thing a clerk could write in an hour, and from Maren Kett, who wanted the rule exactly as it was because a rule with a hole in it is a rule with a second one in it by next year.

Then Ordway asked the question that had been waiting under the whole morning.

“Where is the pause?”

The room went quiet in the specific way a room goes quiet when everybody has noticed the same gap and nobody wants to be first.

“If the burden falls on the body whose labor carries it,” Maren Kett said, “and a keeper stops a load, then the stopped load has no body, and the rule has nothing to say about the stoppage. That is not a detail. That is the whole reason a keeper's pause cannot be written by a district.”

“Then write it,” Ottla Prenn said.

Prenn was from the fen district and had been quiet for two hours, and she was now writing on a slate, and Ilyan had been watching her write for forty minutes and had not known until that moment that the documentarian at the table was the dangerous one.

Prenn's slate had nine lines on it and by the fourth hour it had eleven, and the eleven lines were, without any question, the best piece of drafting Ilyan had seen in this city.

**A COMMON MAINTENANCE CLAUSE, DRAFTED FOR ADOPTION BY ANY DISTRICT THAT CHOOSES IT.**
**THE SPAN IS RELEASED FOR PAUSE BY A NAMED KEEPER WHO HAS PERFORMED A NAMED INSPECTION AND FOUND THE CONDITION OF THE SPAN UNSAFE.**
**THE KEEPER NAMES THE INSPECTION, THE HOUR, AND THE NUMBER OF BELLS. THE SPAN IS RELEASED UNTIL THE COUNT RETURNS TO MARK.**
**THE BURDEN OF THE PAUSE FALLS ON THE LOADS THAT DO NOT CROSS. THE AMOUNT OF THAT BURDEN, THE RATE, AND THE HOURS ARE ENTERED IN THE SCHEDULE BY THE DISTRICT.**
**THE RULE HAS NO DURATION AND NO END, AND MAY BE ADOPTED OR REFUSED BY A DISTRICT BY ITS OWN ASSEMBLY.**

“That is a real clause,” Maren Kett said. “That is the first time in twelve days anybody has written the ninth bridge's problem down in a form another district could use.”

“It is a real clause and it is the wrong clause,” Oren said.

He had not spoken for an hour and a half, which Ilyan understood had been a discipline and not a hesitation. He stood up at the back of the hall where he had been sitting like a man at a funeral.

“Three things are wrong with it,” he said. “One: *the count returns to mark.* That is my bridge. My joint comes back to mark because the water under it carries the fine back. A joint that does not come back is a bridge that does not get released, and the clause says nothing about that span, and the clause is the rule everywhere, and everywhere there is now a rule that works on the ninth bridge and does not work on the fourth.”

“The second?”

“The keeper has to *find the condition unsafe*,” Oren said. “That is my whole week in one phrase. I have found it. I have written it on a board. I have told a district office in a rented room with a late schedule, and they have written me down a day of stone. I am not the man who finds it. I am the man who cannot do anything about it. Find is a thing you do with a lever and a slip. What I need is *stop*.”

“And the third?” Ordway said.

“The third is that the district enters the burden in a schedule,” Oren said, “and the district will enter it late, and then a keeper stops a load and the schedule has not got a line for it, and the keeper has done the right thing and the person whose cart did not cross has no wage for the morning, and the district will say the keeper should have waited for the schedule, and the keeper waited twelve days for a schedule and the seat is empty.”

“That is three objections and none of them is a sentence,” Maren Kett said.

“No,” Oren agreed. “They are what I have instead of a sentence, and I would like the room to know that they came from a man with an empty strongbox and a bridge with a hole in it.”

Ordway let it stand for a while, and then said the procedural thing that ended the morning, in the voice of a man who had been a chairman long enough to know exactly when a room has spent itself.

“Before we can adopt a common clause, we need a witness,” he said. “Not a witness to the span. A witness to the rule. A rule that says a keeper may stop a load has to say how anybody in three districts finds out whether the keeper stopped it for a reason or for a mood. That is the witness part of a covenant and every one of us knows it and none of us wants to write it.”

Nobody wanted to write it. That was the first honest hour of the morning.

“Which brings us to lists,” Ilta Breen said, from the clerk’s table, in the voice of a woman who has been waiting three hours for her turn and has a document. “A common witness provision needs a common list. Every district maintains an index of where its bridge maintenance terms are kept, so that a district can find a term it has lost. The three districts have filed theirs. I have them here, and I read them, because the rule cannot be adopted on terms nobody can produce.”

And she read them, standing at the table, the way Moot had read a shelf index in a cloth hall a week ago, and the same thing happened that had happened then, which is that a room full of people who had been arguing about principles suddenly found themselves looking at a date.

The fen district: bridge maintenance terms held at the fen district plate office, incoming index complete.

The eastern district: held at the eastern routes office, incoming index complete.

And two districts, both of them small, both of them with one bridge each and both of them represented in the room by a man who had come a long way — Ansel Nyle, of the low fen, and Ordwin Sarn, of the cut banks — with nothing on the list at all.

“Your district has not filed a list,” Ordway said.

“My district files its terms at the district office,” Nyle said.

“Then the district office has not sent it. That has been asked for twice.”

“It has been asked for twice,” Nyle said, “and I have written for it twice, and I have been told both times that the terms are held under a standing preservation arrangement and cannot be produced to a conference, and that the preservation arrangement is the reason the terms are safe.”

“And the arrangement is with whom?” Ordway said.

“With the office that holds them,” Nyle said. “I am not being funny. I am a bridgekeeper in a district with one bridge, and the office that holds my maintenance term is not in my district, and I do not have a name for it, and I have never met a person in it.”

The room did not go quiet, because the room was already quiet, and what happened instead was that every person in it looked at somebody else, because this was not a moral question and there is no way to be angry at a shelf.

Ilta Breen had the conference's own working index open, the one that recorded where each district's terms were held, and she read down the custody column and stopped and went back up and read it again.

“Low fen,” she said. “Held at the continuity desk. Cut banks, held at the continuity desk. The fen district, held at the fen plate office. The eastern district, at the eastern routes office.”

“Where is the continuity desk?” Maren Kett said.

“There isn't one on the list,” Ilta Breen said. “There is a code. The column for those two districts is a code and a stamp, and the code is the only thing on this page that has no office written against it, and I have been filing maintenance indexes for eleven years and I have never seen that code in a district column before, and I would like the room to understand that I noticed it and that I am reading it because I read everything, and not because anybody in this room has asked me to.”

The code was two characters and a dash and two characters, and the first two characters were the same two characters that were sawn off the back of a district work slate and off the margin of a maintenance schedule book, and Ilyan had written them in his own notebook twice and had not been allowed to say the rest of the sentence out loud.

“C—2,” Nyle said. “That is the code. That is the code they always give me. *Held for preservation, C—2.* I asked what C—2 was, four years ago, and I was told it was a filing arrangement and not to be concerned with it, and I have not been concerned with it, and I am telling you this morning that I cannot produce my own bridge's maintenance term because a filing arrangement I have never seen is holding it.”

The room went somewhere else entirely, and Ilyan watched it go, because he had been in rooms where this happened twice and it always went the same way: the argument about the sentence stopped, and the argument about *whose sentence* started, and people began reaching for the shape at the end of the chain.

Maren Kett got there first and got it right, which Ilyan did not expect and would remember.

“Stop,” he said. “All of you. This is a filing question, and we do not have a filing question in front of us, we have a rule that two districts cannot support because two districts cannot produce a document. I am not going to let this room build a common maintenance rule on the back of a gap in somebody's shelf and call it a principle. If we write the sentence and leave the two districts out, we have written a rule that applies to two thirds of the region and is silent about the third, and in four years the third will be a hole and the hole will be somebody's bridge.”

“Then we adjourn,” Ordwin Sarn said. “We adjourn, and we have failed, and I have come four days to fail.”

“We have not failed,” Prenn said. “We have not got to the sentence. There is a difference and it is the only difference anybody in this room has today.”

Sera was not here. There was nobody here whose job it was to keep him inside his own vocabulary, and Ilyan discovered that what he actually knew about the paperwork in this room was almost nothing, and that the almost-nothing had a shape he could feel.

He knew the shelf marks, because shelf marks are numbers and dates and he could read those. He knew the fen and the eastern terms were complete, because Ilta had said so. He knew two districts were not, and that the code on the list was the code he had been forbidden to pursue in a public room a week ago, and that the district which had lost the terms was a district with one bridge and a keeper who had written twice for them.

He could not read the maintenance clause itself. He could not read the adoption line, or what “any district that chooses it” bound, or the words that said what happened to a district that adopted a rule and then could not pay for it. Those were on the page in front of him and they were clerk's language, and the only thing he could do with them was ask somebody to read them, and asking somebody to read them, in a room of forty-one delegates, on the morning when a rule might be written, was the most expensive thing he could have done and he knew it while he was deciding whether to do it.

He did it anyway, because the alternative was the sentence he was worst at.

“The clause is a good clause,” he said, standing, and forty-one people turned around. “I want to say that before anything else, because I think half of you have decided I am here to be the man who says the clause is not good enough, and I am not. I wrote a rule in a workroom five days ago and did not file it, so I am the last man in this city who gets to vote on anybody's sentence.”

“Your name cannot be cited,” Maren Kett said, pleasantly.

“My name cannot be cited,” Ilyan agreed. “Which is the correct outcome of a decision I made in a fish-market hall and have been apologising for since, and I am not going to ask anybody in this room to break an assembly's limited word because I have arrived with a good idea. But I am allowed to ask a question, and I am going to ask one, and I would like it read back so that it goes into the minutes without my name on it.”

“Go on,” Ilta Breen said.

“Two districts cannot produce their maintenance term,” Ilyan said. “That is a fact about shelves. It is not a fact about bridges, and it is not a fact about whether a keeper should be able to stop a load, and if this room goes home today and says *we could not agree because two districts could not produce a witness list*, then in six months a clerk in one of those districts will use it, and the sentence they use will not be about shelves.”

“Or it will be accurate,” Ordway said, “and an accurate sentence that gets used well is worth having.”

“An accurate sentence gets used for whatever the reader has,” Ilyan said. “I know that because I spent twelve days being a sentence.”

He sat down, and it was the most useful thing he did all morning, and it was useful because it was small and it was a warning rather than an argument, and forty-one delegates ignored it, which he had expected, and which he wrote down afterward in four words he was going to argue with himself about for two days: *they heard me and agreed later.*

Ottla Prenn was still writing when the room started to break up, and she had the slate face down on the table with her forearm across it, in the universal posture of a drafter who has something and is not finished.

Ilyan came over because he had nothing else to do and because he was curious, and she lifted her forearm and let him look, and it was the eleven lines, with an addition at the bottom that was in a different ink.

**THE WITNESS TO ANY PAUSE UNDER THIS CLAUSE SHALL BE APPOINTED BY THE ADOPTING DISTRICT AND SHALL KEEP A LIST OF ALL PAUSES, WITH THE HOUR, THE KEEPER, THE NAMED INSPECTION, AND THE FIGURE THAT RETURNED THE COUNT TO MARK.**
**THE LIST SHALL BE OPEN TO ANY PERSON WHO CROSSES A SPAN GOVERNED BY THIS CLAUSE.**

“That last line is not in the clause,” Ilyan said.

“No,” Prenn said. “That last line is the only reason this is worth adopting and it will not survive contact with a clerk, because a clerk cannot open a bridge load list to any person who crosses, on the grounds that a person who crosses is not a party to the maintenance of the span. That is the single objection that will kill this rule and it is the correct one and it is going to be raised by the eastern district in a fortnight.”

“Who certifies the list?”

“The district,” Prenn said. “Or the guild. Or the court.” She put her chin on her hand. “And here is where the morning went, so I will say it plainly and then I will go and get a cart of salt. A common rule needs somebody to be the witness. It cannot be the keeper, because the keeper is the one pausing. It cannot be the district, because the district appointed the keeper and the district pays the stopped loads, so the district certifies its own arithmetic. It cannot be a court, because a court that is asked to certify every pause on every span in three districts will, inside two years, be asked to certify a pause it has never seen and will do it on a filing clerk's word.”

She tapped the bottom of the slate.

“So either one of those three carries the list, or the rule passes without a witness, and a rule without a witness is a rule anybody can break with a good story.” She looked at the room, at the forty-one people putting on coats over four districts' worth of dignity, and then at Ilyan, deliberately, the way you look at the only person in a room you have not yet decided about. “Which leaves a man who cannot be cited.”

“Ah,” Ilyan said.

“You cannot be cited as a permit, a schedule, a levy, or an acknowledgment, and you may not be entered as the bearer of labor,” Prenn said, reading it off as though she had it on a slip, which she did, because everybody in the room had a slip. “Which means you cannot be bought, you cannot be instructed, and you cannot be removed by a district that changes its mind. If the witness list for a common maintenance pause rule were kept by a man whose name cannot be entered as authority anywhere, then the rule could not be captured by any of the four districts, and every pause in the region would have to be written down and read by somebody who is not the district and not the guild and not the bench.”

The room had not emptied. Or rather, it had emptied by two thirds, and the third that remained were the people who had noticed that a drafter had just said the most frightening sentence of the morning out loud, and they were all looking at Ilyan, and they were all looking at him for the same reason, which was not that he was the man who knew about bridges.

It was that he was the only person in the room with nothing to lose by being the record of it.

He felt the whole thing arrive, and it arrived the way things arrived in this city: as a sentence that was already half true and entirely reasonable and would cost four other people their livelihoods and a woman on a quarter-bell shift a working route home.

He stood there in a weighhouse with chalk dust on the floor and forty-one people's coats going on, and he understood that he had spent twelve days building a small, careful, bounded practice of not putting his name on anything, and that a room of strangers had just handed him the one job in the world that consisted of being the name on everything.

“I can’t be cited,” Ilyan said. “Which means I can’t carry it. Not legally, not practically, and not honestly. If I keep a witness list for a rule that three districts adopt, then the first time one of those districts wants the list changed, the argument becomes an argument about me, and every pause that goes in the book goes in because a foreign record-holder is attached to the ninth bridge, and in eighteen months this is the Saltwheel Road with a different bridge on it.”

“You would be the only one in the room who could not be instructed to change it,” Prenn said.

“Yes,” Ilyan said, “and I would also be the only one in the room who could not be asked to leave. You have just described a post with no door in it, and I have spent twelve days watching what happens to people who sign things in a charter with no door.”

He put his hand flat on the table next to her slate, not on it.

“If a witness list is the right answer,” he said, “then it cannot be one person. It has to be a body that is still there after the person who started it has gone, and there is exactly one thing like that in this room and it is not me.” He looked at Oren, who was standing at the back with his bag on his shoulder and had not moved. “It is six crews and ninety-four hands who will be here after I am not, and they have no mandate from anybody, and they cannot be instructed by a district because a district does not employ them, and they cannot be removed by a clerk because a clerk does not write their roster. That is what a witness list wants. It wants somebody boring, local, paid, and impossible to promote.”

Oren said, from the back, in a voice that carried exactly as far as it had to, “And a guild that keeps a list of pauses gets named in every dispute about a pause, forever, in three districts, and a guild that is named in every dispute cannot say no to anything it is asked, because the first guild that says no once is a guild nobody trusts with the list.”

“Which is the second thing that is wrong with every answer in this room,” Ilyan said. “Including mine, three days ago, on a bench in a workroom, where I wrote a rule that nobody could refuse without losing something. We are not out of ideas. We are out of anybody who can be the record without the record becoming a lever.”

Prenn looked at the slate for a while, and then she turned it over, and wrote a twelfth line, and read it out.

**THE WITNESS TO THIS CLAUSE IS NOT NAMED. TWO DISTRICTS CANNOT PRODUCE THEIR MAINTENANCE TERMS. THIS DRAFT IS NOT ADOPTED.**

“The conference has failed,” Ordway said, from the door, with the salt cart already moving in his mind. “For the record: we have failed on procedure, and not on opinion, and the difference is worth writing down, because in two years somebody will say this room refused a common rule and the truth is that two districts could not produce a document and one clerk in this room would not accept a sentence about that. Draft eleven is not adopted. Draft eleven is circulated, with the witness question open, to three districts and one bridge that is not in any of them.”

“Eleven lines,” Ilyan said.

“Eleven lines that work,” Prenn said, “which is eleven more than anybody in this city has on any subject I have been to a conference about.”

And that was how the room ended: with a good sentence in a dead draft and a witness question nobody would answer, and a code on a custody list that two districts had been told not to be concerned with for four years, and forty-one people going out into the afternoon to go and be right about bridges in their own districts.

Oren walked back to the ninth bridge with Ilyan and did not say anything for the first mile, and then he said, at the top of the fen road, with the span coming into view below them and the count running on it because the count always ran:

“Your clause is a detail.”

Ilyan did not have an answer to that and did not pretend otherwise.

“Eleven lines and a witness question, and the witness question is the whole of it, and the witness question is not about my bridge,” Oren said. “I went into that room with a bridge and came out with a region that cannot find two of its own terms. I have spent ten days learning that a clause was cut out of my charter, and this morning I learned that half the region does not know where its charters are.”

“That is worse.”

“It is much worse,” Oren said, “and it is not my job, and I am going to have to decide by the fourth bell whether I want a worse thing that is not my job.”

He shouldered his bag and went down the road to a span with a two-finger drift and a day of stone coming and a bell that still meant nothing, and behind them both the fen crossing kept its dust, and the clerk's index in the weighhouse sat on the table with a code in a column where an office ought to be, and two bridges in two small districts went on being maintained by keepers who had written twice for a document that did not come.

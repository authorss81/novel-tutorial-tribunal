# Chapter 48: The Text on the Table, and What It Cost to Put It There

The light went before the court finished the day's work, and they lit the lamps, and the count went on running by lamplight, and the last day of the week ran out somewhere in the middle of the afternoon argument about enumerated cases without anybody noticing, which is what a working day does.

Oren Sedge read his own proposal into the record standing at the maintenance line, in his own hand, from a slate he had written at dusk on the last day of a week, and he read it in the flat voice of a man who has been told to describe and not advocate and has decided that the difference is a matter of what you do after the reading.

Then he did the thing after the reading.

“I have one further act and I am going to do it now, before the court takes up the text, because if I do it after I will have had time to think about it, and I have thought about it for two days and I am not going to think about it for a third.”

He put the slate down on the bench and took out the guild's acknowledgment. It was a single sheet, folded twice, and it had been served on his crew a fortnight ago and returned unaccepted and entered in the left book as a refusal and read back in public on the approach, and every person in that crowd had been in the room when it happened.

“This is the maintenance acknowledgment,” Oren said. “The district served it on six crews and addressed it to our successors until the charter is amended, and I refused it, and the reason I gave was that the guild will not carry a term it cannot use. I want to read the refusal back because it is in your own record and I would like the court to hear it before I do what I am about to do.

“The guild will not carry a term it cannot use, because the term is the maintenance, and the maintenance needs twenty days unloaded and four props off another bridge and a wage for six hands that the district has not paid this guild in nine years, and if the guild signs a term it cannot use then the guild is signing a debt and a debt passed to six crews is a debt that a keeper dies owing.”

“That is entered,” Roke said.

“I am signing it,” Oren said.

The approach made a sound. It was not a cheer and it was not a laugh; it was the noise a crowd makes when it has been wrong about something for three weeks and has just been told so by the person it was wrong about.

“Not the district's sheet,” Oren said, and Neral was there, and the locksmith's counterman from behind the lock-and-ledger shop, because a guild's book is a bound thing and bound things are his trade, and he had come up with the guild's own acknowledgment, which Oren had written out by hand at two in the morning on the last night, on the back of the district's, in a hand that was worse than Oren's.

He read it out.

> **NINTH-BRIDGE GUILD MAINTENANCE ACKNOWLEDGMENT, ENTERED BY O. SEDGE, KEEPER, IN HIS OWN NAME AND NOT IN THE NAME OF SIX CREWS.**
> **The guild performs the count, the named inspection, the under-deck watch at two hands, the warning bell and the span's release, on this charter as it stands after the court's ruling.**
> **The guild accepts the maintenance burden from the first bell to dusk, at the levy rate, out of the guild's own account, which is empty, and the guild accepts that it is unpaid while the account is empty.**
> **The guild accepts the maintenance of the wheel and the pin of the warning bell, which is not presently inspected on any day, on the same days and at the same rate.**
> **The guild does not accept, and will not accept, any term beyond the review date named in the ruling, and this acknowledgment has no effect after that date, and any successor of the guild's is not bound by it, and no acknowledgment shall be served upon the guild or upon any successor of the guild in respect of this span except with a term and a date and an exit on the face of it.**
> **The guild's four hands at the first bell, second, third, and fourth changes are the guild's own and unpaid from the district until the district's day-book line is opened. That is the price of this acknowledgment and it is named here so that no body may say the guild was paid.**
> **I, O. Sedge, keeper, sign for the work and for the interval. I do not sign for six crews, and if the interval outlasts me then the acknowledgment ends with me, and that is in it, and it is deliberate, and a term that ends with a man is a term that can be refused by a man.**

He signed it, and Ilsa signed under it as the counter, and Coll signed as the apprentice, because a count book has two names in an entry and an acknowledgment ought to as well.

“Six crews,” Oren said, looking at Harrow, “have not agreed to this, and I have told them it is coming and I have told them what it costs, and what they will say when I tell them is that I have signed for a term they have not voted for, and they will be right. I am signing as a keeper. A keeper's term is his own. That is the only part of this document that is not a trick, and the trick is that a keeper's term ends when the keeper cannot do it, and I am the keeper, and it is the first time in this case that anybody has written down a burden with an end inside it that they actually intend to keep to.”

“Entered as a produced document,” Harrow said, “and entered with the keeper's declaration that the guild is not a neutral body, which Mr Sedge has asked to be read with every document the guild puts in this court, and which I am beginning to think he is entirely right about.”

Then Odile read the draft of the amended charter aloud, and the hearing changed shape, because a charter is not an argument and everybody on that approach had been listening to arguments all day and knew what to do with one.

She read it slowly. She read it in the full court register, with the machinery in it, and the machinery is the part that makes a covenant bind and the part that a person who does not have it cannot follow. She read for eleven minutes. Twice, because Harrow asked for it twice, and the second time was worse for Ilyan than the first, because the second time he knew what the sentences were for.

And the second time, at the point where the draft said that nothing in the instrument operated to the prejudice of a person not entered in a register, and then said that the instrument operated only to such persons as were entered, and then set out eleven enumerated cases, and then said that the enumeration was not exhaustive and the court would supply any further case that appeared to it to be within the intention — Odile read the words *within the intention* and Ilyan lost the thread entirely and knew that he had lost it, and could not find it again, and sat on a bench on the last day of a week with a pencil in his hand and no idea in the world what had been decided in the last four hundred words.

Sera had warned him. She had warned him twice, in a lane and then in a room, and she had been right both times, and being right twice in the same case is not a comfort.

“Mr Vester,” Harrow said.

Ilyan stood up, and it was the first time in nineteen days that he had stood up in a room without being asked a question, and his mouth was dry.

“I cannot read that instrument,” he said. “I could follow the figures in the ward rate and the load-fee and the count, and I have followed them for three weeks, and I could not follow what I have just been read. There is a point in that draft where it stops being a set of instructions and becomes a thing the court does, and I lost the thread at the enumeration, and I have lost it now, and I am not going to pretend to a room of three hundred people that I followed it.”

The court record hand's pencil stopped.

“Entered,” Harrow said, “and entered with the claimant's consent, and I am going to say the reason I am entering it, which is that a settlement is going onto this charter with the name of an attached record-holder on it as one of its grounds, and a person who is going to be cited in four districts ought to have it in the record that he does not read the instrument. A precedent that hides the fact that its author cannot read it is a precedent that will be used against him in about two years by somebody who has noticed.”

“That is very nearly the first kind thing you have said to me,” Ilyan said.

“It is not a kind thing,” Harrow said. “It is the same reason I preserved Mr Dain's objection. The record is worth nothing if it is more flattering than the truth.”

“The court cannot have a settlement with a hole in it,” Dain said, standing, and he was not being cruel and he was not even being difficult, he was doing his job, and Ilyan had spent three weeks learning that the two were the same thing on this bridge. “The instrument is going into a charter that changes a burden on four districts. The claimant says he cannot confirm that the terms match what the parties agreed. The advocate confirms. That is the practice, and if the practice breaks on this instrument then it will break on the next one, and the next one will be somebody's shelter.”

“Mr Quill,” Harrow said.

“I confirm it,” Sera said, “and I am going to say what my confirmation is worth, because Mr Vester has just shown this court what a person looks like when they cannot do the job they are nominally on the approach for, and I would rather do the same. I am the recordwright of this file. I have the four facts, the guild's book, the copy with the five terms, the costed proposal, the district's answer with the reason on the back, the count book with five entries and one mark, and the ward's book with a column in it that was sworn this morning by a woman who walked forty minutes with it in her arms. I confirm the terms. I am not a witness to the parties' intent. I am a recordwright confirming that what was said at this bridge is what is written on that page, and if it is not, then the fault is in somebody's mouth and not in the clerk's hand, and that is a narrower claim than a signature and I am not going to pretend it is a wider one.”

“Confirmed,” Harrow said. “And the record is that the attached record-holder cannot read the instrument, that the advocate confirms it, and that neither of those facts is a defect in the settlement, because the settlement is the court's and not theirs.”

Then, from the advocate stool, without being asked, Sera said the other thing, and she said it to the room and not to him.

“Read it back in the guild's words,” she said. “Not a summary. A read-back of the four things that change, in words a keeper could say out loud on an approach, and Mr Vester will read that back, and then it goes in beside the instrument, and the two of them are filed together, and if anybody in four districts wants to know what the ninth bridge's settlement actually says, they can read both.”

“It will not be the instrument,” Ilyan said.

“It is not the instrument,” Sera said. “It is four sentences in a keeper's words, in a record beside a charter, and the charter governs, and everybody who reads the pair will know which is which, and that is a great deal more than this city has ever given anybody.”

The read-back took twenty minutes. It took twenty minutes because Oren and Ilsa and Joren and Dalla and Pev between them would not let a single sentence stand that they could not say out loud on a bridge, and four of the sentences they refused, and Hanne Kolm's fifth one — the refusal column — they would not let go of until they had got it down to twenty-two words that a sixty-one-year-old landlord could say to a clerk.

And the twenty-third sentence, which Ilyan had asked for and Sera had granted and Oren had looked at him for a long moment before writing, was this.

> **A keeper may stop a load.** No.
> **What a keeper may do is call the deck clear, and the deck being clear is the whole of it.**

And underneath that, in the same four-sentence register, Ilsa's line, which she wrote herself and made him read back twice, and which is the reason the whole thing held together:

> **A pause lasts as long as the loads standing on the deck, and when the deck is clear the pause is over. If the deck is not clear inside that time, the bell is a warning and the span is released, and no keeper has held anything.**

---

The license was granted at the second bell of the night, and it was granted to nobody's advantage, and it cost the woman it was granted to a shop and a habit.

“Sera Quill,” Harrow said. “Provisional advocate of record on this matter since the first hearing, limited, twice suspended, once resumed, and now sponsoring the settlement of a full hearing. The recommendation of formal licensing was filed on the day of the first stay. It has waited ten days because the matters it was filed on were not finished. Are you before the court?”

“I am,” Sera said.

“Under what terms would you take a license?”

Sera had a piece of paper in her hand. It was not a prepared paper. It was the back of a carter's tally, because that is what was on the bench, and she had written on it in the recess and Ilyan had not seen her do it.

“I will take a license to carry a public record, not a person,” she said. “I will not be the advocate of the attached record-holder. That is not a refusal of him and it is not a favour to him and I would like it entered that it is not a favour to him, because he is attached to a charter and I am not, and a licensed advocate who is a man's counsel is a man with a mouth and I have spent nineteen days being careful about that and I am not going to stop now that it is profitable.”

“Entered,” Roke said.

“I will take the settlement's public record from this charter, the ward rate and its day-book line, the refusal column and the sworn book behind it, and the household's tenancy and work protection, from this hour to the review date named in the ruling.” She looked down at the paper. “And I will accept four things with it, which are the four things a license does, and I want them said where the household is standing.

“One. A licensed advocate does not withdraw from a matter without an entry in the public record naming her reason. Two. A licensed advocate answers for the record and not for the outcome, and if the record is wrong the advocate is the one who is called. Three. The advocate carries the terms of the matter to the review and to any other district that asks, which means that when a fourth district copies this settlement I am the person who has to stand in their room and say what the nine sentences in a keeper's words were and who was standing behind each one. Four. The advocate takes no matter of her own while she holds this one, which is the whole of what it costs, and it is not nothing, because I have had a shop and a set of rooms and a business that pays for both, and I have been putting my family's claim into that business for six years without telling anybody that it was in there.”

The approach was quiet in the way it had been quiet once before, on a step, with a child.

“Nineteen days ago you told the court that your family record was yours,” Harrow said.

“It is,” Sera said. “And it is not in this case, and it is not going to be in this case, and I am taking this license with the family claim still unfiled, because the family claim needs the Court's own page and that page is missing, and I am not going to use a bridge to get at an archive.”

“Licensed, then,” Harrow said, “as a recordwright of a public record, to the review date, on those four terms, and Mr Vester's own limitation stands and is not altered by this and I am going to say so, because a man who wins a case and does not get an advocate is going to be described in four districts as having lost one, and the record will say otherwise in my hand.”

“Thank you,” Ilyan said, and was not thanked, and did not try to be.

---

The last hour was the hour of the Quiet Chamber, and it happened at the last bell of the night because a licensed recordwright has to enter her instrument before the day is out, and because a court that licenses a recordwright on a span has to tell an archive, and the clerk on the night desk had a note with the case number on it and a line under the number that said the room was to be opened.

Sera went with a clerk at the last bell of the night and did not ask him to come, and he came anyway, and stood in the cold room while she did the entry, and read the shelf, box, week, class, and routing of somebody else's losses out of habit.

The archive had not opened on the last day of the week in four years. It had been told to, by a court, in writing, and the note had a case number on it, and the clerk who opened it wrote his own name and the hour at the top of the page before he let either of them in, because a clerk who opens a room on an instruction has to be able to say afterwards who sent him, and then he did not use the fact for anything else.

The slot was where it had been. The thread was cut. The inventory mark beside it was in a newer hand, and the facing leaf — the leaf on the other side of the cut — had always been there, because a facing leaf is not part of the page, it is the page next door, and nobody had ever had a reason to look at the page next door to a page that was missing.

Except that a page carries its first line over onto the leaf behind it when the binder sets it, and the first line of a removed page leaves its shadow on the one after.

Sera read it standing up, with one finger, and did not say anything for long enough that the clerk came and looked.

> *"a refusal is entered, and the entry is against the burden and not against the person, and the entry is open to the ward."*

“There is more,” Sera said, “but there is a shadow and not a line, and the shadow is the rest of it, and I am not going to guess at a shadow in a public archive and put it in a record. I am going to write the one line I can read and the fact that there is a shadow of more and that I did not read it, and I am going to date it, and I am going to sign it, and it is going to be the first thing I have entered under a license, and it is going to be about a page that is not mine.”

“Entered,” the clerk said.

“There is no eighth column anywhere in this city,” Sera said, “and there has not been for as long as any of these volumes reach, and I have spent six years looking for it in a family chest and a guild book and a landlord's book, and it is in a page that was taken off a shelf in a week I was not born in, and it says *the entry is against the burden and not against the person*.”

She wrote it down and signed it and dated it, and then she stood in the cold room with her hand flat on the shelf and said the thing that Ilyan had known was coming since the sixteenth day and had not been ready for anyway.

“It does not tell you who took the page,” she said. “It tells you what the page said, and the page said that a refusal was an entry, and the entry was public, and the entry was made against the burden and not against the person. Which means that the register in this ward with a column for objection and no column for leaving is not a register that forgot a column. It is a register that lost half of one, and the half it lost is the half that said *burden*.”

“It is not the right of refusal,” Ilyan said.

“It is not the right of refusal,” Sera said. “It is one line of a removed page, and the removed page had a clause in it that we do not have, and I am not going to stand in a cold room and tell a man that a fourth of a sentence from a facing leaf is the answer to anything. What it is, is the reason a ward register cannot be repaired by adding a column, and the reason a sworn book about a burden is a different instrument from a sworn book about a tenancy, and the reason Hanne Kolm is the right person to hold the one and not the other, and the reason the thing we wrote on a bench this afternoon is two hundred years older than both of us.”

She locked the volume and gave the key back and they walked out into a city that had a charter with a hole in it and a road on it that was very thin indeed.

“Ilyan's terms, when the text went in,” Ilyan said, on the steps. “You asked for the read-back. I did not ask for the other thing and I want to say the other thing in the corridor, where it is not a record and I can be wrong without cost.”

“Say it.”

“Make it citable,” Ilyan said. “Enter the settlement as a precedent witness available to other districts. With the grounds on it, and with my name on it, because the attached observation is one of the four grounds and a precedent that names nobody is not a precedent anybody can use.”

Sera stopped on the step.

“You know what that does.”

“I know what it does. It puts my name on a document that three districts will read in a year, on a document that says *lawful traveler* in the access condition because that is two hundred years old and it is the charter's own word and it means registered, and somebody is going to read that document and use that word to shut a road. I have been telling people for three weeks that a remedy copied beyond its original place becomes a different instrument, and I got it copied into a road in another district myself, and I told a room the same thing last night in front of a clerk.” He put his hand flat on the rail. “So either the settlement is citable or it is a local fact on one bridge with one keeper's name on it and no force anywhere, and I would rather it be citable and wrong than invisible and right, and I would rather the wrong happened in a document that has my name at the bottom of it than in one that does not.”

“That is the first sensible thing you have said about precedent in three weeks,” Sera said, “and I am not going to praise it in a corridor, and I am going to ask you one question about it, and you are going to answer it in the corridor and not in the room, because in the room you will be careful.”

“Ask.”

“Your sequence is in a public Veyran record twice already and you cannot un-enter either,” Sera said. “Is that what this is? Is this you trying to get a third thing you cannot take back, on a document other people will have to live inside?”

“No,” Ilyan said, and it was the first completely clean answer he had given in this city. “The other two were a man's vanity and a man's fear. This one is a door. If it is not citable, then in four districts the answer to a ten-year-old's refused acknowledgment is *do not read the recordwright's book*, and the person who does that is a person with a road and a bench and no need for a court at all, and they will never be cited because there is nothing to cite. A template with my name on it can be argued with. A silence cannot.”

Sera looked at him for a while.

“Then put it to the court in daylight, with your name on it, and let Mr Vester and Mr Larch and Mr Anhalt object to it, and let the objections stand if they are right,” she said. “And do not call it a template. Templates travel. What you are proposing is a rule that a person in another district can be shown the terms of, and I would like the word for that to be *precedent*, and I would like you to be frightened of the difference in a way that shows on your face in about a year.”

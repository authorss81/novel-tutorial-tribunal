# Chapter 47: The Account of a Machine

She pulled the rope once, and the wheel turned, and the hammer came down inside the bell, and the note went out over the river and up the fen road, and three hundred people on a bridge approach heard exactly what they had heard every working day for three weeks, which is a noise, and which is the only honest thing in the case.

Nobody spoke for a while. Then Tamsin let the rope go and turned round and faced the court.

“The bell on the western frame,” she said, “is a rope over a wheel. The wheel is on a pin. The pin is worn on the underside where nobody can see it. Uncle Oren says it will last a year and then somebody has to change it, and he has written it down, so I know it, and everybody can read it on the board, and it is in the guild's book too, because I asked him to put it there after I said it.

“When somebody pulls the rope the wheel turns, and the hammer inside hits the bell, and the bell makes a noise. That is all it does. It does not touch the bridge. It is not attached to the bridge. There is nothing on this bridge that the bell can do.

“The rope is fixed to the post. The wheel is fixed to the frame. The pin is fixed to the wheel. That is the whole of what it is fixed to.

“It is rung by a person. A person has to walk to it and pull it and let go and walk away.

“When it rang on the day the span moved, the people on the span got off, because that is what it means to them, and they got off, and the span went on. Nobody on the span thought the bell was going to stop the span. The bell did what it is for. The span did what it does.”

She stopped there, and looked at Neris, and Neris said, “That is all of it,” which put an end to the first part of it in front of about three hundred people.

Harrow had Roke read the account aloud. All of it, in the flat voice, with no improvement, and when the reader came to the terms he read them as slowly and as badly as he could manage, which Ilyan suspected was a deliberate choice by a man who had read them twice already.

“Terms set by the witness. One: an account of a machine, not of a meaning. Two: said once, in the workroom, the span in view. Three: not written for her and not improved. Four: withdrawable until read aloud once, and after that it stands. Five: it may not be used to cure or support any refusal or acknowledgment, and any use of it that puts it in front of a person as a reason to sign is a misuse of the witness and not evidence of the bell.”

“Entered,” Roke said. “And entered with it, on the same sheet, the guild's declaration that the guild is not a neutral body and has its own interest in the fifth term, in Mr Sedge's hand, and the witness's four conditions on the copy, one of which is that this court has said out loud that the account went to the guild first and that nobody made her, and which the court has said out loud.”

“Thank you,” Tamsin said, to Harrow, and not to anybody else, and Ilyan watched a ten-year-old's whole negotiation with an institution get settled in two words and a chalk line and did not reach for his notebook, because there was nothing in the notebook that was his.

Then the court began to ask, and it asked well, which was the thing Ilyan had not expected and had stopped being able to predict about this man.

“Miss Ro. When the bell rang, where were you?”

“Under it.”

“Was the bell rung before the span moved, or after?”

“Before.”

“Did anybody on the span try to stop the span?”

“There was nothing to try with.”

“Who rang it?”

“I do not know,” Tamsin said. “It is rung by a person and I was under the bridge and I did not see who.”

“Then I want to be careful,” Harrow said. “The court is not asking you what the bell meant. The court is asking you what it was attached to and what happened when it was pulled, and those are different questions and one of them is easy and one of them is hard, and you have offered the easy one and the court is going to take the easy one and let the hard one go, and I am telling you that I am letting it go on purpose.”

“You are going to let it go,” Neris said.

“I am going to let it go,” Harrow said, “and I am going to record that the court was asked a hard question and chose not to ask it, and the reason is the witness's own fifth term, and the court is not above a child's term.”

Dain had been standing at the rope with his arms folded for eleven minutes, and now he unfolded them.

“Marshal,” Harrow said, “you have something.”

“I have a procedural problem and it is not a trick and I would like it treated as one.” Dain came four feet inside the rope, which he had not done in three weeks, and stopped. “Mr Quill. Your household's proposed terms include a maintenance acknowledgment. I have spent three weeks telling a foreign record-holder that a household that will not acknowledge maintenance is a household that other people pay for. The district's answer is now on the frame and the district is offering the stopped hour and the second hand and the pump's shortage. All of that is a wage. A wage paid by the district to hands at a rate implies a recognition that those hands are the ones doing the maintenance.

“The account just given describes a warning that a person rang and that emptied a deck on the day the span moved. That is evidence that a maintenance condition was known, that it was known to be unanswerable, and that it was known to be known. In nine years of my work I have never had a piece of evidence like that, and if the court enters it, then a court in four districts has a reason to require a maintenance acknowledgment from every party to a span, because the acknowledgment will not be an imposition. It will be a recognition of something that already happened.”

It was, Ilyan thought, entirely correct. That was the horror of Dain and it had been the horror of Dain since the second day: the man was not lying, and he was not cruel, and the thing he wanted was a signature, and every argument he had ever made had been one that a good lawyer could have made.

“Mr Quill,” Harrow said. “Is there an objection.”

Sera did not look at Ilyan. She looked at the copy in Roke's hand, and she read the fifth term out loud, herself, in the flat voice, at a bridge, in front of three hundred people, and the words she read were the child's words and the guild's hand, and she did not soften one of them.

“And I want the court to hear me say this next,” Sera said, “having read it. The account cannot be used to support the acknowledgment. Not because using it would be false, and not because anybody would be lying, and not because the district is acting in bad faith. Because the account describes a bell that empties a deck, and the acknowledgment would be a signature saying the household accepts responsibility for the maintenance of a span, and the two are not the same claim, and if the court used the account to support the acknowledgment, then in four years a clerk in another district would read the acknowledgment and read the account and find a witness who described a machine, and the machine would be doing the work of a signature, and it would have been made to do it by this court, and this court is the one that cannot be asked about it later.”

“I am obliged to the witness,” Harrow said.

“You are,” Sera said, “and so am I, and so is Mr Sedge, and the guild is not a neutral body and has said so on the page.”

“Entered,” Roke said, to nobody in particular, in the tone of a man writing down the most important line of a morning. “The district's proposal of a maintenance acknowledgment from the household is not supported by the witness's account, and may not be. The witness's fifth term is read into the record as the reason.”

“Objection,” Dain said.

“Preserved,” Harrow said. “And the district's procedural observation is also entered, because it is good, and because a record that keeps the objection and the ruling is a record that a person can check in four districts instead of take on trust.”

Dain looked at the ground for a moment, and then back up.

“Then the household acknowledges nothing,” he said, “and the district pays the wages anyway, and the district is right that the wages imply a recognition, and the district is going to carry that gap on its own book where anybody can read it. I would like that entered too, because in three months somebody in my office is going to be told that the ninth bridge is maintained by people who have accepted responsibility for it, and it is not true, and I am putting it in the record now while I have the standing of the man who proposed the thing that did not happen.”

“Entered,” Roke said.

Tamsin had not moved. She was standing on the deck by the frame with her hand at her side.

“May I say the rest of the machine,” she said.

“You may say the rest of the machine,” Harrow said.

“The pin is worn where nobody can see it,” Tamsin said, “and the rope is four feet off the ground, which is a grown person's height, so a small person has to reach, and I am nearly a grown person's height and I had to stretch. And the wheel is above the rope and it is on the frame and the frame is on the post, and the post is in the ground on the western side, and the western side is the side with the hole under it.

“So if the post goes, the bell goes, and the post is on the ground on the side of the span that has nothing under it.”

Nobody said anything.

“And when the pin wears through, the rope goes slack and the wheel stops turning and the hammer does not hit the bell, and a person pulls it and nothing happens,” Tamsin said, “and nobody on the deck would know, because they would be hearing nothing, and the only person who would know is the person pulling it, and the person pulling it is standing on the approach with a rope in their hand and nothing coming out of it.

“So the bell needs somebody to look at the pin,” she said, “and it needs it on a day, and it needs it written down, and it needs two people and not one, because one person is the person who pulls it.”

The count book on the bench had four entries in it and a mark in the margin and a line that said *held at one, not returning* with Ilsa Bray's name under it, and after that line there was a space, and Harrow looked at it.

“Enter the pin,” he said. “Enter that the ninth-bridge warning bell's wheel and pin are part of the span's maintenance and are not presently inspected on any day, and enter that a keeper who rings a bell which does not sound is not a keeper exercising a power but a person pulling a rope, and enter who said it.”

“Entered,” Roke said, and wrote, and it took him longer than the rest of the account together.

Oren came out of it last, and he came out of it the way he had said he would come out of things on the first morning, which is by describing the span and letting the room do what it wanted.

“I have run this span for twenty-two years,” he said, “and I have rung that bell four hundred times and I have never once looked at the pin, and I have had it on my board for a year as a thing that lasts a year, and a thing that lasts a year is a thing you do not look at. I found out this morning from a child standing on my bridge that the other half of that sentence is that when it is finished nothing happens, and I am not going to be sentimental about it in a public field, and I am going to say the thing that is useful.

“The bell empties the deck. It has always emptied the deck. There is no load on this bridge that has ever been held still by a bell, and there is not going to be one, and if this court writes a clause that says a keeper may stop a load, then the clause is describing a machine that does not exist, and the first time a district needs it, a carter will stand on a bridge at four in the morning and say *I have been told I may not*, and he will be quoting a sentence nobody could have obeyed.

“Write what the bell does. It empties the deck. And write what happens if the deck does not empty. And do not write anything at all about who may not get off, because I cannot stop a man and I have never been able to and I am not going to be able to, and the day somebody tells me I can, somebody is going to rely on it, and the pin will be worn, and the rope will go slack, and the deck will not empty, and it will be my sentence in his mouth.”

“And the bound,” Sera said. “Mr Bray's finding at the third bell. A load that stood and did not go across came back most of the way and stopped short. So the pause is bounded by the loads standing on the deck and not by anything the keeper says. If the deck is clear, the pause is over. If the deck is not clear inside the stated bells, the span is released.”

“Released,” Oren said. “Not held. Released.”

“Released,” Sera said, “and the bell is a warning and not an order, and if a load does not come off, that load's time is its own and nobody's, and the only thing this court buys is hands.”

The System put the entry into Ilyan's sight, and it was a record and not a finding, and it was the third time in nineteen days that a piece of a child's handwriting had gone into a court record with a keeper's declaration attached to it that the keeper was not a neutral body.

**THE WITNESS'S ACCOUNT, ENTERED AS A COPY, WITH THE WITNESS'S FIVE TERMS WRITTEN ON THE COPY IN THE GUILD'S HAND. THE COURT HAS STATED OUT LOUD THAT THE ACCOUNT WENT TO THE GUILD FIRST AND THAT NOBODY MADE THE WITNESS.**
**THE ACCOUNT IS AN ACCOUNT OF A MACHINE. THE WARNING BELL IS FIXED TO THE POST, THE FRAME, THE WHEEL, AND THE PIN, AND IS NOT ATTACHED TO THE SPAN, THE JOINT, THE BRACE, THE COUNT, OR THE RELEASE.**
**THE ACCOUNT MAY NOT BE USED TO CURE OR SUPPORT ANY REFUSAL OR ACKNOWLEDGMENT.**
**THE WITNESS STATES THAT THE WHEEL AND PIN ARE NOT INSPECTED ON ANY DAY, AND THAT A KEEPER WHO RINGS A BELL THAT DOES NOT SOUND IS A PERSON PULLING A ROPE. ENTERED AS STATED. NOT INSPECTED. NOT ON ANY DAY.**
**THE WITNESS IS NOT CALLED AGAIN.**

Five lines, and none of them a rule about the world, and every one of them about a rope over a wheel on a worn pin on a post standing on the side of a bridge with nothing under it.

Tamsin walked back along the deck to her mother and sat down on the lower step, and about four seconds afterwards, from somewhere in the crowd, somebody started clapping, and it was not many people and it stopped almost at once, and it was the sound of a crowd that did not know what it was supposed to do with a thing that had gone correctly.

Neris Ro put her hand flat on her daughter's back and left it there, and did not look at her, and said, “Right. That is done. And I am not going to say anything about it, and if anybody comes up here to shake my hand about it I will tell them to get off my step, because my daughter has been asked to stand on a bridge twice in three weeks and both times it was somebody else's need and one of them is going to be the last.”

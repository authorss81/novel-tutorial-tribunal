# Chapter 33: An Institution Wins Something

The lower ward's maintenance office was two rooms above a weighbridge office that had gone out of business, and the rent was paid monthly by the district to a man who had never once met a clerk.

The whole of it was one room, really, with a partition that had been moved twice and a door that closed on a wedge. There was a strongbox in the corner with a district seal on the front of it and a lock that had never been re-keyed, and inside the strongbox was the retained version-three page of the working schedule book, cut from the binding gutter by somebody who had wanted it, which was a thing Ilyan had looked at four times now and still could not make himself stop looking at.

There were three people in the office. Bevin Larch, the ward's schedule clerk, who had come up the stairs with a roll he had not been able to deliver to anybody in twelve days. Meridith Vale, who had the case on his knees. And a district inspector the Ro household had never seen before, who had a name on a lanyard and had not said anything for the first eleven minutes.

Oren came in with Joren Vell and did not sit down, and asked for the wall.

“Give me the maintenance schedule for this span,” he said. “The current one. Not the retained one. I want to see what the district is actually paying for.”

Vale put the book on the partition and turned it round and stepped back from it, which was a courtesy and also a professional habit, and Ilyan understood that a clerk who stands back from his own book is a clerk who has been asked once already not to be standing next to it.

Oren read the page the way he read a span, from the top, with his finger a hand's width above the line so that he did not smear it.

He read it for a while.

“Crushed stone,” he said. “Two loads. And lime, four sacks, and binding fiber, and it says *delivered to the line and not into the yard*, which is a good instruction, and somebody wrote that instruction, and it was not me.” He went down a line. “And all of that is a standing purchase and not a grant — the yard does not care whose bridge it is for, and the office has been buying it since January, and it has not been attached to this span because nobody ever asked it to be. And here. *Load-held day, one, for the west seat, on a date entered by the office.*”

“Entered when the office gets to it,” Bevin Larch said.

“Not entered by the office,” Oren said. “Entered. The office chooses the day. The guild does not know the day, so the guild cannot get the six hands, so the day arrives and it is a day with two hands on it, and two hands on an open west seat is a day that makes the seat worse.” He put his finger on the word. “I want the day named and I want it named with the hands and I want the hands on the same page as the stone.”

“Materials are materials,” Vale said. “The district supplies the stone. It is not the district's business how many hands the guild brings to a day the district has given it.”

“It is exactly the district's business, because the levy is spent and the day is the day the guild would be working for nothing,” Oren said. “If the district gives me a day and the wage, the day is a wage. If the district gives me a day and no wage, the day is a favor, and a favor on a west seat is a hole.”

He turned round and looked at the room, and Ilyan saw him do the thing he always did, which was to stop asking for the thing he wanted and start asking for the thing he was owed, and Ilyan understood about four seconds later what that was going to cost, and by then it was already happening.

“I am not asking your district for a clause,” Oren said.

The room changed.

“Every day for twelve days, somebody has told me the ninth bridge needs the maintenance-review term put back,” Oren said, “and I have said I would carry every hour that term costs and put the hours on the board. I am not saying that today. Today I am asking for two loads of crushed stone, four sacks of lime, binding fiber, and one day with the wage attached, and I am asking for them because my own book says I have been doing the work for nothing since Thursday and my own board says what the work is.”

He turned to the partition and put his thumb on the guild's line in the district's own book.

“This line says *salt crack and under-deck watch.* That is the ninth-bridge maintenance obligation. It has been on this page since January. It has been paid at one hand since the year the crew list came in with one name on it, and I signed that list, and the hand I did not have was a hand I did not know I was missing, and the consequence of that is a west seat that has been emptying for six years and a stain I found on Friday that was there on Tuesday.” He took his thumb off the page. “I am not asking you to change what a keeper may do. I am asking you to pay for the shift that the clause you already have says we work, at the number of hands that shift actually takes, for one day, so that one day is a wage and not a favor.”

“Where does the six come from?” Vale asked.

“From the levy,” Oren said. “Six at every change. It is on the guild's board and it is in your own column and it has been in your own column since the year the bridge was certified, and the guild has four, and the two that are missing are the two that would have been underneath the west seat on Tuesday.”

The inspector, who had a name on a lanyard and had not spoken for eleven minutes, said, “That is an argument about the past.”

“It is an argument about a shift,” Oren said. “The past is in my book. I have the book. I do not need the district to look at it.”

Dain came in last, in the borrowed capacity, with the district objection already folded in his coat, and read the room in about four seconds.

“He wants a day,” Dain said.

“He wants a day and a wage and the materials, and he has said out loud in front of three clerks that he is not asking for a clause, and he is going to keep saying it in that voice for the next hour, and in about forty minutes it is going to be very difficult for this office to refuse.” Dain took his coat off and hung it on the wedge of the door, which was a thing he did as a courtesy and which everyone in the room understood was also a signal that he was going to be there a while. “I am going to say the district's position once, in order, and then I am going to sit down and let the keeper say his, and if the keeper says it better than I said mine I will lose and I will lose correctly.”

“Your position,” Oren said.

“A span that cannot be stopped is a span that is cheaper to maintain than a span that can.” Dain said it without heat, the way he said everything, and it had the effect of a man laying out nails. “Every clause that lets a keeper put a load off this bridge moves a cost onto somebody, and the somebody is the district, because the district is the only body here with a schedule and a fund and a book that a clerk can read. The guild has a board and a box that is empty. The ward has a whistle. The household has a room. I have three people and a schedule I am three weeks behind on, and if I fund a pause on the ninth bridge I have funded it on a bridge that has no way to be reviewed, and the day somebody in this room decides they would like the guild to be able to stop a load, the district pays for the stoppage and the district did not choose to stop anything.”

“Write it down,” Oren said, “and add to it: the district has been paying for one hand of a two-hand watch for six years, and the west seat is empty on the underside, and the district is being asked today to pay for the difference.”

“I will write it down,” Dain said, “and it will be in my report, and it will be true, and it will not change the position, because the position is about the future and the report is about the past.”

“Then say the position about the future without the past in it.”

“The future is this,” Dain said. “You want a span a keeper can stop. Fine. I am the enforcing officer and I will tell you exactly what that costs and exactly who pays. Every stopped load is a cart that does not cross, a water tank that does not cross, a wage that is not earned, a schedule that is not met. I do not have a fund for stopped loads. I have a fund for worked hours. If you want a keeper who can put a load off this bridge, give me a line in the schedule called *stoppage*, with a wage, and an hour, and a rule about how long a stoppage may last, and I will carry it. Without that line, every stoppage is an hour of somebody's work that the district has not bought, and the district will not buy an hour because a man on a bridge thinks the stone is tired.”

“Then put the line in,” Oren said.

Dain looked at him. “I cannot put a line in that I have no authority to fund, and I cannot fund a stoppage on a charter that has no clause saying a stoppage is a thing a keeper may do. If I pay for stoppages, then every keeper in the district stops loads, and the district has just bought a power it has never voted for.”

“There,” Vale said, quietly, from behind the book. “That is the sentence, and it is the right one, and it is why this office has not put it in.”

Nobody looked at him, which Ilyan was later sorry about.

“Because I have no authority,” Vale went on, in the same voice. “Not a keeper's authority, not a district's. The schedule I keep is a schedule of works and hours. A line in it called *stoppage* would have to be a line in the bridge charter, and the bridge charter is the Bench's, and this office does not amend the Bench's charter. So the district is being asked to pay for a power it cannot create and cannot refuse, and I have been sent three documents in twelve days that each wanted me to write down a thing I have no power to write down, and the honest answer to all three is the same and it is four words long.”

“Which four,” Dain said.

“The office has no power here.”

He said it looking at the book and not at the room, and Ilyan watched him do it, and understood that Vale had said that sentence to the district, to the ward, and to a household in a lane, and had never once said it in a room with people who needed it, and had just now said it in front of five of them, which was either the most or the least useful thing he had ever done.

“The stone and the lime are the same in every version of this,” Vale said. “The district buys stone. I buy stone. The district has a standing purchase with the yard off the fen road and the yard does not care whose bridge it is for. So: two loads of crushed stone, four sacks of lime, binding fiber, delivered to the line and not into the yard, and one load-held day, and the day is entered on the maintenance schedule and the schedule chooses the day and the wage for that day is at the levy rate for six hands for two days of work, which is a figure the office can find.”

He wrote it on a slip, and then stopped, and read it back, and then did the thing Ilyan had watched him do twice, which was to turn the slip over and write the reason on the back in his own flat hand.

“It is the true reason,” Oren said. “Read it out.”

Vale read it out.

“The district will not decline the materials and the day, because declining them would require this office to certify, in writing, that the ninth bridge is fit to carry an unlimited number of loads on a west seat that a keeper has reported as empty on the underside, and this office is not willing to certify that and is not willing to say so on a form, and so the district is paying for stone.” He looked up. “That is the true reason and it is a reason about this office's certification and not about the merits of a keeper's pause. I am entering it in that form. If the district later wants to say the concession was a kindness to the guild, the answer is on the back of the slip, in my hand, with the hour on it.”

Nobody argued with him, and Ilyan understood that the district had just lost an argument by being honest in public, which was the first time he had watched that happen to the district and it looked exactly as bad from this side as it would have looked from theirs.

“Stone and a day,” Dain said. “No acknowledgment.”

“No acknowledgment,” Vale agreed. “No acknowledgment, no signature, no maintenance term, no *and their successors*, and no column.”

“Then what is it?” Bevin Larch asked, genuinely, because he was twenty-four and had been a clerk for a year.

“It is a supply of materials and a supply of a day, entered as a supply, in the supply column, at the hour, with my name on it,” Vale said. “It is the district paying a bill. It is not the district agreeing to anything. If the guild wants more than a bill it comes back and I will say the same four words.”

He looked at Oren.

“The office has no power here,” Oren said.

“The office has no power here,” Vale said.

Oren read the slip. He read it twice, the way he read everything, and then he took out the guild's chalk and looked around for a board, and there was not one, and he wrote on the back of Dain's objection, which was a liberty, and which the district had done twice before him and would do again.

“Two loads, four sacks, fiber, delivered to the line,” he said. “One load-held day, six hands, two days' work, levy rate. The day is the office's to name and the guild will be told the name a day before, and if the guild is not told, the guild will say so in the book and the book is the guild's.”

“That is accepted,” Dain said, and did not look pleased, and was not concealing that he was not pleased, and Ilyan found that he trusted Dain considerably more for it.

Then Dain turned around in the chair and looked at Ilyan, who had been standing by the door for forty minutes with a notebook he had not opened.

“You,” Dain said. “I want to say something to you and then I am going back to the schedule.”

“All right.”

“I have spent twelve days reading your requests and the answers to your requests and the papers around your requests, and eleven days ago I was certain you were the most dangerous man in this district, and I have been certain of that every day since until about a quarter past eleven this morning, when a bridgekeeper stood in my colleague's office and asked for stone.” Dain put his forearms on the partition. “I want you to understand what happened in this room. Nobody cited you. Nobody used your name. Nobody in that workroom this morning had any reason in the world to want a foreign record-holder's finding in their argument, and Oren Sedge did not say your name once, and he won a day of stone.”

“I know,” Ilyan said. “I was standing at the door.”

“You were standing at the door being useful in the way you are useful, which is watching and not being cited.” Dain shook his head once. “I have spent twelve days constructing an objection to you. I have had it in my coat every morning. And this morning the object of my objection won an argument in a room where nobody needed him, and I am going to have to find a better objection, and I would like you to sit with the fact that the better objection is not about you at all.”

“Sera said almost exactly that, in a different building, with less politeness.”

“Then Sera is ahead of me and I will tell her so and she will enjoy it,” Dain said, “and I would like you to understand the other thing, which is that when I do find the better objection, it will not be about you being foreign or your name or your report. It will be about the fact that a span with a keeper who can stop a load is a span the ward and the household and the road all stop for, and the district will be the one standing in the rain holding the schedule, and I will be right about that, and I will not have made it up.”

He said it without apology, and then he was a marshal on a schedule again.

Ilyan walked down the stairs behind Oren and Joren into the weighbridge yard and stood in the bad light while the yard's clerk weighed a load of nothing in particular.

“You could have used me in there,” Ilyan said. “You had a case with a record in it and a Bench that has started calling this a continuity matter, and you could have made a speech about the record and won it twice over.”

“You could have,” Oren said.

“Why didn’t you?”

“Because the moment I put your record in that room, the district spends the next year answering the record instead of the bill,” Oren said. “And then in a year the bill is not paid and the seat is still empty, and the district is right, and it is right about a thing I made into a fight.” He shouldered his bag. “Also I do not trust a man who is not in the room. You have not been in thirteen days of the ninth bridge. I have. If I need a fact from the case I will come and get it and you will give it to me and I will decide what it is for.”

“That is fair.”

“It is not fair, it is a working arrangement,” Oren said, “and I have one of those now, which is new, and I am not going to lose it to a speech.”

Vale came out with the slip in his hand and a second slip, and gave the second one to Ilyan without comment. The face of it was the district's own numbers — a supply column, a quantity, a delivery instruction — and none of it was a sentence, and he could read it.

**DISTRICT SUPPLY, NINTH BRIDGE. MATERIALS AND ONE LOAD-HELD DAY. SUPPLY COLUMN. NO ACKNOWLEDGMENT ENTERED OR OFFERED. NO CHARTER TERM AFFECTED.**

“The back is not the office's numbers,” Vale said, before Ilyan could ask for it. He turned the slip over himself and held it out, and it stayed in his hand, and the hour was on it.

“Take it to the road party,” Vale said. “The road party has to know a day is coming, and the road party is not the guild and I am not allowed to tell them which day.”

And then, because he had been a clerk for nineteen years and had a professional weakness he had never once been able to cure, he added, in a slightly gruff voice, as though it were an aside about the weather:

“The office has no power here. It is a bad week to be able to. Twelve days ago a materials store would have gone into that house without anybody having to think about it, and there is a version of this week where the ground floor of that shelter is a lime store and I wrote the entry and slept well. I would like somebody to be annoyed with me about that in about a month, when there is nothing left to be annoyed about. In the meantime you may all regard it as a continuing entry.”

Then he went back up the stairs.

Oren did not say much on the walk down to the bridge. What he said was at the chalk line, with the count running and Ilsa on the scale and the pale drift line sitting a finger's width from its iron ring, exactly where it had been since the field.

“Here is the thing you should have,” Oren said. “I won a day of stone this morning in a room with three clerks in it and I did it by not asking for the one thing I want. That is a real thing and it worked. And I have been a keeper for twenty-two years and I have never once won anything in a room, and the reason is that every time I have gone into a room I have taken the clause in with me.”

“So the trade is the clause for the stone.”

“The trade is that the clause is the only thing I have ever gone in there to win, and it is the only thing in that room that was worth more to me than the stone, and I have just discovered that if I put the stone first I can get the stone,” Oren said. “And I am going to sit with that for a while, because a man who finds out what he would trade a thing for has had a bad morning and a good one at the same time.”

The district's price came down at the second bell, delivered by Bevin Larch up the lower steps with the roll he had finally been able to deliver, which was a document about a room in another town with the lower ward's stamp on it.

A regional maintenance conference, tomorrow morning, at the old weighhouse at the fen crossing — convened in under a day, by a letter the lower ward had written and dated the previous afternoon.

Three districts, and the fourth chair, and on the standing agenda, printed in a plain hand at the top of the roll: *a common bridge maintenance rule for adoption by any district that chooses it.*

Oren read it twice at the chalk line with the count running under him, and then gave it back and stood looking at the span for a while.

“They are going to write one sentence,” he said. “One sentence that three districts can put in a book and a night clerk can apply. And I have spent twelve days standing in rooms telling them the sentence is wrong, and I have been wrong in the way a man is wrong when he has the truth and no sentence.”

“A common rule could put the pause clause in everywhere,” Ilyan said.

“A common rule could put the pause clause in everywhere,” Oren agreed, “and it could put it in everywhere with no second witness and no exit and no word about what happens to the household that cannot cross, and then the ninth bridge would be governed by a rule three districts wrote about a bridge none of them maintain.”

“Or it could be the room where the clause gets written properly.”

“Or it could be,” Oren said. “And I have no mandate from six crews to be in that room, and if I go I am a man from this bridge with a thing he wants, and the first thing they will ask me is which district pays for the pause, and I will not have an answer that is better than Dain’s answer, because Dain’s answer is the true one.”

He picked up the roll and looked at the address at the top of it.

“They are asking the guild to appear,” he said. “Not me. The guild. Six crews, ninety-four hands, and a bridge with a keeper who can see the failure and a bell that means nothing. And they are asking us to go and stand in a room where the sentence might get written for every span in three districts, and the sentence might have my clause in it.”

“Do you want to go?”

“I have no idea,” Oren said. “I have never wanted anything less than to be in a room, and I have never had less than a day to think about it.”

He looked at the date at the top of the roll again, in a second clerk’s hand.

“They wrote this yesterday afternoon,” he said. “Before you came up to my office, before Vale put his name on the supply slip, and before I had said the word *day* out loud in a room with the district in it. Somebody decided yesterday afternoon that three districts should be in one room tomorrow morning, and I have not worked out yet what that is for, and I would like every person in that weighhouse to know that I have not worked it out when I arrive.”

He put the roll down on the bench next to the empty strongbox.

“Ask me at the fourth bell,” he said. “Not before. I have to run a count first.”

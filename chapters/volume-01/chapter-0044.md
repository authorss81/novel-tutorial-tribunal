# Chapter 44: What the Span Did While the Court Was Arguing

Getting a load that could not turn round took forty minutes and it took the whole eastern approach to argue about, because every carter on the fen road understood in about four seconds what had been asked for and every one of them understood that a cart that cannot turn round on a bridge is a cart with a road behind it.

Ardan's man brought it. It was a salt cart, loaded to the axle bands, and the reason it could not turn was not the bridge at all: it was that the fen road at the eastern end narrows at the weighbridge and there were two loaded carts already stopped at the narrowing because of a broken axle, and the man who brought the salt cart had a company of loaded carts behind him and a company of loaded carts in front of him, and after four bells of that the salt cart was going as far as the west end of the ninth bridge and not one step further.

“He cannot turn,” Joren said, from the eastern stake, where he had spent the whole of it waving at carters. “He cannot go back and he cannot go on and he is sitting on the west end over the thing we have been talking about for an hour.”

“Then it is a standing load,” Oren said, “and a standing load is not a thing the guild has a reading for, because every reading in that book is a reading of something going across.”

He put his hand on the rail and looked at Puck, who was on the hatch with a rule in his hand and his sleeve wet to the elbow and an expression on his face that Ilyan had not seen on a working man before and did not want to see again.

“Say the rule out loud,” Oren said, “and say how you got it, because this court is going to write it down and I am not having a keeper's number in a record that a man measured in a hurry under a hatch with a lamp.”

Puck came up. He had a carpenter's rule with a brass slide, worn, and he had been using the end of it as a probe, and the end of it was wet and had grey clay on it.

“I could not get it under the seat,” Puck said, “because the seat is bedded and the bed is what is gone. So I went in at the drain side, where the water comes up, and I put the rule up the gap and I have a mark on the rule where it stopped being stone.”

“How far down.”

“Seven inches. Then it is not stone, it is lime, and the lime is a skin, and under the lime it is not anything, and the water is in the not-anything.” Puck held the rule up so the room could see the mark. “Two hands flat, keeper. In a place where there is supposed to be a course forty years old and eleven hands deep.”

“How long has it been like that.”

“I do not know. The lime on the outside of the seat is the only clock any of us has and it has been washed white, which I would say is a season, and I have been under this seat four times in two days and I have watched the water come up in four inches, and it comes up in four inches in a day.” Puck put the rule down. “I am not a measure and I said so at dusk. I am a man with a stick and I am telling you the stick went in seven inches and stopped being stone.”

The field tightened across the span by about a shade, the way it did, and the light on the deck changed, and two hundred people on an approach felt the same small tightening in the back of the teeth.

Harrow held up a hand. “Roke. Enter the witness's figure as given, with the method, and enter that the witness has said twice that he is not a measure. Mr Sedge, is that your practice, to have a man under the seat with a stick at the fourth morning bell of a full hearing.”

“It is my practice to know what is under my seat,” Oren said, “and it is a bad practice to do it by myself, and I did it by myself four times in two days, and today I have got a court in front of me, and if the court is worth anything it is worth the four minutes it takes to do it in front of witnesses.”

“Entered,” Roke said.

Then the count began, and it was the ordinary count, because the only thing that could be done on a bridge with a court on it was the thing that is done on a bridge every day, and Oren had thought of that first and had not said so.

“Coll, book. Ilsa, beam. Puck, under, and I want the rule held where I can see it. Oren on the deck and I mean the deck, not the rail.”

“Out one and a half,” Ilsa said, at the first read, with a light cart standing at the west end that had been set down there before the court came. “And back. All the way, and the half is there on the light.”

“Then nothing,” Oren said. “Write nothing.”

They wrote nothing. Coll wrote the word *nothing* in the count book, which is a thing a count book is not supposed to contain, and Ilsa made him keep it, and he kept it, and Oren watched it go in and did not say anything about it, and Ilyan understood that a book that can hold the word *nothing* is a book that can be trusted to hold a number that frightens people.

The next hour was the hour the guild had wanted and the district had not. A carter came off the fen road at the second bell with a load of stone out of the low fen, light, and it went over and the joint walked out one and a half and came back, and the brace held, and the bell gave one note before and one after, and a woman with a handcart came up the lower stair and went across and Ilsa read her at half and the half came back, and about eleven people watched a handcart cross a bridge and treated it as an event, and it was.

Then, at the third bell, they brought the salt cart back up to the west end and told the carter he had four bells and no more, and the carter said something Ilyan could not understand and Oren translated it as *tell my man where I am* and went and said it in the carters' words and came back.

“Out two,” Ilsa said. “On the standing load. And back. All the way.”

“Write it as a band,” Oren said. “And write whose reading it is. One and a half on a light, up to three on a heavy, and it has never accumulated. Those are the numbers for a load going across, and they are the only numbers in that book, and every one of them was taken with a moving cart. Nobody has ever put a cart on this span and stopped it, so there is no figure for a standing load, because there has never needed to be one.”

“Out three on the standing load,” Ilsa said, and then stopped, and looked at the beam, and then at the deck, and then at the beam again, in the way of a woman who has read a scale four thousand times and has just read something she would rather not have read twice. “Out three. And back. And back. And—”

She stopped.

“Ilsa,” Oren said.

“It has stopped,” Ilsa said.

The count book had a second in it that nobody had called for. The span had come back from three fingers to a finger and a half and had then stopped at a finger, and it was sitting a full finger out from mark on the west side, and the water under the seat had gone quiet in a way that was much worse than the noise, and the pale line on the deck — a finger's width from the iron ring, the permanent set, the number they had kept carefully separate for a fortnight — had not moved at all, and everybody in the field saw that it had not moved, and Oren said it out loud so that it would be in the record.

“The drift line has not moved,” Oren said. “Say that in whatever words your court uses. The pale line is where the bridge has settled and it is staying where it has settled. What Ilsa has is something else and it is the first time it has been seen and I do not know what it is and neither does she.”

“It is the load standing,” Ilsa said. “It is not a load going. It came back most of the way and it has stopped short, and it has never stopped short, and I have been reading this scale six years and I have never once had a number go out and not come all the way back.”

“Can you get it back?”

“It came back on its own, with the cart still standing on it, which is the part I cannot account for. It came back in about the time it takes to say a thing. It has not come back all the way.” She held the beam out at arm's length and looked along it instead of at the number, which was what a counter did when she wanted another pair of eyes. “It is at a finger. It was at mark yesterday and the day before and on the day the child was under it, and it is at a finger now, and I am not going to write a word over that number until somebody tells me what to do with it, so write *held at one* and put my name on it and put the time on it, and if it does not come back then somebody has to say what we do at dusk.”

“Coll,” Oren said. “Write *held at one, not returning* and the bell and the time and *I. Bray, keeper and counter*, and do not round it.”

Coll wrote it. It took him longer than any other entry in that book had taken all month.

“Now the bell,” Harrow said, from the middle of the deck. “Mr Sedge. Ring it.”

Oren did not move.

“Mr Sedge.”

“I heard you,” Oren said. “I want to say one thing into this field before I do, and I have never in twenty-two years wanted to say a thing into a field, and I am aware of how that sounds coming from a man who describes and does not advocate, and I am going to say it anyway and I am going to say it as a description.”

He put his hand on the rope.

“This joint went out and did not come all the way back, under a load that was standing still, and the standing load is over the west seat, and the west seat is where the stone is gone.” He looked at the frame, and at the wheel, and at the pin. “I am about to ring a bell, and the bell is going to say *get off the deck*, and it is going to mean it, and everybody on this deck is going to go, including the court, because a bell that is rung in a room full of people and only means it for carts is a bell that is a lie within one hour of being rung.

“And when everybody is off, the salt cart is still on the west end, because the salt cart cannot turn round, and the span is going to be standing there with a load on the empty seat and nobody on it.”

“Then it is released,” Sera said.

“It is what it is,” Oren said, “and the word for it is *released*, and a keeper may release a span under the old terms, and a released span with a load on it is a span with a load on it and no one on it, and I have done that twice in twenty-two years, both times in the dark, both times to a cart that came off afterwards because I went under the deck with a lamp and made it clear by standing there and shouting until a carter understood that a keeper was asking him personally to reverse off a bridge with a full approach behind him.”

He pulled the rope.

The wheel turned, the hammer came down, and the note went out past the eastern approach where a hundred and forty people were standing, and every person on the deck of the ninth bridge got off it, including Sel Harrow, who went off it the way everybody else went off it, without a word, holding the tray above his head, and the court clerks went off it, and Sera went off it, and Ilyan went off it and stood on the western stair with his hand on a rail that was colder than the air.

On the span there was a salt cart with a company of loaded carts behind it and nobody else at all, and the count book on the bench on the eastern side was open to a page with *held at one, not returning* on it in a young man's careful hand, and the west seat was carrying a load it had never carried before, by itself, in public, with a court watching.

Nobody rang a second bell, because there was nothing to ring it for, and a hundred and forty people watched a bridge hold a cart it had no clause to stop and no money to pay for and no keeper's power to release, and the only thing in the field that had changed was the number on Ilsa Bray's beam.

Harrow came down the western stair and stood beside Ilyan at the rail, and did not say anything for about a minute, and then he said the only question anybody asked that morning.

“Mr Vester.”

“Yes.”

“In your own words, and not the record's: what is the court looking at.”

Ilyan watched the cart on the deck and the joint he could not see and the beam Ilsa was carrying back to the mark in a hand that had held a figure at one and had not let go of it.

“A load that came back and stopped short,” he said, “under the one condition nobody had ever put it under, which is that it was not going anywhere. And a hole under the west seat that a stick goes into seven inches, and a water that comes up four inches in a day, and a bell that empties a deck and cannot take a cart off it.”

“Can you tell me what the rule is that makes that number go where it goes?”

“No,” Ilyan said. “I can read the number and I can read the time and I can read the load, and the moment the number becomes a finding I lose the sentence that joins it to the other numbers, and I have been losing that sentence for nineteen days and I have stopped pretending to the guild that I have it.”

Harrow wrote that down himself, which no clerk in the court had ever seen him do, and then he said, “Then the court will have a physical witness who cannot explain the mechanism, which is the ordinary condition of every bridge in this city, and which nobody has ever before been quite so honest about in my hearing.”

He turned round to the whole approach.

“This court is in recess for one interval,” Harrow said. “Not a bell. An interval, and I am going to call it back before the light goes, because I have heard three remedies and I have now heard a span, and I intend to make at least one of the arguments I have heard myself, and I do not intend to do it in front of a load.”

Coll wrote *held at one, not returning* in the count book, and Ilsa wrote her name under it, and Oren wrote the bell and the time, and the four of them stood round a bench on the eastern side of a bridge in the light of the fourth morning of the last day of a week and did not say anything at all for a while, because there was nothing to say yet that the book did not already say better.

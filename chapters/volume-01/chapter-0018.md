# Chapter 18: The Family Appeal

Sera did not let Ilyan carry the comparison frame back to her room.

“Public records stay in the public desk until the clerk seals the chain,” she said. “If you touch it now, the next person will call your hand a continuation of whoever removed the page.”

He withdrew his fingers. The frame was cold through its narrow bars, and the fresh replacement line caught the lamplight.

The public archive desk occupied a side room beneath the court hall. It was staffed by two night clerks and a recordwright who had been given permission to close the shutters while they copied the chain. Neral worked at the long table. A locked wooden box sat between them, its lid open to reveal the empty Quiet Chamber sleeve.

Sera had requested the box after the assembly. She stood before it without touching it. Ilyan remained at the doorway, the signed report in his notebook and the court’s copy of Neris’s disputed account safely absent from his hands.

“This is my family’s appeal,” Sera said. “You may check the ordinary chain. You are not its advocate.”

“I know.”

“You do not know what that means until I tell you what I will refuse.”

She turned to Neral. “Show the original removal record, the custody ledger, and every request that allowed a person to enter the Quiet Chamber archive during the relevant period. Hide nothing about my family’s claim, but do not bring the ward witness into it.”

Neral looked at Ilyan. “The external witness is attached to the bridge case.”

“Then his access is limited to records entered into that case and the records I authorize as a record-checker. He does not speak for the Quill family.”

Ilyan nodded. “I accept the limit.”

Neral opened a second drawer and placed a narrow ledger on the table. It contained the original page’s inventory mark, the date of the page’s removal, and a sequence of custody seals. The ink beside the removal mark was fresh enough that its edges had not yet darkened. The thread of the empty sleeve had been cut cleanly, not pulled free. Someone had opened the binding, removed the page, and replaced the sleeve.

Sera looked at the date. “This is before Ilyan crossed.”

“It is before his report entered the public case,” Neral said. “The inventory mark was made before the first night field. The original page was already absent when the current working copy was certified.”

“And my duplicate?”

“Your duplicate was not entered in the public inventory. It was in your private box the night before the first field. It was gone during or after that field. We cannot place the loss in this ledger.”

Ilyan studied the two dates without touching the paper. “Two removals.”

“Two known absences,” Sera said. “Do not make them one event because both involve the same page.”

She looked at the empty sleeve in the archive box rather than at Ilyan. “My grandmother’s household had a well under a shared covenant. When the family wanted to leave, the current Bench said the house could petition for correction. The well remained tied to the people who stayed. That is why the family appeal matters to me. It is not an abstract return of a beautiful old right. It is a question about a house, a name, and the cost of leaving without becoming a stranger.”

Neral nodded but did not offer sympathy. “A family appeal requires a claimant, the old covenant, the burden of staying, and a remedy for leaving. The original page would help. Its absence does not make the claim invalid. It makes the chain harder to prove.”

“Then we prove the chain we have,” Sera said.

Ilyan watched her place two fingers beside the inventory mark. The motion was careful, as if touching the paper might make it speak. He understood why she had not made the road case carry her family’s claim. A public archive could be useful only if it did not become a lever in someone else’s dispute.

Neral opened a third ledger. “There is a continuity request associated with the original page.”

The request was written on thin gray paper. Its seal was valid, deep enough to leave an impression in the desk. The author field had been replaced by an office mark. The text was formal and clipped:

**RECORD THE PRESENT STATE. PRESERVE THE CHAIN. DEFER REMEDY UNTIL CONTINUITY IS STABLE.**

Beneath the seal, a marginal code had been added in a different hand: **C—2**.

Ilyan leaned closer. “What does C—2 mean?”

Neral opened a reference index. “It can mean second continuity copy, second custodian, or second witness. The code is used for more than one kind of record.”

“Which one is this?”

“The entry does not say.”

Sera’s fingers tightened on the edge of her sleeve. “The request is valid. The office can authorize a preservation copy without naming the person who requested it.”

“So the seal proves the request was lawful,” Ilyan said. “It does not prove who wrote it.”

“It proves a lawful office used the seal,” Neral said. “The person may have been a clerk, a recordwright, or a person with authority to speak for the office.”

Ilyan looked at the clipped wording. The cadence matched the line beneath the System. It also matched the replacement line on the old sleeve. For a moment he felt the satisfaction of a sequence closing: page removed, witness name removed, request entered, replacement line written. Then he saw the gap. The sequence showed an action, not a hand. It did not show whether the person behind the seal wanted preservation, concealment, or simply an orderly record before a larger change.

He reached toward the page, then stopped.

Sera noticed. “What would you infer?”

“That someone with access used a continuity request to remove the refusal page before the bridge case began.”

“Why?”

“The timing.”

“The timing is evidence of sequence, not guilt. The request could have been a preservation measure. The witness name could have been removed because the person could not be found. The page could have been copied, not destroyed.”

“Then why was the sleeve left empty?”

“Because an empty sleeve is a record of absence. It may be honest, careless, or designed to make the absence visible.”

Ilyan wanted to argue that the clean cut and fresh ink made innocence unlikely. The thought was not foolish. It was merely too easy. A person could commit a careful act for many reasons, and a valid seal could be issued by an innocent hand under a bad policy. He had spent his former life treating a reproducible defect as proof of a single author. The habit survived the crossing.

“I cannot infer guilt from the seal alone,” he said.

Sera looked at him for a moment. “Good. Keep the sentence.”

Neral turned to the custody ledger. “There was a missing clerk’s signature at the time of the request. The clerk’s name is not in the public copy.”

“Missing, or removed?” Ilyan asked.

“The ledger says missing. The page edge says cut. I cannot tell which happened first.”

“Who signed the neighboring entries?”

Neral pointed to two names. “A rota clerk and a quiet-room witness. The witness’s line is present on the inventory, but the name is gone from the active witness list. The older witness index preserves the full name: Sava Kest.”

Sera’s voice tightened. “That is the second missing witness.”

Neral nodded. “The code C—2 may point to it. It does not identify the person. It points to a second absence in the chain.”

Ilyan copied the code, the dates, and the two kinds of evidence into separate columns. He did not copy the clipped request into his own report. It belonged to Sera’s archive inquiry unless she authorized more.

Sera watched him. “You could use the page to challenge the road petition.”

“No.”

“You could say the old refusal language proves the copied burden is unlawful.”

“That would make the page carry a claim it did not make.”

“You could say the continuity request connects the ninth bridge to the district.”

“Maybe it connects a process. It does not tell us who owns the result.”

Her expression softened by a fraction. It was not forgiveness. It was the recognition that he had learned a distinction under pressure.

Neral slid a thin carbon sheet across the table. “The request entered the chain through a second routing mark. It went to the Quiet Chamber, then to the bridge plate office, then to the public petition desk. The bridge plate office received it before the first hearing field.”

Ilyan looked up. “Before the bridge failed?”

“Before the field opened. Whether it was intended to affect the bridge is not recorded.”

Neral traced a thin line on the custody sheet. “The bridge plate office kept a copy of the request number. The public petition desk received a later extract without the office mark. That is how a clipped sentence can travel while its source disappears.”

Sera’s jaw tightened. “And the missing clerk’s signature?”

“Still missing. The rota names a person, but the public copy does not preserve the hand. We can request the rota and the plate-office receipt. It will take time.”

“Do it,” she said. “File both requests separately. One for the original page and one for the duplicate’s last known custody. Do not let the archive office call them the same loss.”

Ilyan looked at the dates on the sheet. The original’s absence belonged to the time before his crossing. The duplicate belonged to the night of the first field. The continuity request sat between them as a process, not a proven culprit. He felt the old impatience rise. A missing signature could be recovered by pressure, a missing page by a clever comparison, a hidden office by a better question. The better questions had a way of becoming the same demand dressed in different clothes.

“Time matters,” he said. “If the route is public, the road can enforce a copy before we know whose hand made it.”

“Time matters,” Sera agreed. “But speed does not give you custody of a record you did not request.”

She closed the ledger. “Then we file two separate records. One for my family’s withdrawal claim, with the original page’s removal and the missing witness. One for the road petition, with the copied language and the local burden. The records may meet later. They do not meet now.”

Ilyan wanted to ask whether the continuity request could have caused the bridge’s reserve loss. He stopped himself. The passage had consumed the reserve before the hearing. The request might have been connected, but connection was not cause. If he put the two together because the pattern was compelling, he would be doing what he had done with Neris’s account: making a useful shape out of someone else’s private record.

“What do you need from me?” he asked.

Sera looked at the request, the empty sleeve, and the date marks. “Check the ordinary chain. Tell me when a record was entered, who could see it, and whether a later copy changed the wording. Do not look for a villain. Do not ask me to use the bridge case to shorten my family’s appeal.”

“I can do that.”

“And when you find a delay?”

“I will tell you the delay is a delay, not a decision about your family.”

She placed the request back in the ledger. “That is the first useful answer you have given me tonight.”

Neral prepared a fresh custody sheet. The two dates remained separate: the original Quiet Chamber page had been removed before Ilyan’s crossing; Sera’s private duplicate had vanished during or after the first night field. The request connected them in process, not in authorship.

At the bottom of the gray page, beneath the clipped lines, the marginal code **C—2** caught the lamplight again. A second witness was missing somewhere in the chain. Ilyan could not tell whether that witness had been removed, absent, or merely hidden by a later clerk.

Sera closed the box.

“Tomorrow,” she said, “we take this to the road. The local review cannot carry my family’s claim, but the district should know that the public record has a missing witness before it decides what a valid precedent looks like.”

Ilyan followed her into the court corridor. Behind them, Neral sealed the request and wrote the two dates on the outside of the box. The seal did not name a culprit. It only promised that the chain would remain available to whoever came next.

# Chapter 29: The Review That Cannot Convene

The assembly hall had been hired, mopped, and lit before anybody discovered that it could not legally happen.

Halden Moot was the Lower Ward Assembly’s quorum clerk, a small dry man with a brass badge and a stack of notices he had been carrying since dawn in order to prove something to nobody. He stood at the middle aisle with the notices in his arms and said the sentence that ended the evening’s first attempt at a review.

“There’s no quorum,” he said. “Nine benches and a threshold of seven. Six are filled. The other three are the ward’s, and the ward’s cannot be filled, because the ward is not an address. Seven is the threshold the assembly set for itself in the spring, and it is not a rule about this matter, it is a rule about the room, and the room is what I am responsible for.”

“It was an address on Tuesday,” Tarin said. “There is a petition box in the ward with a string on it and a woman named Mava Rusk who has answered every notice the district has sent since the flood year.”

“She has answered them,” Moot said. “She has answered them to the box. The box is not the ward’s address. The box is a box. A notice to a box is a notice that has not been delivered, and an undelivered notice does not fill a bench, and an empty bench is not a ward, and I am not allowed to pretend that it is.”

He said the last part with the particular weariness of a man who had been told by three separate people in the last two hours that he should be more practical.

Ilyan stood at the back of the hall and felt the whole structure of the evening fold in on itself, and understood immediately that the first thing he wanted to do was the wrong thing.

The wrong thing was to say *the ward is here*. Four of its people were in the room. Mava had not come; she had sent word that the Cinder Ward would not be spoken for by a bench she had never been assigned, and had sent Lio Fenn and Dalla Rusk with a slate of their own and standing instructions. Neris had come, on the footing that she was speaking for a household and not for a street. And a cart owner from the eastern yard had brought forty people who were not a ward and not a district and had been counted by a different clerk entirely.

If he said the word ward, he would have a quorum, and the review would convene, and the review would be a review of the ward.

“They are not the ward seats,” he said instead. “I am not asking you to fill the ward seats.”

Moot looked up. “Then you have six of nine and nobody in the three that are missing, and there is nothing to review.”

“There is a claimant for the ninth bridge, and there is a record, and there are more bodies in this room than there are benches, and each of them can be heard on a named part of it, and every one of them has told me what part they will speak to and what part they will not.” He stopped. “I am not going to make the ward one voice to get a quorum. I have watched that be done twice in a week and I am not doing it in a hall.”

“Then I cannot open the review.”

“Open the evidence session.”

“There is no such thing in this hall,” Moot said. “There is a review or there is a sitting where people talk.”

Behind him, the wall clock showed the second bell, and the seventh morning bell was the next morning away, and the whole purpose of the evening had been to put the local institutions’ findings in front of the bridge review before anyone woke up.

Maren Kett had taken a bench without asking for one, and the district clerk had taken another, and Dain stood at the back by the door with his district’s objection already folded in his coat, because he had brought it and intended to enter it and had not yet decided on what ground.

“May I say a thing that is inconvenient,” Maren said.

“You have been saying inconvenient things for a week,” Dalla Rusk said. “Go on.”

“The district invokes the copied precedent.” He did not stand. He had learned that standing in this hall made him the man speaking and sitting made him the man present, and he had decided which one he wanted to be. “The Saltwheel extract is a public precedent witness. The assembly’s own limited word says the attached name may not be cited as a permit. Both of those are true, and neither of them touches what a copy is allowed to do. The copied petition remains in the record. It says an unlisted ward’s refusal costs a shared road. It is still mounted, it is still legible, and it is still the sentence a cart driver on the eastern approach can go and read.”

“So what are you asking the review to do?” Ilyan said.

“I’m asking whether it’s going to be dealt with, and if not, when.” Maren spread his hands. “I have spent a week being cited by a petition I did not write. I’d like the record to say the district has asked for it to be answered.”

“Then the record says the district has asked,” Ilyan said. “That is a small thing and it is the only thing I can give you today, and I am sorry it is small.”

The problems came out one after another after that, in the way that institutional failures do, each one reasonable.

The Ro household’s access and housing evidence was incomplete. Hanne Kolm had sent her book — every room, every name, every month, twelve years of a landlord’s memory in a hand that had never been sworn — and the district’s objection was that a book was not a record. It was not entered, not sealed, not deposited, and the district did not have to accept it. Which meant that Neris could say that her tenancy was provisional and the district could say that she had proved it with her own landlord’s handwriting.

The work evidence was worse. Lio Fenn had a tally of every hour the ward had worked and every hour the district had paid for and every hour the district had credited to support, and the district’s objection was that the ward had no wage authority, and Lio’s answer, which was that the ward had been doing unpaid work for a levy it had refused, was not a record either.

And Sera’s family claim was in the room, because Sera was in the room, and a man near the door said what everybody had been thinking for a week.

“He’s not the authority here,” the man said, pointing at Ilyan. “He said so himself. And the recordwright’s family wants the old refusal put back, and she’s standing next to him, and this whole bridge business is her family’s claim with a child in it.”

Sera turned around in her chair.

“Say the sentence properly,” she replied. “Say what you mean, which is that the ninth bridge’s maintenance condition, the Ro household’s access, the Cinder Ward’s refusal and my family’s withdrawal claim are four records and that you are using one of them to make the other three less real. Then I will answer it, and the answer will take longer than the accusation.”

“The recordwright’s family —”

“My family wants a right of withdrawal restored,” Sera said, “in a public covenant from before the Codification, and that claim is in a Quiet Chamber file that is missing a page and a witness, and it depends on a document that may no longer exist. I am the recordwright on this case because the Bench assigned me a provisional advocate slot, and my scope is the bridge charter, the maintenance record, the disclosure, and the copied-precedent review. It is not the family claim. If the family claim is false, the bridge record does not fall. If the bridge record falls, my family gains nothing.” She looked around the hall. “I have said that in a hall in Merefen, and I will say it here, and it is written on the record of both, and you are welcome to read either.”

Nobody had anything after that. It was the third time she had separated those two claims in public and it had begun, in a way she would have hated, to work.

“The claimant’s standing is challenged,” Moot said, reading a slip, “on the ground of his public account two days ago, in which he affirmed that a line in the stay which he accepted without objection enabled the copied petition.”

“That is correct,” Ilyan said.

“Then the assembly is asked to consider whether a record-holder who has said in public that his own convenience made a copy possible may sit in the review as anything other than a witness to his own convenience.”

“Ask the record-holder,” Dain said from the door, “whether he intends to submit the pre-arrival paper.”

The room stopped. Ilyan had learned over the week to distrust that particular stop, because in this city a silence was usually a record of something that had just been decided without him.

Sera stood.

“No,” she said, and her voice was the flat one.

Dain nodded, as if a clerk confirming a delivery. “The paper is in the record. It carries a routing code and a receipt before the public notice. If the attached record-holder has that chain and will not put it in front of the assembly, then the assembly is reviewing a bridge with a withheld hand in it, and I have asked the question in public so that his refusal is in public too.”

“He is not refusing to submit it,” Sera said. “I am refusing to have it submitted.”

Which was worse, and which Ilyan understood in about a second and a half.

“Sera —”

“Sit down,” she said, and then, because that had come out of the place she kept for the things she said to him in rooms with four people in them, she said it again differently. “Ilyan. Sit down, and let me say this once in front of witnesses instead of in my room.”

He sat down.

“A request to reopen the ninth-bridge record before the review suspends every relief entered under that record, including the stay,” she said. “You worked that out at my table two nights ago and you thought it was a catastrophe for the archive. It is worse than that. It is the Ro household’s crossing. The stay is the only lawful thing that puts Tamsin Ro on that span. Reopen the record and the stay is suspended until the reopened record is settled, and the Bench will not name an hour for that, because nobody can, and she is eleven streets away with a bad leg and a mother who will not come to a hall to be asked.”

“She would survive a suspension,” Ilyan said.

“She would survive it the way a person survives a winter,” Sera said. “Yes. I am not going to stand here and tell you a child cannot be cold. I am going to tell you that you do not get to put my family’s missing page into a public record to save a bridge, and I am not going to let you put your chain into a public record to save a bridge either, and the reason is the same, and the reason is that neither of those documents was given to us by the people it will be used against.”

“The paper is already in the record,” Dain said.

“The paper is in the *court’s* record,” Sera said, “on a routing out of a plate office, entered at a slot, by nobody who can be named. It is not evidence of anything until somebody states what it is evidence of, and the moment he states it, it becomes a request to reopen, and the moment it is a request to reopen, the stay stops. I will not do that to that household on the strength of a chain that has two locations and no speaker.”

Ilyan sat in a rented cloth hall where a voice raised at the back arrived at the front a half beat late, with a woman in it telling him no for the second time in three days, and he felt the old sickening pull toward the sentence *the record is not the only thing that matters* and he did not say it, because it was the sentence he had used on Neris in a room above a lock-and-ledger shop, and he had learned in a single afternoon what it was worth.

“All right,” he said. “I will not submit the paper.”

Dain sat down with visible disappointment, and Maren looked at Ilyan for a long moment, and then at Sera, and said nothing, which from Maren was a kind of comment.

“I am not submitting nothing,” Ilyan said, standing. “I am entering the fact. I have two locations of one incomplete routing mark and one distribution strip for a print run that reached a plate office six days before the public notice. I am entering that as an attached observation, in the open case, with no document from the Quiet Chamber and no document from my family. If the assembly can test the routing, it may do so. If it cannot, then the observation is a question with a place in it, and a question with a place in it is worth more than nothing.”

“Not much more,” Maren said.

“It is what I have,” Ilyan said, “and I have said that out loud twice this week, and it has cost me a district’s citation and a four-day examination, and I am going to keep saying it until somebody has a better offer than a man with two locations and a voice like his.”

The hall did not applaud. There was a scrape of a chair and somebody said *well* half under their breath, and a ward woman said *that is the first true sentence anybody has said in here for a week*, and neither of those was important except that both of them happened.

Moot set down his notices.

“There is no quorum,” he said again, more gently, as though the repetition might soften the fact rather than confirm it. “I cannot open a review. I can open an evidence session, and an evidence session has no findings, no decisions, and no order, and it does not bind anybody, and everything said in it is a record and not a result.”

“Can you do that without filling the three?” Ilyan asked.

“I can do that without filling the ward seat,” Moot said, “because an evidence session is not a body. It is a room with a clerk in it.”

“And a boundary,” Tarin said.

He had been standing at the door the entire time, in the position he had chosen when he came in, and he came forward now for the only time all evening.

“A boundary is not a formality,” he said. “There are six bodies in this hall and none of them is a bench. If I mark who is speaking for what, and how long, and what they decline, then the room has a sequence, and a sequence can be recorded without a quorum, and the record will show who was here and what they would not say. I will not use force. I will not clear the hall. I will hold the rope and the board.”

“You are offering a route-arbiter’s presence at an assembly evidence session,” Moot said, “with no district order and no quorum behind it.”

“Yes,” Tarin said. “That is exactly what I am offering. Do not dress it as more.”

“Accepted as a presence,” Moot said, and wrote it, and it was less than a review and more than a complaint, and it was the most that a room with six bodies and a drawer full of answers could lawfully contain.

They took the evidence in the order the bodies had named.

Lio Fenn gave the ward’s hours, and declined to give the ward’s position, and said that a position given by him was a position given by one man with a tally and not by a street. Dalla gave the water route and the pump hours and refused, in the middle of her own answer, to speak for the households the pump served, which made three people in the hall object and made Joren Vell nod at the door like a man watching a road hold. Ilsa gave the west joint, the two fingers, and the salt crack, and said the words *until reviewed and thereafter* out loud in a room of officials, and Tarin wrote them on the witness board in his own hand so that the sentence existed in public.

Hanne Kolm came at the last bell, which meant she had walked down from the lower road in the dark on a leg that was already going, and she put her landlord’s book on the clerk’s table and said, “That is twelve years and I will swear to it and I will not let them seal it, because if they seal it I lose the only copy.”

“Recorded as an unsworn landlord’s record,” Moot said, “offered, not admitted.”

“Good,” Hanne said. “Write that it was refused. Somebody should have to read that.”

Neris did not give a speech. She gave the crossing hours, the route to the dye-yard, the shift, the room, the provisional lease, and the half-hand that had been credited against the guild’s wage bill every morning since the night of the stay, and when Moot asked her whether the household wished to be recorded as refusing the maintenance acknowledgment, she said, “My refusal is already in the guild’s book, in the district’s book, in the ward’s book, and on the back of a strip of paper from a print shop. I am not going to say it a fifth time so that a room full of officials can feel that they obtained it.”

The hall let it stand, and the letting stand was the single most useful thing anybody had done all evening.

Moot began to close the sitting, and stopped with his pen above the book, because of a noise from the corner where the ward’s notices were kept.

“That’s not a ward notice,” he said, to himself, and went over.

It was a routing index. Every notice the assembly sent went out with a routing, and the routing told the clerk which desk had produced the text, and the desk was how the assembly found its own paperwork when somebody came back with a question. Moot had pulled the wrong shelf and found the index for the plate and bridge-office desks, which nobody had consulted in a decade because the plate office had been folded into the maintenance office and had left behind a stamp and a shelf.

“It’s all here,” he said, half to himself, turning pages. “Custody entries, not contents. When a desk is folded in, its incoming goes to the successor desk. It isn’t indexed by desk after that, so — so this is the last one before it stopped.”

He read down a column of dates, and then stopped, and read one line twice, and then turned the index around so the middle aisle could see it.

Moot read the four lines aloud, twice, and then did the thing that made them a record instead of a story told in a hall: he wrote the shelf, the box, the two dates and the stamp on the session sheet, and entered the page as an exhibit in the sitting. That was the only way anything got into a record in this room with no quorum behind it.

The middle aisle could not read it from where it stood. But the page was now an entered exhibit inside a boundary that a court clerk had already set across a public record, and custody indexes are records, and the translator had taken it. Ilyan saw the formal lines appear in front of him like weather coming over a ridge.

**MAINTENANCE-REVIEW CONDITION, NINTH BRIDGE: ORIGINAL ROUTING, BRIDGE PLATE OFFICE.**
**REROUTED ON EXECUTION OF THE CURRENT WORKING PLATE, TO CONTINUITY DESK, C—2.**
**THE C—2 DESK HAS NO INCOMING INDEX AFTER THE DATE OF THE REROUTING. THE CHAIN BEFORE THE REROUTING IS COMPLETE. THE CHAIN AFTER IT IS NOT.**
**THE DESK IS NOT NAMED. A STAMP WAS USED.**

Two sentences, in a shelf nobody had opened in ten years, in a hand that was not the hand of the office that had done the rerouting, written by somebody whose job had been to keep the index rather than to act on it. It said the maintenance condition had not been quietly dropped in a copying accident. It had been taken off one desk and put on another, at the moment the working plate was replaced, and the other desk had no name and no clerk and only a stamp, and its incoming record stopped existing on the day it was used.

A charter missing its own review clause. A plate removed and a certified duplicate inserted in the same hour. Two documents arriving in the same week from a direction that had never once been a person.

Moot put his hand flat on the index, and did not say the word he was thinking, because he was a clerk and clerks do not say the word they are thinking in front of a room.

“That is the office,” he said. “That is the same office as your scrape and your print strip. And there is no list of what came into it. There is only a stamp, and the stamp has no name on it.”

The evidence session closed at the last bell with no findings, no decision, and no order. Six bodies, a boundary, a rope, a public witness board, an unsworn landlord’s book refused admission, a household that had said no a fifth time, and an index page in a corner shelf that said a bridge’s own maintenance clause had been walked, on a day in the past, by an office with a stamp and no name, from one desk to a desk that stopped existing the moment it was used.

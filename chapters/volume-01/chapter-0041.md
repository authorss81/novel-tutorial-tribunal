# Chapter 41: The Hours the Arrangement Does Not Have

The last load of the last day came over at dusk with the light going out of the water, and it was a load of salt out of the eastern yard, and it was not the last load, and everybody on the approach knew the difference and nobody said it out loud.

A. had the tally board on his knee at the eastern stake with the lamp hooded to a slit, because a cart book does not need to be read by a hundred people, it needs to be right by one. He wrote the eighth of the load-fee against Ardan's name in a hand that got worse as the day went, the way a hand does when the entries are the same eleven times and the man writing them has started to hear how they sound.

“It is short,” he said, without being asked.

“How short,” Joren said.

“Short the way the last day of anything is short.” A. turned the board round. Looked at Ilyan over the top of it. “Nine carts came over. Seven came over loaded. On a working day it is eleven loaded and four empty and the empties are the ones that pay for the loaded, because an empty cart on a bridge is a cart that has already done its work somewhere else and does not want to be on it. Today the four empties went east at the second bell and did not come back, and they will not come back, because tomorrow is nothing, and a carter does not pay a toll to a road that does not exist tomorrow.”

“Then tomorrow is thin,” Joren said.

“Tomorrow is a thin road,” A. said, “and a thin road is still a road, and I will take the eighth of what comes over it, and I will write down what does not, and I would like the frame to say that as well, because in a week somebody is going to ask me what this span was worth and I am the only one who kept the answer.”

He wrote it. Ilyan could read a number in a column and had never once been able to read the sentence under it, and the sentence under it, tonight, was that the money for the arrangement was going to be smaller than the arrangement assumed, and that the difference would come out of somebody's hands, and that the only hands left in it were four.

Oren had the count coming back at dusk with two on the beam and Coll in the book, and the west joint came back to its mark and walked out two fingers and came back, and the bell gave one note after the change the way it had given one note after every change since the first day of the case, and the sound went out over the river and came back thinner than it went.

“Out two,” Ilsa said. “And back. And the half is there on the light one.”

“Write the half,” Oren said, because that was their whole exchange now, and because the half had stopped being a correction and become a fact.

Puck came up out of the under-deck hatch with the lamp still in his hand and water on the sleeve of his coat, and he had been down there long enough that the lamp glass had gone opaque.

“It is worse,” Puck said, and everybody turned, because it was not a thing anybody said about that hatch.

“Better or worse,” Oren said, coming down the steps. “Those are the two words. Pick one.”

“Neither, and I do not know which is the word for it, and I have been down under that seat for two hours with a lamp.” Puck held it up. The glass was not wet. It was grey, the way glass goes when it has been held against clay. “There is no stone in the bed of the course under the west end. Not loose stone. There is a hole. The lime is still there and the fines are gone out of it, and the water comes up through the lime and not through a crack, which means the lime is what is left, and the lime is not what was put there to be the thing that holds.”

“How big,” Oren said.

“I do not know big. I am not a measure. I am a man with a lamp.” He put it down on the step. “It is the shape of a hand.”

“Then get me a rule and go and eat something,” Oren said, “because you have been under there since the second change and the arrangement ends at the fourth morning bell and I am not having you fall into a hatch in front of a court.”

Tarin came down the western steps at dusk with the boundary roll over his shoulder and no lamp, because he had never once used a lamp on an approach in eighteen days and Ilyan had stopped finding it remarkable somewhere around the fourth day.

“The count ran to the change and back,” Tarin said. “Nobody crossed in the last two bell intervals who was not on the arrangement. One woman came off the lower stair with a bundle that was not a roof bundle and told Coll the roof bundles went east, and Coll wrote it down, and I would like the court to know they went east yesterday as well, so that nobody in a room tomorrow says the span's condition took them.”

“They went east this morning too,” Joren said.

“They went east this morning because the carrier came at the third bell and looked at the frame and read what was on it,” Joren said, “and the frame says the span ends at the fourth morning bell, and a carrier does not put a load on a road that ends, and I am not blaming him and I would like him not blamed, because he is doing the only sensible thing a carrier can do with a road that has an end date and nothing behind it. Neris named the household it goes to first. Room nine. And the roof goes on at the east bank to a household with two pairs of hands, and Besh cannot be on a ladder, and his mother cannot get up one.”

“Then say it where Marda Rennick is not,” Ilyan said, and then, immediately, to himself: “No. Say it where she is. She should not have to hear her own roof from a lane.”

“She is not here,” Neris said, from the steps.

She had come down at dusk with Tamsin, and Tamsin was sitting on the bottom step with her leg up and her stick across her knees, and she had not gone out onto the span, and she had not come to the rope, and both of those things were noticed by about forty people and neither of them was a surprise any more.

“Then I will say it at the shelter and I will say it to her,” Ilyan said, “and I will say who decided it, which is the district, and I will say that it is not a reason for the bridge and it is not a reason for anything except a roof.”

Neris looked at him for a moment.

“You will say it at the shelter,” she said, “and you will not say the word *carrier* in front of him, because he is nine, and you will say that a load went east because the span has an end date, and that the end date is the district's, and that the district knows it, and that the district wrote it down. Is that the sentence?”

“That is the sentence.”

“Then say it exactly like that,” Neris said, “and not one word more, and I will be standing there when you do, and I will not be standing between you and him.”

The costing began at dusk on the board, because that is where everything on this bridge had been decided for eighteen days, and because the board was the only surface in the lower ward that everybody in the case could read without standing in a queue.

Oren had the slate under his arm and the chalk in his hand and the look of a man who has been given a job by a court and has discovered the job is a sum.

“Ordered to produce a costed pause proposal,” he said. “At the review. I have had it since the seventh bell and I have had it in front of me the whole day and I could not do it in daylight because I could not do it, and I could not do it because I did not want to write down the number, and tonight I am going to write down the number, because the number is the only thing anybody has asked for in three weeks that I have not been able to avoid.”

He put the slate on the bench and Ilsa came and stood at his shoulder because she was the one who would check it, and Coll took out the pencil he had started carrying for exactly this, and Oren wrote.

“First the work,” he said. “A pause is not a word. A pause is: a keeper reads the joint on a named inspection. The keeper rings the bell. The bell means clear the deck and stand off. The loads that can come off come off. The loads that cannot come off — and there are always some, and today there were four on the deck when I rang it for the change and two of them had the span ahead of them full — those loads wait.”

“How long,” Sera said. She had come down from the advocate stool and was standing at the bench with the case file under her arm, and she had not been in the workroom all day because she had been in a room with a print shop's answer in it.

“That is the whole question and I have been avoiding it for eight hours,” Oren said. “How long.”

They worked it out on the board in front of about twenty people, and it took forty minutes, and it is the only piece of arithmetic in this whole case that anybody has ever been able to check, and Ilyan wrote it all down because he was the record and had nothing else to do with his hands.

A load standing on a deck while the deck is cleared. The span is one hundred and thirty-one feet of oak on two courses. The west end is where it is empty. A load at the west end is the load that pays.

“No,” Oren said, before anybody had finished the sentence. “No. It is the load at the *east* end that pays, because the west end is the end with the hole under it, and the load that stands closest to the hole is the load that has to move first, and the load that has to move first is the load with a road behind it that is full, and a cart cannot turn round on a bridge.”

“So it is the eastern approach,” Joren said.

“It is the eastern approach and everything on it,” Oren said. “And that is not my arithmetic, that is the ground, and I have been standing on that approach for twenty-two years and I can tell you that a cart on the east end with a full road behind it does not come off in any time you are going to write on a board.”

Ilsa did the sum anyway, because she was the counter and counters do the sum.

“Two bells to clear a load that can turn,” she said. “Six bells for a load that has to be reversed off a bridge with a full approach. That is not a number anybody is going to like at four in the morning.”

“How many loads,” Dain said.

He was at the rope, in the same borrowed capacity, with the folded paper still in his coat, and he had not come inside the boundary at any point in eighteen days, and he was asking a question, which was the single most remarkable thing that had happened to him in three weeks.

“Depends on the day,” Joren said. “Today, four on the deck at the change. On a salt day, nine. On the last day of a week with nothing behind it,” he said, and did not look at A. on the eastern stake, and A. did not look at him either, and the whole approach understood that Joren had just cited a cart book as evidence, “three, and the three are light, and the light ones can turn.”

“Then a pause is between two and eight bells,” Dain said, “and the eight is the honest one, and the two is the one a clerk will write down. And in eight bells a cart on the eastern approach has not done a quarter of an hour of a day's work, and the hour is the hour, and the hour belongs to a carter who is standing on a bridge at four in the morning because a man with a beam said so.”

“Yes,” Oren said.

That was the first time in eighteen days that the keeper of the ninth bridge had said yes to a district officer about anything at all, and Dain took it the way a man takes a thing he has been waiting for and does not trust.

“Say the rest of it,” Dain said.

“The rest of it is the number,” Oren said, and put the chalk on the slate, and Ilsa had already written it, and it was in a hand she did not shake, which for Ilsa was the loudest available form of shaking.

**THE COST OF ONE PAUSE, NINTH BRIDGE, AT THE WARD RATE.**
**TWO BELLS FOR A LOAD THAT CAN TURN. SIX FOR A LOAD THAT MUST BE REVERSED WITH A FULL APPROACH BEHIND IT. EIGHT IS THE CEILING, AND EIGHT IS WHAT THE CARTER LOSES.**
**SIX HANDS UNDER AND ABOVE, THE WHOLE TIME, AT THE WARD RATE, WHICH IS IN L'S BOOK.**
**THE FIGURE, WRITTEN OUT, IS WHAT NOBODY IN THIS ROOM CAN AFFORD TO PAY OUT OF ANYTHING THEY HAVE.**

“That is the honest sum,” Oren said, “and I have had it since the seventh bell this morning, and here is why it is honest and why it is useless. If a keeper may stop a load and the stopped load is not bought, then the keeper is a man who can take a carter's afternoon for nothing, and every carter on this span learns that inside a week, and after that the bell is a thing you ignore, and the day the keeper rings it because the joint is doing something it has never done, the bell is a thing that gets ignored, and I will have spent the last three weeks of my life writing a clause that cannot be heard.”

He put the chalk down.

“That is the whole of the costed proposal,” he said. “A pause is not free and it is not the district's and it is not the guild's. It is bought, every time, by somebody, at the ward rate, on the day, in public, or it does not exist. And I cannot find the somebody. The guild's account is empty at the fourth change, which is a sentence I have written on this board every night for three weeks. The road party's hours are at the road rate and come out of the load-fee, and the load-fee is thin tomorrow because there is nothing behind it. The pump is two days short and Dalla has written the two days in public where I can read it every morning. And the district has a lapsed schedule and no instrument and no reason in the world to send anybody to this span.”

Nobody said anything for long enough that the water moved audibly under the west abutment, which it does, at that hour, when the tide is turning.

“So the clause cannot be written,” Pev Sorrel said, from the corner, without turning round, because that is how Pev said everything.

“The clause can be written,” Oren said. “The clause can be written this evening and it will be beautiful and it will be worth nothing, because a clause nobody pays for is a bell nobody rings, and I have been a keeper for twenty-two years and I know exactly what I would do, and what I would do is not ring it, because I would have to look at a carter and tell him the hour is his.”

“That is the first true thing anybody has said about a rule in three weeks,” Dain said, and did not enjoy it, and folded the paper out of his coat for the first time in a fortnight and did not open it.

Then he opened it.

“The district has an answer,” he said, “and it is not mine, and I have been carrying it since the fourth day and not producing it because the district is not entitled to produce it in a room where the charter is the thing in dispute, and I am going to produce it now, on the span, in public, with my name on it, because the guild has just said out loud that the clause is unwritable and the district is the only body in this case that has money, and I would rather be the man who put the money on the table than the man who said the table was empty.”

He did not unfold it all the way. He read four lines off it and then folded it again and put it back, which Ilyan understood to be a man who had learned something about rooms in the last eighteen days.

“The district will not carry a keeper's power,” Dain said. “It has not created it and it has not voted for it and a district that funds a power by habit is a district that has learned to rule by habit. What the district will carry is the hour. The stopped hour, at the ward rate, out of the district's own road funds, on a named day-book line, entered by the ward's clerk, posted on the frame in chalk where a carter can read it before he commits to the span, and paid to the person who stood there, not to a ward, because there is no ward to pay.”

He stopped, and looked up, and the next part was the part he had clearly not wanted to say on a public approach in front of a bell-runner.

“It requires the under-deck watch restored to two hands,” he said. “One on the deck and one beneath. At the ward rate, from the district's road funds, every change, from the first bell. And I know exactly what that is, because my office asked for it in the first week and I said the office had no power here, and the office was right and I was right, and neither of us was in a position to do anything about it, and here is the position.”

“It is the second hand,” Ilsa said.

“It is the second hand,” Dain said, “and without it there is no count, and without a count the keeper's inspection is a man looking at a joint he has looked at every day of his life, and the second hand is the entire difference between a review and a habit, and a crew of six at every change would have found the hole in your west seat four days before you found it, and the reason you did not is that the two hands that went were the hands that went when the second witness went, and Mr Sedge has been saying that on a board for a week and he is right.”

Oren said nothing at all for a while.

“Then the district pays for the hour and for the second hand,” he said, “and the guild keeps the keeper and the count and the bell, and the guild's own account carries the rest of the hands, and the rest of the hands are not paid, and I know that, and I will say it on the board in the morning in chalk as big as the end date.”

“The guild's rest is not paid for ever,” Dain said. “It is paid for until somebody opens a book and votes, and if that book never opens then your six crews are doing maintenance for nothing in a city that has nine bridges, and that is not a settlement, Mr Sedge, and I will not pretend to you that it is. It is a span held open by a keeper who has not been replaced by anything.”

“I have been replaced by nothing for twenty-two years,” Oren said. “I am not complaining. I would like it written down that I am not complaining, because a man who is not complaining is a man who has agreed, and I would like that agreed in chalk.”

And Ilsa, who had been standing at his shoulder with the slate, said the thing that turned the whole night:

“Then write the clause so that the hour comes off the load and not off the person,” she said.

Everyone looked at her. She had the beam still in her hand from the dusk count and she turned it over while she talked, the way she did.

“You have written it as a keeper stopping a cart,” she said. “That is the district's version and Dain is right that it is expensive, because a stopped cart is a person losing an hour. But look at what actually happens. The bell rings. The people get off. On the day it rang with a child under the span, everybody who was on the deck got off and the span went on, and that is what a bell has always been on this bridge. It has never held anybody. It has emptied the deck. And a load that is standing on a deck that has been emptied is a load that is standing over a hole with nothing on the west end to relieve it when it settles.”

“So the load comes off,” Oren said slowly.

“The load comes off, or the load *moves*,” Ilsa said. “There is no third thing. A pause that is longer than the count of the loads standing on it is not a pause, it is a jam, and I have watched a joint walk three fingers out and hold for the length of one count and I would not want to be a carter standing on it for eight. So the keeper does not stop the traffic. The keeper empties the span. And if a load cannot get off, then the keeper's power is not power over that load, it is a power to *send* it, and the sending is a thing a carter can do and a keeper cannot do, and so the keeper's bell is not a command, it is a *condition*, and the condition ends when the deck is clear, and if the deck does not clear inside the stated number of bells then the bell is a warning and nothing more.”

“That is a smaller power,” Dain said.

“That is a *true* power,” Ilsa said. “It is the only power a rope over a wheel on a worn pin has ever had, and you cannot write a keeper's clause that is bigger than the machine it is rung on, and if you do, then in a year somebody will say the bridge can be stopped by a man with a bell, and it cannot, and the day it does move somebody will use the false word and the real clause will not be in his head at all.”

Oren put the chalk back in his hand. He wrote the line. Ilsa checked it. She made him change one word, and he changed it, and neither of them argued, and that was the entire shape of the next morning going to be decided in a chalk line at dusk on the last day of a week while the tide turned under a bridge nobody could afford to stop.

**THE KEEPER'S MAINTENANCE-REVIEW CONDITION, PROPOSED BY THE GUILD AND PRICED BY ITS COUNTER.**
**THE JOINT IS READ ON A NAMED INSPECTION. IF THE READING IS OUTSIDE THE BAND THE KEEPER HAS KEPT, THE KEEPER RINGS THE BELL. THE BELL EMPTIES THE DECK.**
**THE PAUSE IS NOT THE KEEPER'S TO HOLD. IT IS THE SPAN'S, AND IT ENDS WHEN THE DECK IS CLEAR. IF THE DECK IS NOT CLEAR INSIDE THE STATED BELLS, THE BELL IS A WARNING AND THE SPAN IS RELEASED.**
**THE HOUR IS BOUGHT AT THE WARD RATE, ON THE DAY, IN CHALK, TO THE PERSON WHO STOOD THERE.**

“There,” Oren said, and stepped back, and looked at the count book under the weight with the five entries and the mark in the margin, “is the thing I have been holding in my pocket since the seventh bell. And it is not a clause, and it does not amend anything, and it has no appeal in it and no exit in it and no name on it, and it is the first sentence anybody has written in three weeks that a person can be hurt by and know it.”

“Can it be hurt by,” Ilyan said. “Can you say what happens to a carter who hears a bell and does not get off?”

“They stand there and take the hour,” Dain said, “and if the joint does something in that hour then the joint does something with a carter on it, and that is the cost of a rule that pretends a warning is a command.”

“Then that is the fifth bell,” Oren said. “The bell is rung and the load does not move. The keeper releases the span. And nobody may say afterwards that the keeper stopped the cart, because the keeper did not stop the cart, the keeper rang a bell, and a man who will not get off a bridge when somebody rings it has been told.”

“Who writes that in a charter,” Sera said. “In a sentence a district's clerk can copy without asking anybody. Because a man who will not get off a bridge is a man who does not believe the bridge, and there are people on this span every day who do not believe it, and I have met two of them and one of them is me.”

Nobody answered that, and the not-answering was the first honest thing that had happened all evening.

Then a rider came up off the fen road at a fast walk with a district's roll and a light, and it was Larch, twenty-four and a clerk for a year, and he had walked from the rented office because there was no cart on the last day of a week for a ward clerk to be carried in.

He came to the rope and stopped, and did not come inside, because Larch had never once come inside a boundary without an order and tonight there was no order.

“Vale sent me,” he said. “With the district's answer, and with two other things, and he said to say that if you want to argue with either of the other two you will have to argue with me, because he is not coming down here tonight, and he said to say *that* as well, and I have written down that he said it.”

“Read them,” Sera said.

Larch unrolled it against the rope-post, one-handed, holding the corner with his chin, the way a young clerk holds a document at a rope at the end of a day he did not expect to have.

“The first is the answer,” Larch said. “The district accepts a maintenance-review condition on the ninth-bridge charter. The district will pay the stopped hour at the ward rate out of the district's road funds, entered on a named day-book line and posted in chalk at the frame. The district will pay the under-deck watch at two hands at the ward rate, from the first bell, at every change. The district will pay the pump's two-day shortage, which is a line in an arrangement written by a pump worker and not by the district, and the district is paying it, and D. Rusk is not required to say thank you and I am instructed to say that she is not required to say thank you.”

Dalla Rusk, who had been standing at the top of the steps with her hand on the pump slate, said nothing, and did not move, and two people near the back of the crowd looked at each other.

“And the second thing,” Larch said, and stopped, and looked at the roll, and then at Ilyan, and said, “the second thing is a question and it is addressed to you and not to me, and I am the messenger and not the asker.”

“Read it,” Ilyan said.

“It says, in the ward's own hand and not in Vale's, that the district cannot pay the ward rate to a ward.” Larch swallowed. “It says that the ninth-bridge maintenance watch is two hands at the ward rate, and that the ward rate is the ward rate and not the district's rate, and that the district's road funds may pay at the district's rate and not at the ward's, and that the difference is not a matter of principle, it is a matter of a line in a book, and that the line may only be paid to a body with a name and a ward has no name, and therefore the district's answer is arithmetically impossible on its own terms.”

“It is,” Ilyan said.

“It is,” Larch said, “and that is not the end of it, because I have the third thing, and the third thing is that the district office will enter the money against a named party to a party that accepts it in a register, and there is a register, and I wrote it, and I know every line of it, and the register has seven columns and the seven are party, span, source, hours, date, verification, and objection, and there is no column for a body that owes money, and there is no column for a body that has refused, and there is no column for a person going.”

“Neris,” Ilyan said.

“Neris Ro,” Larch said. “You are not to say her name in front of the district,” Neris said from the steps, “and I am saying it, so write it down that I said it and that he said it first, because a clerk who puts a woman's name in a district's roll because a foreign record-holder said it out loud on a public approach is a clerk who will be able to do it again.”

Larch wrote that down on the roll, in the margin, and read it back, and Ilyan watched a twenty-four-year-old man add his own sentence to the end of a district document because a woman told him to and because it was true.

The tide finished turning under the west abutment. The bell hung on its frame, and the rope hung four feet off the paving, and nobody put a hand on it, and the count came back one last time at dusk from a span with no money behind it, and out two, and back, and a half on the light one.

**THE NINTH-BRIDGE SPAN IS OPEN TONIGHT. IT IS CLOSED TO NOTHING. IT IS MAINTAINED UNTIL THE FOURTH MORNING BELL BY FOUR HANDS, PAID FOR BY NOBODY, OUT OF AN ACCOUNT THAT IS EMPTY, UNDER A CHARTER THAT HAS NO CLAUSE IN IT AND NO COURT BEHIND IT UNTIL MORNING.**
**A CARTER WHO CROSSES TONIGHT IS CROSSING ON A BRIDGE THAT HAS AN END, AND THE END IS FOURTEEN HOURS AWAY, AND IN THE MORNING HE WILL BE ABLE TO READ WHETHER THERE IS A DAY AFTER THAT.**

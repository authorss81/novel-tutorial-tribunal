# Chapter 35: The Ro Household Under the Copy

Bevin Larch came up the lower road with the roll under his arm and a face that had been walked on for two days, and he got to the shelter before he got to the door.

“Ro household,” he said. “I have a notice and a page, and I am going to read both, and I would like to do it in the lane.”

“Why the lane?” Neris asked.

“Because a notice delivered to a door is a notice delivered to a household,” Larch said. “A notice read in a lane is a notice read in front of witnesses, and I would rather it was the second one, and I have been trying to do that all week with no particular success.”

He was twenty-four and had been a clerk for a year and had learned the one lesson the job teaches before anything else, which is that the place a thing is said decides what the thing is.

He read it in the lane, standing on the stones, with Hanne Kolm at the door with her ring of keys and two of the four rooms behind her and Tamsin on the drying bench with her leg up and her stick across her knees.

**NOTICE TO A PARTY ENTERED IN THE LOWER WARD COMMON MAINTENANCE REGISTER.**
**THE RO HOUSEHOLD, LOWER ROAD, IS ENTERED AT THE NINTH-BRIDGE ENTRY AS A PARTICIPATING PARTY, PENDING VERIFICATION.**
**A PARTICIPATING PARTY IS A PARTY WHO MAY BE CONSULTED ON, AND NOTIFIED OF, ANY CHANGE TO THE MAINTENANCE OR LOAD PATTERN OF THE SPAN TO WHICH IT IS ENTERED.**
**EFFECTIVE FROM THE DATE OF ENTRY. THE ENTRY IS NOT A CONSENT TO ANY MAINTENANCE BURDEN AND DOES NOT AMEND THE BRIDGE CHARTER.**

The last line was the only one in the whole notice that was in the ward’s own working register rather than a clerk’s, and Ilyan could read it, and read it twice, and it did not tell him what he needed to know.

“Participating party,” Hanne Kolm said. “What is that?”

“That is a party,” Larch said, carefully, “to the ninth-bridge entry in the common register.”

“That is a bridge. What is a party to a bridge?”

“It means the register has you in it.”

“It means something has put my tenants in a book they did not ask to be in,” Hanne said, “and it has done it in a form where they are already inside before anybody knocks, and I have been keeping a book of rooms for twelve years and I have never once had to write down that anybody agreed to be here. That is not in a landlord’s book. That is in a court, and a court is a different animal, and I do not keep a court.”

“That is fair,” Larch said, to the lane, and meant it, which is a thing about him that had never once been tested and was true.

Sera took the page from him. “Where is the entry dated?”

Larch showed her the head of the page. “The entry is dated to the date of the record.”

“What record?”

“The attached record’s public account,” Larch said. “It is the trigger in the register’s own draft. I can read you the draft, if the household wants it, because the household is a party to the entry and a party to the entry is entitled to see the rule that made it.”

Nobody had ever in twelve days been told that a household was entitled to the rule that had made it a party. Ilyan watched Sera decide not to make a speech about that, which cost her something, and then take the second slip out of Larch’s roll and hold it up so that the lane could hear it being offered.

“Read it,” she said.

“*Where a matter carrying an attached record is entered in a district docket, the parties to that record whose hours, work, and crossing are themselves entered in a ward record are carried into the common register as participating parties pending verification, so that one notice may serve the record and its parties together.*”

The lane was quiet for a moment, and the quiet was not agreement, and Ilyan understood that the second half of that sentence was the entire district's reason and the first half was its legal basis and the two together were the machine.

“So a record comes in and the household comes with it,” Ilyan said.

“No,” Sera said, without looking at him. “Read the trigger again and read what the register calls a party. A record comes in and the *parties whose hours are entered in a ward record* come in with it. The district is not reaching for you. The district is reaching for a row in its own book, and your row is unusually complete.”

“Our row is complete because a recordwright spent twelve days separating it into four.”

“Yes,” Sera said. “And the district has been saying for twelve days that four records are four doors. It has now built a gate.”

---

They worked it out in the lane, in pieces, the way the whole case had been worked out all week: by putting one question in front of the people who owned the answers.

Neris put the first one.

“Where does it say we agreed?”

“It does not,” Larch said.

“Where does it say I agreed?”

“It does not.”

“Where does it say anybody in my house agreed to anything at all?”

“It does not,” Larch said, and then, with the terrible honesty of a young clerk who has read the whole page, “and I have been looking for an hour and there is no column for it. The register has a column for the party, a column for the span, a column for the source of the entry, a column for the hours on the record, a column for the date, and a column for verification. There is no column that says what the party thinks.”

“Then it is not consent,” Neris said.

“It is not consent.”

“Then it does not matter what I think.”

“It matters enormously,” Sera said, “and not to you, and that is the problem.”

She said it to the lane, in the flat voice, and Larch wrote it down because he had understood by then that she was talking to witnesses.

“A participating party is a party the district may consult,” she said. “It is not consent. Consent is a thing a person gives. Consultation is a thing an office does. And an office that may consult a household does not need the household's permission, because consultation is not a burden, it is a courtesy, and a household cannot refuse a courtesy without being the party who refused a courtesy.”

“So they can ask Tamsin,” Neris said, very quietly.

“They can ask Tamsin,” Sera said. “They can ask Tamsin about the load path. They can ask Tamsin about the bell. They can ask the household about its crossing hours, and they can ask whether the household accepts a maintenance acknowledgment, and if you say no, they do not have a refusal, they have a party who was consulted. That is the whole trick and it is a good one and I have never seen it done before in nine years.”

Neris got up off the drying bench's edge and stood in the middle of the lane, which she did not usually do, and which put her in front of her daughter.

“No,” she said.

“No is what you say,” Sera said. “It is not what the register says.”

“Then I will be in it and I will say no every time they ask me and they will write down that they asked.”

“They will write down that they asked,” Sera agreed. “And in a year there will be a clerk who reads the register to find out whether the Ro household was consulted about the ninth bridge, and the register will say yes, consulted, and the no will be on a separate paper in a separate book in a different building, and the two will be read by two different people and the register will win because the register is the thing that gets read.”

The lane went on being a lane, with the drain grate and the drying line and the dye-store wall ticking as it cooled.

Tamsin had not said anything for eleven minutes. She had her stick across her knees and she had been looking at the notice, and Ilyan had watched her read it three times, which for a ten-year-old is a long time and for a child who has spent twelve days being asked to be legible is a very long time.

“What does being a party get me?” she asked.

Nobody had an answer ready, and Tamsin looked around the lane at the people who were supposed to have them.

“When they change the load pattern, they have to tell us,” she said. “Before. So I would know.”

“Yes,” Sera said.

“And they can ask me things.”

“They can,” Neris said.

Tamsin nodded slowly, doing arithmetic, and Ilyan watched a child weigh a real gain against a real cost and refuse to be relieved of either.

“Then what do I have to do to get out of it?”

That was the question, and Ilyan knew before anybody answered that it was the question the whole case had been about since the first day he had landed on a riverbank with a spent blackglass token in his hand, and he had heard a version of it asked badly perhaps forty times since.

“Your mother asks for the entry to be taken out,” Larch said, and then, honestly, because he had read the page all the way down: “and I would enter that request, and it would be entered, and the register can carry an objection, because objections are a column. But it would be an objection. It would not be an out.”

“So the register has a column for saying no and no column for leaving,” Tamsin said.

“Yes.”

“That is a strange book.”

“It is a strange book,” Hanne Kolm said, from the door, “and I have kept a strange book for twelve years, and mine is strange in the other direction. Mine has a column for leaving and no column for anybody who is owed. I have written four hundred people into that book and not one of them is a party to anything, because nobody is a party to a room, they are a room, and a room is not an argument.”

She came out into the lane and stood beside the notice, and read it upside down, which she could do, and which told Ilyan something about her that he had not known.

“If you want the entry out, you will have to be named out,” she said. “And to be named out of a register you have to be named in a way that can be gone, and I do not know one clerk in this ward who can do that, and I have asked two.”

“Ask a third,” Ilyan said.

“I will ask a third,” Hanne said, “and I will tell you the answer I expect, which is that the register has a column for the party, the span, the source, the hours, the date, the verification, and the objection, and no eighth column, and that a book with seven columns and no eighth is a book where people are allowed to say no and not allowed to go.”

Nobody had put it that plainly and Ilyan was grateful to her in a way that surprised him.

He asked for a hold.

He asked for it in writing, through Sera, in eleven lines, and it was the narrowest thing he had filed since the west-joint inspection and it did not touch the charter, did not touch the stay, did not touch the maintenance term, and did not ask anybody to stop a cart. It asked the Bench to hold the Ro household's entry in the common register pending verification, on the ground that verification had been pending for twelve days and that a party under verification is a party whose position may be treated as unresolved while being treated as consulted.

It came back in under two hours.

The district's objection was filed first, and it was filed in the Saltwheel sentence, and Ilyan could not have stopped it if he had tried, because it was his own filing being used against him by an institution that had learned to use it from him.

**THE BENCH HAS HELD A RECORD AND DECLINED TO HOLD AN ENFORCEMENT. THE PRESERVATION OF A RECORD IS NOT A RESTRAINT UPON IT. THE ENTRY IS PRESERVED.**

“There it is,” Sera said, in the lane, and read it out, because the last paragraph of a Bench holding is clerk's machinery and Ilyan had been asking her to read those aloud for two weeks. “That is your own request, in somebody else's mouth, and it is exactly right, and I would like you to notice that it is exactly right.”

“I noticed.”

“Then say what you notice, because there is a thing underneath it and you are going to have to say it in public in three days and I would like you to have said it to me first.”

Ilyan looked at the lane: at a twenty-four-year-old clerk holding a roll, at a landlord with a ring of keys, at a woman standing in front of her daughter because she had decided to, and at the child, who was looking at the notice on the stones and not at any of them.

“The Bench invented that distinction,” he said. “In the Saltwheel matter. It decided that it could keep a record and could not stop a district doing lawful work. And the district has spent twelve days using that as a permission, and today I have used it against a household, and it worked again, and it worked because I taught it to.” He shut the notebook. “And the next thing that will happen is that somebody in another district files the same request against a household I have never met, and cites the ninth bridge, and cites me, and is right.”

“You cannot unteach a distinction,” Sera said. “You can only decide what to do with the fact that you taught it.”

“Do we have anything?” Neris asked, in a voice that had gone flat. “Do I have anything at all to give that notice with?”

“You have an objection,” Larch said, and held the roll a little tighter. “That is a column. I can enter it, and I have the authority to enter it, and it goes on the register next to the entry, in your words, in my hand, and anybody who reads the register after today reads your no in the same breath as your name.”

“Write it,” Neris said.

Larch wrote it on the roll's back page in a small square hand, and read it out before he inked it, which was something nobody had taught him.

“*The Ro household is entered as a participating party and objects. The household has agreed to no maintenance burden, has consented to no acknowledgment, and has not accepted any part of the ninth-bridge entry. We are named in this register because our hours are in a ward record and a record came in. That is not our doing and it is not our consent.*”

“Add the last sentence,” Hanne said.

“What last sentence?”

“*We are not asking to be removed, because a person who objects is still a party, and we would like the next person who reads this to know that we are in it and we are saying no.*”

He added it, and inked it, and gave the roll to Neris, and then seemed to remember something and took it back and put a second copy on the stones so the household had one and the register had one, which was not in any rule and which Ilyan assumed had been taught to him the same way everything in this city was taught, by somebody being careful once in front of a witness.

The district entered the register page as a produced record in the open case that afternoon, at the household's request, so that the household's own objection and the district's own entry sat in the same file, and the court's translator took it, and the lines came into Ilyan's sight flatly and without any comment on what they meant.

**LOWER WARD COMMON MAINTENANCE REGISTER, NINTH-BRIDGE ENTRY.**
**PARTY: RO HOUSEHOLD, LOWER ROAD. HOURS ON RECORD: FIRST BELL TO DUSK, WALKING LOAD. WORK RECORD: DYE-YARD, PORTABLE. SOURCE OF ENTRY: ATTACHED RECORD'S PUBLIC ACCOUNT, ENTERED AS A DISTRICT DOCKET.**
**STATUS: PARTICIPATING PARTY, PENDING VERIFICATION. VERIFICATION DATE: NONE ENTERED.**
**THE REGISTER RECORDS NO CONSENT, NO ACCEPTANCE, AND NO ACKNOWLEDGMENT. IT HAS NO COLUMN FOR ANY OF THE THREE.**

The last line had not been in the register. It was in the district's produced record because the district had appended it, at Bevin Larch's hand, at the household's request, under his own authority, on the same page.

It was a small act by a young clerk with a roll and a year of service, and it changed nothing, and it was the only thing in the lane that Ilyan would still have believed in a month.

---

They got the insertion date out of the register's own draft at dusk, because the draft is a draft and a draft keeps its working, and the working showed the row before it was a row.

Neris read it out on the drying bench with the lamp on her knee, and it took her twice.

“Sixth day back,” she said. “The day of the public account. In the hall.”

“That is the trigger,” Sera said. “That is the register's own rule and the rule says an attached record's public account, and the account was on the sixth day back, and on the sixth day back somebody in this ward read a line on a schedule and entered four parties, and one of them was you, because your hours were in the book.”

“So a man stood up in a hall and talked about himself for an hour,” Neris said, “and my house ended up in a register.”

“He was ordered to,” Sera said. “The assembly required a public account in three days. He did not ask for it and he could not have refused it without losing the thing that let him stand in the room at all.”

“It does not matter why he did it,” Neris said. “It matters that it was about him.”

“Yes,” Sera said. “That is the correct sentence and I am not going to argue with it and I would like it written down in exactly those words, because in three days a court is going to ask him why an institution responded to a record rather than to a person, and the honest answer is that he is the reason a record was in the room, and the dishonest answer is that he is the reason your household is in a register, and only one of those is true.”

Ilyan stood in the lane in the dark with the lamp at his back and did not say anything, because a man who answers for a household in that lane has made the household his sentence, and nobody thanked him for it either.

The notice was still on the stones where Larch had laid it out for the lane to read, and the household was a party, and the household had said no in two columns of a book that had no eighth column, and the entry was dated to a day on which a man had been made to account for himself in public and had done it honestly and had thereby caused a district clerk to carry a household into a register it could now consult without asking.

Somewhere underneath all of it, on a desk that had never had a name, an office had once taken a bridge's own review clause off one shelf and put it on another on the day it replaced the plate that recorded it, and had stopped existing.

And the district had just done the same kind of thing with better manners, in daylight, under a rule printed in a draft, and the thing it had moved had a woman and a child in it instead of a clause, and there was nobody anywhere who could be shown to have decided to.

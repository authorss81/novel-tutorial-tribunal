# Chapter 40: What the Field Left Standing

The frame on the western approach had three things on it, and by the second morning of the arrangement they had become the most-read document in the lower ward.

The guild's notice, in Oren's chalk, naming the work and the hands and the fact that the levy was spent. The district's register notice, which Larch had re-chalked the day before and pinned back up because he had decided a notice should be where the people it was about could reach it. And, underneath both, in the biggest chalk any of them had managed:

**DUSK TO THE FOURTH MORNING BELL. AND NO LONGER.**

“Nobody has taken it down,” Tarin said, from the eastern stake, at the first bell, which was the only observation he made for the first two hours.

“Nobody has a reason to take it down,” Joren said. “It is true, and it is the only true thing on the board.”

Oren had the count running at four hands because there were four hands. The arrangement's hours said four at every change and six at a stopped load, and there had been no stopped load since Tuesday, and nobody in the arrangement thought a stoppage was a good idea, and everyone in the arrangement understood that a rule you never use is the only kind that is still there when you need it.

Ilsa read the scale at the change.

“Out two,” she said. “And back. And a half on the light one, which is new, and which I am going to write down as a half and not as a two, because a counter who writes two because two is the number people are used to is a counter who has stopped counting.”

“Write the half,” Oren said.

---

Ilyan had it on the bench at the second bell in four lines, because he had learned that the case had been lost several times to a document with nine lines in it, and that four was usually enough.

He had written it the day before, in the room above the lock-and-ledger, in the last of the light, and Sera had read it and said nothing, which had been worse.

Sera read it out loud at the workroom bench, in the flat voice, with the count running underneath, and it was this.

> **1. The west joint of the ninth bridge drifts two fingers on every load and returns.**
> **2. The district's maintenance schedule lapsed because the guild refused the maintenance acknowledgment.**
> **3. The maintenance-review condition is absent from the active bridge charter, and was entered into preservation in the same week the current working plate was executed.**
> **4. The plate in the court locker is a load record, not a keeper's plate, and it and the older witness index are one document, cut once.**

“Stop,” Sera said, after the first one.

“Two fingers is the figure the guild has been reporting—”

“The guild has been reporting a figure between one and a half and three, and the reason it has been reporting two is that Ilsa has been reading the band off the load and rounding, because a rounded number is a number a clerk can enter.” Sera turned to Ilsa. “Is the half real?”

“The half is real this morning,” Ilsa said. “It was not real on Friday. The light loads do not take it out at all and the heavy ones take more than two, and I have been saying two because two is the figure that got written on a tray by a court clerk with a good hand.”

“Ilyan,” Sera said. “That line is false as written and it is false in the direction of worse. If a district's assessor stands up in two days and asks whether the drift is uniform, and you say *two fingers on every load*, and he asks to see a light load, and the light load shows nothing at all, then your whole finding is a keeper's rounding error with a court stamp on it, and the span goes back into the argument about whether it is dangerous, and we lose days we do not have.”

“Write it as the band,” Ilyan said.

“Write it as the band and write that it is a band and not a figure, and write that it is at the *low* end on a light load and at the high end on a heavy load, and write that the joint has never been observed to accumulate.” Sera tapped the second line of the first fact. “And write who reads the scale. Not *the west joint drifts*. *The keeper's counter, I. Bray, reads the scale and reports.* If it is the bridge drifting, the district will say the bridge is the party. If it is a named woman reading a beam, then the district has to say the beam is wrong, and I have never in my life seen a district say a keeper's beam is wrong.”

“Stop,” she said, after the second one.

“That one is right,” Oren said, before Ilyan could argue, which cost him something and which was the reason the guild had begun to be usable in rooms.

“It is not right, it is adjacent,” Sera said. “Read it back. *The district's maintenance schedule lapsed because the guild refused the maintenance acknowledgment.* The schedule lapsed because a term ran out on an instrument that nobody re-issued. The guild's refusal is a reason the instrument was not signed. It is not the cause of the lapse. The cause of the lapse is that a term ran out, and the reason the term ran out is that it was written to run out, and the reason it was written to run out is that the instrument the schedule rested on was a signature, and signatures run out.”

“The guild refused it,” Oren said.

“The guild refused it in public, on the approach, in front of the district's clerk, with the reason stated, and I am not asking anybody in this room to soften that.” Sera looked at him. “But in two days the district is going to stand up and say *the bridge is unsupervised because a keeper would not sign*, and half of that sentence is true and the other half is a choice the district made when it wrote a schedule that rested on a signature instead of a term. If we let the district say the first half we have handed them the whole sentence.”

“So what do I write?” Ilyan asked.

“You write that the schedule's authority was a signature, and that a signature was refused, and that the schedule therefore lapsed on its term, and that the span is now maintained by a local arrangement with a named end date and no schedule behind it.” She put two fingers on the bench. “All four of those are true. None of them is an accusation. Every one of them is a fact the district would have to answer, and the district cannot answer any of them without saying the word *schedule*.”

Which was the whole of her gift and the whole of her trade, and Ilyan stood there and took it and did not pretend to be gracious about it, because gracelessness would have been a way of making her pay for being right in public.

The third and fourth lines she read and left alone, and he noticed that she left them alone, and understood that leaving them alone was a vote.

Then Halden Moot came up the steps with the assembly's notice board under his arm, which he had not carried up the steps in two weeks.

“I have been sent,” he said. “Not by anybody. I came because there is a bench with nothing on it and a notice that has to be recorded, and the assembly's limited word binds the attached name and I am its clerk, and a thing that binds a name has to be recorded by the person who wrote it.”

“Record it,” Sera said.

Moot wrote it on the board, in a small square hand, in the open, with the count running.

**THE ATTACHED RECORD-HOLDER HAS NO REMAINING CONSTITUENCY. The lowland conservancy has withdrawn its citation. No district has entered the name as authority, the assembly having forbidden it. The household named in the stay is a party under objection in the ward's common register. The ward seats remain unfilled. The provisional standing granted at the first hearing lapsed at the seventh morning bell and has not been renewed, no new ruling having been made. The record-holder is therefore to be heard at the full hearing as a witness to his own attached observation, with no body behind him.**
**HIS ONE ENTERED MATTER IN THIS CASE REMAINS THE ATTACHED OBSERVATION OF THE ROUTING FACTS. IT IS PRESERVED AND IT BINDS NOBODY.**

Nobody read it twice. They did not need to. It had been written by the most procedurally correct man in the lower ward and every word of it was true, and it had the effect of a door closing in a room where a man had been standing for sixteen days waiting for somebody to walk in behind him.

“Nobody behind you,” Tarin said, from the stake, without any sympathy in it at all, which Ilyan had earned.

“Nobody,” Ilyan said.

“Ardan came to a weighhouse and took the paper off you and said he was not a district,” Tarin said. “The ward will not be a body. The household will not sit. Vale is a clerk and will do his job and be right. Oren has a guild that is out of money and a bench in two days.” He turned the stake over once in the ground to make it firm. “And there is a bell-runner with a line book who would follow you into a room if you asked him and who is seventeen and has a route to keep open for people who cannot write to a court, and that is not a constituency, and you should not use it as one.”

“I was not going to use it as one,” Ilyan said, and was, a little, and was glad to be caught.

“I know,” Tarin said. “That is why I said it out loud.”

He walked up the steps and came back and picked up the boundary roll, and the roll was the only thing in the whole business he had ever wanted.

---

The delegation took an hour and a half and went the way delegations go, which is badly, and then well.

“Oren,” Ilyan said. “The physical witness. The span, the west seat, the water under it, the stain, the two fingers and the half, the day, the stone at the yard, the wage board and the empty strongbox. You have run that span for twenty-two years and you have never once advocated in a room, and I am asking you to advocate in one.”

“I will not advocate,” Oren said. “I will describe. There is a difference and I have thought about it since the weighhouse and I know the difference now. Advocacy is asking a room for something. Describing is putting a span in front of a room and letting the room do what it wants with it, which is slower and which is the only thing I have got.”

“Take it.”

“Under-deck. The stain. The course and the water and the channel that a boy on a courier's line drew on a strip of oiled cloth that no court will admit and that is more accurate than the district's map.” He looked at the bench. “And the one hand. I am going to say in that room that my crew has been watching this span with one hand for longer than I have been in the post, and that the two hands that went were the hands that went when the second witness went, and I am going to say it about my own crew and not about a dead woman whose name I will not use.”

“Ilsa. The measurement.”

“The scale, the band, the loads, the order, the under-deck readings, and the number of counts since the field.” She turned the beam over in her hands the way she did at the end of a run. “And the half, which I know you are going to make me say twice. The half is mine now. If I write it and the district says the span does not move at half a finger, then I have made a bigger claim than the bridge can carry, and I have to be able to stand behind it, so I have to keep counting. Yes. I will take it.”

“Joren.”

“The road,” Joren said. “The apron, the curbstone, the wedge line, the fall, the salt in the tanks, the wheel that took the eastern turn fast on a slate with a name in the authority row. And the load sequence, which is the thing the conference could not get, because you cannot have a load sequence without a road and a bridge and a person who will say what order the loads came in and why.”

“Joren, the road is the district’s.”

“The road is nobody's this week,” Joren said, “and it will be somebody's on the fourth morning bell, and if the only road witness in that room is me then the road gets whatever the room gives it, and I would rather it were me than a schedule that has not been kept. And the load fee, and the nine cart owners, and A.'s book.”

“Ardan is not a district and is not going to be one for me,” Joren said, “but he keeps a book of what he collects and what he pays and the ward wants it in the ward's copy, and the ward has a copy now, because the district's copy stopped on Thursday. So yes. The fee is mine to produce.”

Tarin took the boundary and said so himself, without being asked, and then said the thing that made it worth having him.

“I will hold the line and I will keep the record of the line, and I will say in that room what a field does and does not do, and I will say it about the mark in the count book, and it will be the truth, and it will be a statement that there are three hands in that book and no way to tell which one and that the court cannot tell either.” He looked at the bench, where the count book was lying with its five entries. “And if anybody asks me who cut the mark I will say I do not know, in a public room, under oath, and I will not enjoy it.”

“Agreed,” Ilyan said.

Which left himself, and which he had known since the first bell, and which he had been putting off because it was the one that had a cost in it.

“I keep the record,” he said. “That is all. I take no witness, no measurement, no argument, and no burden. I will produce the four facts and the routing observation and I will read the household's register entry and the district's lapse and the index line, and I will not characterise any of them, and I will say in that room that I am the attached record and not an advocate and that I have no constituency and no standing and that the four facts are the four facts.”

There was a long moment at the bench.

“Say the last part again,” Oren said.

“The four facts are the four facts,” Ilyan said. “And if a court finds a fifth thing, I will not have been the one who went looking for it, and if a court says the four are not enough, then the four were not enough, and that will have been visible in the room rather than in a clerk's file.”

“That is either the last honest sentence anybody has said on this bridge,” Oren said, “or it is a man arranging to be blameless. I have not decided and I am not going to, and I am going to go and run a count.”

He went and ran a count.

---

Tamsin came down at the fourth bell with her mother and her stick and she was not asked to and said so before anybody did.

“You cannot use it,” she said, to Ilyan, before she was asked, “because it is in the guild's book and the guild's book is not the court's book, and the only way it comes into that room is if the guild lets it and the child lets it, and the guild's part is my mother's to give and the child's part is mine.”

“Oren has agreed to the five terms,” Ilyan said, “and the fourth of them says you can withdraw it until it has been read out loud once, and it has not been read out loud yet, in any room, and I am not going to pretend to a clerk that it has.”

“It has been read once,” Tamsin said. “Ilsa read it. Once, and I did not stop her, and the fourth term says *read aloud once*, and Ilsa read it aloud.”

“Then the fourth term is spent and you know it and I know it,” Sera said, “and I am not going to argue with a child about her own contract, and I am going to say the four things you are entitled to say about it, and you may stop at any of them.”

“Four,” Tamsin said, to show she had counted.

“First: the court may have a copy and not the book, and the copy carries all five terms on it, written on it, in the guild's hand, so that the terms travel with the account and cannot be separated from it by a clerk who wants a sentence. Second: you are not called. Nobody asks you to stand in that room. Third: if anybody uses the account in front of Neris Ro as a reason to sign anything, the fifth term is read first, in that room, before the sentence they want to use. Fourth—”

“Four is mine,” Tamsin said. “Nobody writes four for me.”

“You may write four,” Sera said, “and I will put it in the copy exactly as you give it to me and I will not improve it, and that is not an insult, it is the only promise I am in a position to make.”

Tamsin thought about it with the stick across her knees.

“Four is that the court has to be told the account went to the guild first, and that I gave it there, and that nobody made me,” she said, “and that if the court uses it, then the court is using something a child gave to a guild and not to a court, and the court has to say that out loud.”

“The court will not like it,” Oren said.

“The court does not have to like it,” Tamsin said. “It has to say it.”

Neris said, “I agree to the copy and I agree to the four things and I am not signing anything else, and if the day comes when somebody puts my daughter in a room I will stop the whole thing myself and I will not consult anybody first, including him.”

Which was not a legal position and was the most important sentence said on that approach all week, and Ilyan wrote nothing down, because it was not his and there was nowhere in the case to put it.

---

The last hour of the day went on the frame.

They cleaned the chalk off the guild's notice and rewrote it, because the wage line had changed and a board that was a day out of date was a board a district could use.

**NINTH-BRIDGE WORK, NAMED. west-joint brace and pin; load-count at every change; warning bell before and after; saltglass dust and lime for the joint course; under-deck watch at the west seat.**
**HANDS BY THE ARRANGEMENT: guild, four at every change, from the guild's own account, empty at the fourth change. Six at any stopped load.**
**PAID IN WAGES BY THE DISTRICT: nothing. THE DISTRICT'S SCHEDULE LAPSED ON ITS TERM. THE STONE IS BOUGHT AND IS AT A YARD.**
**NOT PERFORMED: the load-held day. NOT RESTORED: the maintenance-review condition, by which a named keeper may release the span after a named inspection. UNDER THIS CHARTER A KEEPER MAY SUMMON A PAUSE AND MAY NOT RELEASE THE SPAN.**
**THE WEST JOINT IS MEASURED AT ONE AND A HALF FINGERS ON A LIGHT LOAD AND UP TO THREE ON A HEAVY ONE, AND RETURNS TO MARK ON EVERY COUNT OBSERVED. THE PERMANENT SET OF THE DECK LINE IS ONE FINGER'S WIDTH FROM THE IRON RING AND HAS NOT MOVED.**

They put the count on the board, and the band, and the two measurements kept apart, because a drift and a loan are two different numbers and a board that mixed them would be a board a clerk could use.

Oren wrote the last line himself and made Ilsa check it, and Ilsa checked it and made him change two words, and he changed them without arguing, and that was the whole of the delegation working: five people, one board, and no sentence anybody had to swallow.

Sera came out to the rope at the last bell of light with the case file and a slate and the look of a woman who had been waiting two days to say something and had decided to say it at a rope instead of in a room.

“You reached for it again last night,” she said.

“Once.”

“Twice. The second time you did not finish the sentence, which means you thought better of it, and I am going to give you that, and I am also going to say that the fact that you nearly used my grandmother's page to buy a scheduling is now on my list of things about you, and the list is short.”

“What is on the list?”

“That you taught a district the distinction that has now been used against a household, and that you said so out loud before I had to stop you, and that you accepted a standing that had lapsed without once asking me to find you another one.” She put the slate down on the gate. “And that in two days you will have one lever left in this case, and it is the second item in a preservation index with a C—2 routing and a date in the same week as the bridge's own clause, and it has never been produced, and if you put it in front of that court you will get a hearing you cannot get any other way.”

“Yes,” Ilyan said.

“And I am telling you not to use it, and that the reason I am telling you is not purity. It is that a sealed item in a preservation index has no author and no witness and no office behind its name, and if you put it in front of a court, the court will have to rule on it, and I do not know what happens to an item that a court has ruled on. Nobody does. That is the entire problem with this city and you are holding the only thing in it that has not been touched.”

“Then it stays untouched.”

“It stays untouched,” Sera said, “and we go into that room with four facts, a guild's book, a strip of paper from a print shop, a lapsed schedule, and a district with no obligation and an argument it has not yet won.”

She picked the slate back up.

“I will say this once and then I am going to go and stand in a lane,” she said. “You have spent sixteen days trying to be the man who holds the finding. That was never going to be available to you, because you arrived with nothing and everything in this case is somebody else's. Today you gave it away in public, in a workroom, in daylight, to a bridgekeeper, a counter, a road man, a route-arbiter and a boy with a line book. If the full hearing goes badly, that is the only thing you will have that is worth having, and it is not a victory, and I am not going to call it one, and you are not to either.”

“I am not,” Ilyan said.

“Good.” She went down the lower steps.

---

The count came back at dusk. Out two, and back. The bell gave one clear note before the change and one after, and two hundred people heard it and none of them moved, because a bell is a rope over a worn wheel on a pin and it is not attached to the span, and there was nothing on the ninth bridge it could do.

The count book went on the bench under a weight, with four figures and a half in it and a fifth mark in the margin that three hands and no clerk and one court clerk who said he did not type it could account for. Two loads of crushed stone and four sacks of lime were at a yard off the fen road at the district's expense, and the load-held day had not been named, and the arrangement ended at the fourth morning bell, which was the morning of the full hearing, and which was also, as it happened, the day the district's schedule would have ended anyway if the guild had signed, which it had not, and would not, and which nobody anywhere could now say was a decision so much as a sequence of instruments running out in the order they were written.

And underneath all of that, in a bound volume in a cold room behind a print shop on a court lane, a standing authority whose name was nowhere on the paper had taken eleven things into preservation in a single week and routed every one of them to a desk that had a stamp and no clerk and no incoming index, and one of those eleven things was a bridge's own maintenance-review clause, taken on the very day the plate that recorded it was replaced, and one of them was a sealed request with a valid office seal and a C—2 routing and a missing witness index, and a name that was cut out of an active list, and neither of them had a person at the end of it, and the office that held them both was the most defensible body in the city and had never once been asked to explain itself.

Somewhere in a cloth hall off the fish-market lane there was an empty bench with a slate on it that said *WARD — VACANT*, and it had said it for a decade, and a house on the lower road had a girl in it who could hear a bell and could not stop a bridge, and a bench on a frame had a date chalked on it in the biggest handwriting anybody on that approach could manage, and the date was two days off, and the ninth bridge was, in every way that could be measured and in several that could not, one day more safe than it had been the week before, and not one single day more secure.

In two days a court would sit down at the ninth bridge and put three remedies on the table: amend the charter, break the span, or hold the hearing open.

And the household at the edge of it — a room, a work hour, a crossing hour, an open objection in a register with no eighth column — would be standing there with none of them signed, and would find out which one of the three was going to be chosen for them, and by whom, and whether a machine in a guild's book in a child's handwriting could hold a door open that a charter with no door in it had left standing.

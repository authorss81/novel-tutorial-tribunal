# Chapter 2: The Current Beneath the Bridge

The child’s fingers vanished before Ilyan reached the stair.

He dropped the last three steps and caught himself against a damp stone post. The bridge above him groaned again. Water surged through the lower channel, lifting a dark tangle of rope and splinters against his shins. The air smelled of wet ash and something sharper, like heated metal cooled too quickly.

Ilyan put one hand on the wall and made himself count. One breath. Two. The room on Earth was gone. The blackglass token in his palm had no light. Behind him, the sealed maintenance stair descended into black water. Ahead, a narrow arch opened toward a lit work passage.

He had an exit. He did not know whether it led out or merely deeper into the bridge.

A man in a dark, quilted coat appeared beneath the arch. He had a short blade at his hip and a black cord looped around his forearm. His face was lined, and the first thing he did was look at Ilyan’s hands, not his face.

“Stay where you are,” the man said.

Ilyan understood the shape of the warning more or less. The words were a silver tangle, and the man’s tone supplied what the syllables refused. He lifted both hands.

The man studied the spent token, then the paper notebook crushed under Ilyan’s arm. His eyes stopped on the signature.

“First Defendant,” he said, and the title sounded translated even in his mouth. “You are inside the ninth bridge’s lower field. Do not touch the anchor plate.”

Ilyan pointed upward. “Child.”

The man glanced toward the span, then back at him. “The child is not on the register.”

“That is not—”

He caught himself before the sentence became a plea. The bridge shifted. A heavy joint knocked against stone somewhere above, and water surged through the arch, forcing both of them against the wall. When the current dropped, the man seized Ilyan’s coat and pointed to a ladder built into the far side.

“Escape route. Climb.”

“I need to know what the words mean.”

“They mean the route is closed to you.”

“That cannot be the whole meaning.”

The man’s mouth tightened. He said something longer, and the foreign sounds folded over one another. A small black plate at his belt lit with a line of amber. Ilyan’s vision translated only that line, and only because it had been entered into his case.

**CLAIM: The ninth bridge is open to registered lawful travelers.**
**EVIDENCE: The current charter limits passage to names carried in the Orison register.**
**BURDEN: A person who crosses outside the charter must be named as the cause of the next crossing.**
**CHOICE: Amend the charter, break the bridge, or hold the hearing open.**

The panel hung in his sight, clean and useless. It did not say what amendment would cost. It did not say who held the bridge, who would repair it, or whether the child beneath it had a name.

Ilyan looked at the man. “I cannot choose from that.”

The man glanced at the amber plate. “The Bench has offered remedies.”

“It has offered verbs.”

For the first time, the man’s expression changed. Not warmth. Recognition, perhaps, that the stranger could distinguish a promise from a button.

“Tarin Keel,” he said, tapping his own chest. “Route-arbiter. You will answer questions later. If you have a way to climb, climb. If you do not, stay on the left stone.”

Ilyan looked for a ladder on the left. There was none. The only route was the one Tarin had indicated, and the water had risen over its lowest rung.

A third voice came from the work passage. It was older, roughened by shouting.

“Tarin, do not bring the foreign burden onto my bridge.”

A broad man in leather work clothes came through the arch carrying a coil of silver-gray cord. He moved with the caution of someone who had learned that speed made bad injuries feel worse. His hands were blackened at the knuckles. A metal tool hung from his belt, and a narrow strip of dark glass was tucked into his belt pouch.

Oren Sedge, the name on the bridge notice. The System supplied it without making the man any less real.

Tarin supplied the meaning in clipped pieces before Oren continued.

“The bridge is failing,” Ilyan said.

Oren stopped. “You understand that much.”

“Your work lights just went out.”

“Then you understand it better than the crowd upstairs.”

Oren moved toward the ladder, then stopped as the bridge shuddered. A line of blue light flickered through the stone above them. The lower arch tightened, and the water pulled backward before rushing in again.

“Before you came, the western joint was already drifting,” Oren said. “The emergency reserve was what kept that drift from spreading through the count. Your passage spent it. You did not create the fault. You made it immediate.”

Tarin pointed toward the arch. “An unlisted traveler entered the field.”

Oren’s gaze dropped to Ilyan’s shoes. “And the child?”

“Tamsin Ro,” Ilyan said. The notice had given him the name, but he had not yet heard anyone speak it. “She was under the span.”

“Was?”

“I saw her hand.”

“Her mother says she went for the warning cord,” Oren said. “The cord snapped. If she is beneath the span, the load has already found her.”

Ilyan turned to the wall. Beneath a layer of pale mineral crust, a thin black line ran from the anchor plate toward the lower passage. It was not painted. It shifted when the water moved, as if the stone were trying to keep two positions at once.

Oren followed his gaze. “Do not touch that.”

“Show me the load marks.”

“The charter shows the marks.”

“The charter is upstairs, and it is not telling me how the bridge is moving.”

Oren’s mouth tightened. He crouched and brushed aside the silt around a shallow iron ring set into the paving. Three cuts radiated from it. Two were filled with gray metal. The third was empty.

“This is a north mark,” he said. “The bridge rests on a promise of counted passage. When the count changes, the load finds the nearest witness. The child is not named, so the span has no place to put her weight.”

Ilyan crouched beside him, keeping his hands above the stone. “If she crosses while registered people cross, what happens?”

“The span chooses one condition. It does not ask nicely.”

“And if we add her?”

Oren looked at Tarin. The route-arbiter’s hand moved to the blade at his hip, not drawing it.

“That is not your decision.”

“It is hers,” Ilyan said. Then he stopped, because he had only just learned the name and did not know whether the child could answer.

The bridge bell rang above them. The sound was deep enough to shake dust from the arch. One strike. Then a second, farther away, as if the city were answering.

A blackglass notice unfolded in the air between them. The voice that came with it had no gender and no direction.

**FIRST DEFENDANT: ILYAN VESTER.**
**ORIGIN RECORD: EXTERNAL TESTER SIGNATURE ACCEPTED.**
**STATUS: AUTHORSHIP CLAIM UNDER REVIEW.**
**REMEDY: A. AMEND THE CHARTER. B. BREAK THE SPAN. C. HOLD THE HEARING OPEN.**

Beneath the three choices, in a smaller line, another sentence flickered into view and vanished before Ilyan could focus on it.

**Continuity preserves the record before remedy.**

He reached toward the notice. The spent token in his other hand warmed, though it had no light. Tarin caught his wrist before his fingers touched the air.

“No,” Tarin said. “You do not accept a choice by touching it.”

“I’m not accepting anything.”

“The Bench does not always distinguish.”

Oren rose. “You can help with the brace or you can stay out of the load path. Choose before the next bell.”

Ilyan looked at the empty cut, the narrow ladder, and the water climbing the wall. He had no local language, no authority, and no idea how a bridge in a world outside Earth could recognize an unlisted child as a physical condition. He had a sequence: observe the state, change one input, watch what moved.

He pulled the paper notebook from inside his jacket. The report signature was smudged but intact. On the next clean page, he wrote four short labels in Earth shorthand: **condition, witness, burden, remedy**.

“Show me where I can stand without becoming the next condition,” he said.

Oren looked at the labels as if they were foreign children’s marks. Then he pointed to a narrow stone ledge beside the work passage.

“Stay there. When the bell sounds, move one step left. If the water reaches your boot, you go back into the arch. If the stone moves, you leave. I am not asking for your courage.”

“I was not offering it.”

Oren almost smiled. “Good. Courage is expensive here.”

He and Tarin moved toward the work passage. Ilyan followed only as far as the ledge. The bridge’s load shifted above him, and the empty cut in the iron ring filled with a thin line of red water.

Then Tamsin’s hand reappeared beneath the span, farther out this time. The stone folded toward her with a slow, deliberate movement.

Ilyan reached the anchor plate as the span began to fall.

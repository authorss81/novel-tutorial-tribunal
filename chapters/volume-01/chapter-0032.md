# Chapter 32: The Work the Levy Does Not Pay

The levy ran out on Thursday at some point between the second bell and the third, and nobody found out until Friday morning, when Oren went to the strongbox in the workroom and there was nothing in it that was not already spoken for.

He came back with the empty strongbox under one arm and set it down in the middle of the bench, upside down, the way a man sets down a thing he wants a room to look at.

“The ninth-bridge maintenance fund is closed,” he said. “As of Thursday, some time between the second bell and the third. The district's clerks entered the last three days against the count, and there is no hour after Thursday in the whole of this week that anybody has paid for. From this morning the nine slots are nine slots the guild is asking for and cannot buy.”

For a moment the only sound in the workroom was the empty strongbox lying on its face on the bench, and eleven days of a wage board going up in chalk had made the room expect the number to be true, and it was true, and the true part was that it was over.

“Then we are working for nothing,” Jessa said.

“We are working for nothing until somebody funds it,” Oren said. “Which is a different sentence and I would like it kept.”

He wrote the change on the board in chalk and did not soften it, and the line went up where anyone crossing the approach could read it, which was where Oren put everything.

**LEVY: SPENT AT THE SECOND BELL, THURSDAY. HANDS FROM TODAY ARE ON THE GUILD'S OWN ACCOUNT OR THEY ARE NOT HAPPENING.**

“What does not happen,” Ilsa said, reading it, “is the count. Say the rest of it.”

“The rest of it is the under-deck watch, the salt crack, and any work on the seat, and if the seat wants doing, the seat waits until somebody pays for it,” Oren said. “I will not put four people under that span for nothing and call it care of the bridge. I have done that twice in this job. I know what it costs and it is not the money.”

“Then we find out what the joint wants,” Ilyan said, “before we find out who pays for it.”

“No,” Oren said.

He had not raised his voice. He put the chalk down in the chalk groove, which was the thing he did instead.

“You do not go under that span.”

“I was not going to go under it.”

“You were going to go under it at the fourth change with Ilsa’s hands on the scale and mine on the brace, and you were going to look at something and come back and tell us what you saw, and in four days you would be telling a room what you saw instead of what we saw,” Oren said. “You may keep the book. You may keep the figures. You may write down every count we run and the hour we ran it and what came back. You are the second pair of eyes and a second pair of eyes is two eyes and not four hands, and this morning the four hands we have are being paid for out of a box that is empty.”

“Understood,” Ilyan said.

“You are not sulking.”

“I am not sulking. I am going to be extremely well behaved and completely useless, and I would like it noted that those are different things.” He took the notebook out and did not open it. “Then tell me what it is and I will write down what you tell me, and if I write down a thing wrong you will tell me, and if you tell me wrong on purpose I will not know for six months.”

Ilsa made a small sound that was almost a laugh. “He will tell you wrong on purpose inside a week,” she said. “Not to be dishonest. Because a keeper who has decided something does not stop being sure because a man from another country is writing it down.”

“I have never been sure of anything in my life,” Ilyan said. “I have been well documented.”

The day broke into the four candidates, the way the guild's work always broke, and Ilyan wrote them at the top of a clean page and then spent the rest of the day failing to solve any of them alone.

**WHAT IS MOVING THE WEST JOINT TWO FINGERS AND BRINGING IT BACK?**
**(1) THE JOINT ITSELF — WORN. (2) THE APRON — SETTLED. (3) THE WATER. (4) THE ORDER LOADS COME OVER IN.**

Joren killed the second one at the third bell by putting his heel on the fourth span joint and shoving, and the thing everybody had been arguing about for four days moved four inches and came back.

“The apron is set and the apron is sound,” he said. “I have been telling Oren since the field that his joint is not the joint. The apron has held every load the span has taken and it has taken them from the road side so the fall has not closed. If the apron was the answer, the count would go out on the first run and stay out, and it does not go out at all until the span is loaded and it comes back before the span is clear.”

Which left the joint, the water, and the order, and the order was the easiest to look at and told them nothing, because the count came back to its mark on every one of ten runs with six different orders of cart, tank, and lime, and a thing that happens regardless of order is not caused by order.

That left the water, and the water was under the deck, and nobody under the deck had been paid for since the third change on Tuesday.

Which is when Bram Orren came down off the eastern approach with his satchel and his strip of oiled cloth and the manner of a seventeen-year-old who has been waiting two days for a conversation he was not going to be invited to.

He stopped at the chalk line and did not cross it.

“Bell-runner,” Oren said. “You rang the change yesterday and asked for the figures.”

“I asked for the figures.”

“They are on the board.”

“The board is the guild's board,” Bram said. “The board is wiped every morning. My line book is not wiped every morning, and my line book is the only record anybody keeps of what crossed this bridge and when and in what order, and I have run this line since I was twelve.” He took the satchel off his shoulder. “I am not here to be useful. I am here because the March's line crosses your span and a span that stops is a line that stops, and I would like that in your head before I say the rest of it.”

“Then say the rest of it,” Oren said.

Bram Orren put the satchel down on the stones and unrolled it, and inside was a strip of oiled cloth about the length of a forearm, and on the cloth was a map, and it was not a map of a bridge.

It was a map of the ground. River, banks, roads, the cut of the eastern approach, the line of the old channel drawn in a thin brown ink, and then, crossing it, a second line in a heavier hand that went under the western abutment and came out on the far bank four hundred yards downriver.

“Where is this from?” Ilyan said.

“From me,” Bram said. “And from fourteen children between the second bridge and the flats, and from a footman at the salt wharf who would not give his name. The court map has the old channel under the eastern approach, because the old channel was under the eastern approach when the map was made.” He put a finger on the heavy line. “The channel moved. It moves the way a channel moves, which is slowly and then all at once. It went west four years ago and it has been going west since. The eastern approach is on fill that the water is not using any more. The western abutment is standing in the bed of the thing.”

Nobody said anything. Oren had both hands on his knees.

“The district will tell you the map is a courier’s doodle,” Bram said. “It is. That is why I am showing it to the guild and not to the review, because a courier's doodle in a review is a thing a clerk throws away, and a crew that has looked at the ground is not a thing a clerk can throw away. You can check it. Go under the west seat with a lamp and look at the stone, and the stone will tell you, and I am not going to pretend I read stone.”

“Where did you get the *year* right?” Oren said.

Bram looked at him for a second.

“You are the ninth-bridge keeper,” he said. “Sedge.”

“Oren Sedge,” Oren said.

“Orren,” Bram said. “Bram Orren. My father is the March's second mapmaker and the March does not use my name for a bridge that is in yours, and if somebody says *Orren* on your approach they mean me and they mean somebody else's country, and I would rather correct it now than have a clerk correct it for me later.” He rolled the cloth half up, then stopped. “The year is wrong. I put it four years because that is when the footman at the salt wharf said the west bank went soft. He was talking about the bank. The bed moved slower than the bank. Call it six.”

“Six is better than four,” Oren said. “Sedge gets called Sedge. I have been called Sedge by three districts and one river.”

He stood up.

“Jessa, get me a lamp and a wedge. Puck, under-deck at the west seat, now, and not at the fourth change, now, and I know what the board says, and I am the keeper.”

“It is on the guild's account,” Jessa said.

“It is on the guild's account,” Oren said, “and Puck goes now.”

They went.

Ilyan wanted to go down and did not, and stood at the chalk line with his notebook shut and felt the specific frustration of a man who has been told the truth about himself by a bridgekeeper at eight in the morning. The rule was not arbitrary. Oren had watched a name on a board change how a cart driver drove, and watched a district put a man's hours on a slate he did not answer for, and was not going to let a fourth thing happen because the man was helpful and meant well and had a good hand for figures.

So he did the only thing left, which was to be the second pair of eyes and take the count properly.

He wrote every run: the hour, the order of loads, the weight band off Coll's call, the figure off the scale as Ilsa gave it, and the time it took for the joint to come back. By the fourth change he had eleven runs and a page that said more than any page in the case, and the page said:

**THE JOINT RETURNS TO MARK IN EVERY RUN, INCLUDING RUNS OF ONE LOAD AND RUNS OF FIVE. IT DOES NOT ACCUMULATE. IT IS NOT WEAR.**
**IT LEAVES ON LOAD AND RETURNS ON RELIEVING. THAT IS NOT HOW A WORN BEARING BEHAVES AND IT IS HOW AN EMPTY ONE DOES.**

Puck came up out of the water at the west seat at the fifth change with lamp oil on her hands and a look Ilyan had not seen on a person before and hoped never to see again.

“It is not the joint,” she said. “Oren, come down.”

They went, all four guild hands, and Ilyan stayed above, and what they found was in the ledger afterward because Oren wrote it, in his own hand, at the bench, and read it out before he let anybody add to it.

“The west seat is bedded on a course of bank stone and lime laid about forty years ago, and it is sitting in the bed of a channel that has been moving west for six years, and the water is coming up under it,” Oren said. “Fine stone is leaving the course. You can see where it has gone. It has gone into the joint, and the joint drinks it, and when the load comes off, the water carries a little of it back, and the seat stands a fraction again. Two fingers. Then the next load takes it, and the water takes a little more, and the water takes a little more.”

“So the joint is not settling,” Ilsa said. “The joint is being emptied.”

“The joint is being emptied and the water is doing it slowly and it has been doing it for long enough that the permanent set is what it is.” Oren tapped the board. “That pale line is what the joint has taken and kept. That is a different number from the two fingers and it never comes back. The two fingers are what one load borrows while the water puts it back. I have been calling the two fingers the drift because that is the word we use, and it is the wrong word for it. The drift is the pale line. The two fingers is a loan.”

“And when the loan stops being a loan?” Ilyan asked.

“When there is no fine left under the west seat to come back,” Oren said. “And then the joint goes out two fingers and stays out, and the second morning after that it goes out four, and there is no clause on this bridge that lets me stop a cart when that happens, because the clause that would have let me stop it is not in the charter.”

He said that last part without any weight at all, which was how he said the heavy parts.

“All right,” Ilyan said. “What fixes it?”

“Take the load off, open the seat, take out what is left of the course, and bed it again in crushed stone and lime, and let it cure long enough to be worth something. Three weeks if the weather holds. A day to open it, a day to bed it, and twenty days you do not load it.” He wiped his hands. “And crushed stone, and lime, and a crew of six for two days, and a span with no load on it for a day while we do it, and every one of those five things is somebody else's money and none of them is the guild's.”

“Which one is cheapest to refuse?” Ilyan asked.

“The day,” Oren said. “Everything else can be argued. If I have materials and hands, the day is a nuisance. If I have the day and no materials, I have an open bridge in the rain and a great deal of very expensive evidence about my own competence.”

“There it is,” Bram Orren said, from the chalk line.

He had not moved. He had been standing on the same stone for two hours with his satchel at his feet, and he had not asked to be let in, and Ilyan had been watching him not ask, and had begun to understand that this was a boy who had learned that asking to be useful in a room of working people is the fastest way to be given nothing.

“If you take the day,” Bram said, “the March's line stops. The line runs this span because this span is a day shorter than the four bridges above it. The people who use the line are not people who can pay a district to carry a paper for them. They use the line because the line is free and it is short and it comes.” He shifted the satchel. “I am not asking you to not take the day. I am telling you that four households on my line have standing arrangements with people who cannot write, and if the span closes for a day without notice, those arrangements do not pause, they break. And I would like the guild to know that before it costs me my line.”

“Then you should have said it at the third bell instead of the eighth,” Oren said, without heat.

“I said it at the second,” Bram said. “I said it to the court record hand, and he wrote it on his tray, and it is in the room now, and I did not want to be a boy in a guild workroom asking for a day. I am sorry it came out sideways.”

Nobody forgave him, because there was nothing to forgive. Oren wiped a strip of the board and wrote one line on it in Bram's hearing.

**NINTH BRIDGE, LOAD-HELD DAY. WHAT IT COSTS: the March's line, four standing arrangements. Stated by the bell-runner, entered by the keeper.**

“That is the first thing a person has said to me this week that I did not know,” Oren said.

---

The arithmetic took the rest of the afternoon and it was not Ilyan's arithmetic, because every figure in it belonged to somebody else and he could only carry them.

The guild's count book for the week sat open on the bench, and Jessa read the hours out, and Oren did the sum with a stick of chalk on the board the way a man does a sum he has done four hundred times.

“Eleven runs of the west-joint sequence at every change, four hands, since the field,” Jessa read. “Under-deck watch at every fourth change: nine entries. Salt crack, twice, eight hours, once from the guild's account. Apron, road party, road rate. Whistle, ward, ward rate.”

“Now tell me what the levy paid for the under-deck watch,” Oren said.

Jessa went back four pages. “The under-deck watch is entered at one hand. It has been entered at one hand every week since the year began.”

“Read me the shift line.”

“The under-deck watch is a two-hand job,” Ilsa said, before Jessa could find it. “One on the deck and one underneath, because a stain under a seat cannot be seen from above and cannot be reached from below alone without a second pair of hands to hold the light and work the seam. It has been one hand since the list I signed came in.”

“How long ago?” Oren said.

Ilsa did not answer straight away, and the room waited for her, because everybody in it had learned by now that the pause was where the fact was.

“Since the year the crew list went to one name,” she said. “Same year. I looked it up the week I was asked about the plate, because the plate is a load book and a load book is kept by two, and I wanted to know whether the two had ever been two in this century.” She put her finger on the page. “The watch was two hands until that list. It has been one hand since. And the stain under the west seat has been in the salt crack's entry for six weeks, and the salt crack is done from the deck, because the salt crack is a surface thing, and a surface thing cannot see a seat.”

“Four days ago,” Joren said.

Everyone looked at him.

“The channel,” he said. “It came west in the wet season. If Puck had been under that seat on the second change on Friday with a lamp and a second pair of hands, she would have seen the colour change in the joint where the fine is going in, because that is the first thing it does, and it does it before it does anything else. You would have had it in time to warn the district, and you would have had a figure, and the figure would have been a number a clerk could act on.” He shrugged. “You had the hour. You did not have the hands. The levy pays six at a change. Six is one more than five and five is one more than four, and four is the number of hands this bridge has had for eleven days, and the extra two went to the count because I asked for them and I asked for them because I did not know what the water was doing.”

“So a crew of six would have caught it four days ago,” Ilyan said.

“A crew of six at every change would have caught it four days ago,” Oren said. “And that is not an argument for the pause clause, and I want it written down that I am not making one. That is an argument for the levy paying for the shift the guild actually works. Which it does not. Which is why there is no money today.”

He put the chalk in the groove.

Then he wrote one more line, low on the board, under everything, in smaller letters, and read it out because it was going to be read out in a room eventually and he wanted the room to have his wording first.

**THE STAIN WAS THERE FOUR DAYS BEFORE WE FOUND IT. THE WATCH THAT FINDS THE STAIN WAS ONE HAND. THE WATCH WAS ONE HAND SINCE THE LIST WENT TO ONE NAME. TWO HANDS WENT WHEN THE SECOND WITNESS WENT.**
**A BOOK WITHOUT A SECOND HAND IS ONLY A RECORD.**

He did not look at Ilyan when he said it, and Ilsa did not look at either of them, and neither of them said a word about where in the room it had first been said, four days earlier, by a woman who had been fourteen and standing at the back.

The board went up on the frame at dusk with the empty levy strongbox sitting under it, which was a thing that ought not to have been under it and which Oren left there on purpose, because a district that came to look at the ninth bridge in the morning ought to be able to see that the money had stopped before anybody had to tell them.

Bram Orren took his satchel up the eastern approach and stopped at the top, where the rope had come down, and looked back at the span for a while in the failing light, at the pale line a finger's width from its iron ring, and the joint sitting exactly where it had been sitting all day, waiting for the next load the way it had been waiting all week.

“Two fingers,” he said. “And back.”

He wrote it in his line book, both figures, and the hour, and the number of the count, and then he wrote the third line under it, which was the one nobody had asked for.

*Bench mark on the west deck, chalked by the guild this morning, one finger's width from the ring. Not the same figure. Keep them apart.*

Then he went up the road to run a line that had four days of light left in it and a closed span somewhere in the middle of it that he had not been able to argue out of, and had not expected to, and had wanted very badly to be asked to.

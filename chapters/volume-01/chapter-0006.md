# Chapter 6: The Name of the Bell

The temporary shelter had been made from bridge canvas, old shop awnings, and a wall of salt-soaked brick that stopped the wind without pretending to be a house. Tamsin sat on a folded blanket with her injured leg propped on a folded coat. A healer had cleaned the cuts on her palms and left the ankle wrapped. Neris sat beside her, a bowl of cold broth untouched between her hands.

Ilyan stopped at the edge of the shelter. The canvas roof gave a small flap in the wind. Beyond it, the ninth bridge waited under a low sky, its repaired brace visible from the public road. Someone had tied a strip of white cloth to the approach rope. It marked the spot where the crowd had been cleared.

Sera was not with him.

He had returned Neris’s account before coming. The paper was folded along the same creases, but its contents had changed. The court’s preservation mark crossed the first line, and beneath it a clerk had written that the account was disputed and not yet offered as evidence. The words were small. The mark was not.

Neris looked at the paper in Ilyan’s hand. “You brought it back.”

“I brought the record. The court already has a copy.”

“That is not the same as bringing it back.”

“No.”

He set it on the low table between them. Tamsin’s eyes went to the page, then away.

Ilyan had prepared several explanations during the walk from the workroom. None of them made the public mark disappear. He could have said that the court had preserved the account automatically, that he had needed the timeline to prevent another collapse, that the words had been entered in an emergency and could not hurt anyone by themselves. Every sentence was accurate in some part and false in the part that mattered.

He knelt so the child would not have to look down at him.

“I filed your flood account without asking,” he said. “I told myself the account would make the missing condition visible. It did. That does not make the choice yours.”

Neris’s fingers tightened around the bowl. “You knew I said not to use it publicly.”

“Yes.”

“And you filed it because you needed the court to see it.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because I thought urgency was more important than your permission.”

Tamsin stared at him. The shelter’s cloth moved above her head. “You said you would ask.”

“I did say that.”

“Then you lied.”

Ilyan accepted the word because it was the shortest accurate one. “I did.”

Neris set the bowl down. “Do not apologize like a person waiting for me to stop being angry.”

“I am not asking you to stop.”

“Good.” She took a breath. “The guild says if Tamsin gives testimony, the bridge will stay closed. They say the court will use her to make the span unsafe for everyone.”

Before the local petition removed them from the register, Neris had watched an earlier flood obey the old strict count. Registered households crossed while the bridge held; unregistered families waited behind a shuttered stair. She had chosen that rule over a crowd once. The petition had turned the protection she remembered into a permanent exclusion.

“They are afraid of what a child’s account can expose.”

“They are afraid of what it will cost them.”

“Both can be true.”

“That is the first useful thing you have said since the court.”

Ilyan looked at the account. “I can ask the court to mark it withdrawn from future use. The copy already preserved cannot be erased, but it can be marked as not offered by you unless you offer it again.”

Neris’s expression did not soften. “And if the court says the emergency does not allow that?”

“Then I will tell the court the account was entered without consent and ask it not to rely on it.”

“Ask.”

“I will.”

Sera had told him the distinction before they left the workroom. A withdrawal mark affects future use and the status of the docket. It cannot make a preserved record vanish. Ilyan had wanted a clean reset because clean resets were easier to carry. The court’s process would not give him one.

Neris looked at the blackglass tablet on the table. The court notice had followed them, displaying only the steps it was willing to recognize.

**RECORD STATUS: EMERGENCY COPY PRESERVED.**
**FUTURE USE: DISPUTED UNTIL THE AFFECTED WITNESS OFFERS OR REFUSES THE ACCOUNT.**
**HEARING: NO TESTIMONY COMPELLED.**

Tamsin leaned toward the tablet. “It says I can refuse.”

“It says you can refuse,” Ilyan said. “It does not say the court will like it.”

“No.” Tamsin’s fingers touched the blanket seam. “I want to say something.”

Neris looked at her daughter. “You do not have to.”

“I know.” Tamsin’s voice was quiet but firm. “I want to say the bell rang before the bridge moved.”

The shelter went quiet except for the wind tugging at the canvas.

Ilyan did not reach for his notebook. He waited.

Tamsin looked at him. “Not the flood. Not the names. The bell.”

“Why?”

“Because everyone says it rang because the bridge was already unsafe. It rang first. I was on the stair. The rope went tight before the stone dropped.”

Neris closed her eyes. “I heard it too.”

“I know,” Tamsin said. “They are saying I was confused.”

“You were not confused,” Neris said. “You were frightened.”

“Frightened is still knowing.”

Ilyan felt the difference. A fact was not less true because the person who carried it was afraid. A witness was not a clean instrument because the court preferred a simpler account.

“Tamsin,” he said, “the court will hear only what you choose to say. It may ask about other things. I can tell them you have not consented to those questions.”

“Can you make them stop?”

“No.”

Her mouth tightened. “Then I will decide again when they ask.”

“That is allowed.”

Neris looked at her daughter. “I will not let them turn the bell into a performance.”

“It is already a performance,” Tamsin said. “I am only deciding whether I will be in it.”

The words landed harder than Ilyan expected. He had wanted to protect the child from being turned into evidence. Tamsin was telling him that the attempt itself had made her visible whether she spoke or not.

Sera’s notice appeared at the shelter entrance. She stood outside the canvas, not crossing the household’s line. She spoke in Veyran, then gave Ilyan the court’s narrow translation before continuing.

“Court notice,” she said. “The first night field is scheduled to open for one marked bell interval after the hour mark. The field will test the current charter and the maintenance record. Anyone inside the boundary may be asked to step aside. The Ro household is not compelled to attend.”

Neris looked at her. “Will you represent him?”

“No. My provisional representation of Ilyan is suspended until the disclosure is addressed. I am here to explain the notice, not to speak for him.”

Neris looked at the tablet. “Will you represent the household’s request?”

Sera’s gaze moved from Neris to Ilyan. “I can represent the household’s limited access request and keep custody of the disputed account. I will not sponsor Ilyan’s challenge or speak for his report while the breach is open.”

“Will that be enough for the field?” Ilyan asked.

“It is enough to let the household ask for a review. It is not enough to let you claim their refusal as evidence.”

Sera placed a narrow slate on the outer post. “The field requires a claimant and advocate. I am entering as advocate for the household’s request. Ilyan remains the external witness under the accepted filing.”

Ilyan looked at the two women. “I do not want another advocate chosen because of my case.”

“Then do not make it your case,” Sera said. “The household can still withdraw that limited request. If it does, the Bench can hold a public review without a named advocate, but the access request will be harder to state.”

Neris stood. “I want to know what happens to my words.”

Sera’s expression softened by a fraction. “The court will show you the exact copy. It will mark your withdrawal or dispute. It will not allow you to erase what it has already preserved. The flood account will not be used as evidence unless you renew it.”

“And if the court says it needs my account to keep the bridge open?”

“Then it must explain what narrower fact it needs and let you choose whether to give that fact.”

Neris looked at Tamsin. “You can say only the bell.”

“I want to,” Tamsin said.

Sera nodded. “Then the household can offer the bell fact, not the flood history. That offer remains limited.”

Ilyan saw the future shape of the case: Tamsin’s bell, Oren’s load sequence, the copied petition, and the public cost of every repair. It would not be a clean rescue. It would be a field in which people could say no and still be affected by what no meant.

Neris picked up the account. She did not hand it back to Ilyan. She placed it in the inner pocket of her coat.

“I will make the withdrawal mark,” she said. “The words remain yours until I say otherwise. You may read them in the workroom. You may not carry them into a hearing.”

“I understand.”

“Do you?”

“No,” Ilyan said. “But I will.”

Sera looked at the notice. “The field will form around the bridge. If the household attends, Tamsin can enter with Neris or remain outside. There is no honor in being inside. The court may treat the bridge itself as a witness.”

Tamsin’s eyes went to the white cloth on the approach rope. “Will the bell be there?”

“No,” Ilyan said. “The bridgekeeper may ring it. If the field changes the condition, no one can promise what the bell will do.”

Neris turned to him. “You are not coming into our shelter and telling us what to do.”

“No.”

“You may wait at the boundary. If you cross it without permission, the route-arbiter can remove you.”

“That is fair.”

Sera glanced at him. “You accept the boundary.”

“I need to.”

The shelter’s canvas lifted. Beyond it, the court bell sounded once. Not the warning bell at the bridge. A deeper note traveled through the city, and blackglass lines began to appear in the air above the public approach.

They were faint at first, then bright enough to divide the road from the shelter. The hearing field was forming around the ninth bridge, and its boundary reached the edge of the household’s canvas.

Tamsin pulled her blanket over her knees. “I want to go to the edge.”

Neris looked at her. “You do not have to.”

“I know.”

Tamsin looked at Ilyan. “Will the court hear me if I stay here?”

“Only if you offer the fact in the record. The field is at the bridge.”

“Then I’ll go to the edge. Not inside.”

Sera’s gaze moved to the child. “That choice is yours. The field can ask you to step back. You do not have to answer.”

Tamsin nodded. “I know.”

The blackglass boundary completed itself with a sound like a page closing. The next bell had not yet struck, but the field had begun to count toward it.

Ilyan stepped back from the shelter. Sera remained outside the line. Neris put one hand on Tamsin’s shoulder and the other over the pocket containing the account.

The field waited for its named witnesses.

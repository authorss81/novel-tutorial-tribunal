# Chapter 36: What the Household Will Not Say

They had come to the workroom for the district's answer to the hold request, and they got a child instead.

It was the second bell. The count was running on the span, four hands because the levy was gone, and Coll was in the book with Oren's name beside his again, and the court boundary was up because Oren had put it up himself without asking anybody, which was a thing he had discovered he was allowed to do and had been waiting two days to do.

Bevin Larch was at the rope with a notice in his hand and the particular expression of a young man who has been sent to do a job he no longer believes in.

“The district would like the Ro household at the review of the maintenance condition,” he said. “Specifically the household's witness. She is the only person who was under the span when it moved, and the guild's keeper has given a figure and a count, and the district's position is that a figure from a scale is not the same as an account from a person who was there.”

“Who asked for that?” Sera said.

“The office asked for that,” Larch said. “I delivered it.”

“There is no review to attend,” Oren said, from the maintenance line, without turning round. “The matter is consolidated. The review is continued into a docket with no date. You cannot attend a review that has no day.”

“Then the district's position is that the household is being asked to account while there is no proceeding to account into,” Sera said, “which is the Saltwheel sentence again, and I would like it written on the notice so that the family can read it.”

“It is not in the notice,” Larch said.

“Then it is not in the notice,” Sera said, “and the notice asks a child to attend something, and I am the recordwright on this case and I am telling you in front of the rope that the household is not required to attend, and that if the district wishes to be told again it may write to me and I will write back the same sentence.”

Neris Ro was sitting on the workroom step with the household's copy of the register objection in her hand, and Tamsin was beside her with her leg up and her stick across her knees, and neither of them had come to the rope.

“Your mother has nothing to say to the district,” Ilyan said, to Tamsin, and then wished he had not, because she looked at him the way a person looks at a man who has just walked into a room he was not invited into.

“I know what my mother has to say to the district,” Tamsin said. “I have been in the room every time she has said it.”

“I know. I was trying to say that nobody is asking.”

“You were trying to say that the district is asking and my mother is not answering,” Tamsin said, “and those are not the same, and the second one is mine.”

Oren rang the bell before the change and the bell did what it did, and the crew moved, and Ilyan stood at the chalk line with his notebook shut, because that was the arrangement now: he held the book and he did not reach.

When the count came back and the crew stood down, Tamsin got up off the step.

She did it the way she did everything, which was by announcing the fact and not asking, and she came down the workroom steps on the stick and out past the boundary rope, which is a thing a person may do, because a rope on short stakes is a measurement and not a wall, and then she walked out onto the span and stopped at the frame where the warning bell was hung and looked up at it.

The frame was old iron on a stone post at the western approach, higher than her head by a good deal, and the bell itself was about the size of a water bucket, and the rope came down from it in a loop about four feet off the paving, where a grown person could take it in one hand.

Tamsin put her hand on the rope.

Nobody stopped her. Nobody had any power to stop her, and Tarin, who had appeared at the eastern stake without anyone having seen him arrive, watched her hand on the rope and then watched the rope and not the hand, because that was the entire difference between a boundary and a person and he had been practising it for a fortnight.

“This is a rope,” Tamsin said. “It goes over that wheel. The wheel is on a pin. The pin is worn on the underside where nobody can see it, and Uncle Oren says it will last a year and then somebody has to change it, and he has written that down, so I know it.” She did not pull it. “And when somebody pulls it, the wheel turns, and the hammer inside hits the bell, and the bell makes a noise. That is all it does. It does not touch the bridge. It is not attached to the bridge. There is nothing on this bridge that the bell can do.”

She said that last part to the crew, and to Ilsa, and to a count book on a bench, and she said it in the flat voice of somebody describing a bucket.

Ilsa Bray put her hand down off the beam she had been holding.

“The warning bell is a deck warning,” Oren said. “It is rung before a count and after a count, and on a bad load, and at the change. It means *clear the deck and stand off the approach.* It has meant that since before I was in the post.”

“It means *get off*,” Tamsin said. “Not *stop the bridge.* Nobody has ever thought it meant *stop the bridge*, because it is a bell on a post and not a handle on a machine. On the day it moved, it rang, and everybody who was on the span got off, because that is what it means, and they all got off, and it was still going when the last one was over the west stair.”

She turned round and looked at the span, at the pale line a finger's width from the iron ring.

“I was under it,” she said. “I am not going to talk about being under it. What I am going to talk about is that the bell did its job and the bridge did not do its job, and everybody has been telling me for two weeks that the bell is the important thing.”

Somebody at the eastern stake said, “That is the first thing anybody has said about the bell that I have not thought of before.”

“That is because everybody has been asking me what the bell meant,” Tamsin said. “Nobody asked me what it is.”

The workroom got very quiet, and the quiet had a shape to it that Ilyan felt in his chest, because he had asked her what the bell meant in a workroom two weeks ago, in front of a clerk, in order to get a sentence into a record, and she had given him the sentence and refused the assembly, and the sentence had been in the file ever since and it had never once been the useful thing.

He could follow all of it. That was what made it land: the vocabulary he had been taught was load vocabulary and bell vocabulary and rope vocabulary, and for eleven minutes a child had described a piece of municipal machinery to him in a register he could follow completely, and everything she said arrived in his head already in the guild's own words, and the sentence she had given him two weeks ago — the warning bell rang before the bridge moved — turned out to have been about a *sequence* and he had gone looking for a *meaning*.

“Then say it for the book,” Ilyan said, and heard himself say it, and heard Tarin's voice come from the eastern stake before Tamsin had answered.

“No,” Tarin said.

The workroom looked at him.

“You may not put that in the book as a witness to your own theory,” Tarin said. “She is nine or ten years old and you have been attached to a record about a warning bell since the first field, and everything she says will be true and everything she says will be what you needed. That is the one combination I will not hold a rope for.”

“I am not—”

“You were reaching for your book,” Tarin said. “I have watched you do it eleven times this week. You do not have to want to. Your hand goes to it.” He did not move off the stake. “I have no order against it. I have a boundary and a rope and a rule I made for myself, and the rule is that a witness is not a thing a person uses. Say so in front of her mother, in front of her, and then say it again after she has had a night, and then let her decide.”

The workroom was quiet again, and this quiet was not about machinery.

Tamsin looked at Tarin for a while, and then at the rope in her hand, and then at the bell.

“Does he think I am doing it for him?” she asked.

“I think he thinks you are doing it for a bell,” Tarin said. “I do not know which is true and neither does he, and that is exactly why nobody is going to use you today.”

Neris came down the steps then, and she came slowly, and she did not look at Tamsin, and she said the thing that everybody in the workroom had been waiting for and nobody wanted to hear.

“No,” she said.

“Neris—”

“No,” she said again, and it was not louder, and it was more final than anything else that had happened on that approach. “She has answered a room twice. She answered a clerk's notice in a lane. She answered a public count in this workroom because I asked her to and because I thought it would help. I am not sending her to a review that has no day to answer a district that has already written down that she is a party and may be consulted.”

“Neris, the district is asking because the guild's keeper has given a figure and no one can put a person's account beside it.”

“Then let them have the figure,” Neris said. “The figure is true. The figure is worth more than my daughter, and I have thought about that for an hour and I have decided it, and I would like the room to know that a woman thought about it.”

“Mother,” Tamsin said.

“No,” Neris said, and then stopped, and put both hands flat on the step, and looked at the ground for a while.

Ilyan had spent two weeks learning that there were things he could not buy with a good argument. He stood at the chalk line and let the refusal stand and did not reach for his book, and it was the hardest thing he had done in this city, and it was also, he understood, the only thing that made the next ten minutes possible.

Then Neris said, to the ground, “There is a reason and I am not going to say it here.”

“Neris—”

“I am not saying it here because I have said everything I am going to say in front of this room four times and I will not say the fifth thing in front of a rope,” she said. “It is not a secret because nobody has asked me for it. I will tell you, Ilyan, and not them, and not now, and you can do what you like with it.”

She looked up.

“Oren,” she said. “The guild's book. Would you take it?”

Oren had been standing at the maintenance line through all of it without moving, and he took his chalk out of the groove and looked at the board and at the frame and at the child on the span with her hand on the rope.

“Tell me the terms,” he said.

“The guild's book,” Tamsin said. “Not the court's. Not a hall. Nobody writes it for me.”

“The guild's book is the guild's,” Oren said, “and what goes in it is what the guild can stand behind, and a child is not a member of the guild.”

“Not about the guild,” Tamsin said. “About who can be handed it. Nobody can take something back out of the court's book. Everybody can call a guild's book whatever they like and say the guild wrote it for itself.” She said that without any weight, and Ilyan had to put his hand on the bench behind him. “I want it in a book somebody wants, because then it is not mine. If it is in a book that wants it, then nobody can say I made it up, and nobody can say somebody made it up for me, and both of those are going to be said.”

“Terms,” Oren said, in a voice with nothing in it at all.

“Five,” Tamsin said. “One: it is what the machine did, not what it meant. I am not going to say the bell was a warning. I am going to say what it is attached to and what it is not attached to, and what everybody did when it rang. Two: I say it once, in the workroom, where the span is. Three: nobody reads it back to me to make it better. If somebody reads it back, I say *that is not what I said* and it does not go in. Four: I can take it out until it has been read out loud one time. After that it stays, because it is true and I said it, and I am not going to be a person who takes back a true thing because it is inconvenient for her mother. Five—”

She stopped, and looked at her mother, and Neris Ro looked at the ground.

“Five,” Tamsin said, “it may not be used to cure a refusal. If anybody takes what I say and puts it in front of my mother as a reason she should sign something, this is what I said and it does not mean she agreed to anything, and whoever does that has used me and not the bell.”

The workroom was absolutely still.

Oren Sedge took a piece of chalk, looked at the board, and then looked at the board for a long time, and then put the chalk back in the groove.

“I cannot promise you that,” he said.

“Because you do not know what they will do with it.”

“Because I do not know what the district will do with it and neither do you,” Oren said. “I can put a term in my own book. I cannot put a term in the district's book, and I cannot put a term in the court's book, and the moment your account is read out loud in a room in three days, there is nothing whatever stopping a clerk from writing on a piece of paper that a child of the household told the guild about the bell. I have watched that exact thing happen to a road, in another district, and the road is worse for it now than it was before anybody spoke.”

“Then it goes in the guild's book and the guild's book is the only copy that is not a court record,” Tamsin said, “and when they ask for it, the guild says the guild's book and the guild decides, and the guild does not want my mother's signature, so the guild is the only body in this city that can hold that term.”

Which was true. It was true the way a bridge joint is true — a mechanical fact about which part carries which — and Ilyan stood there and watched a ten-year-old work out a custody problem that two districts and a recordwright and a Trialwright and a foreign record-holder had not worked out, and did not open his notebook, because there was nothing to record that was not a fact about a child and not a fact about the law.

“I will put it in the guild's book,” Oren said, “and I will put your fifth term in the guild's book in my own hand, and I will show it to your mother before it is inked, and if she does not agree with it I will not ink it.”

“Agreed,” Neris said, in a voice that had gone somewhere else.

“Agreed,” Tamsin said.

“Then say it,” Oren said, “and nobody will help you, and if you use a word I do not know I will make you say it again in different words, and that is the whole of how a keeper's book works.”

---

It took forty minutes. Tamsin stopped Ilsa once, over a phrase about the deck, with *that is not what I said*, and then Ilsa wrote the phrase out again in the guild's own hand and did not improve it, and read the whole of it back once, cleanly, and did not read it twice.

Written in the guild's own hand, in a keeper's working book, on a page headed with the span and the date and the count that was running at the time:

> *The warning bell on the western frame is a rope over a wheel on a worn pin, and a hammer inside a bell. It is fixed to the post and the rope. It is not fixed to the span, the joint, the brace, the count, or the release. It is rung by a person. When it rang on the day the span moved, the people on the span got off, because that is what it means to them, and they got off, and the span went on. Nobody on the span thought the bell was going to stop the span, because it is a bell and not a handle and it has never been a handle. The bell did what it is for. The span did what it does. There is nothing on this bridge that the bell can do.*

Underneath it, in Oren's chalk, on the board, and in the guild's book in ink:

> *OFFERED BY T. RO, HOUSEHOLD OF THE LOWER ROAD, at the second bell, in the workroom, with the span in view: the above.*
> *READ BACK ONCE BY I. BRAY, KEEPER AND COUNTER, AND NOT CORRECTED.*
> **TERMS SET BY THE WITNESS: (1) an account of a machine, not of a meaning. (2) said once, in the workroom, the span in view. (3) not written for her and not improved. (4) withdrawable until read aloud once; thereafter it stands. (5) it may not be used to cure or support any refusal or acknowledgment, and any use of it that puts it in front of a person as a reason to sign is a misuse of the witness and not evidence of the bell.**
> *THE GUILD ACCEPTS THE ACCOUNT AND THE FIVE TERMS. THE GUILD HAS ITS OWN INTEREST IN THE FIFTH TERM AND IS NOT A NEUTRAL BODY. THE GUILD HAS NO REASON TO WANT THE HOUSEHOLD'S SIGNATURE AND HAS REFUSED A MAINTENANCE ACKNOWLEDGMENT ITSELF.*

Neris witnessed it and did not sign, which was the one thing in the whole business that everybody understood without being told.

And Oren, having read the fifth term twice in his own hand, said the thing that made it worth writing down, out loud, at the bench, for the board:

“A book without a second hand is only a record,” he said. “And the difference between the guild's book and the court's book is that the guild's book has a keeper's hand in it and a counter's hand and now a child's hand, and the court's book has a clerk's hand, and a clerk's hand is a wonderful thing and it is the reason the plate is in a locker instead of on a wall.”

---

Ilyan walked the child back to the lower road at the fourth bell because Tarin would not, and because it was not a thing to be delegated, and because a woman who had said *the figure is worth more than my daughter* four hours ago would not want a route-arbiter walking her down the lane like a shipment.

They went the long way round, because the short way is the span.

Tamsin stopped at the crest of the lower road and looked back at it.

“Your theory,” she said.

“Which one.”

“The one where the bell is the important thing.” She said it without any edge, which was worse than edge. “Everybody in that room has been saying the bell is the reason the bridge case matters, and it is not, and I have been saying so since the first day, and nobody wrote it down because I am not allowed to be the reason for anything.”

“The file says you are not a reason for the bridge.”

“It says I am not a reason,” Tamsin said. “That is not the same. One is a statement about me and one is a statement about the bridge, and the second one is true and the first one is a thing I asked for because I knew what rooms do with children.” She shifted the stick. “You are going to stand in a room in three days and somebody is going to ask you why the ninth bridge matters, and you are not going to be able to say *because of a bell*, because I will not be the reason, and you will not be able to say *because of a clause in a charter*, because you have not read the clause, and so you are going to have to stand there and give a real answer, and I do not know what it is, and I am not going to help you find it.”

“I am not going to ask you to.”

“No,” Tamsin said. “You have not asked me for anything since the rope, and the rope was not for you, and I have been waiting a fortnight to see whether you would notice.”

At the top of the lane she stopped again, at the drain grate, and looked at the frame on the wall where the guild's notice hung above the district's slate and above the register notice Larch had left in the lane for anyone who wanted to read it.

“Do you know what the worst thing about being a witness is?” she said.

“Tell me.”

“Everybody believes you,” Tamsin said. “That is the worst thing. My mother believes my bell. Uncle Oren believes it. And the district will believe it, and then in a year there will be a rule that says a keeper may stop a load if the warning bell has rung, and somebody will write that the rule exists because of me, and the rule will be wrong, because my bell never stopped anything and never will.”

She looked at him.

“So if the rule gets made, somebody has to say what the bell is. And it will have to be somebody who is not me. Do you understand that? It has to be somebody who is not me, or it will be my fault, and it is not my fault that the bell is a rope.”

“Yes,” Ilyan said.

“Say it back.”

“You want the pause clause built on a machine and not on a child,” Ilyan said, “and you want the machine named in a room where you are not.”

“Two things,” Tamsin said. “Because if you only build the machine, then in a year somebody says *a girl reported a failure and nothing was done*, and that is also true, and it is a worse sentence. You have to build both. The thing I saw and the thing nobody did. And you have to do it in a room I am not standing in, or they will look at me instead of at the rope.”

She went down the lane, and the drying line was still up, and the register notice was still on the stones, and the shelter was three rooms and a bench and the place where a child sleeps, and nobody was going to put anything in it ever again, because the district had lost the power to do that, and had lost it for a reason that was a tenant's room and a sworn landlord's book, and would get it back when somebody with a charter decided whether a household with an admitted tenancy was a household.

At the mouth of the lane Ilyan stopped, because there was one more thing and he had earned the right to ask it, and he asked it in the dark with nobody listening, which was cowardice and also the only available hour.

“Neris,” he called.

She was on the step with the register objection in her hand.

“Yes.”

“You said you would tell me the reason and not here.”

“I said not now.”

“It is now,” Ilyan said, “in the sense that there is nobody here. That is all I am asking for. I will not put it in anything.”

Neris looked at him for a while, and then at the lane, and then at the frame on the wall.

“An inspector came to the shelter on the eighth day,” she said. “Not Larch. A woman with a lanyard, and she was pleasant, and she sat on the bench, and she did not ask me one single question about the register or the acknowledgment or the room. She asked whether Tamsin would be willing to say, on the record, that the warning bell had rung and had not been answered.”

“And?”

“And she explained, kindly, that if the child could say the bell was not answered, then the household would not be refusing maintenance. She would be describing a fault in the bridge.” Neris's voice was completely level. “That is what she said. Not in those words. In a kind way, in a way I could repeat word for word, and I have repeated it to myself about forty times since to make sure I heard it right.”

Ilyan stood in the lane and did not say anything for long enough that the drying line creaked twice.

“So they were offering you the bell instead of a signature,” he said.

“They were offering me my daughter,” Neris said. “As a reason. And the worst of it is that if she had said it, she would have said it truthfully, and it would have been true, and it would have cured a refusal that has been costing us for a fortnight.”

She folded the register objection along its crease.

“That is why I will not send her,” she said. “Not because she cannot be trusted with her own account. Because I cannot be in a room with people who have made my daughter's honesty into a door, and I cannot be the one who decides whether she goes through it, and I am not able to be a person who sends her anyway.”

“I understand,” Ilyan said, and understood rather less than he said, because what he had actually taken in was that a woman had been standing in a doorway for a fortnight refusing to be a mechanism, and that he had done the same thing in a workroom in front of forty people, and that the two of them had arrived at the same place from opposite ends of the city.

“Do not comfort me,” Neris said, reading it correctly off his face, which she had got very good at. “Go and be uncomfortable somewhere useful. And if you stand in a room in three days and you need a fact about a machine, you have one now, and it is in a guild's book, and it is not mine and it is not yours, and I would like you to leave it that way.”

The rope of the warning bell hung four feet off the paving on the western approach, and Oren Sedge's crew ran a count on the span behind it every change, and the joint came back to its mark and walked out two fingers and came back, and the pale line sat a finger's width from the iron ring where the bridge had settled into a shape it did not like.

It did not stop. It had never stopped. It was a rope over a worn wheel on a pin, and a hammer, and a bell, and a person, and there was nothing whatever on the ninth bridge that it could do, and the only account of that fact in the whole city was in the handwriting of a ten-year-old, in a keeper's book, under five terms a guild had agreed to in front of her mother.

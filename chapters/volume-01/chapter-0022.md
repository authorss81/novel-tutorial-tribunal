# Chapter 22: A House at the Edge

The rent board on the lower road was nailed outside a door that had been repainted four times and had never once been the same color twice.

It listed eleven rooms, their rents, and the small line of local custom that a room could not be renewed to a household whose crossing record was provisional. Hanne Kolm kept the board and the keys and had told Ilyan, before he had gotten two steps past the shelter pole, that the recordwright was expected and the bridge man was not.

“He can stand there,” she said, pointing at a patch of paving with a drain grate in it. “He cannot come inside. I have four rooms with unlisted children in them and the district has been asking questions all week about how many beds I have.”

“I am not here to count beds,” Ilyan said.

“You are here because your name is on a piece of paper and everybody who knocks comes asking after that name.” She turned the key over once. “That is not the same as wanting you in my doorway. Sit on the grate. It is the only warm place on this road and I am not paying for it.”

The Ro household’s shelter stood at the end of a short lane off the lower road, three rooms and a drying line, built against the back of an old dye-store wall so the wind could not get at the door. Neris was folding a work coat when Ilyan and Sera came up the lane, and Tamsin was sitting on an upturned pail with her leg on a folded blanket and a stick in one hand, tapping the rhythm of the bridge bell against the pail rim without noticing that she was doing it.

The notice came by hand before Ilyan had finished explaining why he was sitting on a drain grate. It was a district summons on thin gray paper, folded twice, and it was addressed to Tamsin Ro in language that treated her as a resource.

**THE MINUTE OF THE WARNING BELL IS OFFERED AS WITNESS. THE NINTH-BRIDGE STAY IS ATTACHED TO THAT WITNESS. YOUR ATTENDANCE IS REQUESTED AT THE LOWER WARD ASSEMBLY, THIS AFTERNOON.**

Tamsin read it without asking anyone to read it to her. She read it slowly, twice, and then she handed it to her mother instead of to Ilyan, which Ilyan noticed and did not comment on.

Neris read it standing up.

“They want the bell again,” she said. “In front of the assembly. Because the bell is the one thing that makes the bridge case look necessary, and the bridge case is the one thing that makes the stay look permanent.”

“The witness has already offered the bell once,” Sera said. “It is on the record. The fact does not become stronger because it is said in a hall.”

“It becomes *legible* in a hall.” Neris folded the summons along its own creases. “That is what they actually want. A fact in a record is a fact a clerk can weigh. A fact in a hall is a fact that holds a room.”

Ilyan heard the distinction and did not argue with it, because it was correct and because arguing with it would have meant telling a mother that her daughter’s consent was a public resource.

Tamsin tapped her stick against the pail twice more, then stopped.

“I will say the bell,” she said. “Once. In the workroom, to the people who work there, where they can see the span while I am talking about it.”

Neris turned. “You do not have to say it at all.”

“I know.” Tamsin looked at the drain grate, then at the lane. “But yesterday the bell rang and the bridge did not stop. Uncle Oren said he can ring it and he cannot make it stop. If I say the bell was working before the bridge moved, and he rings it and the bridge does not stop, then people will say the bell was working for me and not for him. They will say it was a child’s fact. They will use my one thing to make the bell stop meaning what it means to the people holding the iron.”

The lane went quiet enough to hear the dye-store wall ticking as it cooled.

“So say it where it is useful,” Ilyan said. “Not where it is wanted.”

“Not in the assembly,” Tamsin said. “And not on a paper somebody else writes.”

Neris watched her daughter for a long moment. “Then I will not say anything at the assembly either, and I will tell them why, and the reason will not be a secret. The reason is that I am her mother and the account is mine to refuse.”

“Why?” Sera asked.

“Because they will use the two of us as a single household with a single position.” Neris sat down on the shelter step, which put her below Tamsin for the first time all morning. “The ward already learned what happens when a whole street is treated as one voice. Mava refuses for three generations of Rusk and they write *the ward refuses* and it sounds like a mob. I will not let my house be a ward with one door.”

Sera inclined her head slightly, which from her was a considerable concession.

Hanne Kolm arrived while they were still standing in the lane, and she did not come alone. Behind her was a district clerk with a schedule roll under one arm, and Ilyan recognized the seal before he recognized the man: newer than the district’s ordinary marks, unfamiliar, and carried with the settled confidence of someone who had entered an instruction at three in the morning and slept perfectly well afterward.

Meridith Vale had come up the lower road with a short leather case and the flat unhurried manner of a man who believed the paperwork was ahead of him.

“Kolm,” the clerk said. “I need the shelter counted.”

“For what?”

“Materials store. Three days, during the bridge count.”

Hanne Kolm put a hand on her ring of keys. “That is a dwelling.”

“It is a dwelling with a provisional occupancy,” Vale said. “The district maintenance schedule can hold materials in a structure whose occupancy is provisional, because there is no recognized tenancy to protect. I do not need the household’s consent for the ground floor. I need a count of what is on the ground floor.”

The three of them looked at the shelter. The ground floor was a drying room, a bench, and the place where Tamsin slept.

“If it is a materials store, the drying room is a materials store,” Neris said.

“Yes.”

“And the bench is where a bench is.”

“Yes.”

“And my daughter sleeps under a tarp in a room you are going to fill with lime sacks.”

Vale did not look at Tamsin. He had the professional stillness of a man who understood that looking at a child while explaining an administrative advantage is how a record acquires a witness. “I am describing the category the schedule needs. The household can be moved for the three days. I am not saying where.”

“You are saying where,” Sera said. “You are saying the only place they can go is somewhere with no address, because a household with no recognized address cannot be given a notice about its own removal.”

Vale glanced at her and did not deny it.

Ilyan stood up off the drain grate. The old move in him was to reach for a sentence that would make the district’s schedule unlawful, and he had that sentence, and it would have been very short, and it would have destroyed three other people’s morning while doing it.

He said, instead, to Sera: “Four records.”

Sera was already reaching for her slate. “Say them.”

“Access. The household’s crossing hours, the repaired load path, the review date. That goes to the bridge and the court. It is the one record where the stay already applies.”

“And it is the one record Meridith Vale’s schedule was written to override,” Vale said mildly. “A crossing that is provisional is a crossing that can be recorded as support. There is nothing improper in counting a household’s presence.”

Hanne Kolm made a small sound like a cough.

“Second,” Ilyan said. “Housing. The room on the lower road, the rent, the lease, the key. That is a landlord record and it goes to the landlord and to the Bench, not to the district. A district has no standing to say whether a family may keep a room.”

“The district pays part of that room’s repair,” Vale said.

“Then the district gets a line saying what it paid for. It does not get a key.”

Hanne Kolm looked at Ilyan with something that was not yet trust and was more than she had offered him at the door. “I have a book,” she said. “Every room, every name, every month. It is not a legal record. It is a landlord’s book. I have never shown it to a district clerk.”

“Do not show it to this one,” Ilyan said.

Vale’s mouth moved a fraction. “I am not here to take the book. I am here because the schedule expires at dusk and I would rather enter it truthfully than leave a blank.”

“Enter it truthfully and it will be refused,” Sera said. “That is also a true entry.”

“Third,” Ilyan went on, ignoring him. “Work. Neris’s shift at the dye-yard, her tally hours, her foreman’s record. That is an employment record. It goes to the dye-yard and to the district employment desk. It is not testimony, it is not access, and nobody in this lane has the right to read it to prove a point about a bridge.”

Neris said, “The foreman has already written to me.” She took the letter out of her coat and handed it over, because she had read it four times and had stopped being able to see what was on the page. It was employment register, and employment register was on Oren’s sheet, so the hours and the post and the word for provisional came to Ilyan without effort. The sentence that mattered was the shortest one on the page, and it was in the dye-yard’s own hand, where nobody had thought to simplify anything.

“What did he say?”

“He said my hours were being reassigned because my crossing is provisional and he cannot promise a quarter-bell start to a woman who may not be on the span at a quarter bell.” She said it without heat, which made it worse. “That is a real problem. It is not a public one. If you put it in the record as a public fact, the dye-yard will find a reason to be afraid of the record.”

“Then it stays a work fact,” Ilyan said, “and we say plainly that the reassignment follows from the crossing record, so that if it is challenged later, the cause is on paper.”

Neris nodded once. “Yes. That is what I would have said.”

“Fourth,” Sera said, taking it over, because she had been watching where the argument was actually going. “Testimony. Four people, four choices, four limits. Tamsin offers the bell, once, in the workroom, and says where. You offer your hours, your route, and your room. I offer the register removal and what was written in it. Ilyan offers the sequence he observed and nothing about what it means for you. Nobody’s testimony is attached to anybody else’s, and no one gets to be a household instead of a person.”

Vale listened to all of it with his case under his arm and then said the thing that made the shelter lane go cold.

“That is four records about a household the district cannot serve notice on,” he said. “Which means when the materials store expires at dusk, none of the four will be there. And by the end of the week the ground floor will be full of lime sacks and a tarp, and the record will show four people who were each individually answered.”

He lifted the roll a little.

“You have made four doors where there was one locked gate. The gate at least knew it was a gate.”

Nobody answered, because it was true, and because the true thing about a bad record is almost always that it is simpler.

Neris stood. “Then record what the district is proposing to do to us while all four doors are open. Materials store, three days, provisional occupancy, no address. Put that in the schedule you are going to enter, with the seal on it, so that when the ground floor is full of lime, the reason is written down and not remembered later.”

Vale paused. It was, Ilyan understood, the first moment in the conversation at which he had been asked to do the thing he was actually good at: write down the true reason, under his own name, in his own hand, on a record that could not be quietly retyped later.

“It is already on the roll,” he said. “The ground-floor structure, three days, provisional occupancy, no recognized tenancy. The district does not hide it. The district simply does not treat it as a burden, because the person it falls on has no recognized tenancy to be burdened in.”

Hanne Kolm laughed once, without humor. “There it is. That is the whole of it, and you said it in one sentence without needing a witness.”

Sera’s stylus was already moving. She wrote the proposal into the access record, the housing record, and the work record simultaneously, and then stopped, and struck the work line out, and left it in the housing one. Neris watched her do it and did not ask why.

Ilyan went down to the workroom at the midday bell, where the wage ledger for the bridge levy was kept on a nail because nails were cheaper than cases. A guild apprentice let him read it under Oren’s standing instruction, which was that anyone could read it and only three named people could write in it.

The ledger was arranged by day, and each day carried four columns: hands, hours, materials, and unlisted support. Oren had chalked the fourth column two days earlier in irritation and never had the heart to remove it, because it was how the district’s schedule reached the guild’s books.

Most entries in the fourth column were material: **cart turn, 2 hands, district**, **clerk, 1 hand, district**, **record hand, half, district**.

Then, four nights back, on the night the stay was entered into the district’s book, a line that Ilyan read three times before he understood what he was looking at.

**RO HH — PRESENT. CROSSING HOURS 1st BELL TO DUSK, WALKING LOAD. ½ HAND PER CROSSING, DAILY. CREDIT TO WAGE BILL.**

Underneath, in the guild apprentice’s copying hand and then in a second, smaller hand, the entry had been matched against the district schedule by number. The number was a district form number and not a guild line, which meant it had come off a sheet printed once and issued to more than one office. It meant Vale could match a line here that had been handed to him in a lane eleven streets away, without the line ever having been his line in the first place. And the hand that had matched it was the same unhurried, pressed hand that had stood in the shelter lane and explained the ground floor without once looking at a sleeping child.

Ilyan stood in the workroom holding a ledger he had no right to alter, and the arithmetic was simple and complete. The district had not forged a signature. It had not even needed a form. Neris Ro had crossed a bridge on her way to work, and somewhere between the ninth bridge and a schedule roll a half-hand had been counted out of the guild’s wage bill, and the household had been paying, in its own footsteps, toward a maintenance burden it had refused in front of the Bench and would refuse again in front of anyone.

In Merefen, a street had been made to sign a road by being left no alternative. Here, a household had been made to pay for a bridge by continuing to go to work.

The entry was lawful. The wage bill had genuinely come out smaller. The guild had genuinely been relieved of half a hand it could not spare. And the Ro household now owed, in a column of a public ledger, a maintenance contribution it had never chosen, on the strength of its own daily obedience to a schedule that governed somebody else’s hours.

Ilyan did not touch the page. He copied the schedule number, the date, the hand, and the four words *unlisted support* into his notebook, and then he closed it, because the man who could change that column was not the man holding the notebook, and the man who had written it was still on the lower road with his roll and his seal.

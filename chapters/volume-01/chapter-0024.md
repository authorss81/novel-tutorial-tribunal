# Chapter 24: The Assembly’s Limited Word

The Lower Ward Assembly met in a rented cloth hall off the fish-market lane, and the hall had been chosen for its acoustics rather than its dignity.

Sound behaved oddly in there. A voice raised at the back arrived at the front a half beat late, which meant that anyone who wanted to be heard by the whole room had to stand in the middle aisle and project, and anyone who wanted a sentence to die before it reached the benches had only to keep talking. Three cart owners discovered this within the first ten minutes and immediately became a choir.

Ilyan stood at the middle aisle with Sera on his left and Oren three rows back with the guild’s wage notice rolled in his hands. Maren Kett came in late, because Maren Kett always came in late, and took a seat on the eastern bench without hurrying, and placed a folded paper on the clerk’s table in full view.

Sera asked to read it before he spoke. The clerk hesitated. Maren nodded.

“It is a mandate,” he said. “Read it.”

Sera read it aloud in Veyran and then in English for the room, which she did without being asked, because the room contained people who could not follow either.

**MANDATE OF THE EASTERN DISTRICT REPRESENTATIVE, READ INTO THE HALL BEFORE ARGUMENT: to oppose any local body that treats the ninth-bridge stay, or the name attached to it, as authority to schedule work, levy a burden, or compel an acknowledgment; to require that any burden fall on the person whose labor or property carries it; to require that such rule be legible to any clerk at any hour.**

Sera folded it. “It is narrower than his reputation.”

“It is longer than his patience,” Ilyan said.

The hall did not settle. It had four visible interests in it and no interest in becoming one. Cart owners wanted the bridge and the road open. River-ward residents came to stop the road being closed by a name, and every one of them lived or worked within an hour’s walk of a span that might not be there in February. The guild wanted its labor paid and its liability finite. And along the back wall, in a block that had arrived together and would leave together, sat people who had read the Saltwheel extract and the ninth-bridge stay in the same week and had drawn the conclusion that if one household could hold a bridge open, every household in every district could hold every road open, and that this would be the end of roads.

Maren stood when the clerk called the single question.

“The assembly can answer one question,” the clerk said, reading from a slate that had been wiped and rewritten so often the corners were furred. “One. The rest stands where it stood.”

A formal notice opened above the middle aisle and translated itself into Ilyan’s sight.

**THE QUESTION BEFORE THIS ASSEMBLY: MAY THE ATTACHED NAME BE CITED AS AUTHORITY FOR LOCAL MAINTENANCE WORK?**
**THE ASSEMBLY MAY ANSWER YES, NO, OR ANSWER ONE PART AND DEFER THE REST.**
**ANSWERING THIS QUESTION DOES NOT AMEND THE NINTH-BRIDGE CHARTER.**

Ilyan read it twice. There was no rule about refusal, no exit line, no burden named anywhere in it, and the whole instrument was built for exactly the kind of answer he was good at producing.

He had also spent a week learning what that answer cost, and he decided to let the hall ask before he offered one.

“Answer the question first,” he said. “Then hear what the answer does not cover.”

A cart owner near the front said what she wanted covered. “If the name is not authority, the district has to name a person. I do not care which person. I do not care if it is the keeper or a clerk or the man from the foreign country. I care that somebody whose name I know is the one who decides when my cart does not cross.”

“That is the whole of my request for the ninth bridge,” Ilyan said, “and I am not asking the assembly to make it a rule for the lowland conservancy, which is nine cart owners with a copied sentence and no meeting minutes.”

Maren turned. “I am asking the assembly to make it legible everywhere. You keep offering a bridge. Nobody outside that bridge has ever heard of it, and the people who have heard of it have heard your name.”

“Then make it legible about the name,” Ilyan said. “Say what my name can be used for. Say what it cannot.”

Maren sat down, which from him was a concession, and the river-ward block at the back became louder.

The first speaker from that block was a woman with a delivery board under her arm.

“You have a house that cannot keep its rent because your crossing is provisional,” she said, to Ilyan, not to the hall. “You have a guild that cannot stop a cart because your bridge cannot stop a cart. And you want the assembly to decide what your name means. How is that different from the district deciding what our refusal means?”

“It is different,” Ilyan said, “because the district decided your refusal without asking, and this hall is being asked.”

“No, this hall is being asked to agree with you.”

“Yes,” he said. “That is what you are being asked.”

A man at the front said the thing that had been waiting behind everything else all morning. “If one unlisted household can use a bridge without acknowledging maintenance, and that household can refuse without losing the room or the work, then what is left to give the Cinder Ward, and what is left to give a dye-yard whose workers cross at a quarter bell? The rule has to apply to somebody or it is a favor, and favors are how districts go broke.”

“The rule applies to the work,” Ilyan said. “The work has a name, hours, a wage, and an end. The person refusing it does not become the work.”

“Then the person refusing it becomes the danger,” the man said, “and my lime sits on the eastern stones until the Bench decides.”

“The lime can wait one bell if the work is finite. If the work is not finite, the danger is the work.”

Maren stood again. “That is a good sentence. It is not a rule. Any clerk in any district can agree with it and then write something else at three in the morning, and the person affected will not know until the cart does not come.”

He turned to the clerk’s table and put two fingers on his mandate.

“My mandate asks for one legible rule: the burden falls on the person whose labor or property carries it. That is a rule a night clerk can apply without an advocate and without a hearing. It is what I have wanted since I read your stay and found it had a bridge in it and a review date and a named crew. If the stay had said *the burden falls on the person whose labor carries it*, four districts would not be arguing this week.”

Sera stood. “Your rule tells a clerk who pays. It does not tell a person that they may say no.”

“It tells a person that the district may not pay for them,” Maren replied. “That is not nothing.”

“It is a good half.” Sera did not soften it. “It is the half that makes the road safe. It is not the half that makes the refusal survivable. If the rule has no line about what happens to the person who refuses, then the answer to refusal is invented locally, by whoever is nearest, and you have just described a district that invents answers about roads and a ward that invents answers about water. You have built a rule with a floor and no roof and called it common.”

“The assembly can add a roof.”

“The assembly can add a roof. It cannot add a roof for every district from this hall, and if it tries, the roof becomes the next copied sentence and we are back at the fish-market lane with a worse piece of paper.”

Ilyan felt the sentence arriving. He had been carrying it since Merefen, out of the back of a cart at four in the morning, and it was the correct sentence, and it was the largest true thing he had learned in this city.

**No acknowledgment may be inferred from continued use.**

It was the sentence that described the Ro household’s half-hand in the wage bill, and the Cinder Ward’s shelter delivery board, and the ward’s labor turned into consent, and a week of everything. It was true. It was also, as he understood perfectly well while the hall watched him, a universal rule proposed by a man whose legal standing was limited to one bridge in one district, about four other districts he had never seen, on the strength of a notebook.

He opened his mouth.

Sera did not raise her voice. She put two fingers on the front of his notebook, not touching his hand, and looked at him the way she had looked at him at the archive desk a week earlier, with a request on the table and his name nowhere in it.

“No,” she said, in the middle aisle, in front of the hall.

The hall went quiet in the way that halls do when something they did not expect happens in the aisle.

“Your standing is limited to the ninth-bridge case,” Sera said. “You have not been to the lowland conservancy. You have not read their charter. You have never seen a cart that has been turned back at their road, and you do not know whether they are inferring acknowledgment from use or whether their register is simply silent. If you put that sentence into this hall as a general rule, the first clerk who adopts it will be somebody who wants a weapon, and the ward will be asked to defend a rule that no resident of this ward voted for, in a hall where a rule is made to look like a decision of the people.”

“I am not offering it as a general rule,” Ilyan said. “I am offering it as a condition on the case I am attached to.”

“Then say the condition and its limit, and stop reaching for the sentence that would let you not be wrong in six districts at once.” She did not take her fingers off the notebook. “Ask for the ninth bridge’s work slate. Ask for the wage column. Ask that the household’s half-hand come out of the bill and that the refusal be entered as a refusal. That is four facts and you have all four, and it can be decided here, today, by people who were in the lane this morning.”

“And the rest of it waits.”

“The rest of it waits,” Sera said, “and if waiting costs somebody, we write down who waited and how long, which is more than your sentence would have done.”

The river-ward block was grinning. It was not a kind expression. It was the expression of people who had been waiting a long time to see an advocate stop somebody and had begun to think it would not happen in a hall.

Ilyan stepped back half a step, which cost him more than anything else in the room, and looked at the clerk’s table, and asked for what he could actually support.

The clerk wrote: *no acknowledgment may be inferred for the ninth-bridge stay; the stay’s labor, hours, materials, and household access to be recorded as named entries; the Ro household’s crossing not to be credited as maintenance labor; the charter not amended.*

Then he asked for the one thing he still wanted, and it was the thing he had come in with.

“The ground floor,” he said. “The materials store. The district’s own roll says *provisional occupancy, no recognized tenancy*, and it would take the drying room and the place a child sleeps for three days. I am asking this assembly to hold that, so the district cannot enter it while the household’s tenure is provisional.”

The clerk read the request back, and the hall waited, and the refusal came from the eastern bench in a form Ilyan had to listen to twice.

“No hold,” Maren said. “And I want the reason read out.”

He had the mandate open on the table in front of him and he was not reading from it.

“In the Saltwheel matter,” he said, “the attached record-holder asked the Bench to hold local enforcement while the record was preserved. The Bench refused. It preserved the levy ledger entry by entry and it declined to hold the levy, and it said the difference was on the record. The Bench said the record is not a protection. That was the week before last, in a matter where the same name was attached and the same person asked.”

He looked at Ilyan.

“I am not doing this to be cruel. I am doing it because a rule that you can invoke for a household and not for a district is not a rule, it is a favor you can carry, and the moment somebody with a seal and a bad week decides to use it against the road, we will all be back in this hall arguing about whether the record is a protection. You have lost that once in front of a public body and the losing is still doing work in Merefen tonight. I am not going to pretend the Bench said something it did not say.”

The hall did not move.

“So the district may take a dwelling because the people in it are provisional,” a ward woman said.

“For three days, with materials named, with a count before and after, and with the district paying the household for the use of the ground floor,” Maren said. “The district is offering that now, and the household may refuse it, and refusing it will cost them the ground floor, and I will say that plainly because I would rather be disliked than be the man who hid it in a clause.”

Neris, standing at the back with Tamsin beside her, said, “You have never been disliked. You have been agreed with by people who wanted someone else to be the villain.”

“I have been agreed with,” Maren said. “It is worse.”

The clerk called for the single word.

The assembly’s answer came out of the middle aisle, folded, and smaller than a victory, which Ilyan had been warned it would be by almost everyone who had ever seen an assembly answer anything.

The limited word had three parts.

**THE ATTACHED NAME MAY BE CITED AS A RECORD OF WHAT WAS FOUND.**
**THE ATTACHED NAME MAY NOT BE CITED AS A PERMIT, A SCHEDULE, A LEVY, OR AN ACKNOWLEDGMENT, AND MAY NOT BE ENTERED AS THE BEARER OF LABOR.**
**THE ASSEMBLY REQUIRES THE ATTACHED RECORD-HOLDER TO GIVE A PUBLIC ACCOUNT OF HIS FIRST TESTIMONY AT THE NINTH BRIDGE, IN THIS HALL, IN THREE DAYS.**

The hall’s own clerk read the last line twice before he read it at all, and then he put his stylus down on the table in a way that was not accidental.

“The assembly was asked one question,” Moot said, “and the question was whether a name may be cited as authority. I did not ask to be given a second power, and that is a power, and I would like the hall to know that I read it and that I think it is a gift rather than a rule.”

“It is a cost,” Maren said. “If the name is a record, the record has to be able to answer for itself where people who lost things can hear it. Gifts are what the hall gives people. I have spent four days giving them.”

“Then let the record show who asked for it,” Moot said, and wrote his own name in the margin, which was more than he had to do and cost him something he would think about later.

Sera read the assembly’s answer aloud. Ilyan read it above her head at the same time, which he was not supposed to do, and did not stop.

The second part was a lock. He had asked the hall to say what his name could be used for, and the hall had answered by taking away the thing he had been using. From the moment the word was read he was a name in a record and could not touch a slate, a schedule, a levy, or an acknowledgment, and the standing of that would outlast the examination by as long as the assembly cared to keep the clause, and every district in the region that had wanted to point at him would now have a document explaining that pointing at him was worthless. Maren’s district would be free of his name in one week. The lowland conservancy would find it had been citing an ornament.

The third part was worse, because it was not a lock. It was an examination.

“Account of his first testimony,” the clerk repeated. “At the ninth bridge. In this hall.”

“In three days,” Maren said. “He has spent a week telling this region what the record says. He has never stood in a room and been asked what he did, in order, and who paid for it. If his attached name is to be a record rather than a permit, then the record has to be able to answer for itself in a room where people who lost things can hear it.”

Neris’s head came around. “He is not the only person who spoke at the bridge.”

“No,” Maren said. “But he is the only one whose name is on the finding. A record that cannot be examined is a permit with better manners.”

Ilyan looked at the hall. The cart owners had stopped being a choir. Two of them were looking at the floor. The river-ward block was watching him with an expression he could not read and did not want to guess at. Oren, three rows back, had unrolled the wage notice and was reading it again with the air of a man who had already decided what he thought and was checking the numbers.

He had asked for a legible rule about his name. He had received a legible rule, an examination, and a refusal in the same afternoon, and the refusal was the one he had earned by asking for the wrong thing the week before in a different room.

“Yes,” Ilyan said.

The clerk wrote it down.

Nobody cheered. It was, in the way that mattered, the first time in a week that a public room had responded to his name with a task instead of a claim. He found that he did not know what to do with his hands, and that the not-knowing felt more like being in a room than anything he had been given before.

As the hall emptied, a clerk came up the aisle with a slip.

“Public account, three days, in this hall,” she said. “It requires a notice to the bridge, the guild, the household, and the ward seats. And the ward seats are empty, so if the ward wishes to attend, the assembly is asking the ward to send somebody to sit on a bench that does not exist.”

She said it as a formality and then, unexpectedly, as a fact.

“Which means they have never resolved the address question at all,” Ilyan said.

“No,” she said. “They have never resolved it, and they have had a petition box collecting answers since the ward was created, and the answers go into a drawer.”

Sera took the slip and read it twice.

“Then the examination will have a room with one empty bench in it,” she said, “and the district will point at the empty bench, and the ward will point at the drawer, and neither of them will be able to explain what the bridge has to do with either.”

“Three days,” Ilyan said.

“Three days,” Sera agreed, and folded the slip, and did not offer him anything else, which was the correct professional thing to do and also the exact thing he most wanted from her and would not have accepted.

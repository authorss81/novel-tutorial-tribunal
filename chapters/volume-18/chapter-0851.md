# Chapter 851: Sixth Day Of The Hundred And Forty-Third Week, And A Sunday, And A Man Of Fifty-Four Who Cannot Read Enters Today’s Line On A Printed Leaf And Says The Day Is Not In His Hand, And Four Inches Over The Top Step And A Fourth Thing Withheld Until Monday, And A Man Of Thirty-One Says Out Loud He Wants The First Line Ever On The Ninth Line And Takes It Back, And A Boy Of Thirteen Says One Is A Figure Of A Day, Not A Week

Sixth day of the hundred and forty-third week. His six hundred and first morning. Four hundred and fifty-one days after the settlement. The fever eighty-one weeks and one day old. Four hundred and five days since the division. Three hundred and fifty-one days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

At about the sixth hour a man of fifty-four put a line on a leaf, and the day was printed at the head of the leaf, and he could not read what was printed there.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four. He holds the gravel there, he cannot read, and he has asked for a day against the ninth line for a hundred and thirty mornings. The book came up the cart road four miles from the bakehouse on Saturday. It cost him fourpence, and he has said the fourpence out loud twice this morning so that nobody puts it in a book as a gift.

It is a day-book. A leaf for every day and no more nor fewer than that, with the day printed at the head of each leaf by somebody who was not here. He found today’s leaf with a thumbnail, put four strokes on it, and then put a figure he could not write, because there is no way to write a figure and only read one.

“*Four strokes and a figure. That is four barrows, and one that was half a barrow, and the half is a figure of a barrow and not a figure of a day.*”

“*I am not going to pretend those are the same thing, and I am not going to pretend that the two of them are worth the same either.*”

“*And I want about nine of you to notice what I have just said. I have been saying gravel figures out loud on this bank since the day I came, and nobody has ever written one of them on a leaf.*”

“*And I cannot read the top of this leaf. The day is printed there, and it is the right day, because a boy of thirteen read it to me twice, and it is not in my hand.*”

Then a boy of thirteen read the printed day at the head of that leaf out loud, twice. He gave the figure for his own days, and then the small thing he has been holding since Saturday.

Wat Marshe is thirteen and has been in this county a hundred and ninety-eight days, counted in the lane before the light came up. A hundred and ninety-eight is eight hundred and fifty-one less six hundred and fifty-three.

“*A hundred and ninety-eight days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.*”

“*The day at the head of that leaf says Sunday. The day outside says Sunday. Both of them are right, and only one of them is in his hand.*”

“*And here is the small thing. Four is a figure of barrows, and one is a figure of a day, and if he had put six there this morning it would still have been a figure of a day and still a figure of nothing else.*”

“*So I am not going to let about four of you add his four to my one and call the result five. It is a figure of a day and not of a week, and I have never counted a week in my life, and I do not know how.*”

At about the seventh hour the man of forty-three gave the water, said what he was not going to give, and gave the morning he was going to give it on, so that nobody would spend four days asking him for a thing he has already promised a day for.

Corvin Tace is forty-three and has kept that landing nineteen years, and gives three things every morning. The fourth thing is a figure of how many of those ninety steps are under. On Saturday he said out loud, for the first time in eighteen days, that he would begin again on Monday. This is not Monday.

“*About four inches over the top step at the seventh hour, and it is seven days of the coming back, and the next time about ninety of those steps can be walked is in seven days.*”

“*And I am not giving the fourth thing this morning, and I am not going to be asked for it in about nine of your ears until tomorrow, and I said on Saturday that I would give it on Monday, and I meant Monday.*”

“*A man of thirty-one asked me on Friday why I would not say it today, and I told him the truth, which is that a figure I do not have is worse on a Sunday than it is on a Saturday, and that I have four days to get used to saying a thing again.*”

“*That is the whole of the reason. It is not a good one, and I would rather give you a bad reason than a good invented one.*”

Then a man of fifty-four asked for a day against the ninth line for the hundred and thirtieth morning, and a man of thirty-one told him what the ninth line is, and it was a thing Barnaby Crove had not been told in eleven years of asking.

“*A hundred and thirty. One more than the morning before, and it is eight hundred and fifty-one less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one, and I cannot do that one out in my head.*”

“*I do it every morning anyway. I have never once got it wrong, and I would like somebody to notice that before I say the rest of it.*”

Ilyan Vester is thirty-one and is nobody’s, and has been in this county two hundred and ninety-seven days, two hundred and ninety-seven being eight hundred and fifty-one less five hundred and fifty-four. The cut across that palm is a hundred and sixty-six days old, a hundred and sixty-six being eight hundred and fifty-one less six hundred and eighty-five.

“*It is a run of days. That is the whole of what it is. It has a leaf for every day, and it is four hundred miles off, in a room I have never been in.*”

“*And there is not one line entered on one of those leaves, not on any of them, and there never has been.*”

“*And here is the part I have had to say out loud twice before I could say it here. There is nothing missing from it. No leaf torn out, no page cut out and used. It is whole, and every day it covers is on it, and nobody has ever written on one.*”

“*You have asked for eleven years for a day to go down on a thing that is not damaged, and I am not saying that to be unkind. I am saying it because you are the only person on this bank who cannot read, and the only person on this bank who would have believed a page had been cut out.*”

Barnaby Crove heard it, and did not take it as an answer, and did not take it as a loss either, and said the thing he had come down off the road to say, which is that a whole book is worse than a broken one when you are the one who has to write on it.

“*I have been eleven years asking for a day against that line, and I have been asking it as if something had been taken off it.*”

“*And you are telling me nothing has been taken off it. Then the day I have been asking for is a day nobody has ever put down, on a book that is not broken, and that is a different thing to be unhappy about, and I have not got the right word for it yet.*”

“*And I am not going to use a wrong one to be quick. I am going to ask on Monday, and I am going to go on asking until somebody writes one.*”

“*And if the first one is ever written, it is going to be written by whoever holds the pen. About four of you have just heard me say that, and about four of you were not going to hear it, and that is the correct number of people to not hear a thing like that said.*”

Then a man of thirty-one said the want he had, in the ordinary voice and in about nine ears, and a man of fifty-four gave him the working of why it could not be done, and the man of thirty-one took it back in the same breath he had said it in.

“*I am going to say a want, and it is mine, and it is about me, and I would rather say it badly than not say it before a Sunday.*”

“*I want to put the first line on the ninth line. Not write on it. The first one. Ever. Because there has not been one, and there is a morning coming in which there will not have been one, and I do not want to be a man who was standing here for that and said nothing.*”

Barnaby Crove, fifty-four, who cannot read, put the leaf down on the dry stone, gave the working of it out loud, and said it was not a judgment and that nobody had asked him to make one.

“*No, and I am not doing it because I have got a hold on it. I am doing it because you have just told me that book is whole, and a whole book that nobody has written on does not want a man of thirty-one in the front of it.*”

“*You put one line on it, and then it is a book with one line in it, and that line is yours, and in about four weeks, when somebody four hundred miles off asks who has been writing on the ninth line, the answer will be a man of thirty-one who is nobody’s.*”

“*And that answer will be true, and it will be the only true thing anybody can say about that run. I have kept gravel in chalk on a post for nineteen years, and the reason nobody has ever had a true thing to say about that post is that I have never put a pen near it.*”

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, withdrew it in one breath, in about nine ears, and said a man of fifty-four had taught him a thing about that on Sunday morning and had taught it to him in a better shape than the one he had just used.

“*Then I withdraw it, in the shape you gave it to me in, and not in a better shape of my own.*”

“*I hold no seat, I am nobody’s, and I refused one on the Tuesday of this week because a man who keeps the sheets is a man whose handwriting is on them, and this morning I asked for the first line on the ninth line, which is the same want from the other side of the same door.*”

“*And I am not going to be thanked for noticing it late. About four of you now know that I want a book to write in, and that is a fact about me and not a thing I did this morning, and I would rather it was said out loud than worked out.*”

Then the man of thirty-one gave a figure he could not stand behind, a boy of thirteen told him what was wrong with it in one sentence, and he took the figure back before he had finished explaining it.

“*And while I am standing here being honest, I am going to say how long that ninth line has been empty, which is about eleven years.*”

Wat Marshe, thirteen, did not wait for the end of the sentence. He said what was wrong with the figure: a figure of years with no day on the end of it is not a figure of anybody.

“*Eleven is a figure of years, and you have not said a day. If you had said a day, then I could have counted from it on this bank, and I would have been able to say whether you were right.*”

“*And a figure of years that nobody can count from is the same as no figure. I do not know whether I worked that out or whether I only noticed it this morning.*”

“*I know it is a true thing about this leaf. The day is printed at the top of it, and he cannot read it, and I can.*”

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, said the figure was gone and that he was not going to put another one in its place, and gave the two figures that are his own, with the subtraction in each, and said the third is where it was on Saturday.

“*The figure is gone, and I am not going to replace it with a different one this morning, and that is the first time in this county I have said a figure out loud and taken it back inside the same minute.*”

“*And I am not going to tell you it was a small thing. It was not, and about four of you would have let it stand because it was mine.*”

“*Two hundred and ninety-seven days in this county, and that is eight hundred and fifty-one less five hundred and fifty-four, and I have been nobody’s for all of them.*”

“*The cut across this palm is a hundred and sixty-six days old, and that is eight hundred and fifty-one less six hundred and eighty-five, and nobody has said whether it will keep, and I have not asked, and I am not going to ask today.*”

“*And the offer asked at the seven-hundredth is a hundred and fifty-one days old, and a hundred and fifty-one is eight hundred and fifty-one less seven hundred, and it has not been answered. A day-book that came up the cart road on Saturday is not an answer to it, and I am not going to let about four of you decide otherwise because it has a leaf for every day.*”

About nine of them were on that landing at about the eighth hour of a Sunday, with four inches over the top step and seven days of the coming back, and a man who cannot read had put four strokes and a half a barrow on a leaf that a shop had printed the day on, and a man of thirty-one had asked to write the first line in four hundred miles and had taken the asking back.

---

The fever was eighty-one weeks and one day old. He had been in that county two hundred and ninety-seven days, and the cut across that palm was a hundred and sixty-six days old, and a hundred and fifty-one days after an offer that was asked at the seven-hundredth and has not been answered.

A day-book came up the cart road four miles from the bakehouse on Saturday, and it cost a man of fifty-four fourpence, and he said the fourpence out loud twice so that nobody would call it a gift. It has a leaf for every day, and the day printed at the head of each leaf by somebody who was not here, and he cannot read what is printed there. A boy of thirteen read it to him twice, and he put four strokes and a half a barrow on the leaf, and said the half was a figure of a barrow and not a figure of a day.

A man of fifty-four asked for a day against the ninth line for the hundred and thirtieth morning, and was told what the ninth line is. It is a run of days with a leaf for every one of them, four hundred miles off, and not one line has ever been entered on any of those leaves.
Nothing is missing from it. No leaf torn out, no page cut, and a man who cannot read had believed for eleven years that something had been taken off it.

A man of thirty-one said he wanted to put the first line on it, ever, so that there would be one, and a man of fifty-four gave the working of why it could not be him. If one line is his, the run has a man in it, and the answer to the question who has been writing on the ninth line would be a man of thirty-one who is nobody’s, and it would be the only true answer anybody could give.
He withdrew it in the same breath and said that about four of them now knew he wanted a book to write in, and that he would rather it was said out loud than worked out.

And he said a figure of about eleven years for how long that line has been empty. A boy of thirteen told him in one sentence that a figure of years with no day on the end of it is the same as no figure, and the figure was taken back and not replaced.

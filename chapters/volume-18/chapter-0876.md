# Chapter 876: Two Thursdays Seven Days Apart

Third day of the hundred and forty-seventh week. His six hundred and twenty-sixth morning. Four hundred and seventy-six days after the settlement. The fever eighty-four weeks and five days old. Four hundred and thirty days since the division. Three hundred and seventy-six days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Eight inches stood on the top step at about the seventh hour and it was six days of the coming back. About eighty of those ninety steps were under. The man of forty-three gave the three figures in the reverse of the order he has used all week, and said why, and said it was the last morning he was going to change anything.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, has held that landing nineteen years, gives four figures every morning off that one stone, and has changed the order he says them in four times this fortnight without once being asked to.

“About eighty of those ninety steps under, six days of the coming back, and eight at the seventh hour.”

“I have said the inches first all week. I am saying them last this morning. It is not a different morning and the numbers have not moved, and every one of you will write them down in whichever order you last heard them, which is the reason I keep saying them out loud.”

“And there is nothing wrong with the water this morning. Four inches a day. I have said it nineteen years and all of you have stopped wanting me to say it, and that is the correct thing to stop wanting.”

Then a man of fifty-four who cannot read said out loud, in front of the bank, that a phrase you all use every morning names two different days, and gave the working.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for a hundred and fifty-five mornings, which is eight hundred and seventy-six less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“A hundred and fifty-five, and I am going to say something this morning that half of you will think is the ravings of a man who cannot read, and I am going to say it anyway and I am going to give the working.”

“Here is the phrase, and I am saying it word for word because I cannot write it and this is how I have heard it. The Thursday of last week. And that is two Thursdays. It has been two Thursdays all fortnight and neither one of them is a lie.”

“I do not know which one I have said when I say it. That is not because I am slow and it is not because I cannot read. It is because the words do not carry it. If I say it on a Thursday then last week is a week back and the day I meant is eight days back. If I say it on a Friday then last week is a week back and the day I meant is one day back.”

“I worked it out this morning with a stone. This morning is eight hundred and seventy-six and it is a Thursday of the hundred and forty-seventh week. The Thursday of the hundred and forty-sixth week is eight hundred and seventy-six less seven, and that is eight hundred and sixty-nine.”

“So. If I say that phrase this morning, last week is a week back and the day I meant is eight hundred and sixty-nine. And if I say the same phrase on the Saturday of this week, last week is still a week back and the day I meant is this morning, and that is eight hundred and seventy-six.”

“Two Thursdays, seven days apart, and I said the same five words on two mornings of the same week. Neither one of them is a lie, and some of you have written one of them down, and not one of you could tell me which one I meant. Neither can I.”

“And the fix is not me. The fix is that you say the week. Eight hundred and seventy-three is a figure that anybody can carry and check. A bare phrase with no week on it is a thing only the man saying it can carry, and he cannot carry it either, and I have been failing to carry it for nine years.”

“I said a thing out loud on the Saturday of the hundred and forty-fourth week and somebody wrote it down, and nobody knows whether I meant the Thursday of that week or the Thursday of the one before it. I do not know either. I have known that for nine years and nobody has ever noticed.”

“Nobody is to be thanked. I am not asking for a day today and I am not asking you to change a single thing you write down. I am asking you to say the week, and that is a thing you can do on your own and it does not cost anybody anything.”

Then a man of about fifty-two said his own sentence for the fourth time in six mornings, out loud, and said there was not going to be a fifth, and said again what three of the nine positions are.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, he cannot see well, and there is no other man on that landing who can put a line under six of the nine positions and leave three of them alone.

“The six being checkable does not make the three checkable. And I said that on the Wednesday of the hundred and forty-second week, and I have said it on the Wednesday of the hundred and forty-fifth week, and on the seventh and last day of the hundred and forty-fifth week, and on the Thursday of the hundred and forty-sixth week.”

“That is three times and this morning is the fourth and there is not going to be a fifth. I am not improving it and I am not working on it at night. It is finished and it was finished when I said it the first time.”

“And half of you have been hoping all week that I would get tired of saying no and say something better instead. There is nothing better. I said that on the seventh and last day of the hundred and forty-fifth week and I am saying it on the third day of the hundred and forty-seventh week and it is the same sentence twice for the same reason.”

“What three of the nine positions are is still open. I want that said by me, in a mouth, out loud, because you have all spent a fortnight hoping I would answer it, and the reason I cannot is not that I do not know. It is that knowing which of the nine a person can be shown is not knowing what the other three are, and if I said one of the two out loud in front of the bank this morning you would all go off and believe I had said the other.”

“And there will not be a fifteenth. There will not be a sixteenth. If anybody wants one they can go and find their own and I am about fifty-two and I have fourteen and they are all still true.”

Then a man of thirty-one said that a man who keeps a run of days for a boat is a holder of something, and ought to be counted with the people who hold things, and was refused by the man who keeps the boat.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and twenty-two days. The cut across that palm is a hundred and ninety-one days old.

“Three hundred and twenty-two days in this county. The cut across this palm is a hundred and ninety-one days old. Nobody has said whether it will keep.”

“And the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is a hundred and seventy-six days old. It is unanswered. I have not asked it and I am not going to ask it on a Thursday when the weather on this bank is generous.”

“So. Four people keep four runs of days on that landing and not one of them is on a page. And a man of fifty-two has just told this bank that six of the nine positions can be put a line under.”

“Those are not the same set and I have known that since the Wednesday of the hundred and forty-seventh week and I said it out loud anyway, and that is the sixteenth wrong thing in sixteen mornings.”

Linnet Ord was on the bank at about the tenth hour and said that he was not a holder of anything, and gave the whole of it in one go.

“I am not a holder and I am not to be counted with anybody, and you are not to write me down next to a woman of forty-nine who holds one of the nine.”

“A holder means there is a list. That is what the word means. There are nine things on that list and three of them a list does not carry lines for and I am not one of the nine and neither is the gate and neither is the drain and neither is the school.”

“You told me on the Sunday of the hundred and forty-sixth week that you would not put me in a fourth thing with nobody holding it. And on the Tuesday of the hundred and forty-seventh week you tried to give me a day off the front of your own run. And now this.”

“You keep doing the same thing in three different shapes and every one of you will tell me you cannot see the shape, and I am telling you it is one shape. You keep wanting to put a person in a list because a person in a list can be counted, and a person who cannot be counted is a person you cannot tell anything to.”

“I am not going to speak to you for the rest of this morning. That is not a punishment and it is not going to teach you anything. It is because I have about forty leaves and nobody is going to say anything else about them this morning except me.”

Then a woman of thirty-three asked a man of fifty-four whether he had found his day, and told him the truth about why she was not going to help him.

“A hundred and fifty-four. And I have told him twice that I will write a line for him when he names a day and I am now going to tell him something I have not told this bank, and it is that I am not going to help him find it.”

Nessa Toomy, thirty-three, keeps a run of days for a drain, a hundred and fifty-eight days of it, anchored to a step that went in flat.

“If I helped him find it then the day he named would be a day I found for him, and there would be a line on my run about a man of fifty-four, and there would be one of mine on it too, and he would not know which one was his.”

“And I would have taken the whole of what he asked for and given it to him and made him feel better, and half of you would have said how well that went. He has asked a woman of forty-nine for eleven years and nobody has done that for him and it is not because nobody thought of it. It is because it does not work.”

“He will find a day or he will not. There is a woman of twenty-eight four miles down that cart road who cannot be sent for, and a man who keeps a boat who has no day at the front of his run at all, and a man of forty-seven whose gate got shut this morning whether or not anybody was standing on that bank. Those three got on without anything from anybody and I am not going to be the fourth thing that comes along with a line.”

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and twenty-three days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and twenty-three is eight hundred and seventy-six less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and twenty-three days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.”

“And nobody has asked me for the count I am keeping. That is fifteen mornings, and a man of fifty-four has just told this bank that he has been meaning the same Thursday for nine years and getting it wrong, and I have been keeping that count since the Thursday of the hundred and forty-fifth week and I am still not saying it.”

“And half of you have just worked out that a man of fifty-four cannot read and cannot check which Thursday he said, and that I am the only person on that landing who can add it up. That is true, and it does not mean I am going to say the count.”

Sena Dorr entered today’s line on the ninth run and read it out herself, and said what a run of days is for, in one sentence, about her own.

“That is the nineteenth morning in front of anybody and it is a figure of mornings.”

“And I am going to say the short version of what a man of forty-seven and a woman of thirty-three and a man of thirty-one have said about their own runs this week, because half of you have put three sentences where there is one.”

“A run of days is not for checking a position. It is for putting down that a day happened. Mine has a hole at the front of it and I have said on the seventh and last day of the hundred and forty-fifth week that the three blanks were never blanks, and today I am entering a line on it as I do every morning, and today is not a gap.”

“What three of the nine positions are is still open. It was open on that Monday and it is open this morning and nothing that was said on that bank this week has changed it by one day.”

Barnaby Crove asked for a day against the ninth line for the hundred and fifty-fifth morning and said what he thought about a man who cannot read naming a day he cannot check.

“A hundred and fifty-five. And I have said the working of the two Thursdays and half of you have gone quiet, which is a different quiet from the other quiet, and I have noticed it, and I am not going to be thanked for noticing it.”

“And I have not got the day. I have said that on the Wednesday of the hundred and forty-seventh week out loud in front of the bank, and I am going to go on saying it until it turns up. If it does not turn up then in about four weeks I will still be a man of fifty-four standing on that bank asking for something I cannot name, and not one of you will have anything for me. Having nothing is not the same as saying so out loud. That will be the correct amount.”

Barnaby Crove did not go home at eleven. He sat on the dry end of the top step with his back to the water and worked his thumb along the edge of one phrase, and Simon Rook went past without stopping, and neither of them said anything to the other about it.

# Chapter 872: The Same Words And Not The Same Fact

Sixth day of the hundred and forty-sixth week. His six hundred and twenty-second morning. Four hundred and seventy-two days after the settlement. The fever eighty-four weeks and one day old. Four hundred and twenty-six days since the division. Three hundred and seventy-two days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Sixteen inches stood on the top step at about the seventh hour and it was holding at that figure. It was two days of the coming back and about sixty of those ninety steps were under. The man of forty-three gave the two figures and then said he was not going to give them in the same words again.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, has held that landing nineteen years, and stands on the same stone at the same hour and gives four figures off it. Three of them are water. The fourth is a figure of steps. Yesterday he said he would say the word holding every morning. This morning he has taken the other one back.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour, two days of the coming back, about sixty of those ninety steps under, and it is holding. I said yesterday that I would say that word every morning and I have had a night to think about it.”

“I am not going to say the word every morning. I am going to say it when it is true and I am going to say full when it is full, and a man who says one word every morning has turned a figure into a habit, and I can see that you would rather have a habit than a figure.”

Then a woman of thirty-three came up that cut with a wet hem, and a man of fifty-four gave her name and her age out loud, and said how he knew them.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has carted for the man who was her father and he has held that gravel since the Wednesday of the hundred and forty-second week.

“That is Nessa Toomy and she is thirty-three and I am saying both figures out loud because half of you cannot hold a name past the morning it is given in, and I cannot write it down. So it is said, and it is said twice.”

“I knew her father before I knew her. I carted for him eleven years and he cut the stone that is at the head of that drain, and it went in flat on a Tuesday, and that Tuesday is the day her run starts and it is not on a piece of paper I have ever seen.”

Nessa Toomy is thirty-three and keeps a run of days for a drain. One leaf for one day. One line a day saying what the water was doing.

“One hundred and fifty-eight days, and the anchor is the day that step went in flat, and that is a step and not a figure, and I am not going to give you a number for the day because I was not there for it and my father was.”

“And the figure I give every morning is inches of water over that step. That is the whole of my morning and it is a figure of a thing and it is a figure of inches over a step.”

“Now. There is a man of forty-four at the head of the Red Basin works whose figure is inches of water over a step. His step is a sluice step and my step is a stone step and we have got the same figure of the same kind of thing, and I can see two or three of you have already added the two of them together in your heads. I would like you to stop.”

“They are two drains. They are not one drain. A figure of inches over a step is a figure of inches over a step and it does not say which step, and two men who both say inches over a step have said the same words and not the same fact.”

A man of forty-seven came up about the tenth hour and sat on the flat stone above the cut without being asked, and gave his figure, and said what a run of days is for when nothing on it is one of the nine.

Rod Veal is forty-seven and keeps a run of days for the low gate at the bottom of that bank. One leaf for one day. The hour it was shut.

“Two hundred and six days, and the anchor is the first evening I shut that gate myself and wrote the line on the same night with the same chalk, and it is a hinge and a date in my own hand and that is all the anchor there is.”

“And there is no page four hundred miles off with my name on it and there is no list and there is no stone and nobody has ever asked me for that figure, not once, not in nineteen years.”

“So here is what it is for. I know you have all been waiting all week for somebody to say it. A gate is shut every day whether or not anybody is watching. The boat goes or it does not. The drain runs or it does not. The school opens or it does not. The gate is shut or it is not.”

“That is what a run of days is for when the thing it is about is not one of the nine. It is not for being checked. Nothing will ever check it. There is no stone in front of it to stand at and there is no index that carries it.”

“It is the only place that fact lands. A gate got shut this morning and it got shut whether you were all standing on that bank or none of you were. And if there is no line on a leaf about it by dark, then the fact happened and it is nowhere.”

Ilyan Vester said that was a reason to put the drain lines in the order of the inches, so that two mornings of the same depth sat next to each other and could be added up.

Nessa Toomy did not answer him straight away. She put her hand flat on the stone step at the head of the drain, and then took it off.

“No. And you did not ask me, and I want that said in front of the bank before anything else you say this morning.”

“I write them when I walk it. I walk it at about the seventh hour and I write it at about the eighth. On a morning when I am late I write it late. That is not sloppiness, that is the only honest shape of it that there is, and I am not going to write a day early so that a man of thirty-one can put two of mine side by side and add the inches up.”

“And what you have actually asked for, without saying it, is that I should enter the drain in the order of the water instead of the order of the days. And if I did that then the first leaf on my run would not be the first day and every figure on it after that would be out by however many mornings I had done it late.”

“You were standing at that step when you said it. All of you, and a man of fifty-four, and a man of forty-seven. I would like that noticed, because in about four weeks none of you will remember it was said and I will.”

Ilyan Vester said he had not meant it as an order and that he only wanted two mornings of the same depth to sit together.

“Then it was worse and not better, because you wanted the record to be easier for you and not truer for anybody else. I would rather walk a wet step in a bad order and know what I wrote than have a tidy leaf I cannot stand behind in a week.”

“Three hundred and eighteen days in this county. The cut across this palm is a hundred and eighty-seven days old. Nobody has said whether it will keep.”

“And the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is a hundred and seventy-two days old. It is unanswered. I am not going to ask it.”

Then a woman of forty-four came back down the cart road and said what she had done on the Saturday of the hundred and forty-sixth week, and said it was not a decision.

Orla Fennimore is forty-four, has kept twenty-nine chairs at thirteen shillings and fourpence a year for nineteen years, and put her question down for the fourth time on the Wednesday of the hundred and forty-sixth week, one word of it not altered.

“I opened the book and I did the same thing I have done for nineteen years and I have done it since, and that is what Saturday is.”

“Nobody answered my question on the Wednesday of the hundred and forty-sixth week when I put it down and nobody answered it on the Saturday of the hundred and forty-sixth week and nobody is going to answer it this morning. A day can be put down for a thing that happened before anybody thought to write it, and is that day the same as a day put down at the time. That is still the question and it is still the fourth time and one word of it has not moved.”

“And I went up that road and I sat with a woman of twenty-eight for two hours and I did not use her name once, and a couple of you would have made a fourth thing out of it by Tuesday, so you did not get it.”

At about the tenth hour a boy of thirteen counted the figure in the lane before the light came up, and a man of fifty-four asked again, and a woman of forty-nine entered today’s line and gave her figure of mornings.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and nineteen days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and nineteen is eight hundred and seventy-two less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and nineteen days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.”

“Nobody has asked me for the count I am keeping. That is eleven mornings. You have all stopped asking. I am going to say one thing about it, because it is a Sunday and Sunday is when you expect a boy of thirteen to give something up.”

“It is not a number of mornings a thing was done. It is a number of mornings I did something nobody asked me for. Those are not the same figure, and some of you write them down as though they were.”

Sena Dorr is forty-nine. The ninth of the nine positions is hers, and it is a public archive and not a stone, and she has entered a line on that run every day for twenty-three years.

“That is the fifteenth morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings and not a figure of you, and I am not going to say anything else about it.”

“And a boy of thirteen has just told this bank that two figures of mornings are not the same figure. He is right. And half of you are going to go on writing them down the same anyway.”

Barnaby Crove asked for a day against the ninth line for the hundred and fifty-first morning and said what he had worked out on the Tuesday of the hundred and forty-sixth week, and said who he was not saying it to.

“A hundred and fifty-one. It is a Sunday and a woman of thirty-three has just told me she would put a line in for me if I asked her and I have not asked her and I am not going to this morning.”

“I worked out on the Tuesday of the hundred and forty-sixth week that there is nothing on my line to go and look for, and I have not said it to a man of thirty-one because he will do something with it. He will carry it up that bank and put it next to something and I will never get it back as mine.”

“I am going to ask somebody for a different thing on Monday. You will be disappointed and somebody will say I said that last Saturday, and I did.”

Nessa Toomy walked her drain twice that morning and wrote nothing on either walk, and Rod Veal shut the low gate behind him and stood with his hand on it a while, in no mood to explain it to anybody.

# Chapter 878: A Column Is Not A Column

Fifth day of the hundred and forty-seventh week. His six hundred and twenty-eighth morning. Four hundred and seventy-eight days after the settlement. The fever eighty-five weeks old. Four hundred and thirty-two days since the division. Three hundred and seventy-eight days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Sixteen inches came up on the top step at about the seventh hour and that was a full bank, with four days of the coming back and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave both words and named the day he had named on the Friday, with the week on it.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, has held that landing nineteen years, and gave four figures off one stone at about the seventh hour of a Saturday. He named this morning on the Friday of the hundred and forty-seventh week, with the day and the week on it, and it came out right.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour, a full bank, four days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under.”

“And I named that on yesterday. I said the Saturday of the hundred and forty-seventh week and a full bank and four days of the coming back, and half of you heard it and some of you wrote it down, and it has come out right.”

“That is the second morning running that a thing I said out loud has been right. That is not a good record. It is a Saturday and a man of forty-three got two figures right in a row, and half of you would like to make a habit of me.”

Then four people gave four figures of leaves out loud, in front of the bank, each of them with the anchor beside it.

“About forty leaves, and there is no figure of days, and I have said both halves and I am not going to say the first half without the second one again.”

Linnet Ord is thirty-one, and he is not the other thirty-one, and he keeps a run of days for a ferry below the low gate, and the front of that run has nothing at the front of it because the first leaves were never written on.

“The anchor is a boat and a boy on a boat in a year nobody has written down, and that is not a figure and it is not a day and it is not going to become one.”

“About forty leaves. Forty is about forty. I will not tell you it is forty-two and I will not tell you it is thirty-eight and I am not going to be more exact on a Saturday than I am on a Tuesday.”

“And I have no figure of days at all. I said that on the Friday of the hundred and forty-sixth week and on the Tuesday of the hundred and forty-seventh week and I am saying it on a Saturday in front of the bank so that it is three mornings and not a slip.”

“One hundred and fifty-eight leaves and one hundred and fifty-eight days, and those are the same figure, and on my run a leaf is a day and I have never once written two lines on one leaf and there is never going to be.”

Nessa Toomy is thirty-three and keeps a run of days for a drain, and the anchor of her run is a stone step that went in flat at the head of it on a Tuesday.

“And I am going to say the thing half of you want me to say before I have finished. If my leaf is my day and the other two are the same, then three of the four of you can be added together.”

“They cannot. And here is the working and it is one sentence. On my run one leaf is one day, because I write one line a day and have done since the step went in. The school run is the same arrangement entirely. So is the gate run, for the same reason. But on the boat run a leaf is nothing whatever, because the man who keeps it does not know when it started and has never known.”

“A figure of leaves is a figure of leaves. It is not a figure of days and it does not become one because three other people happen to be standing on a bank saying the same kind of figure.”

Sera Quill is twenty-eight and keeps a run of days for a school four miles down that cart road, and its anchor is the first morning she opened that door and wrote the line on the same evening.

“Two hundred and twelve leaves and two hundred and twelve days. One line a day, one leaf a day, and a leaf is a day, and I have never had to write a reason on a second line and I am not going to start.”

“And I am not on that bank. I am four miles down this road and I have said my piece on it once and I am not saying it twice to make anybody here comfortable.”

Rod Veal is forty-seven and keeps a run of days for the low gate, and its anchor is the first evening he shut that gate himself and wrote the line on the same night in his own hand.

“Two hundred and six leaves. Two hundred and six days. And two hundred and thirty-one lines.”

“Yes. Those are three different figures and they are all mine and I am not going to pretend the third one is a mistake. On twenty-five of those days a cart came through after dark and I shut that gate twice. Once when the cart went out and once when it came back. That is one day and two lines and some of you are already writing two hundred and thirty-one days on the back of your hand.”

“A line is a day when the gate is shut once. A line is not a day when a man has shut it twice for one cart. And the figure that matters to me is the day, because the gate was shut on two hundred and six days, and I would like that written down and not the other one.”

Then a man of thirty-one added the four figures of leaves together, out loud, in front of the bank, and called the sum a figure of days.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and twenty-four days. The cut across that palm is a hundred and ninety-three days old.

“Forty and a bit, a hundred and fifty-eight, two hundred and twelve, and two hundred and six. That is six hundred and sixteen days that four people have put down on four different mornings this week.”

“I am going to say the working so that you can add it up after me. Forty is about forty, so the sum is about six hundred and sixteen and I have not pretended that part is exact. The other three are whole. The addition is right. I want that said first.”

“Three hundred and twenty-four days in this county. The cut across this palm is a hundred and ninety-three days old. Nobody has said whether it will keep.”

“And the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is a hundred and seventy-eight days old. It is unanswered and I have not asked it on a Saturday.”

“And there it is. About six hundred and sixteen days, on four runs, kept by four people, none of them one of the nine. That is eighteen wrong things in eighteen mornings and I have said the working out loud first, and half of you heard the working and the sentence underneath it, and half of you only heard the sentence.”

Wat Marshe stood on the flat stone above the cut and gave the correction, and nobody had asked him for it.

“It is not days. That is all. It is not days and you said the working and the working was right and the sentence underneath it is wrong, and those are two different mistakes, and half of you only looked at one of them.”

“On three of those four runs a leaf is a day. On one of them a leaf is not a day and the man who keeps it does not know when it started. And on one of them a line is not a day. So there are four kinds of figure on that bank this morning and you have put them in one column and added them, and a column is not a column if the things in it are not the same thing.”

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and twenty-five days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and twenty-five is eight hundred and seventy-eight less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and twenty-five days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.”

“And nobody has asked me for the count I am keeping. That is seventeen mornings. A man of thirty-one has just been wrong in front of the whole bank, and some of you looked at me before you looked at him. I am thirteen and I am not going to pretend that did not happen.”

“I am not going to say the count. I have got an opposite of the fourteenth thing and I am not going to give it, and you have all stopped asking, and that is the only reason I am still keeping it.”

Simon Rook put a hand flat on the stone and said what a figure of days needs before it can be one.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, and named a fourteenth thing he cannot check on the Thursday of the hundred and forty-fifth week, and said on the Friday of the hundred and forty-sixth week that there would not be a fifteenth.

“I said a thing on the Friday of the hundred and forty-fifth week and I am going to say a part of it again, because there are four ordinary things on this bank this morning and it applies to all four and I only answered for one of them.”

“A figure of days is a figure of days only if a person knows what a leaf is worth. I know what a leaf is worth on one book on this landing and that is my own. I do not know what a leaf is worth on the other three, and neither does anybody on this bank, and neither does the man of thirty-one who has just added them.”

“And a figure of leaves is not a figure of days and it is not a smaller figure of days. It is a different figure about a different thing, and half of you have been wanting it to be a smaller one all week, because a smaller one can be carried up four hundred miles.”

“And there will not be a fifteenth. If you want a fifteenth, you can go and find your own.”

Then a woman of forty-four said what four of them had said this week added up to, and refused it, and a man of fifty-four asked for the hundred and fifty-seventh morning.

Orla Fennimore is forty-four and has kept twenty-nine chairs at thirteen shillings and fourpence a year for nineteen years, and she put her question down for the fourth time on the Wednesday of the hundred and forty-sixth week.

“Four people on that bank have told the truth this week. A ferry. A drain. A school. A gate. And a man of thirty-one has added them up and got six hundred and sixteen days and he has not counted me and he has not counted a woman of forty-nine and he has not counted a man of thirty-eight, and some of you noticed.”

“And that is the whole of what happened on this landing this week and it does not settle my question. Can a day be put down for a thing that happened before anybody thought to write it. And is that day the same as a day put down at the time. That is the fourth time and one word of it has not moved.”

“A day with a hole at the front of it is not a settlement. A sentence about a page is not a settlement. And nobody is going to be thanked for any of the four of them.”

Sena Dorr entered today’s line on the ninth run at about the tenth hour and read it out herself on the Saturday of the hundred and forty-seventh week.

“That is the twenty-first morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and it is on a Saturday, and there were people on that bank while I wrote it.”

Barnaby Crove asked for a day against the ninth line for the hundred and fifty-seventh morning and said what he thought about a man of thirty-one adding things up in a morning.

“A hundred and fifty-seven, and a man of thirty-one added four figures together in front of the bank and got six hundred and sixteen, and the addition was right and the sentence was wrong. I have been on that bank for eleven years with a number I could not check, and nobody here has ever once had the two kinds apart from me.”

“I cannot read and I have been standing in front of the whole of you all week and I want to say one thing about it. A man of fifty-four who cannot read can still tell the two apart. It is the same work. You do not need to be able to write a figure down in order to know which kind of figure it is.”

“Half of you have been treating that as a second-class thing to do. It is not second class. It is the same nine seconds of work and it is the only thing on this landing that would have caught what just happened on that step.”

Nobody stopped him. Six hundred and sixteen was said out loud on a full bank and it stayed said, and the woman of forty-nine put her hand flat on her own book and left it there.

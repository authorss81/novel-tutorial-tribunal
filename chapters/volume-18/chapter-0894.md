# Chapter 894: One Chair Out Of A Room Of Twenty-Nine

Seventh and last day of the hundred and forty-ninth week. His six hundred and forty-fourth morning. Four hundred and ninety-four days after the settlement. The fever eighty-seven weeks and two days old. Four hundred and forty-eight days since the division. Three hundred and ninety-four days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Sixteen inches came up on the top step at about the seventh hour and that was a full bank, with four days of the coming back and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave both words and named the day he had named on the Sunday, and it came out a day early, and he named both sides of the week.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, has held that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, and has said the same thing about Mondays for eleven years without ever once being sentimental about it.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour, a full bank, four days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the seventh and last day of the hundred and forty-ninth week, which is a Monday.”

“I said it was a full bank yesterday and I said it would be a full bank on the seventh and last day of the hundred and forty-ninth week. It is a full bank. I said it a day early and I would rather be out by a day than in by one.”

“Today is the seventh and last day of the hundred and forty-ninth week. Tomorrow is the first day of the hundred and fiftieth week and it is a Tuesday. Both halves of that are on this step on a Monday morning because it happens every seven days and nobody has to be told about it.”

“And the next time the four things have nothing to be about is in four days, on the fourth day of the hundred and fiftieth week, which is a Friday, and that is the twenty-ninth time. I said that day with the week on it on the third day of the hundred and forty-ninth week and I am saying it again today, and about four of you will get it wrong anyway because a Friday sounds like a Tuesday.”

Then the woman of forty-four said the room was hers, and said what would have to happen to the room, and said she was not going to ask anybody to enter a chair.

Orla Fennimore is forty-four, has kept twenty-nine chairs for nineteen years at thirteen shillings and fourpence a year, has put her question down five times, and is not going to ask a sixth person for it.

“Yes. It is my room and it is for the third day of the hundred and fiftieth week, which is a Thursday, and I am not going to be asked twice and I am not going to be thanked for saying yes on a Monday.”

“There are twenty-nine chairs in it and I move them. That is the whole of what I do in that room in the morning and it has been the whole of what I do in that room in the morning for nineteen years.”

“And on the second day of the hundred and fiftieth week I am going to have to take one of them out into the passage and leave it there overnight, and it is going to be standing in my passage when I go to bed and it is going to be standing there when anybody who comes up that road in the morning walks past it, and I will have put it there and I will have carried it back on the third day of that week.”

“I am telling the whole bank that now, on a Monday, two days early, because I am not going to be the one who is surprised on Wednesday and I am not going to be asked on Thursday whether I did a thing.”

“And nobody is to enter it. I am not asking anybody to write down that I moved a chair out of my own room in my own passage. If it wants entering then somebody who was not me enters it, in their own hand, on the day it happened, and if nobody enters it then a chair stood in a passage for one night and that is the whole of what happened to it.”

Then a man of thirty-one said the door should not be shut, and was told by the woman who holds the ninth of the nine exactly what a shut door is for.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and forty days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and nine days old.

“Three hundred and forty days in this county, and the cut across this palm is two hundred and nine days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is a hundred and ninety-four days old, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Thirty-four wrong things in thirty-four mornings, and here is the thirty-fourth, and it is about a door.”

“Do not shut it. Leave it open on the passage. Then it is not a room with a thing in it, it is a step with a step inside it, and about nine of us can stand at the doorway and hear it without being in it, and if anything goes wrong in there we can hear it going wrong.”

“No, and I am going to give you the shape of the no and then I am going to stop talking to you about doors for the rest of this week.”

“If the door is open then there is no room. If there is no room then a thing said in there is said on that step in about nine ears, and about nine ears is where everything on this landing has been said for weeks now, and every figure that has ever left that step has left it in under a day.”

“And I do not act on a figure that arrives with a mouth on it. I act on a figure that arrives with a hand on it. I said that on the first day of the hundred and forty-ninth week and I have said it since and it is not improving as the week goes on.”

“You are asking me to take a thing said in a room and treat it as a thing said in public, and then I am a woman who acts on what a public mouth said, and I have spent twenty-three years not being that woman in a room with nine clerks in it.”

“I am not going to be thanked for the no. Nobody on this landing is going to be thanked for anything this week and I would like it said out loud before anybody starts.”

Barnaby Crove asked for a day against the ninth line for the hundred and seventy-third morning, and said what a Monday is for the second time in a week.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for a hundred and seventy-three mornings, which is eight hundred and ninety-four less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“A hundred and seventy-three. And a woman has just told about nine of us that she is moving a chair out of her own room, and she named the morning she is going to do it on, and she has not asked anybody to enter it.”

“That is the first thing anybody on this bank has said out loud in front of me before it happened instead of after, and I have been standing in front of them for eleven years and I did not know that was a thing anybody did.”

“I said on the seventh and last day of the hundred and forty-eighth week that on a Monday they stay, and that I have been asking the front half of a bank for eleven years. It is true and it is still true and it is the whole of what I know about Mondays.”

“And now I know one more thing about a Monday, and it is smaller. On a Monday a woman tells you what she is going to do before she does it, so that nobody has to stand there and watch to find out whether she meant it. That is what a Monday is for.”

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and forty-one days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and forty-one is eight hundred and ninety-four less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and forty-one days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.”

“Nobody asked me for the count I am keeping. That is thirty-three mornings, and I am not giving it, and a woman of forty-four has just said a thing out loud before she did it and four of you have written it down.”

“Do not write it down. It is a chair in a passage. If it is on a page in about nine weeks then somebody will ask whether she did it or whether the page did it, and she will have to say something about a chair, and she has been asked for nineteen years and she is not going to be asked about a chair as well.”

Sena Dorr entered today’s line on the ninth run at about the tenth hour and read it out herself on the last day of a week, and named both halves of the week while she did it.

“That is the thirty-seventh morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing.”

“Today is the seventh and last day of the hundred and forty-ninth week and tomorrow is the first day of the hundred and fiftieth week and it is a Tuesday. A man of forty-three said both of those at the seventh hour and I am saying them at the tenth hour because I write the day down and he does not, and one of us is going to be the record.”

A chair that had stood in that passage for years was still against the wall of it at about the eleventh hour with its back to the room, and it was not one of the twenty-nine, and everybody who came up that road in the morning had walked past it without once asking where it came from.

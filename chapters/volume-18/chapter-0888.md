# Chapter 888: Counting It Is Cheap And Acting On It Is Not

First day of the hundred and forty-ninth week. His six hundred and thirty-eighth morning. Four hundred and eighty-eight days after the settlement. The fever eighty-six weeks and three days old. Four hundred and forty-two days since the division. Three hundred and eighty-eight days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Sixteen inches stood on the top step at about the seventh hour and it was holding at that figure. It was two days of the coming back and about sixty of those ninety steps were under. The man of forty-three gave the four figures and named the day he had named on the day before.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, has held that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, and named this morning on the seventh and last day of the hundred and forty-eighth week, with the day and the week on it.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour, two days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, and it holds on the first day of the hundred and forty-ninth week and I said so yesterday without being asked to.”

“I said yesterday that today is the first day of the hundred and forty-ninth week and it is a Tuesday, and it is the first day of the hundred and forty-ninth week and it is a Tuesday.”

Then the man of forty-three said a thing about a gap in some stitching, and put no figure in it at all.

“There is a gap in the stitching of the ninth run and everybody in this bank knows it and nobody has put a figure on it, and I am going to say two things about it and I am going to say them with nothing between them.”

“Counting it is cheap. Counting a gap is the cheapest thing anybody on this landing could do this week, and it is cheap in the way a thing is cheap when it takes no time and costs nobody anything and cannot be undone.”

“Acting on the count is not cheap. It is not the same job. It is not even the same kind of job. And it is done by a person, in a room, on a morning, with the figure in front of them and another person in the room who is allowed to say no.”

“That is the whole of what I have got and there is no figure in it and I am not going to put one. About four of you are waiting for me to say how many, and I am not going to say how many, and neither is anybody else on this landing.”

“And I am going to say why I have not said it, because a man of forty-nine does it every morning in a different voice and about four of you have never noticed.”

“The second half is not expensive because it takes time. It is expensive because it commits somebody. Counting commits nobody. To count a gap you have to be willing to say there is a gap, and about four of you are not willing to say that in front of nine other people, and they would rather have a number.”

“So the count is the cheap half for a second reason as well as the first, and both reasons are the same reason, and that is a thing about people and not about stitching and I am not going to develop it.”

Then a man of thirty-one asked a boy of thirteen to be the one who counted, and the boy said no, and nobody told him he had found anything.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and thirty-four days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and three days old.

“Three hundred and thirty-four days in this county, and the cut across this palm is two hundred and three days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is a hundred and eighty-eight days old, and I have not asked it, and I have not asked him for anything either.”

“Twenty-eight wrong things in twenty-eight mornings, and here is the twenty-eighth. Then let the boy do it. He is the only person in this county who reads a sentence out in three pieces for a man who cannot read, and a gap in a stitching is not a sentence, it is a row of stitches with some of them not there, and that is exactly the kind of thing he is good at.”

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and thirty-five days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and thirty-five is eight hundred and eighty-eight less six hundred and fifty-three.

“No.”

“And that is the whole of my answer and I am going to say the rest of it so that nobody on this bank decides later that I was being difficult.”

“You are not making me a clerk. That is what that offer is, and I know what a clerk is, because a clerk is a person somebody counts, and I have watched a woman of forty-nine be one for twenty-three years and I am not going to be one on a Tuesday.”

“You did not ask me whether I had found anything and I have not found anything, so there is nothing to thank you for.”

“And nobody on this bank is going to be thanked for anything this morning, and I would like that said out loud, because there are about four of you looking at me as though something had happened.”

“I am thirteen and I am not a pair of hands and I am not a clerk. Somebody has to say this out loud on this landing and it is going to be the man of forty-three or it is going to be the woman of forty-nine, and both of them have said this week that they will not do it in a morning.”

“If you want my help then ask me for a different thing. I will read a sentence out in three pieces for a man who cannot read. I will say whether one figure is the same kind as another figure. I have been doing both of those since before this week started.”

“I am not doing them because anybody asked. And nobody is to be thanked for what I do, because being left alone is not the same as being asked, and I said that out loud on the seventh and last day of the hundred and forty-seventh week, which is a Monday, and about four of you wrote it down as modesty.”

“It is not modesty. It is the only difference there is between what I am doing and what a clerk is doing, and about four of you nearly took it away from me this morning by looking at me.”

Wat Marshe is thirteen. He has been keeping a count that nobody has asked him for for twenty-seven mornings, and he named the kind of figure it is on the Sunday of the hundred and forty-seventh week, and he has not given it and is not going to.

Then the woman who holds the ninth of the nine positions said why she will not be handed a figure on a Tuesday morning.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years in a room four hundred miles off with nine clerks in it, and holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone.

“Counting it is cheap and acting on it is not, and a man of forty-three has just said both halves in the morning, and I am going to take the second half and leave the first one where it is.”

“If you want me to act on a count, then the count has to come into this bank with a hand on it and not a mouth. I act on what arrives. What arrived on that step this morning was a man telling me it would be quick.”

“Quick is not a kind of number. And there is a difference between a figure that arrives with a hand on it and a figure that arrives with a boy of thirteen standing next to the man who said it would be quick.”

“And I am not going to be the person who goes back to a room four hundred miles off and puts nine people’s counting in front of nine clerks and says act on this.”

“A figure of stitches in a gap cannot be turned into the number of days it stands for. I have said that on this landing more than once and I am saying it again, and it is the same sentence, and I am not going to improve it because improving it would be inventing something.”

“And the reason is the oldest one on this landing. The leaves it stands for were cut out and used, and nobody alive saw them cut, and a thing nobody saw cannot be turned back into a count of mornings by anybody, including me, including a boy of thirteen, including a man of forty-three with his hand on a stone.”

“That is the thirty-first morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it on the first day of the hundred and forty-ninth week, and there is no gap in it and there was no gap in it this morning.”

Barnaby Crove asked for a day against the ninth line for the hundred and sixty-seventh morning, and said what he thought about a morning where nothing was counted.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for a hundred and sixty-seven mornings, which is eight hundred and eighty-eight less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“A hundred and sixty-seven. And nobody counted anything this morning and about four of you are disappointed, and I am not, and I am going to say why.”

“Counting is cheap. A man of forty-three said it out loud with nothing between the two halves of it and I heard both halves and I am the only man on this bank who could not have checked either of them for myself.”

“That is what I have been doing for eleven years. That is the whole of it. Eleven years of the cheap half.”

“And nobody on that bank is going to be thanked for the expensive half, because none of us has done it yet.”

Twenty-eight wrong things in twenty-eight mornings went out that morning with a man of thirty-one standing on the step not saying what he came to say, and about four of them stayed until the light went off it, and the gap in that stitching was exactly the size it had been on the Saturday of the hundred and forty-seventh week.

# Chapter 899: Eighty-Eight Weeks And A Line In Another Hand

Fifth day of the hundred and fiftieth week. His six hundred and forty-ninth morning. Four hundred and ninety-nine days after the settlement. The fever eighty-eight weeks old. Four hundred and fifty-three days since the division. Three hundred and ninety-nine days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Four inches came up on the top step at about the seventh hour, with seven days of the coming back and about ninety of those ninety steps under, and that was a man able to walk all ninety dry-footed again by the seventh hour on a Saturday. The man of forty-three gave the three figures and gave the shape of the morning, because it comes out on a Saturday.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, has held that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, and has never once added to a figure he has given on a seventh Saturday.

“Four at the seventh hour, seven days of the coming back, and about ninety of those ninety steps under, on the fifth day of the hundred and fiftieth week, and it is a Saturday.”

“It came off all ninety yesterday morning at about the sixth hour and that was the twenty-ninth time, and this morning it is four and there is nothing in that which is worth anybody’s morning.”

“And the fever is eighty-eight weeks old this morning and there is no figure of days with it. That is the shape of a seventh Saturday. I have given that figure on every seventh Saturday for nineteen years and I have never once added to it and I am not going to start on the last one of them.”

Then the woman who holds the ninth of the nine entered one line on a sheet of paper in her own hand, in about nine ears, and read it out, and said what it was and was not.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years in a room four hundred miles off with nine clerks in it, and holds the ninth of the nine positions, and has entered one line this morning in her own hand on a sheet with nothing at the head of it.

“On this sheet, in my hand, on this morning: on the second day of the hundred and fiftieth week a woman carried one chair out of her own room into her own passage and it stood there until the third hour of the next day and she carried it back herself.”

“That is the whole line. It is about one thing, done on one day, by one person. It is not a figure of how many things she does in a morning. It is not a figure of how many of the twenty-nine are in that room. And it is no figure of anybody at all.”

“I entered it because I saw it. Nobody asked me to. Nobody told me what to write and nobody suggested a wording and nobody in that room or out of it has read this sheet before now.”

“And I entered it in my own hand and that is the entire point of it, and I am going to say what the entire point of it is: a person can be shown a line that somebody else wrote, and that is all a person can be shown, and it took nineteen years of a room nobody else went into to get one line.”

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and forty-six days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and forty-six is eight hundred and ninety-nine less six hundred and fifty-three.

“No. And I will not say it twice. I will not say it on this step. And I will not say it so that it is not a secret.”

“That is the sixth thing I have been asked for by somebody who meant well and it is the first one where being asked twice has been suggested as the safer shape.”

“A figure said twice is not a figure said safely. It is a figure said twice. About four of you will write it down twice and then somebody in nine weeks is going to ask which of the two times was the one that mattered, and there is not going to be an answer, because there was only ever one time and the second one is a copy and copies are how things stop being checkable.”

“It was not a secret yesterday. There were about five people in a room with a door shut and I said it once and a man of fifty-four was one of them and he is the only man on this landing who cannot write it down, and he has held it since yesterday afternoon and has not said it.”

“If you want it safer than that then you want it on the counter and on the lane and on that sheet, and I have just watched a woman put one line on a sheet today and I am not going to help you fill it up before the light goes off.”

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and forty-five days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and fourteen days old.

“Three hundred and forty-five days in this county, and the cut across this palm is two hundred and fourteen days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is a hundred and ninety-nine days old, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Thirty-nine wrong things in thirty-nine mornings, and here is the thirty-ninth. That is a chair standing in a passage. And that is the first line anybody has entered against anybody in this county in nineteen years, and it is on a sheet of paper with no rule at the head of it.”

“Then put the other one under it. Under her chair, in my hand or hers. Sixty-three, and the woman who holds it, and the day it came into this bank, and about nine of us in a room. It would be the second line and it would make the first line mean something, because a first line on a page is nobody’s line and a second line is a record.”

“No. And it is not because it is you. There is no version of that sheet with two things on it, and I said that yesterday on the way out of that room and I am not going to unsay it because you have come at it from a new direction this morning.”

“A first line on a page is nobody’s line. That is what it is for. The second line is what would make it a page of a thing, and the moment there are two things on it, it is a book, and I have not got a book and I did not ask for a book.”

“I have wanted to be the second person on something since the first morning I stood on that step, and there is not going to be a second person on that sheet, and I am not going to be one on a room either.”

Then the woman of forty-four read her own line, out loud, and said nobody was to be thanked for it, and said she was not putting her question down a sixth time.

Orla Fennimore is forty-four, has kept twenty-nine chairs at thirteen shillings and fourpence a year for nineteen years, has one line on a sheet of paper in another person’s hand, and has put her question down five times and is not going to put it down a sixth.

“That is my chair and my passage and my hands, and I have been putting it down in my own hand every morning for nineteen years, and this morning it is in somebody else’s hand and that is the whole of the difference and I am not going to be made to say anything else about it.”

“And nobody is to be thanked. Not her and not the boy and not the four of you who stood in my room and not me. If I am thanked for this then it turns into a thing that was done for me, and it was not done for me, it was done because she saw a chair.”

“I am not thanking her. I am going to have to work out what I do with a page that somebody else wrote on, and that is going to take me the rest of the week and probably longer, and it is not a gift and it is not a settlement and it does not bind her or me or anybody.”

“And I am not putting my question down a sixth time. It is still open and it is the same question and one word of it has not moved and it is not going to move because there is one line on a sheet.”

Barnaby Crove asked for a day against the ninth line for the hundred and seventy-eighth morning, and said what a hand is.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for a hundred and seventy-eight mornings, which is eight hundred and ninety-nine less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“A hundred and seventy-eight. And I cannot read, and I have just had a woman read a line out to about nine of us that says a woman carried a chair, and I have got it, and I did not have to ask anybody for anything to get it.”

“And I am not going to say that is the same as the thing I have been asking for. It is not. What I have been asking for is a day against a line on a book four hundred miles off, and what I got this morning is a day against a chair in a passage, and those are two different things and about four of you have already put them next to each other in your heads.”

“A hand is a hand. I know that much without reading it. She wrote it and it is hers and it is not mine and it is not the boy’s and it is not yours. That is the first thing anybody has put in front of me in eleven years that has somebody else’s name on it in a way I can be sure about.”

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and forty-six days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up.

“Two hundred and forty-six days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.”

“Nobody asked me for the count I am keeping. That is thirty-eight mornings, and a man of fifty-four just said there are two different things and about four of you have already put them next to each other, and he is right and nobody is going to say he is right except me, and I am thirteen and I do not count.”

Sena Dorr entered today’s line on the ninth run at about the tenth hour and read it out herself on the fifth day of the hundred and fiftieth week, with a sheet of paper in her other hand.

“That is the forty-second morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing.”

“And I am holding a sheet with one line on it in the other hand, and I would like about nine of you to notice that the two things are not the same kind of thing and that I am not going to put them next to each other.”

The sheet was lying on the counter at about the eleventh hour with one line on it in a hand that was not the woman’s own, and nothing else on it, and about four of them had looked at it and had not touched it and were not going to.

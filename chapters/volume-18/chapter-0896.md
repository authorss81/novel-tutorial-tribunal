# Chapter 896: The Chair Is Put Out And Nobody Writes Anything On The Sheet

Second day of the hundred and fiftieth week. His six hundred and forty-sixth morning. Four hundred and ninety-six days after the settlement. The fever eighty-seven weeks and four days old. Four hundred and fifty days since the division. Three hundred and ninety-six days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Sixteen inches held on the top step at about the seventh hour and it was holding at that figure, with two days of the coming back and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave the three figures and said that on this landing a bank that holds is doing more than a bank that is coming up, and that about four of them have never understood that.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, has held that landing nineteen years, and has got up at the fifth hour on every morning of them for nineteen years. He gives four figures off one stone and has never once given a fifth.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, two days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the second day of the hundred and fiftieth week.”

“A bank coming up is a bank doing something. A bank holding is a bank that has already done it and is not doing anything else, and that is the better of the two, and I have never once been able to say that to a bank full of people in nineteen years.”

Then the woman of forty-four put one of her twenty-nine chairs out into her own passage and left it there, and told about nine of them she had not chosen who was coming.

Orla Fennimore is forty-four, keeps twenty-nine chairs, and has kept them for nineteen years at thirteen shillings and fourpence a year. She is not going to put her question down a sixth time and nobody has answered it five.

“It is in the passage. It has been in the passage since the seventh hour and it is going to be in the passage when I go to bed and it is going to be in the passage when I get up, and I carried it out with my own hands and nobody helped me and nobody was asked to.”

“Twenty-eight in the room and one in the passage and I am not going to be asked about it.”

“You are all going to want to know who chose the four of you. Nobody did. I said on the seventh and last day of the hundred and forty-ninth week that I am not choosing, and the first four people who came up my road at the second hour are the four people who are going in, and two of them I had spoken to that morning and two of them I had not spoken to that week.”

“And it does not matter which two those were and I am not going to be asked. It is a room. It is not a reward for turning up and I am not going to have it turned into one by about four of you in the next two days.”
Then a man of thirty-one said the count should be read into the ninth run as the explanation of the gap, and was refused by the woman who keeps it, in her own mouth.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and forty-two days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and eleven days old.

“Three hundred and forty-two days in this county, and the cut across this palm is two hundred and eleven days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is a hundred and ninety-six days old, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Thirty-six wrong things in thirty-six mornings, and here is the thirty-sixth. When she enters the line about a chair on the fifth day of the hundred and fiftieth week, she enters the other one too.”

“Not the count. The count is not a day. I am not asking a woman to enter a figure of stitches as a day, because I know that and I have said so every morning this week.”

“The explanation. The gap at the front of that run has never had anything written next to it in twenty-three years. A line on a run of days says what was done that day. On the day she puts the chair line down, she puts a second line on the same leaf that says why the front of that book is the shape it is, and that is what a record is for.”

“No. And here is the whole of it, and it is one sentence and I am not going to give you four sentences about it.”

“The run of days takes a line about a day that was done. There is nothing else in it. There is no column on it for anything and there is no leaf on it for anything else, and I have been putting one line a day on it for twenty-three years and I know exactly what it takes.”

“If I write why the front of it is the shape it is, then I have entered a line on a day-book that is not about a day, and then anybody who comes along afterwards has to decide whether that day was a day where I sat down and wrote an explanation, or a day where nothing happened and I wrote on it anyway.”

“And there are nine clerks in a room four hundred miles off who would have to decide that, and they would decide it wrongly, and it would be my hand at the bottom of it.”

“You have been refused for six days running and every day it has been a different shape and today it was the closest you have got to being right, and I am not going to say so, and I am not going to be the woman who says so on a Wednesday morning.”

Barnaby Crove asked for a day against the ninth line for the hundred and seventy-fifth morning, on the step outside the room, in the middle of the day.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for a hundred and seventy-five mornings, which is eight hundred and ninety-six less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“A hundred and seventy-five. And I am going to say this on the step and not through the door, because nobody has asked me anything and I am not going to stand in the doorway of a woman in my own passage and be in the way of it.”

“I stood outside a door with the gravel for eleven years and nobody ever told me that was the same thing as this. It is not the same thing and it is the nearest thing anybody has said to me.”

“A woman of forty-four put a chair in her passage at the seventh hour and told nine of us about it, and I have been putting gravel down a cut at the fourth hour since before she started counting, and nobody has ever told me about the gravel.”

“That is the whole of what I have come to say on a Wednesday and I am not going to make it into anything it is not.”

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and forty-three days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and forty-three is eight hundred and ninety-six less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and forty-three days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.”

“Nobody asked me for the count I am keeping. That is thirty-five mornings, and tomorrow it is going to be said out loud in a room by me, and it is still not the count I am keeping and I would like about four of you to be able to tell those two apart afterwards.”

“And a man of thirty-one has spent the whole week offering to write things down and he has been refused every morning, and he is still going to be offered a room tomorrow if he turns up first.”

Sena Dorr entered today’s line on the ninth run at about the tenth hour and read it out herself on the second day of the hundred and fiftieth week, and put the bundle on the counter where it had been.

“That is the thirty-ninth morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing.”

“I put the bundle back on the counter this morning where I put it the first time, and about four of you looked at it, and I am going to say the one thing about it before tomorrow. It is going into that room at the second hour with the door shut, and it is coming back out on to this counter at the ninth hour, and it is not going any further than that.”

Four of them went up a road out of that landing after the second hour with a bundle of about four inches under one arm, and a woman of forty-four went in front of them, and a chair was standing in her passage the whole time.

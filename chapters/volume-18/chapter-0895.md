# Chapter 895: The Ordinary Form And A Sheet With Nothing On It

First day of the hundred and fiftieth week. His six hundred and forty-fifth morning. Four hundred and ninety-five days after the settlement. The fever eighty-seven weeks and three days old. Four hundred and forty-nine days since the division. Three hundred and ninety-five days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

The bank was up and had stopped. Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and holding at that figure, three days of the coming back and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave the three figures and confirmed the day he had named on the Monday, with the week on it, in about nine ears.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, has held that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone at about the seventh hour, and has never once given a fifth.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, three days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the first day of the hundred and fiftieth week, which is a Tuesday.”

“I said on the seventh and last day of the hundred and forty-ninth week that today is the first day of the hundred and fiftieth week and it is a Tuesday. It is. I am saying it again on the day rather than the night before because I said it a day early about the bank and I am not going to be early about a week as well.”

Then the woman of forty-four said the ordinary form out loud, in about nine ears, and said it was not hers, and said it binds nobody.

Orla Fennimore is forty-four, has kept twenty-nine chairs for nineteen years in a room nobody else goes into, and has put her question down five times and is not going to put it down a sixth. She has known what a line nobody else entered is since about the twelfth year.

“A position may be checked from the day its record starts and may not be checked before it. That is the ordinary form and I am saying it out loud on the first day of a hundred and fiftieth week because about four of you have been waiting all morning for me to say something new and this is the new thing this week.”

“And it is not mine. A woman of forty-nine said it on the second day of the hundred and forty-eighth week in about nine of your ears and it is hers and I am not improving it and I am not giving it a name and I am not putting a word in front of it.”

“Here is what it is and here is what it is not, and I want it in about nine ears on a Tuesday. You can check a position from the day its record starts. That is all it does. It does not answer my question and it was never going to answer my question and I am not going to be disappointed in front of nine of you.”

“And it binds nobody. It protects nobody. It is not a remedy and it is not an institution and it is not a seal and it does not fasten anything, and I stopped the word seal on this step yesterday and I am not letting it back in today.”

Then the woman who holds the ninth of the nine gave a woman of forty-four a piece of paper, and named what it was not, four times.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years in a room four hundred miles off with nine clerks in it, and holds the ninth of the nine positions. She has acted on what arrived, in her own hand, for twenty-three years, and that is the whole of her working.

“Here. That is a sheet. It came out of a book four hundred miles off with a gap in the stitching at the front of it, and it has nothing on it, and there is no rule printed on it and there is no column and there is no box for a name and there is nothing at the head of it.”

“It is not a form. I want that said in about nine ears and I want it said by somebody who is not me. A form is a thing that asks you a question and expects an answer and does not let you go until it has got one. That is the thing I have been in a room with nine clerks in for twenty-three years and this is not it.”

“No seat behind it. No clerk. No copy kept anywhere. If somebody does a thing and nobody enters it, then on that paper there is nothing, and there is nothing anywhere else either, and that is allowed and it is the whole of what it is for.”

“And I am going to enter one line on it myself, on my own hand, on my own judgment, about a thing I saw. And I am not going to tell you what the thing is until it has happened, because if I told you now you would all go and do it.”

“And nobody is to be thanked for this. Not me and not her. She is not being given a thing. She is being handed something that does not fit and she has to find a use for it herself and that is her morning and not a gift.”

Then a man of thirty-one said the ordinary form should be written at the head of the paper, and was told what a notice is, and was refused by the woman it was to have gone on.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and forty-one days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and ten days old.

“Three hundred and forty-one days in this county, and the cut across this palm is two hundred and ten days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is a hundred and ninety-five days old, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Thirty-five wrong things in thirty-five mornings, and here is the thirty-fifth. Put the ordinary form at the head of it. In ink. On that sheet, before anything goes on it.”

“Then anybody who comes into that room sees the rule before they see the line, and they know what a line on that paper is for, and you do not have to be in the room with nine of us to find out what the paper is.”

“No, and it is not about ink. It is that a rule at the head of a page turns the page into a notice, and a notice is a form with the door left open.”

“A notice tells you what is going to happen on it before it happens. And what is going to happen on that sheet is one thing a woman of forty-four did that nobody has seen yet, and if the rule is at the top of it then it is not a record of a thing that happened, it is the announcement of a thing that is going to.”

“And it binds nobody is what you said two minutes ago, and a notice binds everybody who reads it, because it tells them what they are looking at and there is nothing they can do with that except believe you or not believe you.”

“I have wanted to write something on something for eleven weeks. It has not come up. I am not going to say that out loud twice and I have said it once, and that is the whole of what I got today.”

Barnaby Crove asked for a day against the ninth line for the hundred and seventy-fourth morning, and said what a sheet of paper is worth to a man who cannot read.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for a hundred and seventy-four mornings, which is eight hundred and ninety-five less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“A hundred and seventy-four. And a woman of forty-nine has given a woman of forty-four a sheet of paper with nothing on it, and nobody is to be thanked for it, and about four of you are looking at me to see whether I am going to say something.”

“I cannot read it. That is the first thing and I want it said before anybody else says the other thing about it.”

“So it is worth nothing to me and it is not nothing to her, and those are two different figures about one sheet of paper, and I have known since the sixth day of the hundred and forty-eighth week that you all come back here with two figures about one thing and put them next to each other and make one big one.”

“I have been holding a number in my head for eleven years because there is nowhere to put it, and if that woman puts one line on that paper in her own hand, then there will be a page in this county with a thing on it that somebody else put there, and that is the first one anybody has ever said out loud to me.”

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and forty-two days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and forty-two is eight hundred and ninety-five less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and forty-two days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.”

“Nobody asked me for the count I am keeping. That is thirty-four mornings, and a woman has been handed a sheet with nothing on it and about four of you have already decided she is going to put something on it that is not a chair.”

“She is going to put a line on it that is about a chair. And if any of you writes anything else on it, in any other hand, then she will not be able to be shown the thing she asked for, and that is the only thing she has asked for in nineteen years.”

Sena Dorr entered today’s line on the ninth run at about the tenth hour and read it out herself on the first day of the hundred and fiftieth week.

“That is the thirty-eighth morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and I entered a sheet of nothing this morning as well, and the two things are not the same kind of thing and one of them is on a run of days and the other one is a piece of paper.”

The sheet was lying on the counter at about the eleventh hour with nothing on it, and it was going into a room on the second day of the hundred and fiftieth week with the door shut, and nobody had written anything at the head of it.

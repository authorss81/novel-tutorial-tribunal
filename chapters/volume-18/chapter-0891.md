# Chapter 891: A Room With The Door Shut

Fourth day of the hundred and forty-ninth week. His six hundred and forty-first morning. Four hundred and ninety-one days after the settlement. The fever eighty-six weeks and six days old. Four hundred and forty-five days since the division. Three hundred and ninety-one days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Four inches came up on the top step at about the seventh hour. That was seven days of the coming back, and about ninety of those ninety steps were under, and a man could walk all of them dry-footed at the seventh hour for the first time since the third day of the hundred and forty-ninth week, which was a Thursday, and that was the twenty-eighth time.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, has held that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, and has never once given a fifth.

“It was sixteen, two days of the coming back and about sixty of those ninety steps under on the third day of the hundred and forty-ninth week, and it came off all ninety at about the sixth hour this morning.”

“This morning it is four inches, seven days of the coming back, and about ninety of those ninety steps under. That is all three and I am not going to give you a fourth out of habit this morning.”

“And today is the fourth day of the hundred and forty-ninth week and it is a Friday, and I am saying that with the week on it because a Friday on this landing has been written down as a Tuesday twice and both times it was in a receipt.”

Then a boy of thirteen said where he would count the stitching and where he would not, and the second half of it was the reason for the first.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and thirty-eight days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and thirty-eight is eight hundred and ninety-one less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and thirty-eight days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.”

“Nobody asked me for the count I am keeping. That is thirty mornings, and it is the same figure as a man of fifty-four’s and it is not the same figure, and I have been keeping it since before he started.”

“And I am going to say the thing about the stitching, because I have been not saying it since the first day of the hundred and forty-ninth week and about four of you have noticed that I have been not saying it.”

“There is a bundle on that counter with a gap in the stitching at the front of it and I counted the stitches in that gap twice, in the lane, and got the same both times.”

“I am not going to say how many. I said on the first day of the hundred and forty-ninth week that I am not going to say how many and I have not said it and I am not going to unsay it on a Friday to get a thank you out of a Friday.”

“I am also not going to say it here. Not on the step. Not in about nine ears.”

Then the woman who keeps the twenty-nine chairs said what a room is, and said that hers is the one, and said that she is not going to choose who stands in it.

Orla Fennimore is forty-four, has kept twenty-nine chairs at thirteen shillings and fourpence a year for nineteen years, in a room on that landing that nobody else goes into, and has put her question down five times and is not going to put it down a sixth.

“Then the boy has not refused. About four of you were waiting for him to refuse and he has not.”

“He has refused a place. That is not the same thing and it is a smaller thing and he did it without being asked and I want the whole bank to notice that he did it without being asked.”

“There is one room on this landing where a thing can be said and not be carried out of it, and it is mine, and the twenty-nine chairs are in it, and nobody has stood in it but me for nineteen years.”

“And here is what I am going to say before anybody agrees to anything at all. I am not going to choose who stands in that room. The first four of you who come are the four of you who stand in it, and if one of them is a man I have not spoken to this week, then that is what the room has and not what anybody wanted.”

Then a man of thirty-one offered to be the one who wrote the figure down a second time, and was refused in a mouth that did not use the word clerk at all.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and thirty-seven days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and six days old.

“Three hundred and thirty-seven days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and six days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is a hundred and ninety-one days old, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it on any morning of this week.”

“Thirty-one wrong things in thirty-one mornings, and here is the thirty-first, and it is about paper. I will write it down.”

“Not to keep it and not for me. I will write it on the back of the piece of paper a woman of forty-four has carried in her apron for nineteen years, and I will write it where two people can see it, and then it will not be one boy’s word in one room. It will be a thing that is on the page.”

“You have not been allowed to write one line since you came up that road and you have been trying to be allowed to write one line every morning since, and you have found the door that is open, and it is not the door you are thinking it is.”

“No. A copy of one count is not two counts and it is not safer. It is further away.”

“I counted it twice in the lane and got the same twice, and the twice is the whole of the check. There is nothing in that but there is nothing wrong with it either.”

“But if it is on the back of her paper then in about nine weeks there will be a figure in a boy’s mouth and a figure on a piece of paper, and about four of you will add them, and it will be your handwriting at the bottom of it. And you will not have meant to and that is the part I am refusing.”

Then the woman who holds the ninth of the nine said what she is going to do with a figure when she gets one, out loud, on a Friday, with nobody in the room yet.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years in a room four hundred miles off with nine clerks in it, and holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and has entered a line in front of anybody every morning since the first day of the hundred and forty-eighth week.

“If a count comes into this bank with a hand on it, then it is mine to act on or not act on, and there is nobody on this landing who can make me do either.”

“I am going to say the other thing now and I am saying it out loud so that about four of you cannot build a better one out of it later. When I get a figure of stitches I am not going to carry it to a room four hundred miles off and put nine people’s counting in front of nine clerks.”

“And I am not going to be the person who says what a stitch is worth. A figure of stitches in a gap is a figure of stitches. It is not a figure of days and it does not become one because a woman standing on this step would like it to.”

“That is the same sentence I said on the first day of the hundred and forty-ninth week and I am not going to improve it, because improving it would be inventing something.”

Then a man of fifty-four asked for a day against the ninth line for the hundred and seventieth morning, and said he would like to know whether he was allowed in a room where a thing is said.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for a hundred and seventy mornings, which is eight hundred and ninety-one less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“A hundred and seventy. And I am going to ask the smallest thing anybody has asked me for on this bank and I am going to ask it out loud so that it is a thing I asked and not a thing I wanted.”

“I cannot read. If the count is said in a room and I am not in the room then I have not got it, and if I am in the room then I have got it and nobody has to write it down for me. That is the whole of what I want and I have not been able to say it until this morning.”

“And I am not going to be told it is a good question and I am not going to be told it is a stupid one. I would rather be refused without either.”

Wat Marshe is thirteen. He has been keeping a count that nobody has asked him for for thirty mornings, and he named the kind of figure it is on the sixth day of the hundred and forty-seventh week, and he has not given it and is not going to.

Sena Dorr entered today’s line on the ninth run at about the tenth hour and read it out herself on the fourth day of the hundred and forty-ninth week.

“That is the thirty-fourth morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and today is not a gap and there was no gap in it while I was writing.”

“And the arrangement is four hundred miles off and it is still answering. Nobody on this landing is in the room where it answers, and nobody is going to be put in it, and that is not going to change this week.”

About nine of them were on that landing at about the eleventh hour of a Friday, and a woman of forty-four had offered a room she has not let anybody into for nineteen years, and nobody had yet said they would come.

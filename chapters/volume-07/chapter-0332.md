# Chapter 332: A Man Of Fifty-Two On A Lane, And A Sentence He Does Not Finish

The plank at Coldharrow said sixty-four thousand and twenty-six at the second light on the fifth morning of the sixty-ninth week, and he got it himself, off the wall, at the foot of the ladder, which is the only way he has ever had a figure in this country. What the day behind it stood for was four bells and a stranger, and the stranger was him.

It was his eighty-second morning. He had come into the Briar March in the small hours and slept two hours in a byre with the smell of eleven winters in it and had walked the rest of the dark with the leg, and he was in the county, and the man of about thirty-eight was four days of road further on and could not be overtaken by a man on foot.

---

Nobody has been chosen.

He got that from a woman of about fifty at a bar at the bottom end of the lane, in a lane, at about the fourth hour, and she had it out before he asked, the way she says everything, and it was not the first time she had said it and he could tell that from the way she said it.

“Nobody,” she said. “And it is not a question anybody is asking this morning, it is a thing four people at that smithy said two days ago and I have had it off two of them on the way to work. *The man of thirty-eight went out of that market on a cart two days ago at a quarter past seven with a bag and a book he has not decided about, and there is a book on a shelf in a room over a smithy with nobody chosen to it, and the woman with the bag carries it nine times a year and has done for six years and is not going to be chosen either and is right, and I have stood behind that bar for twenty-two years and I am not going to be chosen either.*”

He did not ask her a second thing and he has not asked her anything in this county at all and he is not going to start.

---

Then, at about the seventh hour, he went and found the man of fifty-two, because a man who counts what crosses a place is at a place that things cross, and there is one such place in this village and it is a gate with a bar across it at the near end of the lane.

Tarin Keel was standing at the gate with a bit of lead in his coat and a handcart he had not unloaded, and he was counting, and he was not doing it for anybody, and nobody was standing near him.

He had not been asked to be there. Nobody sent him and nobody had sent for him and nobody in this lane had ever sent for him, and he had been in this county off and on for four months and had not been asked to do one single thing while he was in it, and he has never once said so to anybody, and he was not going to say it to a stranger with a bad leg at a gate on a morning.

*He did not offer. He did not say good morning. He did not put the cart down.*

That is the whole of what the man of thirty-one got out of the first nine minutes and it is more than he got out of a yard of nine people eleven days ago, and he stood off the bar with his weight on the good leg and let the counting go on.

---

Tarin stopped at about the ninth hour when there was nothing crossing, and turned round, and looked at the leg.

“You have come about the book,” he said.

“No,” said the man of thirty-one. “I have come about the six and I have come about that you count things and nobody has ever asked you what for, and I have not got a second question and I am not going to make one up in a lane at a gate to a man who is working.”

Nobody had said anything for nine seconds.

“Then stand there,” said Tarin Keel, “and do not stand in the road.”

---

And then he said the one true sentence, and he said it in the middle of a count, out loud, to a stranger, and it is the whole of what his nineteen years came to and he gave nine words of it and then stopped and did not go one step further.

“There was a gate with a levy behind it once,” he said. “*A levy on loads, and the loads were counted by a man who was not paid, and there is a dead man in the fourth line, and that is all I am going to say about it and you did not ask me and that is the only reason I have said it.*”

The man of thirty-one did not ask him what he was the only person in.

He had wanted to for four bands. He had asked it badly once, on a road four days up a lane from a market town, at about the fifth hour of a morning, and it had not been answered and he had gone on carrying it, and he had promised himself in a lane on the eightieth morning that he was not going to ask it again in a market, and this was a lane and a man and an opening and he did not ask it.

What you were the only person in. The sentence was ready and it was nine words and it had been ready since the fifty-sixth morning and it was ready now with a dead man in the fourth line sitting in the air between the two of them, and he did not say it, and that was the hardest thing he has done in this country, and it cost him nothing, which is how he knows it was the right thing to do.

Tarin Keel went back to the cart and picked up the bar he had leaned against and did not say anything else about the gate, and the dead man in the fourth line, and he has not been asked since and he is not going to be.

---

The finding of the morning was not about the gate.

The honest version of a fund is a wage. He worked it out in a lane in four minutes and it is the same working every time and it never gets any better. Somebody has to be paid, and being paid is a thing a person does at a place on a day, and a place that pays a person is an office, and an office is a room with a door on it and a man in it who is answerable for what the room is for.

And there is no office in this county. There is a room over a smithy with a shelf in it and four people who cannot do it and a woman with a bag who will not be asked to, and there is no form in this country that says a man may appoint anybody, and there is no form that says a man may be paid for keeping a column, and there is nobody in four hundred miles whose job it is to decide it.

“So it is a shelf,” he said, out loud, in the lane, to the man of fifty-two, who had not asked.

“It is a shelf,” said Tarin Keel.

---

And here is where the day turned over, and it turned over in nine seconds and neither of them helped him with any of it.

No form in this country says a man may set a name at the foot of a thing, and he has said so in eleven yards in five counties and it is true and it has never once been the answer to anything, because a man does not need a form to write a name. A man of sixty-three wrote one on the back of a flour bill in a kitchen in eleven days without being asked and did not know he was doing it, and that is the only time in eighty-two mornings that a name has ever come to him, and it came out of a kitchen by accident, and a name said out loud at a plinth in a street is not a name in a place, and the two of them together do not make a person.

So the paper is not the difficulty. The paper is a scrap of flour bill. There is no form and there will not be a form and he has been using the absence of one as a reason to do nothing for eighty-two mornings, and that is a use and it is not a good one.

The difficulty is that a name on a scrap of paper makes a person, and he has not got one, and every person in this county he has met in eighty-two mornings who could do it has said no or has said nothing, and the man of thirty-four is on a cart four days up the road, and the man of thirty-eight is four days up another road with a bag.

He stood at that gate for about an hour and a half and got nothing else out of it and did not try to.

---

A paper with a name on it in this country is a thing a person can be asked about for the rest of their life.

He said that out loud at about the eleventh hour, in the lane, to the man of fifty-two and the bar and the cart, and Tarin Keel put his bar down and said the second half of it in nine words, and then they both went to work.

“A man can be asked about it in a kitchen and in a lane and in a room over a smithy and at a gate, and there is nobody he can go to and say it is not about me, and that is the whole of what a name at the foot of a thing is.”

The day came to four bells and a stranger, which is seven, and the stranger was him, and he was the only stranger in the county that day that anybody had heard of, and a man of fifty-two counted what crossed a gate for four hours and a half and did not say one word about it again, and nobody thanked anybody and nobody was relieved and a book went on sitting on a shelf in a room in another county with nobody chosen to it.

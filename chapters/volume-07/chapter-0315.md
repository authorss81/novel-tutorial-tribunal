# Chapter 315: A Burial At Coldharrow

The plank at Coldharrow said sixty-three thousand nine hundred and twenty-one at the second light on the second morning of the sixty-seventh week, and the day it stood for was four bells and a burial, and a man of about sixty-eight who kept a barrow in the fourth house of this village had died three days before and was buried before the last bell on the second morning of the sixty-seventh week, and the woman of forty-one put a stroke on the plank for it at about the seventh hour with a cloth in her hand.

It was his sixty-fifth morning. Nothing was being asked of anybody in Coldharrow that day and nothing was being asked of him, and he had a leg and a lane and no reason at all, and he went to the fourth house and then he stood in a yard with nine people in it for about an hour and forty minutes and said almost nothing.

---

Nobody in this village could tell him what the dead man did.

He was about sixty-eight and he kept a barrow and he had a bad chest in the winter and he had lived in the fourth house for thirty years and had never in his life been on the back of a flour bill or in a room over a smithy or on a bar or on a boundary. He mended a hedge. He was on the back of a list of nine men who mended the churchyard hedge, and that list is a list of nine men in a village of two hundred and ten people and it is the ninth commonest thing in Coldharrow.

That is the whole of him. A man of about sixty-eight with a barrow who died three days before.

---

The hole was in the corner of the churchyard behind the last of the good ground, and the man digging it was a man of about forty-four who does that for the parish and is paid out of a box and takes what is in the box, and nobody asked him anything about it and he asked nobody anything.

There were nine people in the yard. The man of about thirty-four was the dead man's son and had come from somewhere nine miles away and had not spoken for the first half hour, and a woman of sixty who lives in the sixth house had come about a dog, and the rest of them were neighbours and a man of twenty with a scythe who mends that hedge and had been at the far end of the ground since the second hour.

And the man of thirty-one stood at the back with a leg and no standing and was not asked anything by anybody, and that was the correct treatment and it was the first morning since the fifth of the sixty-sixth week that nobody wanted anything off him.

---

The stroke was the thing he could not get past.

She came down off the ladder at about the seventh hour and put the cloth over her shoulder and came across to the wall and looked at the plank, and the plank said what it said, and there was no way on that plank or in the whole of that county to tell a person which of the strokes on it was the one for a man of about sixty-eight who kept a barrow.

Not because it is difficult. Because it is not written. A day’s figure is a weighted number, and a weighted number cannot be turned back into what was done, and the woman of forty-one has been the only one who could have told anybody and has been asked four times in thirty-one years and has answered three of them and refused the fourth.

He could have asked her. He wanted to ask her badly enough that he got nine feet toward her and then thought better of it in a lane in Ashgill with a woman of fifty-one doing something to a churn, and what he thought about it was this: *if I ask her she will show me, and it will take nine seconds, and it will be the second time in a month a person has asked her something about that plank, and being asked is the only thing that has ever been any use to her, and I am about to spend the only useful thing anybody has ever got out of her on a stroke.*

So he did not ask her, and she went on to the graveside, and he was right.

---

At about the ninth hour, at the side of the yard, by the gate, which is where that kind of conversation goes in a village, a woman of sixty who lives in the sixth house asked the man of about thirty-four a question that had nothing whatever to do with any of the six arrangements in this county, or in any county four hundred miles off, or in this world.

“Will you keep his dog.”

He said thank you first, which people do here, and then he said no, and he said it in nine words, and it was the best answer anybody has given him.

“No, because I have got my father’s barrow and no cart and two acres behind this house and there is nothing in two acres for a dog that is eleven, and he knows this yard. *He has lain under that bench every night since he was six years old and he knows where the water is and he knows which of the three doors we use, and if I take him two fields off he will stand at the gate.* So it is not that I will not have him. It is that he has one place and I do not, and a dog with one place is the best thing there is.”

The woman of sixty said, “Then I will leave him be,” and that was the whole of it, and neither of them said anything else about it, and four people in that yard heard all of it and went on standing about.

And then she said, because she was sixty and it is what a person of sixty does at a burial: “Will you put him in the corner there, by the wall, and not out in the middle where they have to walk round him.”

“Yes, aunt,” said the man of about thirty-four.

That is the whole of it. That is a morning and it is the only morning since he came up this lane in which nothing was being asked of anybody, and two questions got asked and both of them were about a dog, and the man of about thirty-four said yes, and the man of about thirty-one did not say one word in a yard for an hour and forty minutes and nobody noticed that he was there, and that is the best thing that has happened to him in sixty-five days and he is not going to be able to use any of it.

---

The burial was before the last bell. The man of about thirty-four put him in the corner by the wall on his own, with his own hands, in four minutes, and did not ask anybody to help him and nobody helped him, and nine people stood in that corner and four of them had not been to a burial in the ground before.

And then the woman of forty-one stood at the side of it and said the dead man's name out loud, because there was nobody else in that yard who was going to.

Nobody had asked her to. That is the whole of it and it is the same shape as every other price that has been paid in this county for two volumes, and it is the shape of a person of a place doing a piece of work because they are the one who does it, and being the one who does it is not a thing anybody has ever thanked anybody for.

It took her four seconds and she did not raise her voice and she did it in the ordinary voice she uses for loads and for strangers and for the number on a wall, and nine people heard it and four of them understood all of it.

Afterwards she went up the ladder at about the eleventh hour and wiped the plank and put the cloth over her shoulder and went to do her day, and there is no way on that plank to tell which stroke was the burial, and nobody in Coldharrow asked her, and he did not ask her, and that is the whole of the morning and it is not a resolution of anything and it is a cost and it is hers.

The man of twenty with a scythe went back to the far end of the ground at about the noon hour and finished the hedge he had started at the second hour and ate his dinner on a wall, and nobody thanked him for the hedge, because nobody thanks anybody for a hedge.

And the day came to four bells and a burial, which is five, and five is a day that does not happen very often, and nothing whatever turned on it.

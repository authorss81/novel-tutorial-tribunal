# Chapter 323: Four Days Up A Lane, And A Man With A Pencil

There is no plank at the end of this street, and there is no figure on any wall at the second light, and there is no woman of forty-one in this market town, and nobody here has ever heard of a board at Coldharrow or of a lane that goes from village to village. A man of thirty-one came four days up that lane on the third morning of the sixty-eighth week to stand at a set of beam scales, and a stroke does not travel, and there is no instrument in this country that will carry one off a wall and into a street four days away, and a man who wanted a figure this morning would have had to have been standing at the foot of a ladder in another county at the second light, and he was not.

It was his seventy-third morning and the day came to four bells and a market, and the market was running, and four hundred loads go over that bridge in a week in the season, and he had four days of walking in him and had come for one thing.

---

There is a long street, and a cross at the top of it with a market cross on a step that is worn in the middle, and at the end of the street there is a weighbridge and a set of beam scales on a stone plinth that has been re-cut three times in a hundred years, and a man keeps them.

He was seventy and he had his hands behind his back and he did not turn round for forty minutes, and there were nine loads over the bridge in that time and the man of thirty-one stood nine feet off with a leg and said nothing whatever for four hours, which is the hardest thing he has done in this country and the only one of the four hours that anybody noticed.

He could have looked for the join. He has been able to do it for two years and he has told nine people in a market in this street that he can, and it takes about a second and a half, and it is the only instrument he owns.

He did not do it. That is a change and he noticed it doing it and he did not have a sentence ready for it and did not want one. *Six seams in seventy-three mornings and he has stopped looking, and the reason is that a man who can see a join in a market street at the end of a market morning and cannot say what either side of it is for is a man who will be standing in a great many gates for the rest of his life, and there is nothing on the far side of the seventh one.* He wrote that in his own head in four seconds and it did not help and it was the only thing he had.

---

The man of about seventy told him what the toll is for, and he told it unasked, in nine seconds, at about the sixth hour, to a stranger with a bad leg, and it is the first time in forty-four years he has said it out loud to anybody who was not the two of them.

He said it with his hands behind his back and he said it to the end of the street.

“The toll on a load pays for the standard on this plinth,” he said. “And for the water, and for the bridge, and for the wall in the churchyard, and for the cross. *It is not a price on a load. It is a share, and what is left over after the standard is what the town runs on, and it has run that way for sixty years and the cross is standing because of it.*”

Nobody said anything. Four people in that street were within hearing and two of them had heard it before and one of those two is a woman of about fifty-eight with a basket who went into a doorway when he said it, which she has been doing for nine years and which is not a refusal of him.

He waited.

“You are going to ask me what a share is divided by,” he said. “Everybody asks me that in nine words once they have been here a day. *It is divided by the loads, and the number of loads is in the market book, and the market book is in a house at the end of this street, and I have not had it out for two years, and you are not going to have it out either, and there is nobody in this town who would fetch it for you.*”

That was the whole of it. He put his hands further behind his back and looked at the beam.

---

The woman of about fifty-eight was in that street twice more before the middle of the day, with the basket, and she did not come to the bridge, and he did not go to her, and he had one thing to say to her and he did not say it.

He had said on a bridge eighteen days ago that he could tell nine people where their arrangements met. He had been four streets off a burning yard on the morning of the third day of the sixty-seventh week and had sat on a wall for nine minutes and had not said it, and he had carried it seven days up a lane and it is the same sentence, and there is no form in this country that says a man may say a thing nobody asked him for.

*If I am in the room, then what happens in that room is a thing a stranger did.* He had said that in a kitchen three miles up the other lane twelve days ago and had said it twice since and neither time it was an ask, and the reason he is not saying it to her now is not manners and it is not the ninth word she gave him on the bridge. It is that she is six months gone with a child and her father is going to find out tonight what it is like to be the only reader in a market, and a man who walks up to a woman of fifty-eight in a market street on the fourth day of a week and says, *I can see where your father's trade stops being his and starts being yours,* has bought nothing and spent the only thing he has, which is the fact that she already knows.

So he stood nine feet off and said nothing and the beam went up and down all day.

---

At about the ninth hour, when the light had gone flat and the street had emptied out into the yards, the man of about seventy turned round.

He had not turned round for forty minutes in the morning and he had not turned round for four hours and he turned round then, and he looked at the man of thirty-one's leg, and he did not ask him a question about his leg, and he asked him a question about a number.

“How many loads go over this bridge in a year.”

He said it in nine seconds and he said it the way a man says a thing he has never said, and the man of thirty-one understood inside four seconds that a man of seventy had just done the thing he had not done in front of anybody in forty-four years.

Nobody in that market had ever asked him a number. Not his daughter, who does the sums in her head and has been doing them since she was forty-nine. Not the man of about thirty-eight who came through in the spring with a cart and stood at that plinth for half an hour and asked one question about a wage. Not a man of about forty-four who has carried a load in and out of this market for thirty years. Forty-four years, four hundred loads a week in a season, and a stone plinth that has been re-cut three times, and nobody in that street has ever once stood at the end of it and asked the man on it what the numbers are.

He could not answer.

He had been in eleven villages in seventy-three mornings. He had a book of his own two miles behind him that could put two things in an order and could not date either of them, and a woman of forty-one at a wall who had the only seven weights in that county in her head, and a plank four days behind him with a figure on it that he could do nothing whatever with. He had no number for a market street in a county nobody living has stood in, and he said so, and he said it in four seconds, and it was the first time in this country he had told anybody the truth about what he is.

“I cannot do that,” he said. “I have not got it and I cannot get it and I am not going to make one up, and I have been in this country seventy-three days and the only numbers I have are off a wall four days behind me and they are not about loads.”

The man of seventy looked at him for a while with his hands behind his back.

“Then do not do it,” he said, “and stop standing at the end of my bridge in the rain. There is one person in four counties who could answer that and he is four days down your lane in a village that is not on it, and he counts other people's money for a living, and he is leaving that village in a fortnight, and he came through here in the spring on a cart and asked me what my daughter was paid, and I told him nothing is paid anywhere, and he has not been back.”

Nine loads went over. The beam came down and went up.

“He is the only man I have ever met who asked me a question about a figure and then wrote the answer down,” said the man of about seventy, “and he is going four valleys away and he has not asked me one.”

---

The man of thirty-one said the price of the technique out loud, in a lane, in the rain, on his own, to nobody, at about the tenth hour, and it is the fifth time he has said it and the first time he has said it about a thing he was not going to do.

“A person finds out about themselves,” he said, “and nobody asked her, and that is why it is the price and not a fee, and I have said it five times now in five places and I have never once been in the room when it arrived, and this morning I stood nine feet off for four hours and a man of seventy asked me the one thing nobody in that market has asked him in forty-four years, and it arrived, and it did not cost him anything that I could see, and it cost me the whole morning, and *I am going to go and ask that man a question about a figure in a yard, and I know what that costs a man of seventy who has not been asked a question about a figure in forty-four years, and I am going to do it in a yard and not in a street, because a man who asks a question like that in a street has asked it in front of four hundred people and a man who asks it in a yard has asked it in front of one.*”

---

He did not ask the woman of fifty-eight.

That was the decision and it was made at about the seventh hour of the third morning of the sixty-seventh week and not changed since, and it is the only decision he has made in seven days and it is a decision not to do a thing he can do.

He went at the last of the light to the end of the street and looked at the plinth and at the cross and at the wall of the churchyard at the other end of the town, which is a long dry wall four feet high with lime in the joints, and he has no way whatever of telling anybody in that town that those three things and a beam and a man and a woman of about fifty-eight are being held up by a slip in a foolscap book in a house he has not been in.

And the day came to four bells and a market, which is eight, and there was no figure in it, and the woman of fifty-eight was in a street with a basket and was not coming to the bridge, and he did not say the one thing he had come up a lane to say, and he is not going to say it, and nobody is going to thank him for any of it.

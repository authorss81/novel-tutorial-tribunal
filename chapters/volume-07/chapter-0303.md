# Chapter 303: What A Bell Costs, Said Out Loud

What the woman of forty-one wrote on the plank on the fourth morning of the sixty-fifth week, at the second light, was sixty-three thousand eight hundred and fifty-six, and it was four bells and two strangers, and the two strangers were a man and a boy with a handcart who had come up the lane together from the fourth house and had rung at two gates because that is what you do when you come up a lane together with a cart, and a boundary, and there is no column on that plank and there never was one.

It was the fifty-third morning of a man of thirty-one in this country, and he was standing in a kitchen in the third village up the lane with a cup of something he had not asked for, and the man of sixty-three was writing on the back of a flour bill.

---

Nobody asked him. That is the whole of the morning and it is the reason it happened.

He came into that kitchen at about the third hour and said that he was going to walk the four hundred yards of hedge on his own, that day, by himself, and that he was not asking anybody to come with him and was not asking to be counted, and that he would be back before the last bell. And the man of sixty-three said, “Right,” and turned the bill over, and wrote a name on the back of it under the eleven, and did not say a word while he did it.

About four hundred yards is not far. He walked it in about half an hour with a leg that is the leg, and he could see the join twice on it, and both times the join was a place and not a bargain, and both times he could not say what was on either side of it.

At the north end, where the boundary and the lane's own edge come together, the two of them meet. He stood in it and looked at it for about a minute and a half. It is the same join he has been able to see for about two years and it did not tell him anything at all, and there was a woman of about fifty-one doing something else in a kitchen behind him the whole time and she did not come out.

At the south end, where the boundary stops being any kind of line and becomes the edge of a field that belongs to a man who is asleep, it is the same, and it told him nothing.

*He can see a join anywhere.* That is the honest size of it. He walked four hundred yards of hedge that had been in one place for longer than the oldest person in that village could remember and it did him no good whatever, and the only thing he learned standing in it is that a man who can see a join everywhere and understands nothing is a man with no technique at all, and that this is the lowest he is going to be in this country and it is here on purpose.

He came back at about the fourth hour and about nine people were in that kitchen and the flour bill was on the table with eleven names on the back of it and a twelfth.

---

“Name?” said the man of sixty-three, not turning round.

“Vester. Ilyan.”

He wrote it. It took him about four seconds. It went on the back of a bill in a hand that is not good, under eleven names in four different hands going back a hundred and fifty years or more, and there was no date beside it and no day and no wage and nothing at the top of it except the price of flour, and the price of flour is on the other side.

He knew exactly what had just happened and he knew it in about the time it takes to say nothing.

A name at the foot of a thing is the only part of it anybody can be asked about. He has spent fifty-three days telling that to about four people in about nine yards. It is the whole of what he has as a method and it is the only instrument in eleven villages or in any county four hundred miles off that has ever turned a word into a person. And a man of sixty-three has just written one on the back of a bill, in a kitchen, at a table, without asking, because a stranger turned up and said he was going to walk the hedge.

He watched it happen and he did not say a word, and that is the correct thing, and the cost of it does not fall on the man of sixty-three, who does not know what he has done and is not going to be told, and is not a villain and has hidden nothing and will never be told by anybody.

The cost falls on the man of thirty-one, and it is the third time.

---

It is the same price, and it is the same price, and he had said it out loud in about nine yards four times in seven weeks and he said it again in that kitchen on the fourth morning of the sixty-fifth week because there is no instrument anywhere in the world that stops him and he has looked, and he was going to do it whether or not there was anybody to hear.

“I am on that bill,” he said. “And I want it said out loud in this kitchen before I say one single other thing, because I have done this twice in this county and both times somebody has found out about themselves. *The price is that a man of sixty-three has made me a person in a record because I asked to walk a hedge, and I did not ask to be made a person in a record, and he has not been told he is doing it, and I am not going to tell him, and that is the arrangement and it is mine and not his.*”

Nine people in that kitchen. About four of them understood all of it.

The woman of about fifty-one said, “It is a bill. It is a bill for a walk of a hedge.”

“It is,” he said. “And I am not saying it is not. I am saying I have watched this happen to a woman of forty-one over a figure in pencil at the foot of a sheet, and I said the sentence *a word is not a person* in a yard about a month ago and was refused, and I am on the back of this bill now, and it would be a different sentence if it were asked, and it is not going to be asked, and that is all I am going to say about it.”

Nobody said anything.

---

The turn came in the middle, and it came from a man of about thirty-eight who had come up the lane from the tenth village on his own account and had been in the kitchen for about an hour doing something else, which is what a man of thirty-eight does in a kitchen he is not at home in.

There were eleven names. Nine of the eleven came that morning and did the work in about two hours and went home. Two of the eleven had not come in a long time, and one of the two is a man of forty-four who has not come since the mill wheel was rebuilt, and nobody has taken him off, and nobody in that village can tell you the year.

The man of about thirty-eight said the thing about it in a voice a person uses when they have been doing a sum all morning.

“They would come,” he said. “Both of them. I count other people’s money for a living and I have been in this lane about nine times in two years and I have never seen either of them here and I am telling you they would come, and I will tell you why I know, and the reason is the worst reason anybody has ever given. *They would come because they would be ashamed not to.*”

Nobody said anything.

“That is the whole of it, and it is the reason that piece of paper cannot be the arrangement and I have not got a better one and I have been trying since the third hour. Coming is not the arrangement. *The arrangement is the hedge, and the hedge has been there since before the oldest woman in this village was born, and the arrangement did not need those two men this morning and it will not need them in nine days and it does not need any of the eleven of us. It needs the hedge and it needs somebody to walk it. The bill is the truth about who turns up and it is not the truth about the hedge, and those are two different things and one of them has eleven names on it and the other one has none.*”

The man of sixty-three said, “That is a thing to think about, and he thought about none of it.”

He thought about none of it. He went back to his drain at about the sixth hour and about four people in that kitchen understood that he was not going to think about it and were not going to ask.

---

And then the arithmetic happened, and it happened because there was nothing else to do and because somebody said the word bell.

The woman of about fifty-one produced the first figure out of her own memory, and it was a bell at four shillings the pound for the metal and eleven shillings for the frame and the rope, and it was a bell of thirty-one pounds, and she got the metal right and the frame wrong by two shillings, and she was corrected by a man of about thirty-eight who counts other people's money for a living and who was in that kitchen on his own account and had not been sent for by anybody.

“A bell, hung, with a rope, is six pounds and fifteen,” said the man of about thirty-eight, without raising his voice. “Thirty-one pounds of metal at four shillings a pound is one thousand four hundred and forty-eight pence. The frame and the rope is thirteen shillings, which is a hundred and fifty-six pence. One thousand four hundred and forty-eight and a hundred and fifty-six is one thousand six hundred and forty-four pence, and one thousand six hundred and forty-four pence is six pounds and seventeen, and she was out by two shillings on the frame and I am going to come back to the two shillings in a minute because it is the two shillings that matter and not hers.”

“Go on,” said the woman of about fifty-one.

“A keeper is ninepence a day. A hundred and forty days. Nine times a hundred and forty is one thousand two hundred and sixty pence, and one thousand two hundred and sixty pence is five pounds and five shillings a year, and that is the wage of the woman who keeps the count in the first village on this lane, and she is the only one anybody in eleven villages can ask anything of.

“One thousand six hundred and forty-four less one thousand two hundred and sixty is three hundred and eighty-four pence. Three hundred and eighty-four pence is one pound and four. *And that is not a figure anybody should be saying out loud in a kitchen and I have said it and here it is, and nobody in this room is going to be able to forget it, and that is what a wrong figure costs in a country with no room and no clerk.*”

He put his thumb on the table.

“The frame and the rope is eleven shillings, which is a hundred and thirty-two pence. She was right and I was wrong, and I am a man who counts other people’s money and I have been wrong in a kitchen in front of nine people and somebody in that kitchen had better put it in the book, and nobody will. Thirty-one pounds at four shillings a pound is one thousand four hundred and forty-eight pence, and one thousand four hundred and forty-eight and a hundred and thirty-two is one thousand six hundred and twenty pence. *And one thousand six hundred and twenty pence is six pounds and fifteen.*”

Nobody said anything.

“And one thousand six hundred and twenty less one thousand two hundred and sixty is three hundred and sixty pence. Three hundred and sixty pence is one pound and ten shillings, and a pound and ten shillings is a keeper’s year plus a pound and ten, every year, for as long as somebody is alive to ring it and somebody is paid to ring it.”

And the man of sixty-three said it, in his own mouth, in one sentence, out loud, in front of the room, because nobody in that kitchen had asked him and because he had been the fourth name on that bill for thirty-one years and had never been paid.

“A bell is six and fifteen,” said the man of sixty-three. “A keeper is five and five. That is one and ten, every year, and that is the whole of it.”

Nobody in that kitchen had ever had that comparison made in front of them. Not in that kitchen, not in that village, not in that lane. The man of sixty-three had walked a boundary for thirty-one years for nothing, and he did not know what to do with money, and the room went quiet in the way a kitchen of about nine people goes quiet, and a woman of about fifty-one put her hand flat on the table and left it there.

He has a name on the back of a bill in a village three miles up the lane, and a leg, and a bad road in front of him, and a standing of nobody's to spend. He is going back on the ninth day, when eleven people are expected and nine of them are the ones who come. He has not written a word down, and there is no form in eleven villages that says a man may put a thing in front of a room, and there is no room, and the name is on the paper and the paper is on a table and the price of the flour is on the other side of it.

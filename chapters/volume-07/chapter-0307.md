# Chapter 307: The Ninth In The Line

What the woman of forty-one wrote on the plank at Coldharrow on the first morning of the sixty-sixth week, at the second light, was sixty-three thousand eight hundred and seventy-four, and he had it from a man of about thirty-eight who had been at Coldharrow on the second day of the week on other business, and who had said, in a lane, without being asked, that he had gone up to the tower and looked at the wall, and then had said the number out loud in the manner of a man who has been adding things up in his head for eleven years and does not need the paper.

It was his fifty-seventh morning in this country and he was eleven miles down the lane and off it at a place with a name on a sign and a bar, and the stranger of that day was a woman of about thirty who was standing in a gateway not doing anything.

---

Nobody sent for him. There are about forty people in this hamlet and eleven houses and a burying ground on a rise behind the last of them, and the woman in the gateway had come out of her own house to shut a door against a wind and had found a stranger standing on the green looking at the ground.

“Right,” she said. “You have come about the ground.”

“I have come about the ground.”

“You have,” she said, “and I am going to tell you what I am going to tell you and then I am going to shut this door, and if you have come about anything else you will have come to the wrong place at the wrong time and the door is the same either way.”

She shut the door. She did not lock it. She leaned on it with her back to it and stayed there, which is a thing a person does when they have decided not to run and not to go in.

“The one who digs is in our family,” she said. “It has been in our family since before the chapel was a chapel and it is written nowhere. It is not on a paper and there is no form for it and there is no day for it and nobody has ever been asked about it and nobody is going to be. When somebody dies, the one who digs is me. And I am thirty and I am the ninth.”

She said it in about nine seconds and she had clearly said most of it to herself before.

“My mother is eighth and she is sixty-eight and she is well. Her mother is not alive. Her mother’s brother is the one who digs when it rains, and he is about seventy-four, and he is not a member of the line and he has never once been asked how he came to be in it, and I know how he came to be in it, and so do you, and it is a rain. *That is the whole history of this ground. One wet day in about a hundred and forty years and a man who could shift more wet earth than the next in line, and he did, and he was never asked, and it has been the rule for three generations.*”

---

The man of about fifty-one who was right was at the gate when he came back, and he was right, and he was not being cruel, and that is the whole of what happened in that afternoon and it is the whole of why he is worth listening to and the whole of why there is nothing on any paper about it.

He had a spade over his shoulder that was not his and he was on his way down to a hedge at the far end of the ground where a drain has wanted cutting out since the autumn and he has got to it on four days out of five, and he stopped, and he said the thing that everybody in that hamlet knows and that nobody says in front of her.

“A ground with nobody in it to dig gets a different name in about forty years,” he said. “That is not a threat and it is not me being clever. It happens to nine of these in a hundred years and about four of those nine do not notice it for twenty. The register of the chapel gets a different parish against it, and then the people in the next parish bring their own, and after about forty years of that there is a name over this field that is a different name, and the man in it who dug the last hole is in the second field and the family that dug here is in the next village and has been for two generations. *I have seen it. Not here. Twice, and both times it took forty years and nobody anywhere put a stop to it.*”

He put the spade down.

“I am telling her the truth and she is thirty and she is the ninth and she is going to be the last, and there is no form in this country for stopping, and there is no form for stopping because nobody ever thought there would be a person who wanted to, and the day somebody first wanted to there was not a form. That is not a cruelty. It is a hole in a piece of paper and it has been there for four hundred years and she has found it.”

“Yes,” she said, from the door. “You have said all that and I have heard all of it and it is the reason I am standing here instead of doing my work, and I have had about four days to work out a sentence and this is it. *I want to be allowed to stop.*”

She said it in the plainest words she had. She did not say that she was tired. She did not say that she did not want it. She said the sentence and then she stood against a door and waited for somebody to fix it, and nobody in that hamlet was going to, and the man of fifty-one was right and the man of thirty-one had nothing whatever.

*The paper says the right thing and the paper has got no second half on it, and a person with no second half is a permission and not a right, and everybody in this country has one of those about the house and most of them have never had a use for it.* And a man of thirty-one did not think there would ever be a woman standing in a gateway wanting one either, until about the fourth hour of the day before.

---

A boy of seventeen came into that hamlet at about the fourth hour with a book under his arm and a stub of pencil behind his ear, and he had walked eleven miles and off it because he was going that way, and he was the only person in the country Ilyan had ever met who could put two things in an order and could not tell anybody when either of them had happened.

He stood at the edge of the burying ground for about half an hour with the book shut on his knee and did not open it, and then he said, without being asked, and it was the first time anybody had ever asked him about this:

“I cannot make out the order of that ground.”

“There is no order in it,” said the woman. “It is four hundred years and a family and it goes down.”

“There is an order in it,” said Bram Orren. “It is nine deep on the near side and I have been counting from the gate and the near side is deeper than the far side by about four, and a ground that has been dug by one family for four hundred years is deeper on the side the family came in from. That is the order. It is in the ground and not in anything anybody wrote, and it is the only thing in that field that is a record and it is a height.”

He opened the book at last and looked at one page for about four seconds and shut it again.

“There is a third place in that book that I have never been able to make out since I was twelve,” he said, “and it is the only thing in it I cannot do, and I have never met anybody who could tell me what it is, and I have stopped trying, and I have walked eleven miles today to stand in a field and look at the shape of a thing. That is the whole of my errand and I am not going to tell anybody else about it.”

---

The seam was there and it was the best one he had found and it was no use at all.

It was not in the ground. It was in the arrangement, and it sat exactly where the arrangement stopped being a family and started being a hamlet, and it was about four generations back, and there was a woman in it who is dead and has been dead for the better part of a hundred years, and the join runs from her to about nine graves on the far side of the field and he could see the whole of it in about a second and a half.

It is not a place. There is nothing on either side of it that anybody could be entered against, and there is no name at the foot of it, and there is no paper on either side of it, and there is nothing at all on either side of it except nine people who turn up.

He could not say what it was for. He has had that for about two years and it has never once been any use to him and it was no use at all here.

“Who is it for,” he asked, instead, standing at the gate with the wind off the rise. “Not who does it. What is the whole of it for. There has to be a sentence, because a man of fifty-two has told me that every arrangement in this country has got a sentence somebody could say if anybody asked, and I am asking.”

The woman of thirty looked at him for a long time.

“That you are not left,” she said. “That is the whole of it. That is what my grandmother’s people said when they started it and it has been the same sentence in every mouth in this ground for four hundred years, and nobody has ever written it down, and it does not go stale, and it is a better sentence than yours, and you can have it, and I am not going to say it again today.”

That is not a discovery. It is a thing everybody in that field knows and had always known, and the man of thirty-one was the only person in the hamlet who had to be told, and he had nothing to add to a burial custom and he knew it and did not add anything.

---

And then he said the other thing, and he said it in his own mouth, and it is the plainest sentence he has said in this country and the one the whole of what is coming is built on, and he said it to a boy and a woman and a man with a spade in a gateway on a rise in about nine seconds.

“I have to be able to say this and I have never said it to anybody and I have been in this country fifty-seven days. *I cannot be in two of these places at once.* There is a boundary four days up the lane with a man of sixty-three on it. There is a market with a set of scales on it. There is this field. There is a village with a fund in it. There is a mile of road with nine men on it. There is a valley off this lane with a woman in it. *Six.* And I can stand in exactly one of them at a time, and I have not got a technique for the other five, and I have got one for this one, and the one is worth nothing, and there is no room in this country that will let a man be in two places and there is no form that will let a man send anybody, and I have been carrying that about for a day and a half and I am putting it down here because these three people have not sent for me and have not asked me anything and can go home and forget it inside a minute.”

Nobody said anything. The wind came off the rise.

“I thought a man who could see where things joined would be able to use that in six places,” he said. “He cannot. That is what I have learned in fifty-seven days and it is worth about as much as the join.”

---

He did not ask her to say no. He did not tell her she was the ninth and ought to be proud of it. He did not offer to fix it, and he did not offer to find her a form, and there is no form in this country for stopping and there is not going to be one inside his own head either.

He asked her one ordinary question instead, at the gate, at about the sixth hour, with the light going.

“When it rains.”

She did not have to think about it, which is how he knew she had been asked it before.

“My mother’s brother,” she said. “About seventy-four. He shifts more wet earth than me and nobody asked him and he has not been asked since. *The line has already been broken once and a man put it back together on a wet afternoon and consulted nobody, and that is how it is nine deep and not eight.*”

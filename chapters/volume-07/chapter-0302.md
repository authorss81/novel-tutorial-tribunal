# Chapter 302: About Eleven People And A Flour Bill

What the woman of forty-one wrote on the plank on the third morning of the sixty-fifth week, at the second light, was sixty-three thousand eight hundred and fifty-three, and it was four bells and a stranger, and the stranger was him, and the woman of about fifty at the bar read him off her own board in about four seconds without breaking off what she was doing.

That is the whole of a courtesy and it is the first one anybody has done him in this country. He came through the bar at about the second hour and she put a stroke on the board and did not ask him his business, and he has been fifty-two mornings in a county where being counted is the nearest thing anybody has come to being spoken to, and he noticed it, and it took him about nine yards up the lane to work out that she had done it on purpose.

---

The village he came to is called Ashgill and it is about three miles up the lane and has about a hundred and ten people in it, and nobody there has ever called it a village out loud; it is a place where people are, and it has a lane through it with a bar at the north end that is not shut for a different reason from the other bars, because the bar there is not for a levy at all, it is for a bull that is nine years old and has opinions.

He got there at about the fourth hour with a leg that was the leg and no reason.

Nobody had sent for him. He was a stranger on a lane in a country that counts bells, and the thing he had come to look at was about a hundred yards of hedge and a length of field boundary, and he had walked nine miles to look at it because a man of fifty-two had said in a yard in the fortieth week of the year before last that a boundary walked is a thing with a weight of seven on a plank and that nobody in this county could tell him where a boundary was walked on any day that was not his own.

The walk was not happening.

He knew that in about four seconds, standing at the end of the lane, because a man of about sixty-three with a bad knee was in the field behind the last house with a barrow and a crowbar and was doing something to a drain, and the drain was not a boundary, and the hedge he was standing in front of was not the boundary either, and there was nobody else anywhere.

“You will be the one with the leg,” said the man of sixty-three, without straightening up. “I have heard about you from a miller. If you have come about the water you want the second house and the second house is the one with the hens on it.”

“I have not come about the water.”

“Then you have come about nothing, and you are welcome to stand in my field while I do this, and I am not going to stop, because it is going to take about an hour and I have to get it done before the ground dries.”

That was the whole of the first nine minutes.

---

The third village has one kitchen that anybody will let a stranger stand in, and it is the kitchen of the house at the end of the lane, and there is a woman of about fifty-one in it who is doing something else the entire time, and the something else that morning was a churn and a bucket and a look that made it clear she was not stopping for anybody.

The rota was on the back of a flour bill.

That is the whole of the institution, and the whole of what four hundred years of that village is doing is on the back of a piece of paper that somebody needed for another purpose. There is no hall. There is no book. There is no office, there is no officer, there is no wage, and there is nowhere in that village to keep a piece of paper except a kitchen table, and the kitchen table had a bill on it, and the bill had a price on it, and on the other side of the bill somebody had written eleven names.

He saw the join before he could stop himself seeing it.

It is instantaneous and it is not a reading and it cannot be written down, and it cannot be checked by anybody but him, and it is the only thing he has got. What he could see, standing in that doorway, was the exact place where the eleven names stopped being the rota and the flour price started being the flour price. Not a line. A place. About half an inch of the paper, at the top, where one sentence had ended and the next kind of sentence had begun, and the two of them had nothing whatever to do with one another and had been on the same sheet for as long as anybody could remember.

He said a version of it out loud, because he is a man who has learned to say things out loud in about nine yards and cannot help it, and it came out badly, and it came out as a question, and it was about two sentences long.

“There is a place on that bill where one of those stops,” he said, “and I can see it, and I cannot tell anybody what either side of it is for.”

The woman of about fifty-one laughed.

It was not unkind. That is the thing to be said about it, and he has thought about it since. She had a hand on the churn and she laughed the way a woman of fifty-one laughs at a thing that is not a bad thing but is not a thing either, and then she said, without stopping work, that there was a fold in that paper about a thumb's width above where his thumb had gone, and that the fold had been there since before she was in that kitchen, and that whatever he thought he could see was a crease, and that if he had wanted to know about the flour he should have asked her about the flour because the flour was the part of the bill anybody could be asked about.

Nine people in that kitchen and about four of them had stopped what they were doing.

He stood in a doorway in a village about a hundred and ten people and found out that the only thing nobody else in the world can do was look at a join, and that saying so out loud in a kitchen costs about four seconds and buys nothing, and that it was the first time anybody had found him ridiculous in fifty-two days and he had earned it.

“That is fair,” he said. “I have been waiting about a month for somebody to say that and I am not going to argue with it.”

“You are not going to argue with it because you have not got anything to argue with,” said the woman of about fifty-one, and went back to the churn.

---

Then the man of sixty-three came in from the field with the barrow and put it against the wall and drank a cup of something and did not ask the man of thirty-one anything at all, which was the correct thing and cost him a good deal of effort, and about four people in that kitchen noticed that it cost him.

He had a bad knee and he had walked the same length of boundary for about thirty-one years, and he was the fourth name on the bill, and the second one alive, and the eleven had been eleven for as long as the paper.

He is the fourth name. The two above him are dead. The one below him is a man of about forty-four who has not come since the year the mill wheel was rebuilt, and the seven under that are in the same condition in various ways, and there are names in the paper that belong to people who moved away about four hundred years ago and were never taken off, and nobody has ever taken a name off that bill, and nobody in that village could tell you when any of it started.

“You are the fourth one,” the man of sixty-three said, to nobody, in the way a man says a thing he has said to himself many times.

“I am the fourth one,” he said, to the man of thirty-one, and did not explain it, and the man of thirty-one did not ask, which was the first thing that had gone right between the two of them.

And then he said it.

“What is the walk for.”

Nobody had put the question. He had put it himself, in his own kitchen, in a voice with no weight on it at all, the way a man asks for the time.

The man of sixty-three put his cup down.

Nobody in that kitchen said anything. About nine people were in it. A churn went round. The man of sixty-three stood with his hand on the edge of a table with a barrow wheel in the mud on his boot, and he did not answer for long enough that the woman of about fifty-one looked up, and then he answered, and it was ordinary, and it took him about nine seconds.

“The grass,” he said. “That is the whole of it and there has never been any other reason. *We walk it so that nobody has to argue about where the grass is.*”

He said it the way a man says a thing that has been true for thirty-one years and that he has never had to defend, because there has never been anybody there to defend it against.

“There is about four hundred yards of hedge in that one,” he said, “and it has been in the same place for longer than the oldest person in this village can remember, and the oldest person in this village is a woman of eighty-one who was born on the other side of it, and the year her mother was born it was in the same place. And there is not one acre of paper about it anywhere in the world. Not one. No deed, no charter, no form, no entry, nothing. *Nobody has ever wanted one and I have never once been asked to make one.* And we walk it in nine days, and we have walked it in nine days since before I could carry a barrow, and it will be walked in nine days after I am dead, because there is nothing about me in it.”

---

They went out into the field together at about the sixth hour, and nobody asked them to, and about four people from the kitchen came and stood in the doorway and watched, and the two of them stood at a gate in a hedge and looked at a length of ground for about a quarter of an hour and neither of them said anything for most of it.

He was not asked to walk it. He was not turned away. At the end of it the man of sixty-three took the crowbar and the barrow and went to his drain and left him standing in a field, which is the whole of the welcome there is.

And then, at the gate, with his back to the hedge and the crowbar under his arm, the man of sixty-three said the thing that is the last line of that day and is the whole of what a man of thirty-one carried out of a village of a hundred and ten people:

*My father did it, and his father did it, and nobody ever said out loud what it was for, and if a person did not ask then nobody knew, and a thing nobody knows the reason for is a thing that stops when the last one stops.*

Then he went back to his drain.

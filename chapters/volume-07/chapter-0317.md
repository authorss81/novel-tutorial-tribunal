# Chapter 317: A Rain In The Hamlet, And A Man Of About Fifty-One Who Is Right

Sixty-three thousand nine hundred and thirty-four was what the plank at Coldharrow said at the second light on the fourth morning of the sixty-seventh week, and he was not in Coldharrow and had been told it, and the day it stood for was four bells and a stranger and a stranger, and one of the strangers was a carter's boy who came up the lane with a load for a house in the second village and went on, and nobody in Coldharrow knows his name and nobody asked.

It was his sixty-seventh morning and it was raining in the hamlet eleven miles down the lane and off it, and forty people live in that hamlet in eleven houses, and there is a burying ground on a rise behind the last of them.

---

Nobody can say who dug the first hole.

He asked it in the gateway at about the ninth hour, in the rain, standing under a lintel with his leg on one side of him and a spade on the other, and he had come eleven miles for the answer and the answer is that there is no answer, and that is a gap and not a lie, and it took two hours to find that out and almost nobody in that field thought it was strange that it could not be found.

The ground is deeper on the side the family came in from. Everybody in that hamlet will tell you that without being asked and four of them will show you, and it is a slope and not a story. The near gate is at the low end of it. The first hole anybody in that ground can point at is at the near gate, and it is the oldest-looking ground in the field, and there is nothing under it, and no marker, and nothing at the foot of it.

The ninth in the line did not dig it. The eighth did not dig it, and the eighth is sixty-eight and well and standing in her own doorway in the rain watching a stranger ask about her field. The tenth is not born. And the person who is about seventy-four and whose whole life in that field has been to shift more wet earth than anybody in it did not dig it either, and he is in his own house and has not been out of it since the last day of the sixty-sixth week, and nobody in that hamlet is going to say the next part out loud and he said it for them, at about the tenth hour, in nine seconds, without any weight on it, in his own kitchen, to his own family.

---

The man of about fifty-one with a spade was the one who was right, and he was still right, and nothing in that field this morning made him less right, and nobody was being cruel.

He had been at the end of the row that has the drain in it since the second hour, in the rain, and he came up the rise about the tenth hour with his hands full of the top of the field, and the woman of thirty went out to him, and the two of them had nine minutes and four people listening at the gateway and a man of thirty-one standing nine feet off not asking anything.

The man of fifty-one put the spade down in the wet and said the whole of it, and it took him nine seconds, and it is the truest sentence anybody said in that ground in a hundred and forty years.

“Nobody dug the first one,” he said. “That is what I have come up here to say and it is the only thing I have got, and I want to say it properly because you will not get a better answer than this one and there is not one coming.

“*The ground was like that when the family came to it.* The family came in at the near gate, and the near gate is at the soft end, and it is a soft place to get in at, and four hundred years of a family going in at the same soft place has put a slope on that field that you could set a water butt on. Nobody can say who dug first because there was no first. There was a ground that had been dug at for a long time by somebody, and it is not in this family and it never was and nobody alive has any idea what it was, and then the family came and dug in the same soft place because it was the way in, and after nine years it stopped being the easiest way in and it was just where you went.”

He picked the spade up again.

“That is not a mystery and it is not a lie and it is not anybody’s fault, and I have heard nine people in my life say there is a name at the bottom of that field, and there is not, and I have been wrong about most things for fifty-one years and I am not wrong about this.”

---

She asked him a question back.

That is the thing about her, and it is the thing the whole band is made of, and nobody in that field had noticed until that morning except one person. Every other person in the Briar March who a stranger has lately asked a thing of has taken the asking and used it or stood in it. She asked a question back, in the rain, in her own gateway, to a man of about fifty-one with a spade, and the question was about the only thing in the world she wants.

“Then how do I stop,” she said.

The man of fifty-one did not pretend not to know what she meant. He has known what she means for about a year and he has thought about it for about a year and he has not got a way, and that is the whole of what he is entitled to say to her and he said all of it in nine seconds and he did not say one word of it kindly, because kindly would have been a different thing and she would have known.

“You do not stop,” he said. “And I am not going to give you the other thing to hold, because you have had it off me once and I was wrong to hand it over.

“A line does not have an end in it. That is not my opinion, that is what a line is. It has nine in it and it had nine in it before your mother was born and it will have nine in it the morning after you are not in it, and there is nobody in this county with a form that hands a thing to somebody else, and the form that hands a thing to somebody else is the thing that does not exist, and the reason it does not exist is that nobody in four hundred years has ever wanted to be the one who was handed it.”

He shifted the spade to the other shoulder.

“What I will say to you is what I have been turning over for about a year and I have not got it finished. *Nobody in this field has ever chosen where to put a hole. Every hole in this ground is where the last hole made it easy to stand.* That is the arrangement. There is not one other thing in it. And the only thing I have thought of that would be different is a line somebody chose, and I have not got a way of choosing one, and if I had I would not have the standing to give it to you, because I have never been in this line and I got in on a wet day by shifting more earth than the next one, exactly like the man of seventy-four, and you know that because you told me in a gateway ten days ago.”

Nobody said anything. The rain came down the slope.

“That is the honest answer,” he said, “and I have been right about this field for thirty years and I have never once been able to do anything for anybody in it, and you are the ninth and you are thirty and you will be the last, and there is no form for stopping, and it is not a cruelty, and I have never known it to be anything else.”

---

And then she said the thing she wants, in her own mouth, for the second time in a week, and it is not longer the second time.

“I want to be allowed to stop,” she said. “That is the sentence and I have had it ready for about a year and I have now said it twice to two different men in a week, and it is not any longer the second time than it was the first, and nobody has given it to me and nobody has told me it cannot be given, and I am not going to ask either of those men again.”

She looked at the man of fifty-one.

“But I am going to say the other half of it once, because there is nobody here who has heard me say the first half twice. *I do not want out of it. I want it to be mine.* I want to be the one who decides where the next hole goes and I want somebody to write that down somewhere, and I am not asking him to do it because he has not got a form and he is not a clerk, and I am not asking you, and I have not got anybody to ask.”

Nobody was going to grant it. Nobody was going to say that it cannot be granted, because there is nobody in that field who knows, and the man of thirty-one was standing nine feet off a gateway with a bad leg and had said a sentence about seams once in a field in a week and it had not helped anybody, and the field had told him the same thing in the plainest words a person has ever been told it.

There is no money in any of it. That is not a hardship and it is not a virtue; it is that nobody has ever put a price on a hole, and a price is what you would put on it if a person were being paid, and nobody in this field is being paid and nobody is going to be, and the whole of the arrangement is nine people turning up in the rain.

---

The man of thirty-one went down off the rise at about the last of the light with his leg and his four days of walking and the two strangers of that day behind him, and he had asked the only question he had come for and the answer had been that there was nobody to give, and he was no further with the line than he had been on the fifty-seventh morning, and the woman of thirty was still the ninth, and the man of about seventy-four was still in his own house, and the man of about fifty-one was still right.

Nobody thanked him. Nobody was relieved. It rained all afternoon in a field with nine new holes in the top corner of it, and a boy of eleven dug two of them, because it is his grandmother's ground and nobody stops a boy of eleven, and nobody counted the boy.

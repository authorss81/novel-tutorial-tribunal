# Chapter 308: Fifteen Pounds Fifteen

There is no plank in the tenth village. What there is, is a woman of about thirty-four behind a bar at the end of this lane, eleven miles down and off it and a long way from the woman of thirty-four with a bar she cannot lift alone, four days the other way, with a board under her arm that has four letters and a stroke at the top of it, and about nine loads on a market morning, and she read every one of them off her own board and did not ask the man of thirty-one anything, because he had been in this village twice before and she counts strangers.

And what the man of thirty-one had that morning, about the figure on a wall at the bottom end of the same lane, was a number said out loud in a lane on the first morning of this week by a man of about thirty-eight who counts other people's money for a living and who has had it by heart ever since, and who said it again in a room over a smithy at about the second hour, without looking at anything, and got it right.

Sixty-three thousand eight hundred and seventy-eight. It was his fifty-eighth morning in this country and the tenth village was running a market and the day had come to four bells and a market, and there were about nine people in the room over the smithy, and they were there because the smith's wife keeps a room over her smithy for anybody with business with the fund, and every one of them in that room had business with the fund except him and the boy.

---

The fund is a bar with a book on it.

Three pence a load, taken at the bar of this village for as long as the bar has stood there, and the bar is the one at the head of the lane, and the woman of about thirty-four has stood at it for twenty-two years and takes about nine loads on the days the loads come, and there are a hundred and forty such days in a year in this county whatever anybody says about it.

He said the working out loud, in the room, in about nine seconds, because that is what this country does and because he was the man who was going to be asked about it.

“Three pence a load. About nine loads a day. A hundred and forty days. *One hundred and forty times nine is one thousand two hundred and sixty loads, and one thousand two hundred and sixty times three is three thousand seven hundred and eighty pence.* Three thousand seven hundred and eighty pence is fifteen pounds fifteen shillings, and that is the whole of what a village like this one raises in a year, and it is the same figure in every village that has got a bar with a word at the top of it, and there are eleven of those in a lane two days behind you.”

“Steady,” said the man of about thirty-eight. “One hundred and forty times nine. A hundred and forty times ten is one thousand four hundred, and a hundred and forty times one is a hundred and forty, and one thousand four hundred less a hundred and forty is one thousand two hundred and sixty. That is the loads. *And one thousand two hundred and sixty times three is three thousand seven hundred and eighty pence.* A pound is two hundred and forty pence, and three thousand seven hundred and eighty is three thousand six hundred pence of pounds and a hundred and eighty pence over, and a hundred and eighty pence is fifteen shillings to the penny. *So it is fifteen pounds and fifteen shillings a year, and I have never taken a halfpenny out of that book in eleven years and there is not one in it now.*”

Nobody said anything.

“You are right,” said the man of thirty-one. “It is a year. Say the rest of it.”

The rest of it took him about four minutes and he said it standing at the window with his back to a smithy that was not working.

“Fifteen pounds fifteen comes in over about nine loads a day for a hundred and forty days. And it goes out in the same week. *Every penny of it.* There is a man in the ninth house with a bad roof and there is a woman in the fourth house whose husband died in the spring and there are four in this village who have not had a load in this lane for two years and are not going to get one, and the book says who has had what and when, and the whole of what the book is for is that the money goes out the week it comes in, and it has gone out the week it comes in every week for about thirty-one years.”

“And who is paid.”

“Nobody,” said the man of about thirty-eight. “That is the finding and you have had it in four minutes, which is faster than I have had it in eleven years. *Nobody is paid. A fund that pays a person is a wage. A wage is an office. There is no office and there has never been an office and there is not going to be one, and I have been doing this for eleven years and I have never drawn a penny out of it and neither has anybody else, and if I ever did, that would be the end of it, because then it would be a thing with a man at the front of it and men leave.*”

He turned round.

“That is the whole of it. Fifteen pounds fifteen a village a year, out in the same week, to nobody who is a person in a record. And it works. It has worked for thirty-one years. Eleven villages do it and none of them has gone short. I have checked the other ten four times in eleven years and I have never found a hole in one and I have never once been thanked by a person, and I have never once been asked by a person whether I would like to stop.”

---

He has not told the fund.

That is the first fact about that room and it is the fact the whole of the morning is built on, and nobody in it knew it, and the man of thirty-eight had not said it and was not going to say it until somebody put him in front of nine people and made him say it in his own mouth.

He was moving in about a month. Four valleys away. There is a house and there is work and there is a woman who has been patient about it for two years and there is nobody in the four valleys who has ever heard of a bar with a word at the top of it and a book under it.

He had worked the whole of that out in his own head in about a week and had told nobody, and the reason he had told nobody is the reason the whole of it is the shape it is: *to hand it on, he has to stand in a room in front of about nine people and say, in his own mouth, that he cannot do it any more, and there are about four people in this country who understand what those words cost a person, and none of them is in that room.*

---

The seam was in the money and it made no difference to anybody in the room.

He could see it. It was not in the book and it was not in the bar and it was not in the village. It was where the money stopped being eleven villages’ and started being one man’s, and it was in a room, and it had been in that room for about thirty-one years, and the man of about thirty-eight had been standing inside it for eleven of them and had never known and neither had the village and neither had the four people in the ninth house with a bad roof.

He could see the join perfectly well. It was clear and it was exact and it was not a place and it was not a thing anybody could be shown.

And nobody in that room was frightened, and nobody said a word about it, and nothing in the morning turned on it, and about four people in the room went on doing what they were doing, and the smith's wife put a cup down, and the whole of what he has ever been able to do came down in that room to a fact nobody needed, and he had now used the only thing he has four times in front of ordinary people and it had been worth nothing four times, and he was going to keep it anyway, because a man who throws away the only thing he has on the grounds that it has never worked is a man who has decided what he is for without asking anybody.

---

He asked the question at about the fifth hour and he asked it in the plainest words he had, standing in the room over the smithy, and it was the first time in this country that anybody had asked anybody that.

“What happens to the count when you stop.”

The man of about thirty-eight did not answer for about nine seconds.

“The count does not stop,” he said. “A count is not a man. It is a column, and the column is in that book on that shelf, and the sums are in the front of it and I have never had to correct one of them in eleven years and I do not think there is a mistake in the front of that book at all.”

He looked at the shelf.

“*And there is one person alive who can read that column and I am going four valleys away in a month, and nobody has said the book is unreadable, because the book is not unreadable. There is one reader in the world for it and he is leaving.*”

He said it in about nine words and then he stopped, and about four people in that room understood all of it, and it was the sentence of the day, and it was said by a man of thirty-eight who is not a villain and is not a martyr and is a person doing a job he is about to stop doing.

---

Ilyan did not offer to take it.

He had spent two days saying nothing on a bridge in a market town and a day and a half on a cart, and he had four days of walking left in him and nowhere to put it, and there was a room over a smithy with a man in it who was going to leave a column on a shelf in a month.

He did not say *I will do it*, and he did not say *give it to me*, and he did not say the second thing either, which was that there must be somebody else in this country who can read a column, and he did not say that because in about four days he had met six people who can each say exactly what their side is for, and not one of them had said anything about a column, and he was not going to walk into a room and hand a man a stranger.

He wrote nothing down. There is no form in this country that says a man may put a thing in front of a room, and there is no room, and a second copy of a thing in this county is the thing that gets used, and he has written nothing down in fifty-eight days and there is a figure in white chalk at the foot of a woman's wall in a village four days behind him that is his and that nobody has asked him to wipe off.

His own name is not on the book. He checked, because he checked it in a press eleven miles off at about this time last year, and there is no name at the foot of anything, and there is no dash either, and nobody is going to put him there.

He stood in a room he was going to leave in a morning, and a man of about thirty-eight went on with a column, four loads and a debt, the way a man goes on with a thing he has decided to stop doing in a month, and the bar at the head of the lane took a load, and the woman of thirty-four read it off her own board without asking anybody anything, and the book stayed on the shelf where it had been for thirty-one years with one reader in the world for it and a month of that reader's notice left in it.

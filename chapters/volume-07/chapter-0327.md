# Chapter 327: A Day Nothing Is Asked On, In A County That Counts Everything

Sixty-three thousand nine hundred and ninety-seven was on the plank at Coldharrow at the second light on the seventh and last morning of the sixty-eighth week, and the man of about thirty-eight brought the number up the lane in his mouth and said it out loud at the plinth at about the second hour. The day behind it was four bells and a market and a debt called in, which is ten, and neither of the other two was in this market town, and there is a market running in this street this morning and a debt called in at a cross four feet away, and neither of them is on that plank.

It was his seventy-seventh morning, and the market was the biggest it has been in the four days he has been in this town, and carts went over that bridge before the middle of the day, and he was in the middle of all of it and asked nobody anything and nobody asked him anything, and that is the reason this morning is the shape it is and it is not a pause.

---

There is a wall between the yard of the woman of about sixty-one and the yard next door.

It is four feet high and nine feet long and it is dry and it is lime in the joints, and it has been down four times in eleven years, and it is always the same four feet, and the man next door leans a ladder against it to get at a gutter on the house side and does not know that he does it.

A man of about forty-one mends it. He has a barrow and about half a barrow of lime and a bucket of water and he does four feet of it in two hours and he does not take anything for it and has never taken anything for it and has never once said that he would.

He had not been in that lane since the spring. There was a debt called in on him on the fourth morning of the sixty-eighth week by a man from a town nine miles off, and four people in the street knew, and the spring wall had not been mended in it, and on the seventh morning of that week he was in the lane with his barrow, in the rain, mending four feet of wall.

---

The man of thirty-one was in that lane at about the tenth hour and he did not say one word for forty minutes, and then the woman of about sixty-one came out of her own door with a bucket, and she had heard about the debt and the whole of that street had heard about the debt, and she asked him the question.

“You have not mended it since the spring.”

“No,” he said.

“There is a debt on you.”

“There is,” he said. “It is not for the wall.”

She stood in her own doorway nine feet off with the bucket in her hand, and he was on the other side of the yard with a leg and nothing to say, and it is the best answer anybody has given him in a week and it is about a wall.

“You are going to ask me why,” he said, without being asked, and he did not stop working. “I have got one answer and I have had it for eleven years and it is not a good one, and I am going to give it to you, and not because you have earned it, but because you have been in that house fifty years and the other side of that wall is mine and it has been eleven years and nobody has ever asked me and I am tired of it not being asked.”

He put a stone in and tapped it.

“My mother is in the house on the other side,” he said. “*I have not once said that out loud in that street and I have thought about it nine times in eleven years, and every one of the nine times was a different day and the answer was the same every time.*”

He shifted the barrow.

“The answer is that it is her wall. And if it is down she stands at her back door in the cold and looks at a street she has looked at for fifty years. And I cannot make her do anything about it and you cannot either and neither can the man on the stones, and there is no form in this country that says a man may mend a wall for nothing, and there does not have to be one, because it is a wall and not an arrangement and nobody in that street is going to pay me and I am not going to ask and the four feet is the four feet and it has been down four times in eleven years and it will be down again in four years and I will be forty-five.”

The woman of about sixty-one did not say anything. She took the bucket in and shut the door on four inches and that is what she did with it, and four people in that street heard all of it, and he heard all of it and it was not about him and it was not about the six and it was not about the number on a slip in a foolscap book in that woman's back room.

He wrote nothing down. He did not even write it in his own head, which is the first time in seventy-seven mornings.

---

The market went on.

That is the whole of the day and it is the point of it, and he was in the middle of it from the second hour to the last of the light and he got nothing out of it and it was the best morning he has had in this country.

There is no bell in this town that anybody rings for anything. There is a market cross and a woman of twenty-nine who sweeps round it and keeps a list in a church nine miles off, and a stall row that sets up before the sun is on the roofs, and four hundred loads go over a bridge in a week of a season with a man on the stones counting them with his hands behind his back, and it is the ordinary morning of a market town that has four hundred people in it and is four days up a lane that goes from village to village and not by the ground.

A carter came over with nine quarters of bad chalk and would not go until his own mate moved the carriage by about a finger's width with his heel. A woman of about sixty-one came out of a doorway with a basket and put it down and went back in. Nine loads went over in the first hour and nine went over in the last hour and nobody stood about and nobody told anybody anything and nine people in that street had been told something enormous in a yard three days ago and not one of them was talking about it.

---

The woman of about fifty-eight was on a step at the end of the main street at about the eleventh hour with a basket, and she was not coming to the bridge, and he did not go to the step.

He had got a thing to say to her and he had made the decision to say it in a lane at the last of the light the previous afternoon and he had said out loud that he knew it was the wrong one, and it was the wrong one, and he was going to do it anyway, and the fact that he had four hours in a market morning in which to do something about it and did not do it in any of them is the only thing he got right that day.

He looked at the step twice from forty feet and the second time she was not on it, and he went and stood at the plinth instead, because there was nothing on the plinth for him and he had a leg and a lane four days down it to walk.

---

He asked the woman of twenty-nine at the cross one question, and it was the only question he asked anybody in the whole of that day, and it had nothing to do with the six arrangements in this county or in any county four hundred miles off, and it had nothing whatever to do with the number on a slip in a foolscap book in a back room at the end of that street.

“What does a pitch cost at this cross.”

She stopped sweeping.

“No,” she said, and then she said the rest of it, because she is a person of that place and she is twenty-nine and nobody in nine years has asked her a second question about anything at all. “There is no price on a pitch. A pitch is a pitch. It is where you put your table and it is where you have put your table for nine years and it is the same pitch and it is not a price and it is not written anywhere and if you make it a price then a man in a town nine miles off can come here and put a table on it and there is nothing in that cross that can tell him no.

“*And I have been asked what the list at the cross is for nine times in nine years and I have never once been asked what a pitch costs, and it is the same answer to both, and I am not going to be thanked for it, and you can go and stand at your bridge.*”

---

And then the cost, at about the second hour, at the cross, where nine people were standing in the rain and there was nowhere else to stand.

He had been in this market four days and he had watched every load come over, and he had watched who came into the town and who went out of it and who stood at the cross and who did not stop at the cross, and he had been carrying the number ninety in his head since the fifth hour of the previous morning.

Ninety people were on a list in a church nine miles off, and he had been treating it, for a day and a half, as a number.

There was a woman with a basket who had come into that market for a couple of minutes and had gone out again. There was a man with a hired cart who had not been in the market in the sense that anybody who lives in it is in a market, and who had been there for nine minutes and had gone out again. There was a boy of about thirteen with a goat. There was an old man who had not bought anything and had sat on the step of the cross for four minutes and had gone home. There was a woman of about thirty-two who had come in with a hired hand and had a pitch and one more mouth coming in the spring.

Nine people, and eleven, and nine more behind them, and the man of thirty-one could not say how many of the ninety those were and was never going to be able to say it, and the woman of twenty-nine at the cross could not say it either and had never been asked to and had said so.

*About ninety is a broom and a church nine miles off and four names a woman of twenty-nine can put a finger on, and one of the four with a pitch and a child coming in the spring, and eighty-six that a man of thirty-eight has not seen and that a stranger with a leg has not seen and neither of them is going to.*

He stood in the rain at the cross and could not do one single thing about any of it. He cannot compel a person and cannot join accounts and cannot produce a record and cannot carry two burdens, and he cannot be in two places, and nine people walked past him in twenty minutes and he did not know four of their names and was never going to and there is no form in this country that says a man may ask a person at a cross what they are called before he has bought anything.

He wrote nothing down. He has written nothing down in seventy-seven mornings and there is no hand in this county he could put a thing in.

The day came to four bells and a market and a debt called in, which is ten, and that market and that debt were both four days the other way, and carts went over a bridge in a hurry in this town and a man of about forty-one mended four feet of wall in the rain and a woman of about fifty-eight was on a step and then was not on it, and nobody was relieved and nobody was thanked and the market did not stop.

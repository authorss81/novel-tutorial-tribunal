# Chapter 301: The Fifty-First Morning

What the woman of forty-one wrote on the plank on the second morning of the sixty-fifth week, at the second light, was sixty-three thousand eight hundred and forty-nine, and she wiped the cloth over it and put the cloth over her shoulder and went to do her day.

It was four bells and nothing whatever on top of them, which is the figure that board says about most of the days in a year, and it was the fifty-first morning of a man of thirty-one in this country, and he was standing in the yard at the foot of the tower with his hands behind his back and nothing to do until the second bell.

A boy of seventeen came up the lane out of Ellerby with a book under his arm and a stub of pencil behind his ear that he never puts down, and he came through the bar without stopping at it, because she counts strangers and does not count people she has seen before, and there are nine of the second kind in eleven villages and he is one of them and that is the whole of his standing.

“You have been counting the two of us for a minute and a half,” said Bram Orren. “You were not going to write either of us up and you have not, and I have been watching you not do it.”

“I was not going to write you up,” said the woman of about fifty, without taking the board out from under her arm. “I have got nine of you, and you are all in the same county, and that is the only thing I have ever needed to know about any of you.”

He had a book that could order things and could not date them, four bells at the top of every page, two pages lost in the ninth year and not copied and not shown, a question in it with a mark on the end of it that nobody has answered, and a third place in it that he can see and has never been able to make out. He had said all of that in four yards in six weeks and he said none of it that morning. He said one thing.

“Everything in that book is in the order it happened,” he said. “If I put nine things in a row, then you can ask anybody about the ninth one. That is the whole of what it is for, and there is nothing else in the world like it.”

“You cannot date it,” said the man of thirty-one.

“No,” said Bram Orren. “And I have never once wanted to, and I have told three people that I cannot, and it is the only fact about me that anybody in two counties could check.”

---

She went up the ladder at about the second hour, to the third of the four rungs, and the cloth came off the number and went back on the number, and he stood at the foot of it and looked at the arrangement of the Briar March the way a man looks at a thing he has looked at every morning for fifty days and has never once looked *at*.

The bar at the end of the lane, which is not shut and is not shut for the whole of its life and takes threepence off a load, and has no road behind it and never had one, and is not a gate, because a lane does not have a gate.

The lane, which goes from village to village and does not go by the ground, and which is in four words in a book on a shelf in a press eleven miles off, in a doorway, said out loud by a person of Nettlebed.

The hundred and forty-one names on the inside of a door four miles up the lane, with a figure against every one of them and nothing whatever over the column, and nobody has ever added it up, refused twice out loud on purpose, because a column with nothing over it is not a total.

The ninety acres between one bar and another bar, and eleven families grazing them for nine years, and a man who walked a line through that ground in the twenty-second year of a board at Coldharrow and put the marks on two elms, and who is four years dead.

The bell in the tower she is standing under, which is not rung, and the ninepence a day on a hundred and forty days that is the whole of what the tower is worth and does not ring it, and a person in a valley four hundred miles off is owed one of those and has signed for it and is alive in his own field.

Five things. And there is a place where two of them meet.

---

He has known he could do that for about two years. That is the size of it and it is the whole of it, and he has never told anybody, and he has used it four times by his own count, all of them alone, all of them on arrangements of his own, and it had never once been any use to him at all until this morning.

It is not a reading. It takes no time at all. It cannot be written down and it cannot be checked by anybody else and it has never been wrong, and it is not a thing he can do on purpose. It is a thing that is there or is not, the way a join is there between two boards that were not cut to fit.

*The line where one arrangement stops being true and another one starts.*

The bar stops being true about four hundred yards past the last house, where there is nothing behind it, and the lane begins to be true where the last cart chose a bend instead of a straight line. He can see where those two meet. He cannot say what either of them is for. He has stood in a tower four miles up this lane and read a column of a hundred and forty-one figures upside down with a man of fifty-two waiting to be told, and he could not have said one word about what any of the figures counted, and a man of fifty-two has refused to add them up twice, out loud, in front of about twenty people, and he was right both times.

Every person he has met in this country in fifty-one days can say what their side of it is for. The woman of forty-one can say what four bells is for, and what a market is for, and what a stranger is for, and she has never been asked. The man of fifty-two can say what a boundary walked is for, in about nine words, and it is that nobody has to argue about where the grass is. A carter of thirty-eight can say what a load is. A woman of fifty-one who cannot write can say what a hundred and forty-one people said about four hundred years ago, and she has said it in a yard, and she has refused to be thanked.

Not one of them can see the join. That is the whole of what he has, and it is worth setting down in the plainest words available, because he is a man of thirty-one and he is going up this lane to look at six of these things one at a time, and a man who can only stand in one of them at a time is going to find out what that costs: *he can see where one arrangement stops and another one starts, and he cannot say what either of them is for.*

The woman of forty-one was three feet off him on the ladder, and she did not ask him what he was looking at, and she was never going to, because in twenty-five years she has not asked one person in two hundred and ten people what a single stroke of her board was for, and she is not going to start at a stranger on his fifty-first morning.

---

He asked her one ordinary question, and he asked it in the voice a man uses when he wants an answer and not a conversation.

“What is the four for.”

She did not stop wiping. That is the thing to be got exactly right, because everything that follows him up this lane leans on it: she did not stop wiping, and she did not look down, and it took her about nine seconds to answer, and the answer was not a good one.

“Four is what most days are,” she said. “That is the whole of it, and you have asked me something nobody has ever asked me in twenty-five years, and it has taken nine seconds to say, and I have had it ready the whole time. *And I would like it noticed that you did not ask me what the seven was for, because a man who has been in this country fifty days does not ask me what the seven is for. He asks me what the four is for, because four is the one he can get his hand round.*”

Nobody in that yard said anything. The boy of seventeen had gone up the lane about a minute before and had not looked back, which is a thing he does.

“You are the fourth person,” said the woman of forty-one, “who has stood at the foot of my ladder. Three of them wanted a date, and one of them wanted to be told the board was wrong. *Nobody has ever asked me what a stroke was for, and I have been the only one anybody could ask about for thirty-one years, and being asked is the only thing that has ever been any use to me.*”

She went down the ladder, and put the cloth over her shoulder, and went to do her day, and he stood at the foot of a tower for about a minute and a half and did not follow her, and the number on the wall stayed where it was.

---

He went up the lane before the second bell.

That is the whole of the decision, and it took him fifty-one days to arrive at, and it was not made in a room. He had gone up this lane and come down it for most of a month. He had put four things on a piece of paper out of the back of a bill and a woman's whole working life on a wall, and he had defended a district four hundred miles off and got one sentence out of a room, and he had a figure in chalk at the foot of somebody else's board that nobody has told him to wipe off. What he had not done in fifty-one days was go and look at one of these things and ask it what it was for.

He did not have a reason. The woman of forty-one had told him, more than once and in her own words, that asking a person costs that person, and the whole of what he had in this country was a good name of nobody's to spend, and he had spent it twice.

There were six things in his head when he got past the second house, and it was not a written list, and it was not a plan, and it was not on any paper and nothing was ever going to be.

The bar that is not shut. The lane that goes where it likes. The hundred and forty-one names and the column with nothing over it. The ninety acres and the eleven families. The bell in the tower under the ladder that is not rung for want of ninepence a day.

And the sixth, which was not a thing yet, and which he had no name for, and which was the reason he had asked a woman of forty-one what the four was for and had got nine seconds of an answer in four minutes.

---

The lane climbed for a mile and then stopped climbing, which is a thing it does about a mile up, and there was a woman standing at a gate about two hundred yards past the second house with her back to the hedge and her hands inside her sleeves, and she was doing nothing at all, and she had been doing nothing at all for long enough that the road had got used to her.

He said good morning and she said it back and neither of them asked the other for anything, and that is how a lane works.

“You are the man with the leg,” she said.

“I am.”

“You have been up and down this lane for about seven weeks,” she said. “I have watched you go past about forty times and I have never once seen you stop, and I have had about nine questions and I have had them all week and I am not going to ask any of them, so you may as well know that I am not going to.”

“I am not going to ask you anything,” he said, and meant it, and went on up the lane, and about two hundred yards further on he found that he had stopped walking for about four seconds without having decided to.

---

He came back down the lane at about the last of the light, which is four miles further than he had any business walking on his fifty-first morning, and the leg was the leg and he had not expected it to be anything else, and there was no count of anything in it and nobody in this country had ever asked him about it and nobody was going to be told.

A cart was at the bar.

That is the whole of what is at the end of that lane. It is a post and a bar across it that is not shut, and there is no road behind it and there never has been, and a lane does not have a gate, and twenty-two years ago a man put four letters and a stroke at the top of a board about the length of a forearm and that is the only heading any board in eleven villages carries, and it is the reason nobody has ever been able to ask the woman who holds it a second question.

She had the board under her left arm and she had it out, and she was reading the loads off it as they came through, one at a time, under the four letters and the stroke.

“Nine,” she said, to nobody. “Nine and the ninth is a beast and the eight before it are cart.” And she said it the way she says it, which is the way a person says a thing about four hundred times, and the carter of thirty-eight stood at the other side of the bar with his hand on the side of his cart and did not say anything and was not going to be thanked and was not going to ask for anything.

A stranger is three and a market is four and a man she has seen at this bar every week for about a month is nothing at all, and the man of thirty-one was nothing at all, and about four people in that yard knew both facts and not one of them said anything.

He did not stop. She did not ask him anything.

That is the last state of that, and nobody in eleven villages has a form that would change it, and there is not going to be one.

Three hundred yards and a day earlier a woman of forty-one had said, standing on her own ladder, that being asked is the only thing that has ever been any use to her. Neither of the two of them knows the other exists. Neither of them has been thanked. That is how a thing goes from one person to another in this country, and it does not go from one to the other.

# Chapter 312: What The Man Of Sixty-Three Did With It

The plank at Coldharrow said sixty-three thousand nine hundred at the second light on the sixth morning of the sixty-sixth week, and he read it himself at the foot of the ladder before he went off the lane, and the day it stood for was four bells and a stranger.

It was his sixty-second morning. He is the stranger. The woman of fifty at the bar at the bottom end of this lane stopped counting him about a month ago and has never told him she stopped and is not going to, and the woman of about thirty-four at the bar eleven miles down and off the lane has never stopped counting anybody at all, and that is the difference between the two ends of this county and it is the whole of what he learned getting to the second one.

---

He came down the track and off it at about the ninth hour and the day was four bells and a stranger and he was the stranger and she put a stroke on her board without breaking off what she was doing and then she put the board under her arm, and that is how she has greeted him for about a week, and he had stopped being able to think of a county where a person counts a stranger at nine o'clock in the morning and asks him nothing.

He had a day and a half of walking in him and nowhere to put it.

Then she took the board back out.

“He came yesterday,” she said.

---

And she said who before she said what, which is the way she does everything and is the reason anybody believes her.

“A man of about sixty-three with a bad knee and a barrow he did not bring. He came down that track at about the eighth hour and he was not lost and he was not looking for the water, because there is no water at this bar and there has never been any, and he stood where you are standing for nine minutes before he said anything, which I have never had anybody do at this bar in twenty-two years.”

She shifted the board.

“He said his name and I have not got it, and I did not ask for it, and I would not have asked him for it if he had offered it, and I am telling you that so that you know it was available and I turned it down. He asked me one question and it was about a market four days up your lane with a set of scales on a stone at the end of the street. He said, and I am giving you his words and not mine: *Somebody stood in my kitchen and said a thing about that market, and I have never been in it, and I am sixty-three and I am not going to be asked again.*”

Nobody at that bar had ever been asked a question about a place in twenty-two years. She said the last part of it without any weight at all.

“He wanted to know whether the woman who does the sums over there is all right,” she said, “and I told him what I have got, which is that she comes down this lane twice a year to buy thread and she buys it at the far house and not here, and I have not seen her go over the bridge in six years, and that is all I know and it is not about her and it is about me. And then he said thank you and he went back up the track and he did not ask me a second thing and he did not look at my board.”

She put the board under her arm again.

“He is not thanked,” she said. “Do not thank him for coming. *I am not going to be thanked either and I would not know what to do with it, and if you thank me I will put a stroke on my board for you and you can watch me do it.*”

He did not thank her.

---

Nobody in eleven villages had ever asked her a second question, and she had said so out loud in a lane three days ago to a man with a bad leg who had asked her two about a valley and neither of them had been about the valley. And a man of sixty-three had walked eleven miles on a bad knee and asked her one about a place, and she had answered it, and that was the first question anybody had put to her in twenty-two years that was not about a load or about who owed, and the answer had been nothing at all and it had taken nine seconds and she had not enjoyed it.

He did not ask her a third one. He stood at the bar and looked at the yard and worked out that he had come eleven miles to be told a thing he could not use, and that the thing he had done the previous morning had cost a man a knee and a morning and had bought a barwoman one question she had wanted for nine years, and that the two of those facts are not the same size and do not go anywhere near each other.

Then she asked him.

“You have been in this county sixty-two days,” she said, “and you go and ask people what things are for, and I have watched you do it now four times and I have never once been asked what anything is for. And I have been at this bar for twenty-two years and I have been asked a great many things about loads and about who owes and about where a cart has been. I have been asked the first question every day of my life here and I have had the second one twice and one of the two was a man with a knee and one of them was nine minutes ago.”

She took the board out again, which she did not need to do.

“So I am going to ask you one and I have been getting at it since the sixth hour. *The mile of road.*”

The man of thirty-one did not have anything ready for that.

“He wants paying,” she said, before he could say anything at all. “The third one of the nine. He has said so out loud in a lane in front of people and he is right and he is wrong and it does not matter which, and I am not asking you to agree with me about him, I am telling you what I think and I have thought about it since the sixth hour.”

She turned the board over and put it face down on the bar, which she does not do.

“*He is right that he has never been paid and wrong that that is the wrong.* A road is a favour. It has been a favour for ninety years and there is no paper about it and there is no day and no officer and no form, and every one of the nine does his length because the one before him did it, and that is the whole of what is holding it up. A carter is eighteen pence a day and that is what it costs a man with a cart and a beast to do a mile of that road, and I have had that figure off a carter of thirty-eight for twenty-two years and it has not moved once, and I am not going to do the sum now and I did it once in a lane in front of four people and I would do it again.”

He did not ask her to.

“Now the part I have been getting at since the sixth hour,” she said. “If you pay him, the other eight are working for a man. And that is not a figure, that is a week. And if the man goes, eight men are standing in a lane on their own and there is nobody in this county with a form for that either. *And the one thing that road has never needed, in ninety years, is for a man to have written a figure on it, and the reason it has not needed it is that a favour cannot be asked for and you cannot send a bill for a favour, and the day somebody writes one of those numbers down is the day the eight of them find out what it is worth, and then it is worth nothing.*”

Somebody was coming up the track. A cart, and a beast with feet that were not bad today, and a man of thirty on the shafts.

“I do not want a thing done about the mile,” she said. “I want you to go and say one thing about it out loud, somewhere, to somebody, and I want you to understand that it will not help and I am asking anyway, because I have wanted a second question about this lane since about the ninth year and you are the only person who has come up it.”

---

He did it in nine minutes, standing in the road, and he knew it was the wrong thing while he was doing it and he did it anyway and finished it.

He could see the join the whole time he was saying it. It was at the first gate, ninety yards in, where a man mends his own length and the length begins, and it was where a favour stops being a favour and starts being a duty, and it is the same join he has seen in a fund over a smithy and in a market street and on a rise behind a burying ground, and he had been in six counties and four days and had not said what either side of it was for, and about a second before he said the sentence out loud he understood that he was about to do the exact same thing to a road that he had done to a kitchen table and a stone plinth and a rise, which is to hand a thing a sentence it had been getting on without for ninety years.

He said the sentence anyway.

He said that the mile is nine men and ninety years and a length apiece and no payment, and that the whole of what holds it together is that nobody has ever been asked to do it, and that the reason a favour cannot be asked for is that a duty can and a favour cannot, and that this mile has been a duty for ninety years and has never once been sworn to, and that the join in it is at the first gate where the favour stops being a favour and starts being a duty, and he said that out loud, in a road, to four people who had stopped.

The man of thirty with the cart stood nine feet off and listened to the whole of it.

“That is a sentence,” he said, when it was over.

“That is a sentence,” said the man of thirty-one.

“Yes,” said the man of thirty. “And I have had it for eleven years and my father had it before me and it has never once made anybody mend a length on a day they did not feel like it. *I have been waiting nine days for a man to come and say that to me and you have said it and I would go and mend my length now if I had not meant to anyway.*”

He put his hands in his pockets and got the barrow out of the barn behind the first house and went and mowed his own hundred yards, and he did it in the rain, and nobody helped him, and nobody was thanked, and the four people who had stopped at the first gate stood there for about a minute and then went and did their own work.

The man of thirty-one could see the join at the first gate perfectly well. It was there and it was exact and it was where a favour becomes a duty, and it was the same join he had seen in a fund over a smithy and in a market street and on a rise behind a burying ground, and he could see it in the rain, and it told him nothing, and a man of thirty had just told him in nine words that it tells nobody anything, and he had gone and said a sentence out loud in a road to four people and it had cost the day a thing that cannot be got back and it had bought nothing at all.

The woman of thirty-four at the bar read the last two loads off her board that afternoon with the face of a person who has asked one question and got an answer and is not going to be thanked for either.

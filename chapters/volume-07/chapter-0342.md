# Chapter 342: The Sentence Arrives One Day And A Half Late And Nobody Carried It

There is no plank in that street and there has never been one and there is no figure on any wall in that county, and the wall is four days up this lane and off it with a leg on it. The only man in this country who could have had a figure at the second light was a man standing at the foot of a ladder four days away, and he was not one.

It was the first morning of the seventy-first week and his ninety-second morning, and the day came to four bells and a stranger, which is seven, and the stranger is him and he is four days of road from the street all of this happened in.

He had put the sentence into a lane behind a bar eleven miles down that lane and off it on the fourth morning of the seventieth week, and a woman of about thirty-four had said it in her own mouth and had said it worse, and on the sixth morning of that week she had stood at a cross at the bottom end of her own village at about the second hour and said it in the open to nine people, and he had not been there and was not going to be.

That was two mornings ago. And it was in that street this morning at about the second hour, and nobody had carried it.

---

Here is the whole of how a sentence gets from one place to another in this country, and it took her eleven seconds to say and nine to be said.

A woman at a bar, at a cross, in the open, at the second hour, to nine people. Nine people, and four of them are in a cart by the second hour because four of them are always in a cart by the second hour. And a cart in this country goes where it is going anyway, and a man with a cart and a beast who has forty quarters of a load to sell at the far end of a lane does not turn off a lane for a conversation.

She said it at the second hour. One of the four said it in a yard at about the fifth hour to a man who was sharpening a scythe. The man who was sharpening a scythe said it at a bar at about the eighth hour to two men who were waiting for a load. The two men who were waiting for a load got to the far end of the lane at about the ninth hour on the day after next and slept, and one of them said it in a street at about the seventh hour on the fourth morning to a man who was opening a shutter.

Four people carried it. And three of those four were going up that lane anyway and one of them was going to sell forty quarters of a load at the far end of it, and not one of them was asked and not one of them knew what he was carrying and no instrument in four hundred miles would have told him.

---

A market is running in that street this morning. It runs one day in a fortnight and it is in another county and it is not on any plank in the county he was standing in at the second light, and a debt is being called in at a cross at the end of that street by a man with a paper, and it is in another county too. Both of them weigh four and two. Neither of them is on a wall in this country, and a day carries one of each, and a market in a second county is not a second market.

At the end of that main street there is a set of beam scales on a stone plinth, and a toll in a wooden box with a slot in it, and a standard on the plinth, and a channel of water at the end of Church Lane, and a bridge, and a wall in a churchyard, and a cross, and four hundred loads go over that bridge in a week of a season.

There is a man standing at that plinth with his hands behind his back. He is seventy and he has been on that plinth forty-four years and he is not doing anything today. Nobody asked him anything. Nobody has asked him anything since he said nine words in his own yard, and there is a woman of about fifty-eight standing at the end of Church Lane with her hands in her sleeves, six months gone with a child, doing her father's sums in her head the way she has done them since she was forty-nine, and she was not asked either, and she is not going to be.

---

And at about the fourth hour, at the end of a yard off the main street, a woman of about sixty-one came out of her own back door with a bucket and stopped.

She had heard it. It had been said twice in that yard since the seventh hour, once by a man she does not use and once by a woman who was not there, and both times it was about her.

It was a stranger's voice. That is the thing she could not get round in the whole of that morning, and she went over it four times at the standpipe and at the bar and at the cross, and the voice in it was not a voice in that town. It was not a voice at the plinth. It was not the man who reads things out. It was a woman, and the woman was not one anybody in that yard had ever heard speak, and the sentence was about a foolscap book on a shelf in a back room, and about a slip in it, and about what the water in that town is paid out of, and about what would happen to a list at a cross if the honest way of doing it was done.

She never found out where it came from. There is no form in this country that says a man may ask a person where a thing they heard at a cross came from, and there is no form that says a person may tell him either, and the woman who said it at that cross went on to the end of a road that has a bar at it and she has not been thanked by anybody including the man who gave it to her.

She put the bucket down and picked it up again and went back in, and she did not say the water stops, and nobody asked her to.

---

The man who reads things out came through that market at about the fifth hour on his way to the same lane he goes up every week, and he reads things out for a living at three tables in a county four days from here, and he had been in that street in the spring and had asked a man of seventy a question at that plinth and had not been given the answer, and he had gone on carrying it, and he was not going to be asked about it by anybody here.

He stood at the cross with his bag at his feet and did not say it to anybody, and he was not thanked for saying it, and he was not asked.

“Do you know where that came from,” said a woman of about thirty-two at a pitch on the far side of the cross, who was counting on a standpipe the way a person counts on a wall, and who had a child coming in the spring and a pitch and three weeks of it left.

“No,” said the man of thirty-four.

“Because I have heard it four times this morning and two of the people who said it have not been in this town in their lives,” said the woman of thirty-two, “and it is about a book and a slip and it is not about anything in this town, and I am going to have to stand on this pitch in nine days and there is going to be somebody in the queue who has heard it and asks me what I think it is, and I have not got a thing to say.”

“You have got the truth,” said the man of thirty-four.

“I have got nothing at all,” said the woman of thirty-two, “and I have got nine days, and that is different.”

He did not have an answer to that either and he has never had one and he reads things out for a living.

---

The cost of it is not hers and it is not the woman at the bar's and it is not the woman of sixty-one's.

It is a man of about thirty-eight's, and it is four days up a road in the other direction, and it is about a column on a shelf in a room over a smithy in a village eleven miles down the lane and off it, and it is going to reach him at a cross or in a bar or on a road in a county he has no standing in, and nobody has asked him whether he wants his own sentence carried, and there is no instrument in four hundred miles that will tell him.

And he is four days of road from that street, with a leg, and there is no form in this country that says a man may be in two places, and he has known that for ninety-two mornings, and the sentence got there in a day and a half and a cart that was going up the lane to sell forty quarters of a load.

He was not going to be able to pay it and he was never going to be able to call it back and there is nothing in the world he can do with it, and the day came to four bells and a stranger, which is seven, and the stranger is him, four days off, and nobody in that street knew he was anywhere near it.

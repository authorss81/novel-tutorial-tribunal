# Chapter 309: A Mile In A Day

It is a boundary. That is the whole of the arithmetic and it is the reason the day felt the way it did, and the woman of about thirty-four at the bar who counts everybody said so out loud before anybody had worked it out, and she was right, and a boundary is a thing that weighs seven and the day came to what a boundary comes to, and nobody in that lane was going to be told a second thing about it.

He had been up past the tenth village and was back down, and it was his fifty-ninth morning in this country, and he had not been up that way since the sixth day of the week, and he had not asked anybody to send him a figure from a wall, and there was no figure and he was not missing anything, and the day he was in was a day with a boundary walked on it and that is the only honest reason to give for a man who stands in a lane for two hours doing nothing.

---

The road is a mile.

It leaves the lane at a gate and goes down past nine houses and comes back into the lane at a gate the other side, and it is about a mile, and it has been kept up for about ninety years by the people who live on it, each of them a length, and the lengths are not written down and are not equal and are decided every year in about nine minutes of ill temper at a gate.

Nine householders. Each one mends his own length. Unpaid. About ninety years, and nobody can say when it started, and there is no document about it anywhere and no day and no officer and no form, and a road in a country where a cart is eighteen pence a day and a keeper is nine.

That is the whole of the ninth thing he came to look at, and he looked at it for about two hours standing in the middle of it, and the man who keeps it is about thirty and he has a cart and a beast and he is the third of the nine, and he came out of the third house and stood about nine feet off and did not offer to show him anything.

“I know what you are,” said the man of thirty. “You are the one who walks about asking people what things are for. My father told me about you in the spring. He said there was a man in the county with a bad leg and a good name of nobody’s.”

“I have not got a good name of anybody’s.”

“You have not got one at all as far as I can see,” said the man of thirty, “and that is the only reason anybody in this lane will talk to you, and I am going to talk to you, and I am not going to be thanked, and you are not going to do anything about it.”

---

He was right on the facts and wrong on the conclusion and neither of them knew it.

“I could do this mile in a day,” he said. “I have got a cart and a beast and I could do this whole mile in a day with one barrow and a fire basket and a pair of hands and I would not charge anybody for it, and I have never been asked to, and I am not going to ask. *Nine men, ninety years, and not one of us has ever been paid a penny for it and not one of us has ever asked, and I have been the third of the nine for eleven years and I am the only one of the nine who has ever thought about asking.*”

Nobody said anything.

“You have come up this lane and asked about a boundary and a bell and a hole in a market and a book in a smithy, and you have not once looked at a road, and I am going to say why, and then I am going to mow my length. *A road is not an arrangement. A road is a favour. Nine men doing each other a favour for ninety years, and every one of them doing it because the one before him did it and nobody has ever made anybody swear to anything, and if one of the nine is paid then the other eight are working for a man and the man can leave, and if the man leaves then eight men are standing in a lane on their own, and I have seen what that looks like on a man of thirty-eight who lost a gate in the spring.*”

He put his hands in his pockets.

“That is why I have never asked. Not because it is right. Because it is load-bearing.”

---

The man of fifty-two was standing about nine feet off and had been standing there for about twenty minutes, and he said the whole of it in about nine words, and he was right.

“It is a favour and it is a duty and there is no paper that says which,” said Tarin Keel. “A duty can be asked for. A favour cannot. *Pay one man and the other eight are working for him. And I have seen that in four counties and it is always the same and it is always a man of about fifty with a cart who thinks he is being fair.*”

The man of thirty turned round.

It took him about two seconds to find a thing to say, and he found one, and he said it, and it was the correct word and it was entirely unfair, and that is the whole of what happened in that lane on the third morning of the sixty-sixth week.

“You have been the only person in nine counties for nineteen years,” he said, “and you have never once been asked whether you would go, and you have gone every time, and I have met four men in my life like you and three of them were right and one of them was you, and I do not know what to call you, and I am going to call you the man who cannot be asked, and you may carry it.”

Nobody said anything.

Tarin Keel did not defend himself. He did not apologise. He did not say the man of thirty was wrong, and he did not say the man of thirty was right, and he did not shift his weight or look away, and he went on standing there for another nine minutes with the name on him, and about four people in that lane understood that he had not taken it off and was not going to, and one of those four was a man of thirty-one who had watched it go on in a yard four hundred miles behind him and has never got over it.

---

The seam in the road is at the first gate.

Not at the far end, where the road comes back into the lane, and not in the middle, where the ruts are worst. At the first house, about ninety yards in, where a man mends his own length and the length begins.

The join is where the favour becomes a duty. That is all it is and he could see it in about a second and could not write it down and could not show it to anybody, and it is the same join he saw in a fund over a smithy, and the same join he saw in a market street between a father and a daughter, and the same join he saw on a rise behind a burying ground between a family and a hamlet, and the whole of the mile from the first gate to the last is a duty that nobody made anybody swear to.

It did not tell him what to do. There is nothing to do with a thing you can see and cannot say and cannot show, and he had four days of walking in him and a leg and no standing, and a hundred yards of lane in front of him that somebody else was keeping.

---

And then the woman of about thirty-four at the bar did the sum.

Nobody asked her to. That is the whole of it. There were about four people in that lane, which is a great many, and the man of thirty was about to go and mow, and the man of fifty-two was standing there with a name on him, and the man of thirty-one was standing in a road.

“Eighty-four,” said the woman of about thirty-four at the bar. “A carter is eighteen pence a day and I have had that off a carter of thirty-eight for twenty-two years and it has not moved once. A keeper is nine. That is Ottily Roke’s at the bottom of this lane, and she has had the nine every day of a hundred and forty for twenty-five years, and I have stood in her yard and watched her count it, and the bell in her tower has not been rung once in any of the twenty-five.”

She stopped, and about four people looked at her, and she did not stop.

“Nine of you mend this mile. One length apiece, a day apiece, in an ordinary year, in a summer. Nine days of a carter. *I have done it. I have done it the way you do a sum at a bar, which is out loud, and you all heard it.*”

She put her board under her arm.

“And I am not going to write it down and neither is any of you, and I want that said in a lane with four people in it. *A number in a hand is a thing a person can be entered against, and that road has been free for ninety years and the one thing it has never needed is for a man to have written a figure on it, and I have been at the end of this lane for twenty-two years and I have watched what a figure at the top of a thing does to the people under it.*”

Nobody wrote it down. The man of thirty went and mowed his length.

And a carter's day is eighteen pence and a keeper's day is nine, and both of them were said in a lane by two people who knew them and could be asked about them, and that is the whole of the money there is in this, and the sum itself is not on this page and she is not going to let anybody have it.

---

She told him about the valley at about the ninth hour, on her own account, standing at the bar, and she did it the way she does everything, which is as a fact about a place she has never been in.

“Off this lane,” she said. “About two miles, and a track, and a bridge with one rail on it. There is a valley with about sixty people in it and there is not one bar in it and not one bar that I have ever heard of, and nobody goes there for anything.”

“And why do I want to know.”

“Because there is a board in a porch,” said the woman of about thirty-four, “and it has eleven names on it, and it has been in that porch since before anybody living in that valley was born, and there is a woman in that valley who does something on it.”

She shifted the board under her arm.

“I have never met her. I do not know her name. I have not been asked and I am not going to be, and I have stood at that bar since before the valley had a track I could hear people coming up, and I have wanted somebody to ask me a second question about that valley for about nine years and I have never had one, and you have had two, and neither of them was about the valley.”

He did not ask her the second question. He asked her the one that was available.

“How long has it been on that porch.”

“As long as the porch,” she said. “That is not an answer about a woman. It is the whole of what anybody in this lane knows and it has been the answer for nine years and about nine people have repeated it to each other and not one of them has ever been on the track to the bridge.”

---

He decided to go in the morning, standing in a lane, at about the last of the light, with a leg and a boy of seventeen who had already decided to come and a man of fifty-two who was not asked and said he would come and would not be thanked for it.

He did not know what he was going to say to her.

That is the whole of what the fifty-ninth morning came to, and it is the first honest thing he has had since he came up this lane on the second morning of the sixty-fifth week with six things in his head and the sixth of them not a thing yet. *He has never once since the second morning of that week known what he was going to say to anybody, and that is the only thing in the whole of it he has got better at.*

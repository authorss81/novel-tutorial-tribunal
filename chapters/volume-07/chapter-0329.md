# Chapter 329: About Ninety People, And What They Are Not

Sixty-four thousand and eleven was the figure at the second light on the second morning of the sixty-ninth week, and a man of about thirty-eight who does it four times a week carried it four days up a lane and said it out loud behind the cross at about the second hour. The day behind it was four bells and a stranger, and the stranger in that day was in a village four days the other way, and a carter from a town nine miles off came into this market, stood at the cross for a quarter of an hour, bought nothing and went out again.

It was his seventy-ninth morning. The man of about thirty-eight had been in that market town for five days and had two days of road in him and about a fortnight of notice behind him, and he was going out in the morning because the road he wants is four days long and he has not got four days and is not going to pretend to a week he has not got.

---

A man of thirty-one cannot be in two places at once. He said it in a gateway in a field on his fifty-seventh morning and in a yard ten days ago and to one person the day after that, and on the seventy-ninth morning he said it to himself in four seconds in a lane behind a cross and it is the sentence and it is the whole of what he has got, and it is not a complaint.

*Nine towns. A market street. Nine houses.* The ninety names are at the cross in a church nine miles off, and the four that the woman of twenty-nine can put a finger on are in this town, and the man of thirty-eight has not been in seven of the nine towns and has never seen seven of the nine lists, and a stranger with a leg cannot go to nine towns in a week and cannot be in two of them in a day and there is no form in this country that says a man may send anybody.

So he did the only thing there is to do with a number in this country, which is that he went and got two of them.

He wrote down the number two in his own head at the seventh hour and he did not write it down anywhere, and that is the first count in seventy-nine mornings that he has been able to stand behind.

---

The first one was a man of about sixty-three with eggs, and he came into the market on a market morning and had come into it on the same morning of the week for nine years, and he was one of the four the woman of twenty-nine can name, and he is one of the ninety.

He had four acres nine miles off and no other water. The standpipe at the end of Church Lane has been the water for that land since before he was farming it and it runs every day and he has never once had it off and there is nobody in this town he has ever asked why.

He asked him the ordinary question. He has asked it in a kitchen in Ashgill and in a lane eleven miles off a lane and in a gateway in the rain, and he asked it in a market street at about the ninth hour, standing at a table of eggs, and he had no technique for it and he used the same four words.

“What is it for.”

The man of sixty-three put the last of the eggs into the basket and answered in nine seconds, the way a man of sixty-three answers when he has been asked the thing he has never been asked.

“It is for the field,” he said. “*I have got four acres and no other water and there is a standpipe at the end of that lane and it has been on every day of my farming life, and that is the whole of what it is for.*”

Nobody said anything.

“I am on a list at your cross,” he said, and he said it the way a man of sixty-three says a thing he has had for forty years and has never mentioned. “I have been on it forty years. I have paid what I could in the years I could and I have not paid in the years I could not, and nobody has ever asked me what the list is for, and it is for the field, and that is all, and I am not going to say anything else about it to a stranger at my table on a market morning.”

He put the basket on his shoulder and went out of the market and the stranger did not say one word to him, and it is the first person of the ninety he has got, and it is a man with a basket and four acres, and he is on a list, and he counted the field.

---

The second one was a woman of about thirty-two with a pitch at the cross, and she has had it nine years, and she is expecting her second in the spring, and she is the fourth of the four the woman of twenty-nine can name, and she is one of the ninety.

He had no reason on earth to go to that pitch. There was nothing at that pitch that a man of thirty-one with a leg could do, and he knew it at about the eighth hour and he went at about the ninth hour anyway, and he stood there until his leg had had enough and bought nothing.

“You have been stood at my pitch for nine minutes,” she said, without stopping, “and you have not bought anything, and there is nothing at this pitch for a man with a leg, and if you have come about the list then I am on the list and everybody knows I am on the list and there is nothing to be done about that in a market street.”

“There is not,” he said.

“Then go and stand at the bridge.”

“I am going to.”

She put a basket down and looked at him for the first time, and she is thirty-two and she is a person of that place and she has been at that cross every day for nine years.

“I have counted on that standpipe every day of those nine years,” she said. “*Not on a market. On the standpipe. I have got a pitch here and I have got one coming in the spring and I am counting on the end of that lane the way a person counts on a wall, and that is the whole of my business in this market and there is nothing else in it, and there is a stranger on my pitch.*”

And he did not say the figure.

That is the whole of the finding and it is the second time in a week that the only thing he is able to do for a person in a figure he has found is not to say the figure to them, and it cost him nothing and it is the correct treatment and it is the whole of the technique he owns.

He could have said it. He had both numbers in his head and he had them right and he could have said them in nine seconds at that pitch in a market street to a woman of thirty-two who is expecting her second, and she would not have known what to do with them, and she would have carried them out of that market in a morning, and four people at that cross would have told two others before supper, and there is no form in this country that says a person may be told a thing like that in a street and put it down.

He said nothing at all about it, and he stood at her pitch for nine minutes longer and asked her one ordinary question about the spring, and she told him about the spring, and he has not been able to use any of it and is not going to be thanked for it.

---

He could count two.

That is the whole of what he has. Two, in a market street, in two hours, on the seventy-ninth morning, and the two of them were a man of sixty-three with eggs who has got four acres and a woman of thirty-two with a pitch who is counting on a standpipe at the end of a lane, and neither of them had ever heard of a slip in a foolscap book and neither of them is going to be told and one of them told him off and the other one did not.

He cannot count the ninety. He has said that out loud, to himself, twice, in a lane and at a plinth, and it is true and it is the finding: *the number ninety is a number a woman of twenty-nine made with a broom and a church nine miles off, and nine people heard her say it, and it is the only number anybody has said this week that anybody can be checked on, and he has got two of them and cannot get the other eighty-eight and is never going to and there is no instrument in four hundred miles that will get him the other eighty-eight.*

Nine people in that street are standing about a stranger who has done nothing. That is what nine people in that street have seen, and that is the whole of what a stranger with a bad leg and two mornings left in a market town can do for ninety people.

---

At about the last of the light he made the last working decision he is going to make in this market town, out loud, in a lane, to a man of about thirty-eight who was sitting on the step of a cross with a slate against his knee, and nobody said anything about it.

“I am not going to put the ninety on a paper,” he said.

The man of about thirty-eight did not look up.

“There is no paper,” he said, “and a sheet is three pence at Sallowby and three pence at any scrivener over a chandler, and there is no scrivener within nine miles of this cross and no chandler, and if you had one you would have to have a copy in nine towns at once and every copy in a place where nobody has read it is a piece of paper, and the one in the place where somebody has read it is a place somebody can be asked.”

“I know all of that. I said it in a yard ten days ago.”

“Then you know what a paper with ninety names on it is,” said the man of thirty-one, “and it is a bill. *The moment there are ninety names on a thing, the thing has got a list on it, and a list is the thing that comes before a bill, and a bill is the thing that comes before a man saying that the other eighty-nine are working for him. That is the road. That is nine men in a lane and a man in a tavern and four years of not being able to look at anybody, and I stood in that road eleven days ago and watched a man of thirty stop a stranger from saying there is a tenth.*”

The man of about thirty-eight put the slate down on the step.

“So the ninety stay as a number.”

“The ninety stay as a number that a woman of twenty-nine can be asked about, and nine people heard her say it, and two of them have been stood in front of by a stranger who bought nothing, and that is all there is.” He put his leg down and got his weight onto it, which took him four seconds. “I have written nothing down in seventy-nine mornings and I am not going to start on the eightieth, and there is no hand in this county I could put a thing in, and the one name I have got is on the back of a bill in a kitchen three miles up a lane in a county four days down this road, and a man of about sixty-three wrote it there because I said I would walk a hedge, and it is the only piece of paper in this country with my name on it and it is a rota for a boundary and it is not about me.”

Nobody said anything, and the slate went on resting on the step where he had put it.

“Then it is the worst instrument in the world,” said the man of about thirty-eight, “and it is the only one there is, and I am going to take a book with ninety names in it four days down a road to a room over a smithy that nobody has chosen to keep, and I want you to walk me to the cross in the morning, because my leg is not the one that is bad but my back is, and the first day of four days is the day you do it on.”

“I will walk you to the cross.”

“You will walk me to the cross,” he said, “and nobody is going to thank you for it and I am not going to be thanked for asking.”

The day came to four bells and a stranger, which is seven, and that stranger was four days the other way, and a carter came in from a town nine miles off and bought nothing and went out again, and the toll went into a box, and the standpipe ran at the end of a lane, and nobody in that market town was relieved and nobody was thanked and nothing was joined.

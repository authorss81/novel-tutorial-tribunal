# Chapter 346: A Day On Which Nothing Is Asked Of Him

There is nothing at the end of this road that carries a figure, and there is no plank in this village and no figure on any wall in it. The plank is eleven miles up a lane and off it, and a figure only goes on that wall at the second light, and at the second light he was in a lane at the end of this road with a leg and his hands empty.

Four bells and nothing whatever on top of them, which is four, and four bells is the one weight in this county that does not belong to a county, and a day with nothing on it does not care what county a man is standing in or what he has come here for.

It was his ninety-sixth morning, and there was nothing in it, and he had ninety-six mornings of not writing one line down in this country to get through and he got through them.

---

The man of about forty-one was mending four feet of wall out of his own lime and he had been at it since before the light and he was going to be at it until the light went.

He had dug the footings himself. He had mixed it himself. He had brought the sand up from the pit in a barrow himself, and the sand from the pit is a thing everybody in that village knows the price of and nobody has ever said, and he would not have told a stranger and was not asked.

He worked with his back to the lane for two hours and did not look round once and did not stop.

That is the whole of what he said to the stranger, and it took nine seconds and it was not an answer to anything.

“*You are stood in my shade.*”

He went on with it. There was no second sentence that morning and he was not asked for one.

---

The woman of about thirty-two at the standpipe at the end of the lane had a wash and a child and an hour, and she counted the water the way a person counts on a wall, which is not counting the water, it is counting the length of the queue, and it is the same arithmetic and it is the only clock a person in her place has got.

He stood at the standpipe for forty minutes because there was nowhere else to stand.

She asked him one question, in the ordinary way, in about the second hour, while the child was in the sling and the water was coming.

She did not ask whether he was married. She asked whether his mother was living.

“Yes,” he said.

“Mine is at the fourth house and she is eighty-one,” said the woman of thirty-two, “*and she has not been asked for one thing since her husband died, and she is the only person in this village who would tell you to your face that you have been in the way since the spring.*”

She moved the wash along the stone.

“If your mother is living then there is somebody somewhere at this moment who does not know where you are,” she said, “*and there is nobody in this county who is going to find out, and I am not going to ask you anything else about it, and that is the ordinary thing about it and I have said it because you are stood at my standpipe and I have got nothing else.*”

That was the best thing anybody said to him in ninety-six mornings of them and it took eleven seconds and he could not use one part of it.

He stood there another half hour and did not ask her a single question, and he had five he could have asked, and there is a rule in this country that is not a form and is not written down anywhere and he has learned it the hard way in four counties: a person who has told you one ordinary thing about their mother in a lane has not handed you anything, and the man who asks the next question is the man who made it a transaction.

So he asked her nothing and she said nothing else and the queue went and came and he carried two pails for her because she had a sling and did not ask him to, and that is not a rescue and it is not a debt and it is not going to be mentioned again by either of them.

---

The man of about thirty-four came through the village at about the seventh hour on his way up the lane, because he is in this village one day a week and has been for nineteen years, and he reads things out for a living at three tables and he is a person of this village and of Coldharrow and the two never fall in the same week.

He did not stop. He said nine words to the stranger as he went past, without stopping, and he did not slow down for them and he did not look at him.

“There is no column,” said the man of thirty-four.

And that is the whole of it and it took two seconds, and he was talking about the fact that six pieces of paper went out of a room over a smithy yesterday in six hands with no order on any of them and no name on any of them, and there is no column, and there is not going to be one, and he is going up the lane on six different mornings with nothing in his head but a round of seven turns in the order and he has not got a page.

He did not say good morning and he did not say anything else and he has not been thanked for nineteen years and did not say so this morning either.

---

And the woman of about thirty-four at the bar at the end of that road put her board on the sill and looked at him and he looked at her and neither of them said one word about any of it.

A month ago he had said the price of a thing out loud in a lane behind that bar and had asked her for a sentence and she had said *go on* and had said it back in her own mouth and worse. He is not going to ask her for a second thing. He has known that since a bridge with one rail on it and he is not going to find out what it is that he knows, because it is not the price and it is not the cost and it is just what a man has when he has spent a thing on somebody.

She took his eye for a second and a half and then she went back inside, and there are no words available on either side and that is the way of it.

---

He was in that village from the second light to the last of the light and he asked nobody anything and nobody asked him anything, and that is not a technique and it is not a discovery and it is the only morning in a hundred that he has had, and he did not get one thing out of it that he can use.

Here is the whole of what he got.

A man of forty-one mended four feet of wall out of his own lime and said nine words. A woman of thirty-two at a standpipe said an ordinary thing about her mother and about his and carried two pails. A man of thirty-four went past and said nine words and did not stop. A woman of thirty-four at a bar looked at him for a second and a half and went back inside.

He wrote nothing down. He has not written a line down in this country in ninety-six mornings and there is no hand in this country he could have put one in, and the six pieces of paper that went out of a room over a smithy two mornings ago are the only writing he has caused in ninety-six mornings and he did not sign any of them and he is not going to.

Nobody was relieved. Nobody was thanked. Nobody was forgiven and nothing was undone and the bell in the tower at Coldharrow was not rung and the toll in a box at the end of a main street four days up the lane went into the box as it has gone in for sixty years, and a man of forty-one finished four feet of wall with his own lime at the last of the light and put his barrow down against it and went in, and that is what a day with nothing on it is for.

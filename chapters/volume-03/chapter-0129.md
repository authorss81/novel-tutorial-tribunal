# Chapter 129: The Fourth Time

The quarter goes up the coast road in a cart in the fourth week of a season and it is read by the guild’s own office two hundred miles up this coast and by nobody else at all, and it is four weeks of passage money written in a hand that has not changed since before Bettany Rook came to that counter, and on the second of the seventh tide she had started it at the first bell and would be four hours at it and would not be helped.

She got to about the fourth sheet and stopped.

“There is a line in this quarter for a person,” she said, “and it has been in the quarter since the fourth of the fourth tide, and that is nineteen days, and it has been empty for nineteen days, and I am not going to write a name in it, and I am not going to ask anybody to write a name in it, and I have known it for nineteen days and I have said it to nobody for nineteen days, and Jory, put your pen down for a minute.”

He put the pen down.

“The council voted five to four that a person be paid two pounds a year out of what that crossing takes, to count crossings in persons and not carts. That vote is in a minute book in four lines and I was in the room for all of it. The money is not there and four of the nine were right about that and they will be right about it again this winter. So the two pounds is not paid. I have not put him back on the crossing. Nobody has asked me to and I am not going to be the woman who starts asking people for things in the fourth week of a season with a short one coming.”

“He has not asked either,” said Jory Vance. “He is owed two pounds a year and he has not asked me for it, and I have had him in here twice in a fortnight buying lamp oil and I have had the book open in front of me both times.”

“He is thirty and he has been counting that post in his head for four years and got it wrong by about nine hundred and said so out loud in front of thirty people, and he has not asked for two pounds.” She squared the sheet. “I have never once asked anybody for anything in nineteen years at that counter, and I am not going to begin, and the reason I am telling you is that you are twenty-two and you are going to do it one day and I would like you to know what it feels like from this side first. It is a very heavy thing to ask a person to come and stand at a counter and say yes to something out loud. I do not want it and I have never wanted it and I was not going to get it.”

“That is going up the road blank.”

“That is going up the road blank,” said Bettany Rook, “and a clerk in a building two hundred miles from here is going to read a line with a wage on it and no person on it, and he is going to do one of two things and neither of them is a good one. He will write somebody’s name in it, or he will leave it. If he leaves it, the vote of this council sits in a book for a year doing nothing, and that is my fault and I knew. If he writes a name in it, then a man on this coast is holding a wage he never asked for and never agreed to, and there is no way back out of it, because the way out of a wage is a decision and a decision needs the man in the room.”

Jory Vance wrote the quarter’s line out on the edge of the returns board in his own hand because he wanted it, and it says this:

> PERSON TO COUNT CROSSINGS IN PERSONS, TWO POUNDS A YEAR, OUT OF WHAT THE CROSSING TAKES.

And nothing at the end of it.

And she had the rest of the quarter out on the boards inside an hour, which it had not been done in nineteen years, and the reason she gave for that was that a quarter is four weeks of sums and a sum does not stop for a line with a wage on it and no person on it, and she was not going to sit in front of one for the other three hours while it was in the room.

The System put the quarter in front of Ilyan Vester on the lane below the office at about the second bell, and what it gave him was the fifth thing on this coast that was a record of a thing nobody had entered anywhere in particular.

**THE GUILD’S QUARTER CARRIES A LINE FOR A PERSON TO BE PAID TWO POUNDS A YEAR OUT OF WHAT THE CROSSING OF WETHER CROSS TAKES, VOTED FIVE TO FOUR ON THE FOURTH DAY OF THE FOURTH TIDE. THE LINE HAS NO NAME ON IT AND NOBODY HAS ASKED. THE CROSSING TOOKS NINETY POUNDS EIGHTEEN. IT TOOK A HUNDRED AND SIX FOUR YEARS AGO, AND THE LAST MAN ON THAT LINE WAS PAID FOR ELEVEN MONTHS.**
**WHAT IS NOT DECIDED: WHETHER A LINE WITH NO NAME ON IT IS A WAGE THAT HAS BEEN REFUSED BY EVERYBODY ON THIS COAST OR A POST NOBODY HAS APPLIED FOR.**

---

The other half of the quarter was worse and nobody in the building had seen it coming, and it happened because a clerk of twenty-two found a return he had never had to make before.

Every crossing on this coast goes into the quarter as a figure and a figure is made of what is on the road. The bar road four miles above the north end has gone into that quarter once a quarter for eight years as a slate of carts out of a basket, and the office writes the number down and does not check it, because there is nothing to check it against and never has been.

And a return is a person, because the road is a person, and the guild has a custom about that which Jory Vance could state in about nine words and could not date and had never heard anybody say out loud, and which is this.

No person on this coast answers for another person about a crossing.

Not the guild and not the assembly and not the harbourmaster and not a clerk. If a thing on a road is a person, then that person says what it is, in their own words, standing at a counter, and not one word of it is anybody else’s. It is about four hundred years old and it is in nobody’s book and it is the same shape as the reason a man will not sign for a woman and it is the reason a crossing counts a person and not a cart.

“It cannot go in,” said Jory Vance. “That is her road and she is the only one on it and it cannot go in a cart because a man on this coast cannot say what her road is. It has been going in as a number in a basket for eight years because a number is not a person and nobody had to be a person about it. And if I write *kept by Alma Gannock* at the head of it then a clerk in that building two hundred miles off is holding a thing that makes a person responsible for a figure, and he got that from a basket, and she has not said a word about any of it and was never asked.”

“So it goes up blank as well,” said Bettany Rook.

“It cannot go up blank, because a blank is a road with nobody on it and the return would say the bar road is not kept, and it is kept, and it has never once been shut, and a clerk two hundred miles off would write *abandoned* on a road that four carts go over every week of the year.” He put the pen down. “It has to go to her.”

“There is no second door and there is no one to send,” said Bettany Rook. “You cannot write to a woman of eighty-one on a bar road and have a thing come back. If you write to her it goes in a cart and a cart does not come back with an answer in it, it comes back with a cart. And if you send a boy then you have put a boy on that road for a day to stand in front of an old woman, and that is the coast’s whole business and I have watched it happen to four people since you came off the road.”

The sheet lay on the counter with the quarter under it.

And the quarter went up the coast road that afternoon in the cart that goes up the coast road that afternoon, and Jory Vance had the sheet ready for it by the third bell and had put a piece of string round it and a loop for a hand, because a sheet that carries a bar road is a person, and a person does not go in a cart with a load of oil. Nobody gave it to anybody. A man with a bad leg had been in that building since the first bell with his back to the wall and had the whole of it in his ears, the blank and the road and the woman on it and the day and the bell, said out loud twice in a room with the door open, and he had read nothing and understood all of it, and when the clerk put the day’s post out on the end of the counter at about the third bell with the sheet on the top of it, he picked it up off the counter and put it inside his coat. He has been on this coast thirty-eight days and not one person has asked him for one thing, and there was nobody in that building to ask him whether he would like to be the one standing there.

Nobody sent for him. Nobody had standing to send for him, and there was nobody in that building who could have told him not to, and the fourth time he has been produced on this coast it was because he picked a piece of paper up off a counter. Picking a thing up is the whole of the mechanism. It took him about a second and it is going to cost him the best part of a day and he did not know that when he did it.

---

Alma Gannock is eighty-one and she keeps a crossing on a bar road four miles along the coast above the north end, and she knits, and there is a boy of eight in the hut with her because his mother is dead and his father is on the water, and he has a slate and a piece of chalk and he is putting something on it and getting it wrong, and nobody in that hut is going to ask him anything about anything today.

The walk is four miles up the coast road on a leg that has a gate hand of forty-four’s weight in it out of a city four hundred miles behind him, and four miles back, and there is nothing to sit on and it is about a day.

He came over the top of the rise at about the sixth bell and she was on the stool with her back to the water and the chain round her knees and the tarpaulin folded over one arm, and she had the boy on a board with a slate and a stick of chalk and he was doing the thing children do with a slate and a stick, which is nothing, and getting it wrong, and she was not correcting him.

“You have not brought anybody,” she said, before he had got to her.

“No.”

“You have come up four miles to bring me something and you have not brought anybody, and I am eighty-one and I am going to be here until I am not, and I would like to say one thing about that before you say your thing, and then I will listen and then I will decide and you will not get the deciding out of me either.”

“All right.”

“Whatever it is, it is not about me and it is not about that card and it is not about my husband, and if it is, I would rather you had not come up the road.” She went on knitting. “Now then.”

---

He said it in about four minutes and it was the shortest he had ever said anything to anybody, and it was: the guild is making its quarter and a return goes up it for that bar road, and nobody is permitted to say out loud what her road is but her, and so she is asked to come to the counter at the Reach on the third of next tide at the first bell and say, in her own words, what that road is and who keeps it, and it will be in the return in her own words, and there is no form and no seal and nothing to sign.

It will cost her the best part of a day. It is four miles each way and eight in the day on a road that is a cart road, and she is eighty-one, and she has a boy of eight.

“All right,” she said. “Say the rest of it.”

“There is no rest of it.”

“There is always a rest of it. You have come four miles and you have not brought anybody and you have told me the number of the day and the bell and the number of the miles, and nobody walks four miles to say a number.” She put the knitting down on her knee, which she had not done all the time. “You want something out of me while I am here, because you are going to be gone by dark and this is the only chance you will get, and it is about the card.”

“No.”

“It is not the card,” said Alma Gannock. “It is the card and it is you, and they are the same thing and you have known that for about a month and you have come up this road twice and asked me one question each time and both times you stopped halfway down the list. So say it or do not, and if you do not then I am going to knit, and I would rather knit.”

He stood in the middle of her bar road, four miles above the north end, on the second of the seventh tide, with the water coming up over the sand bar about two hundred yards off and the boy on the board with the slate, and he did not say it, and he told her that he was not going to say it, and he told her why, and the why was that he had asked her once already what the bottom line says and had not asked her again, and that a person in this trade is allowed to say no to a person exactly once before it stops being a no and becomes a thing done to them, and he had spent his one, and the second one would come off him like a coat off a hook.

“That is true,” said Alma Gannock, “and it is not the reason, and you know it is not the reason, and I know you know, and there is no point in either of us standing here saying the other one.”

“No,” he said. “There is not.”

“All right,” she said, and picked the knitting up, and looked at the water for a while, and then said the true thing, which is the thing he did not know and could not have got out of her and did not try.

“No,” she said. “I am not going to the Reach, and I am not going to say why, and I have had about eleven minutes to think of a reason that would sound well to a man off a road and I have not found one, and I have decided that I am not going to invent one for you. That is the whole of it. You may write down *she would not say why* and that is true, and it is the only true thing about it.”

“Can I say why I came.”

“You can say why you came,” she said, “and it is a good reason and it is a true one and I am not going to pretend it is not, because you have not once tried to move me and I have been on this road eight years and that is the first time anybody has come up it without trying.”

She went on knitting. The boy put a mark on the slate and held it up and she looked at it and said it was right and it was not, and he put it out again.

“My answer is no,” she said, “and it is not a sulk and it is not about the guild and it is not about the money and there is no money in it. And you are going to go back down that road in the dark and you are going to write it down, and I would like it written down that I did not want to be told anything and that you did not tell me anything, because in about a year somebody is going to come up this road wanting to know, and I am not going to have told them, and I want there to be a page somewhere that says I had the chance and said no.”

“That is fair,” he said.

“It is not fair,” said Alma Gannock. “It is only written down. Come up again in the turn and bring nothing.”

---

He got back to the head of the lane at about the ninth bell, which was dark, on a leg that had gone at the top of the rise and had not come back for two hundred yards and had come back worse, and there was a lamp lit in a shed at the head of it and a man of thirty sitting on a bench outside it with a sheet of writing paper in his hand and a very small pen.

He had two pages to write on. He wrote on the back of one of them, in the back of a penny exercise book in his coat, with three other torn-out pages of the same book and a sheet of writing paper from a city four hundred miles behind him that had two questions on it in a hand he had asked her not to write in.

The date went on it. And that it was his. And the whole of it in four lines: that on the second of the seventh tide he carried a sheet four miles up the coast road and back to a woman of eighty-one and asked her to come to a counter and say in her own words what a road she keeps is; that she said no; that she said she would not say why and he did not ask her twice; and that he did not tell her about the card, and that he had had one question on this coast with her on the second of the third tide and had spent it.

And there was no reason on the page, and there had been no reason on the page for four pages now, and he had never once been able to work out whether that was a discipline or a habit, and it was a habit.

Then he said the other half of it out loud to a shed, to nobody, and it was not a thing he would have written down.

“I was right,” he said. “The thing I was doing this morning was right. What I did was the custom and it is four hundred years old and nobody on this coast could have told me why and two of them could have told me exactly. And it cost her a day and it cost me a day and she has not got a return and the quarter is going up the road with a blank on it that says a road is not kept, and a man in a building two hundred miles off is going to write *abandoned* on a bar road that four carts go over in a week, and every single part of that is correct.”

He put the pen down on the bench.

“So it is right, and it produced me, and it did not produce her, and I have done that four times now in four hundred miles and I have never once asked anybody whether they would like to be the one who is standing there.”

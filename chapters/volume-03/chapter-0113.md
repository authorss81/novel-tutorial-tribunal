# Chapter 113: A Word on a Piece of Paper

It was the fifth day of the fourth tide, and the harbourmaster wrote five words on a slip of paper and gave it to a man who was buying lamp oil at the counter, and the man took it, because he was going up the lane anyway and she asked him to, and it cost her nothing at all.

It said: *Come up to the counter at the second bell.*

That is the whole of it. There is no guild seal on it and there is no form and it is not signed with a name, it is signed the way a woman signs a thing when she is in a hurry, and it is in a hand that two people in that building could read and a third could not.

Seth Garrick, who is thirty and sells lamp oil out of a shed at the head of the lane, put it in his coat and went up the lane and did not read it, because it was not addressed to him and he has been on this road since he was nine.

Corrie Tallow, who is forty-eight and has a stall at Wether Cross, came down to the counter at the second bell with a hand-lead in her fist and a boy of nine holding the other end of it and nine feet of slack between them, and she had shut the stall for a day.

---

“There is one thing I am going to ask you and I am going to ask it badly,” said Bettany Rook, “because I have had a day to do it well in and I have used it on the ledger, and I would rather be asked than admired. Hold on to the boy and let the rope go slack.”

The boy let the rope go slack. He is nine years old and her sister’s, and his mother is at the fish quay and had a day of it, and Corrie Tallow had a day of her stall, and she said both numbers out loud in the room before anybody asked her for either of them.

“Four shillings and twopence,” she said. “That is a day. And a day and a half of my sister at the quay, and she does not get that back, and the boy gets his dinner and no wages, because he is nine and I pay him in bread. That is the cost of standing in this room and it is mine and I have said it.”

“It is a great deal more than I expected to pay,” said Bettany Rook.

“It is a great deal more than you are going to pay me either, so we have both said our numbers, and now say yours.”

“The council voted yesterday, five to four, that a person is to be paid two pounds a year out of what that crossing takes, to count the crossings in persons and not in carts. It will not be paid, because the money is not there and the four who voted no were right. I have nobody to put in the place of the wage. I have nine people on a council and not one of them has ever stood on that flat, and two of them could not tell me the depth of it in a bad seventh without a rule. So I have written down a line in a day book that says a person who guides a cart over that flat is a crossing, and the line is right, and there is nobody standing behind it, and it is going to be read out in this room at the first bell on the first of every tide for as long as I hold the key, and every one of those people is going to be told that this flat is being counted and it is not.”

Corrie Tallow stood at the counter with a hand-lead in her fist and did not say anything for a while.

“Then you want me,” she said.

“I want you. I have wanted you for eleven years and I have not asked you once, and I will tell you why, and it is not a compliment. It is that you would have said yes.”

The room did not make any sound.

“That is the whole of the reason and you may have it for nothing,” said Bettany Rook. “You are the only person on this coast who has counted that flat in persons, every tide, for thirty-one years, into a book of your own that you pay for out of a stall. There is a woman at Slackwater nine miles down the water who has counted bodies on that sand for eleven years for no pay and I have never asked her either, and she is not a woman who is going to be standing at my counter. There is a boy of eleven on that flat and he is not in anything. And there is a woman of eighty-one on a bar road four miles along this coast who takes a penny a cart herself out of a basket and sends the guild a slate of the lot once a quarter, and no second person has ever stood behind her either, and she is not mine to send for and I will not.”

“So it is me or nobody.”

“It is you or nobody, and nobody is a thing I can put in a book in the ordinary way, and I have come to ask you in person, and the asking is free and the answer is not, and I have said before in this room that I have never once had a thing on this coast that was both.”

“No,” said Corrie Tallow.

She said it at once, and without any weight on it, and Bettany Rook had the look of a woman who had expected it and had wanted it anyway and had said so.

“All right,” she said. “Say the reason. I am going to write down whatever the reason is, and it is going in the day book and not in the returns, and it is going in with your name on it, and I want to be able to read it out in this room without having to improve it.”

“Do not put my name on it,” said Corrie Tallow.

“That is yours to decide and I am not going to argue with you about it in a room. Say the reason and I will write it as it comes out of your mouth.”

“Two pounds a year is nine days and a bit of that stall,” said Corrie Tallow. “I have done the sum on the way down here. Two pounds is forty shillings and a day of me is four shillings and twopence, and it is nine days and a bit, and I have been doing it for thirty-one years and I was seventeen when I started and I did it because my mother's book was a bad book and I could do it better, and nobody has ever paid me and nobody has ever asked me and I have never wanted to be asked.”

She put the hand-lead down on the counter, which she had not meant to do.

“That is not the reason,” she said. “Here is the reason. If I take two pounds a year then it is a wage. And if it is a wage then it goes in an order and it comes out of a road, and a wage can be stopped. And in nine months, or eleven, the season will be short again the way it is short now, and whoever holds that council will find the money somewhere, and they will find it on this road, and they will take it off the eleven households by about half a penny, and that will be a decision made in a room by people who have never been on the flat, and I will be the one standing at the far end of it holding a slate saying *this is how many* while the price of being here goes up under my feet.”

She picked the lead up again.

“You have not got anybody else because there is nobody else, and I have just told you what happens to the last one, and I would like it entered that the last one said so first.”

“It will be entered exactly as you have said it.”

Corrie Tallow stood there a moment longer and then she said the other half of it, which she had clearly had ready since the counter.

“And the other half is that I am not going to be a woman who took it. For the rest of my life, on this coast, in every stall and every lane, I will be the woman who took two pounds a year to count what she was already counting. And I would rather be the woman who counted it for thirty-one years and was asked, and said no, and had a day of her stall and said the number out loud. That is the whole of me and I am fifty in the spring and I have a stall and I know exactly what I am.”

---

Bettany Rook wrote it down at the counter in about four minutes with a pen of her own, and read it back, and the read-back took about ninety seconds, and she did not improve one word of it, and when she had finished she turned the sheet round so that the woman it was about could see it.

“There is no name on it,” she said. “You told me not to and I have not. It is the only entry in nineteen years I have made on the word of a person in this room who is not a guild member and has no standing of any kind on this coast, and if it is ever wrong, somebody can draw a line through it and it will be wrong with the line there, which is the second reason and not the first.”

“The first reason,” said Corrie Tallow.

“The first reason is that you are right and I have written it down. Go home.”

---

That is the whole of what a word on a piece of paper is on this coast, and it is worth putting down exactly what it is, because nobody here has ever written it down either and it is four hundred years old.

There is a guild and it has a counter and a counter can put a person in front of it. Nobody has ever been compelled to come to that counter and no one has ever been summoned. There is no hearing at the end of it. There is no penalty for not coming and there is no penalty for coming and there is nothing to appeal to and no second door, and the whole of the machinery is a slip of paper with five words on it, written in a hurry, carried by a man who was going that way anyway.

It worked. That is the finding and it is the whole of it. The woman came. She came on the day and at the bell and she stood at the counter and she heard the offer and she said no, and she did all of it in about nineteen minutes, and the cost of doing it was four shillings and twopence and a day and a half of her sister and a boy's dinner, and she said every one of those numbers out loud before anybody asked her for any of them, because she has been on this road long enough to know what a number is for.

Nobody tested it. It was not tested by refusing, because refusing is the only thing a person in that position can do and it does not stop anything, and the paper does not come back. It was not tested by asking, because asking what is in it is a thing a person can only do by not doing it. It was not tested at all. It simply worked, on the fifth day of the fourth tide, on a woman of forty-eight who had been given a reason to say yes eleven years earlier by a harbourmaster who had a hood on and had never asked.

---

Corrie Tallow went back down the lane at the fourth bell with the hand-lead and the rope and the boy of nine, and she did not go to the stall, which was forty yards from where she stopped.

She stopped at the tally post.

It is a larch post about the height of a man with a chain across the path on two shorter ones, and the post is notched, and so are the two shorter ones, and so are the two stones at each end of the nine hundred yards, badly, by men with a hammer, once a year, at about the turn, when the post is inspected and the old notches are filled flat and cut again.

She stood in front of it with a boy holding a rope and she did not touch it. Then she went back up the lane about thirty yards, past her own awning with the board turned to the weather, and into the shed behind it, and she came out with the slate she has counted on since she was seventeen, and she went down to the far stones, four hundred yards, and stood there.

Corrie Tallow counts what crosses the flat in persons, and she has done it for thirty-one years, and nobody has ever paid her, and as of this morning nobody ever is going to.

# Chapter 114: Nine, and What a Song Is For

Danno Ray came down off the high road at the second bell on the sixth of the fourth tide with a load of lime behind him and about four hours before the turn, and he had been up the coast road and back and he was in the particular state a man is in when he has been in a cart for two days and has used up a thing he does not usually use up.

He put the beasts in and came and sat on the end of the post at Wether Cross, which nobody does, and asked Corrie Tallow for a mug of whatever was going.

“There is a third song,” he said.

Corrie Tallow, who had the slate under her arm and a boy of eleven due on that flat in about forty minutes, said, “There is not.”

“There is a third one and I have had it in my head for two days and it has been doing my head in, and I am going to say it and then I am going to want something out of the yard.”

“There are two songs on this coast,” said Corrie Tallow. “Ours, which is Seth's, and Slackwater's, which is about hours and is not ours. That is the whole of what a coast has.”

“There is one at Sallow Bight,” said Danno Ray, “a hundred and forty miles up, and I have been up there twice a year for nineteen years and I have never once heard yours and I did not know there was one until a carter at a school told me there was a carter at a crossing who sang one, and I have thought about it for two days.”

He said it. It went like this.

*Nine days on the low road, nine to mend it,*
*and after that the carts go, and that’s the end of it.*
*Count it going down and count it coming up,*
*nine, and no more, and don’t be caught in it.*

“That is a working song,” said Danno Ray. “It is sung on the second morning of the turn by whoever gets to the top first, and it is sung so that anybody in a cart knows, and it is sung because there is no post and no mark and nothing else on that coast that says whether the low road is under. Nine. And it has been nine since before I was in a cart, and I will put my oath on that, and the man who taught it to me was taught it from a man who was dead before I was born, and nobody up there has ever counted.”

“Counted what.”

“Anything,” said Danno Ray. “There is no mark. There is a song. There is not one written thing about that road in a hundred miles and I have been looking for one for nineteen years and I have found a song, and a song cannot be a slate.”

Corrie Tallow had the chalk in her hand and she wrote the four lines on the slate, which is a thing she has never done, and she read them back, and Danno Ray listened to them going.

“That is the song,” she said.

“That is the words,” said Danno Ray.

“It is the same words.”

“It is the words and it is not the song, and I could not tell you what the difference is made of, and I have been turning it over for two days and it is the same difference as the difference between me saying a thing to you and you saying it to a man in a shop three days afterwards.” He put his hands on his knees. “A song is a thing about nine hundred people agreeing. You have got the four lines off me and you have got the four lines off about five people and they are all the same four lines, and that is what makes it a song, and it is also exactly what would happen if they had all copied it off a slate, and you cannot tell the two apart, and that is the trouble.”

Corrie Tallow looked at the four lines on her slate for a while.

“Then what is the nine for.”

“It is the number of days the low road is under in the turn.”

“How many is it really.”

And Danno Ray, forty-five, a carter on the high road with a load of lime behind him, took about as long to answer as it takes a man to decide to say a thing he has been carrying for nineteen years.

“It is not a number,” he said. “That is what I have been doing my head in for two days. It is three in a dry turn and it is eleven in a wet one and it is nine in an ordinary one, and there is no turn in a hundred years that has been nine every time, and the song cannot hold three numbers so it holds the worst one, because a song about nine when it is eleven gets a cart into the water and a song about three when it is eleven gets a cart into the water the other way, and a man will not put a number in a song that will let a carter drown in it. So it says nine, and it has said nine for a hundred years, and it is wrong most years and it is dangerously wrong about one year in nine.”

Corrie Tallow put the chalk down on the stall board.

“They believe it,” she said.

“They do not believe it. That is the part I could not get round for two days. Nobody up there believes the song. They believe the ground, the same as everybody on this coast, and they use the song the way you use a bar staff — you do not believe the paint, you believe the water. And the trouble is that there is nothing else. So a hundred years of families up that coast have shut a school for nine days when the truth was often three, and have let a cart down on the third when the truth was nine, and one year in about nine a carter has gone into soft ground to Sallow Bight in the turn because a song told him nine and the song was wrong, and there is a name for him and his name is on the thing about him and the song is still what they sing.”

The water came up over the flat across the channel first and then in two bays, the way it does, and about forty people were on that shingle and a boy of eleven was going to be on it in about forty minutes.

“Can it be changed?” said Corrie Tallow.

“No,” said Danno Ray.

“You have not thought about it.”

“I have thought about nothing else for two days,” he said. “Here is how you change a song. You sing a different one. And then there are two, and every person on that road has one of them in their mouth, and about half of them will have the new one, and the ones who have the old one are the ones who are going to be wrong, and the only person who can tell which one you have is the man standing at the top of the road who is going to get into the water. And the only authority a song has is that everybody sings it, and the moment there are two, the road has no number at all, which is worse than a wrong number, because a wrong number can at least be doubted on the day.”

She went up the lane at about half past and left the slate on the stall board with the four lines on it, face down, and stood at the top of the shingle where the flat begins and waited for the turn.

The stone is a flat-topped one about four feet high, set in the shingle at the head of the flat, and it has been there longer than the guild house and it is not the guild's and the guild has never interfered with it and does not know what happens on it twice a year.

The custom is that anybody standing on it may ask the harbourmaster one question, and she answers it there, in the open, to whoever is on the shingle, and nobody may ask a second, and it is on the fourth and the sixth, at the turn. It is written down nowhere. The song is a different thing, belongs to a different man, and is on the seventh, and about nine people on that shingle have noticed in the last year that those are two customs and not one.

Corrie Tallow had asked on the wrong day once, in the way everybody does, and had been told so by a woman in a hood and had gone home. That was not the sixth. This was the sixth.

She got up on it about ten minutes after the turn with about fifty people on the flat and the water three feet into the second bay and the channel going like a rope with a thing in it.

Bettany Rook came down the lane in the hood, because the light off the Reach is bad in the afternoon and has been getting worse for two seasons, and she stood at the foot of the stone.

“Ask,” she said.

Corrie Tallow asked.

“Can a number be taken out of a song.”

Nobody on that shingle made a sound. It is a well-asked question, in the way a question is well-asked when you have had a day and a half to arrange it, and a dozen people on that flat have heard her ask a question they could not answer themselves and did not have the nerve to put, and Bel Hask was at the top of the lane and heard it and put her hand on the gate post.

Bettany Rook did not answer for a while, and then she said:

“No, and I will give you the reason, and the reason is not the reason you are expecting, so put your weight on your feet and listen.

“This stone is for the water. That is the whole of what it is. It came about because a harbourmaster on this coast has stood in the open in the rain and been asked what the water is going to do and has had to say it in front of people, and that has been worth having for about a hundred years, and it works because there is exactly one answer and I am the person who has it. That is all a stone is. It is a question with one answer from one person, and it is worth nothing at all the moment the question is about anything else.

“A song is not the water. I cannot rule on a song, I have no standing over a song, and there is no body on this coast that has standing over a song. Not the guild — a guild charter says what a passage is and it says nothing whatever about what people sing at a crossing, and I have read the thing on that wall every morning for six years and it is not in it. Not an assembly, because an assembly at Slackwater has thirty-one seats and has never once been asked what a song is and would refuse it in about four minutes, and Pether Voke would refuse it out loud before he started, and he would be right to. Not a school, not a parish, not a man on a coast road. There is nobody. I am the only person on four hundred miles of this coast that anybody can ask, and I have just told you what I can be asked about, and it is water, and you have asked me about a song.”

“And the last time anything was changed on this coast,” said Corrie Tallow.

“The last time, it was me, with a brush, on a post, alone, in about an hour, in a bad light, and nobody standing anywhere near me, and I did it because you can paint over a post.” Bettany Rook put her hand flat on the flat top of the stone. “You cannot paint over a song. That is the whole of the answer and there is nothing else in it, and I would like it written down that I refused you on about sixty people and gave you a reason and that the reason was the sea and not the money and not the guild.

“And I will say the other half, because you have come on the right day and you are owed the other half. If somebody on this coast could change that number, the person who would have the most to gain by changing it is you. You have thirty-one years of that flat in pencil and a want, and if that song were the record of this coast you would have found it four years ago and you would have been at my counter inside the week. The song is not a record. It is a thing people agree on, and a thing people agree on cannot be looked at, and the reason you have been trying to get thirty-one years of pencil into a record for four years is the reason a song cannot be one, and they are the same fact, and you know it, and it is the reason you are standing on this stone asking a woman to do it instead of you.”

Corrie Tallow stood on the stone with the water coming up on both sides of her and did not say anything, and about sixty people watched a woman of forty-eight find out that she had been right and had got nothing, which is a thing that happens on this coast perhaps twice a year.

Then she got down off it and picked up her slate and turned it over and looked at the four lines on it.

“All right,” she said, out loud, to about sixty people, which she had not intended to do. “Then it is nine, and it is wrong most years, and I have written it down, and I am going to go and copy it on to about nine boards up that road over the next year and put the number on every one of them with my own hand under it, and it will cost me nine boards and a great deal of chalk, and it will be nine boards and not one, and that is the best a person can do to a song.”

Danno Ray, from the end of the post, said, “You have understood nothing about songs and I am glad of it.”

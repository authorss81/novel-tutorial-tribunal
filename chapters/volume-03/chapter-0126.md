# Chapter 126: The Tide Was Held

On the sixth of the sixth tide the strait is shut and the flat takes nine, and the guild’s own works were out on it, because the sixth is the one day of the week when nine hundred yards of shingle is the only road there is and there are stone walls on the far side of that water that cannot be waited for.

The chain was down and the two shorter posts were out of their sockets and lying on the shingle in the dry, and about eight men of the guild were capping nine yards of the sea wall at the far end with a course of stone and lime, and the work had been going on since the first bell and was two days old and wanted one more day and a half.

Hal Bevan is fifty-four and he has been the guild’s own man on that flat for nineteen years and it is his whole trade, and he is not a person anybody on this coast would call important. He puts the chain up and takes it down. He knows where the ground is good enough for a cart to stand and where it is not, and he knows it to about a yard, and he has never written any of it down.

“A cart can go over at any of the nine,” he said, “as long as the stone is not on it and the boys are off the line. That is the whole of the rule and there is nothing in it about a tide. You go on the ebb coming off the far bay and you go on the flood going up the near one, and in between you stand at the near end with your beasts and count, and there is room for four carts a tide and I have had five.”

“There is a cart at the near end now,” said Ilyan Vester.

“There is.”

“It has been there since the first bell.”

“It has,” said Bevan. “And it will go on the third, same as the last eight times this year. You are not going to stand about and watch water, are you.”

He went off up the shingle to where two of his own were arguing about a line of lime and did not say anything else for about forty minutes.

---

What he had in his coat, in the inside breast pocket, folded in four, was a sheet of paper in a hand that was not his and not any hand in the guild, and it had been in there since the second bell of the third of a tide, when a man of thirty-one had come down to the works’ lodging house at the north end for bait and had been shown round by Cleve Gannock, who is a dealer and cannot leave a thing alone, and who had read the whole of it out loud, twice, to about nine men in a kitchen, and had then written it out again in his own hand and given a copy to Hal Bevan because Bevan was the man who put the chain up.

Bevan had read it four times over two days. It said:

> ON THE MORNING IN QUESTION THE CHANNEL WAS NOT IN THE LOW. A CART WENT OVER THE WATER AT THE NORTH END AND CAME OFF DRY. THE WATER WAS UNDER SIX INCHES.

And the guild has an instrument. It is on the north wall of the tide office at Kestrel Reach, in an oak frame, and it is about three months old, and it is the only thing on four hundred miles of this coast that anybody will act on when there is a question about water in it, and it carries a figure for the north end for every tide of the year because the north end is where a man with a boat wants to know what the water is doing.

For the morning in question, the north end of that table says four feet at the lowest.

Six inches and four feet is three feet six inches, and the two of them cannot both be right, and there is nobody on this coast able to stand them side by side in a room, because the only thing that would do it is a pane and the only pane in a room is the guild’s and it does not come out of that room and the next one is a year off and the money is not in the arch.

What Bevan had worked out over two days, and had said out loud to nobody, was that he did not have to know which of the two was right. That was the whole of it. He did not have to know. The only thing he had to be sure of was that he was not going to be the man who put a loaded cart over nine hundred yards of shingle at the ninth crossing of a bad sixth on the strength of a sheet a dealer copied out of a stranger’s book, when the guild’s own instrument on the wall said four feet, and when the difference between the two was three foot six.

---

He came back down the shingle at about the third bell and looked at the load, and the load was Ren Tubb’s, and the carter was standing at the beasts with his hands in his sleeves doing what a carter does with his hands for an hour.

“Third tide,” said Bevan. “Same as always.”

“Third tide,” said the carter.

“Not today.”

The carter took his hands out of his sleeves.

“Come again.”

“Not today,” said Bevan, and he said it the way he said everything, which was the way a man says a thing he has already said to himself about forty times and is not going to improve. “That load is not going over the flat this tide and that load is not going over this week, and I am not going to argue about it with you, and I will tell you why and then you will do what you like about it.

“There is a table on a wall four hundred miles that says four feet at the north end on the tide before last, and there is a piece of paper in a dealer’s hand that says six inches, and I have got both of them and I cannot get one of them in front of the other, because the only place they can be put side by side is a room with a fire in it and a year’s work in it, and I am a man on a chain and not that. So I do not know which one is right. And a man who does not know which one is right does not put a loaded cart over a flat on the third tide, and I have been doing this nineteen years and I have never once not known, and I am not going to start on a sheet off a dealer.”

“It is a good sheet.”

“It is a good sheet and it is in a hand I have never seen in this building and it came to me through a man who sells eels.” He looked at the cart. “I have had four feet off a wall and six inches off a dealer and one of them is a thing the guild pays for and one of them is a thing a man wrote in a book, and I know which one I have got more of. That is all that is deciding this and I would like it in whatever you write to your man.”

---

The carter wrote to his man. The carter is Wend Garrow and he has been thirty-three years old for some months and he has been on that road since he was nine, and he was not angry, and he was not loud, and he was tired in the ordinary way that a man is tired at the third bell on the sixth day of a tide with a load of stone standing on the wrong side of nine hundred yards of shingle.

“A day,” he said. “That is a day and a day’s hire and I have got two men on this and the second man is on from the second and I pay him the day whether he is here or not, and the horses have eaten and they will eat, and the stone is paid for and the stone does not care.” He got the beasts turned round where they were, without hurry, which is what a carter does. “And I am not saying you are wrong. I want that written, because I am going to say it up the road to four people before the turn and I would like it said that a man put a chain up this morning and would not put a cart over, and gave a reason, and the reason was a piece of paper and a wall, and did not tell me to go to hell.”

“That is fair,” said Bevan.

“I know it is fair. That is the whole of what is the matter with this coast.” Wend Garrow got his beasts up the shingle about forty feet and stopped, and turned round, and said the thing he had actually come round to say. “Can I ask you one thing and get a straight answer.”

“You can ask.”

“Will it be the same on the seventh.”

And Hal Bevan, who has stood on that chain for nineteen years, and who knows the seventh is the one day the harbourmaster does not know, and who has never in nineteen years pretended to know it either, took about four seconds.

“Yes,” he said. “It will be the same on the seventh and it will be the same the week after. That is what I am telling you and I am telling you plainly and you may put it in your letter: not this tide and not this week and not until somebody puts those two things side by side in a room, and there is not going to be a room.”

---

The man with the bad leg had been standing about thirty feet off for the whole of it, on the dry part, with his weight on his good side, and he had heard all of it, and he had worked out inside about a minute and a half that he had built the thing that was happening in front of him.

Not on purpose. That was the part that kept turning over. Three accounts, one question, a sentence with a date on it, nine minutes in a net loft; then Cleve Gannock had carried it to a kitchen at the north end and read it out twice to nine men and copied it out in his own hand and given it to the man who puts the chain up; and the man who puts the chain up had used it to hold a cart off a flat. It was true, every part of it. It had cost nobody anything to be wrong about, because nobody had been able to check it, and it was now the reason a carter of thirty-three with two men on was going to be a day late with a load of stone he had already paid for, on a coast where nobody had ever once been told a thing that was not in a song or on a post.

He went and stood about ten feet from Wend Garrow for a while and then said, out loud, in an ordinary voice, to a carter who did not know him and had no reason to listen:

“I know where that sheet came from.”

And the carter looked at him.

“I was in the room when the three men said it and I wrote it down and I gave it to him.” He stopped. “I am not able to tell you the other half, and I would tell you the half I have, and the half I have is that the three of them are all right about the water they were in and that is the whole difficulty. I do not know where the channel was that morning. Nobody in that room knows. And the sheet has put three pieces of water in one place and I have not been able to make anybody see the join, and there is no join, and I would like somebody to come and look at it and I have no standing here and nothing to show them.”

Wend Garrow put the beasts a foot further up the shingle.

“Mate,” he said. “I have been on this road since I was nine. In all that time the things that have cost me a day are a gate, a chain, a bar, a lie about a bridge, and a man telling me a thing he did not know. You are the first one I have had stand in front of me and offer.” He got the beasts round a stone. “It does not help. I want to be fair with you about that, because I can see you are going to go and sit on that stone tonight over it. It does not help me one hour. But I will say the other thing, because I have got a day to fill and there is nothing else in it, and I would rather say it than not. Nobody has ever told anybody anything they did not know was the reason.”

He went up the shingle and did not look back, and the cart went up behind him, and the stone stayed on the near side of the flat for the rest of that day and the whole of the seventh.

---

Jory Vance made the entry at the fifth bell, standing up, at the chain, with the board under his arm, because that is where he was and because Jory Vance is twenty-two and four years on the counter and writes down what happened while it is happening.

> THE TIDE WAS HELD AT THE NEAR END OF THE FLAT AT THE THIRD BELL OF THE SIXTH DAY OF A TIDE. A LOAD OF STONE WAS NOT CARRIED OVER. THE MEN ON THE WORKS CARRY ON. THE HOLD IS AT THE ORDER OF THE MAN ON THE CHAIN AND HE GAVE THE REASON THAT AN ACCOUNT OF THE NORTH END AND THE GUILD’S OWN TABLE DO NOT AGREE AND THE TWO CANNOT BE PUT SIDE BY SIDE, AND A CART IS NOT GOING OVER ON ONE OF THEM.

The System put it in front of Ilyan Vester on the far side of nine hundred yards of wet shingle at about the ninth bell, and what it gave him was the fourth thing on this coast that was a record of a thing nobody had entered anywhere in particular.

**A TIDE WAS HELD AT THE NEAR END OF THE FLAT. A LOAD OF STONE WAS NOT CARRIED OVER. THE REASON ENTERED IS THAT AN ACCOUNT OF THE NORTH END AND THE GUILD’S OWN TIDE TABLE DO NOT AGREE ABOUT IT, THAT THE TWO CANNOT BE PUT SIDE BY SIDE ON THIS COAST, AND THAT NO CART IS TO GO OVER ON ONE OF THEM. THE ACCOUNT WAS WRITTEN BY A MAN OFF A ROAD. IT IS TRUE OF THREE SEPARATE PIECES OF WATER AND IT IS IN ONE PLACE. NOBODY ON THIS COAST HAS CHECKED IT AND NOBODY CAN.**
**WHAT IS NOT DECIDED: WHETHER THE TABLE IS WRONG ABOUT THE MORNING IN QUESTION OR THE ACCOUNT IS, AND BOTH WERE MADE BY PERSONS WHO WERE ON THE WATER, AND THE MAN WHO HELD THE TIDE WAS RIGHT EITHER WAY, AND THE COST OF BEING RIGHT IS A CARTER OF THIRTY-THREE WITH TWO MEN ON IT.**

The water came off the flat and went away again and left nine hundred yards of it darker than the rest, and the chain stayed down, because the chain stays down when the works are on, and the stone was still on the near side at the turn, and the seventh was a bar day, and there was going to be a carter standing on that shingle in the morning with the same load and the same question.

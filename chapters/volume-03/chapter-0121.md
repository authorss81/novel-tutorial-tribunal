# Chapter 121: The One Who Was Not There

The first of the sixth tide is a good day and the flat takes six, and the wind is off the west and there is nothing on the water between here and the far side that wants anybody’s attention. Cleve Gannock came down into the drying yard at the second bell to sort a heap of bait for a man at the Quay, and he did not sort it, and he did it badly, and by the ninth bell he had a letter in his right hand that he had read four times and a heap of bait he had not sorted at all.

He is thirty-one. He is the third of three brothers and the only one of the three who does not go on the water, and he is a dealer in what the flat boat brings in, and he has a house up above the yard with a good roof on it that four generations of this coast have known about and none of them says anything in front of their mother.

The letter is four lines and it is from a man who hires carts up the coast road, and it is a courteous letter and it is not a stupid one. He wants the flat boat and the towrope for the turn of the year, for the three days a week the water is on the bar, and he has put one question in it, and the question is whether the Gannock shallow is worked by a family or by one person. He says he asks because he has to write something on his paper and he would rather write the true thing.

Cleve had read it four times before the ninth bell and he could not answer it in one line, and that was because the answer was three lines long and the three lines did not agree.

---

Sorrel was in the yard already, because Sorrel is in the yard most days. She had the flat boat up above the tide line on her bilge with the garboard up and a canvas over her, and she was doing something to the shoe with a file.

She is thirty-six and she has the flat boat and the towrope, and she is the only one of the three who will take a boat over the shallow at the north end on a bar day, and she has said so in this yard more than once, and neither of her brothers believes her, and nobody standing in this yard is going to be asked about it either.

“The letter is off Halloran,” she said, without looking up. “He wants the boat for the turn.”

“He wants the boat for the turn,” said Cleve.

“He wants to know who he is hiring.”

“He wants to know who he is hiring.”

“That is not the same thing and you know it is not.” She put the file down on the boards. “If you write him *the family* he will write *the family* on his paper and put it in front of two hundred people, and there is no family in this yard. There is a boat and a rope and me, and there are two men who will tell him a different thing each, and neither of them was on the water that morning.”

“Neither of them is going to be on the water,” said Cleve, “and that is not going to change this week.”

---

Dell came up off the water about the fourth bell with the pole over his shoulder and the flat in about two feet of it, and he came up the hard in two pulls. He is forty-two and he has the wheel, and four generations of this family have gone out of this yard, and there are three of them in the water trade and the third of those is the one on the flat boat.

“Cawley wants his ways clear by the sixth,” he said, going past.

“He can want it. They are his.”

“They are his, and I have got notches in that garboard that want doing before the winter, and he can want them as soon as I have been in the bottom of it.” Dell set the pole against the boat and looked at the letter in his brother’s hand and then at his brother’s face. “What is that.”

“Four lines off a man who hires carts, about the turn of the year and the shallow.”

“And what are you going to tell him.”

Then Cleve said it out loud, flat, to both of them at once, which was the thing he had wanted to do for a day and a half.

“That father crossed on an ordinary day with an ordinary cart,” he said. “That is what I want said out loud, once, in front of the two of you, and then I can write it to that man this evening and it will be true and it will be short. Four generations and the whole of it comes down to one ordinary day with one ordinary cart, and I would like it said in this yard where it can be heard.”

Nobody answered him. The wind came in under the canvas off the flat boat and moved it an inch and stopped.

“No,” said Dell.

“She will not be able to write that.”

“No,” said Dell. “And here is why, and I am going to say it once and then I am going to mend my own garboard, and you can put whatever you like in your letter about the family. Our father crossed on the fourth of the second tide two years back, and the channel had come out of the low about three weeks before, and the whole flat was running with water from the near stones to the far ones like a river you could name the banks of. The cart was in it to the axle the whole nine hundred yards and I had Os Tern in the water up to his middle on the off rein for four hours because the off rein came out of his hand twice. I had him walking at the far end with a man either side of him and his hands on the boards and his boots off. He was not frightened. He was sixty-one and he was not a hand at a wet cart and he did what he was told, and he was in the water the whole time, and there was not one minute of that day when a man watching from a bank would have called it ordinary.”

“Was the cart in the water, Dell.”

“It was in the water to the axle.”

“Then a man on a bank would have called it ordinary,” said Cleve, “because from where I stood you could not tell.”

“That is your business and not mine,” said Dell.

---

They had it out then, in the yard, in the ordinary way, in about eleven minutes, and it was the ordinary way because all three of them knew the whole of it and had been carrying it two years and none of them had ever had a reason to put it down in front of the other two.

Because Cleve was not on the water. He had the beasts at the head of the lane and the cart loaded, and he was in the yard doing what a man does with a loaded cart, and then he had hold of the head while it came off, and after that he went up the road with the beasts and did not turn round. A cart coming off a flat in the ordinary way in about forty minutes is a thing that nobody looks at twice.

Because Dell had the wheel and had that crossing, and had four crossings that day, and cannot be in two places, and what a man at the wheel can tell you is where the water was.

Because Sorrel had the flat boat at the north end of the same nine hundred yards with the towrope on, watching the gravel go, and what she can tell you is what is over a gravel bar and what is not.

And here is the whole of the thing, and it took the three of them about a quarter of an hour to get to it without any of them noticing they were doing it, and not one of the three of them is lying. The shallow at the north end, where the boat is, is two hundred yards of gravel with a soft place cut through the middle of it. The flat, where the cart was, is nine hundred yards of shingle with a channel down it that moves. They are four hundred yards apart and they are not the same water and they have never been the same water. A man at the north end on a good day sees the channel lying across the shallow, because the ebb out of it drains and leaves it flat. A man at the wheel in the middle of the flat sees the channel lying along the flat, because that is where the running water from the last tide put it. Both of them are the same channel. On the morning in question it had left the low some weeks back and had made a new one across the flat and had not made one at the north end at all.

“It was under six inches at the lowest,” said Sorrel, “the whole two hundred yards at the north end, and the channel was across it and not along it, and I was over there with the boat and the rope on for four hours doing a passage that ought to take forty minutes. And I never saw your cart, Dell. I never saw it. Not once. I looked up the flat twice, the whole length of it, because a boy on a bicycle came past me and I thought it was somebody’s, and it was not.”

“Did your shallow go out at all.”

“It went out the way it goes out. Four inches at the lowest and six on the bar and nothing over the crown, and I have it in a hand about four times a season and it has been the same for eleven years except when the sea runs, and on the day you are talking about the sea was not running because it was a good day and you said so yourself.”

“That is not the same water.”

“It is the same channel going through two hundred yards of gravel,” said Sorrel, “and it was not in the low that morning, and if you had wanted to know where it was that morning you would have had to be on one piece of that flat or the other, and he was on both, and I was on one, and I do not know what it was doing at the other end of nine hundred yards and I will not be told.”

---

The man who had been standing four feet away for about an hour said the thing that had been sitting in all three of them, and he said it into the gap, and none of them looked at him.

He is thirty-one and he came up the coast road at the first bell because the arch at Thornglass was cold and there is nothing to be done at a cold arch, and he has a leg that has been bad for a month and it had been going the whole two miles and by the fourth bell it was in the hip, and he had been leaning on a bollard at the head of the yard since about the fifth bell with a good deal of attention on how he was standing, and the three of them had been arguing for an hour without once asking him anything, which is how it works on this coast and is the only thing he has found out in thirty days about it.

“The same channel,” he said.

Dell turned round. “Say again.”

“The same channel. In two places.” Ilyan Vester had his hand flat on the boat’s stem and the boat was cold and the file was on the boards and he was asking the question he was best at asking, which was the question that made a thing smaller. “Sorrel is not describing a different water from you. She is describing the same channel at the one end of it. You have each got a piece of it, and a piece of a channel is a channel, and there is one channel and it cannot have been in the low at the north end and out of the low on the flat on the same morning unless it was in one place and not the other, and that is not a thing that anybody gets wrong. That is a thing that is true.”

“Then you have told us what we all know and it is of no use to me,” said Cleve.

“No,” he said. “It is the opposite of no use. It is the only thing any of the three of you has said today that could be written down and be true, and I am telling you that it is true and also that it will not do you one penny of good, because the moment it is on paper nobody will be interested in the channel. They will be interested in the cart.”

“I am interested in the cart,” said Cleve Gannock.

“Yes,” he said. “That is the difficulty and it is not a thing I can argue you out of. Your father crossed with a cart, and the cart is what a man hiring a boat for the turn of the year has to be told about, and if he is told the cart was in the water to the axle then he will not hire the shallow, because he does not want a cart in the water, and if he is told it was an ordinary day then he may hire it, and you would be telling him a thing about your brother’s water so that he will hire your sister’s.”

The yard was quiet.

“Say that last part again,” said Dell.

“No.”

“It was the true part.”

“It was the true part,” he said, “and I am not going to say it again, and you may think whatever you like about a man off a road who says the true part once and then will not say it twice.”

---

They went off inside a quarter of an hour, all three of them together, which they had not planned, and there was Cawley’s ways to be cleared and a heap of bait to sort and a garboard to be looked at.

And the man with the bad leg stayed in the yard with the letter on the boards, and he read it, which took him a while and which he would not have admitted to doing.

Then Cleve Gannock came back for the letter, and stopped, and stood about four feet off, and had the fish-scale look on his hands of a man who has been thinking about a thing for a day and a half and has just been told it is not the thing he was thinking about.

“You,” he said. “You have been on this coast how long.”

“Thirty days.”

“Thirty days and you can tell me that three people cannot be made to say one thing.”

“I did not say that.”

“You said the piece of paper was the only part anybody would read.” Cleve put his thumb on the edge of the boards. “Can three people be made to say one thing.”

Ilyan Vester picked the letter up and squared it on the boards and gave it back, and the leg was in the whole of him now and it was going to be in the whole of him for the walk down, and he had about four seconds in which he could have said the true answer, and he said the true answer, because that is what he is and because nobody on this coast has ever once asked him to say anything else.

“No,” he said. “You would not be making them say it. You would write down what all three of them said, and put one sentence at the top of it, and the sentence would be true, and it would be the only part anybody would read.”

“Why is that a bad thing.”

“It is a bad thing when the sentence is the one you wanted before you asked the question,” he said.

And then he stopped talking, and there was a silence of about four seconds, and in the middle of it he worked out — standing in a drying yard two miles along a coast, with a fish letter in his hand and a leg that had not cleared in thirty days — that he had just described, in the plainest words he had used in a month, a thing he had done three times in four hundred miles, and had described it in the future tense, to a stranger, as a way of helping.

“Then I will come and find you tomorrow,” said Cleve Gannock, and took his letter, and went up to the bait.

# Chapter 146: The Gravel

The fifth of the ninth tide is a good day and the flat takes six, and a good day is the day a coast works, and the whole of that north end went to work at about the first bell and had been at it since before the light.

Six crossings over nine hundred yards of shingle, and the boats out, and a lug sail standing along past the point with the wind on her quarter, and a boat at the hard coming up on a flood with two men in her and a warp over her stern, and the drying yard running, and a boat’s carpenter with a hull on his ways going over a garboard with a plane, and the sea going out long and soft off the north end so that the lowest of it was four hours and a bit off the turn and about two hours after the ebb had finished doing its work on the gravel.

The deep at the north end is nine foot of green water on a day like that and there is nothing on this coast with wheels on within forty yards of the toe of that wall, and a man cannot sound it, and cannot get to it, and cannot see the bottom of it, and a wall nobody can get to on four days in seven is a wall nobody has looked at since the fourth year.

At about the third bell Halse Tarrant walked the top of that bar on the inside of the deep, from the hard north about two hundred yards to where the gravel goes down, and stopped about ninety yards along it, and stood there for about ten minutes looking at the edge.

He is fifty-eight and he has been on that wall thirty-one years and he was one of the hands that laid it, and he goes up that bar twice a day and has gone up it twice a day since he was twenty-seven.

“There is a notch in the top of this gravel,” he said, to a man sorting bait in a drying yard and to nobody in particular. “It has been in that gravel as long as I have been coming up here and it is about a foot and a bit deep and about a boot long and it is where the water comes over on the big ones and it is the only place on this bar where the sea ever got over the top, and I have stood in that notch and looked at the sea out there about a thousand times, and it is not a notch any more.

“It is a place where the gravel has come down. It is about eleven yards along the edge of this and it is not a hole and it is not a break and I want that said first, because I have had four people this morning telling me about a hole. It is a lip that is not a lip. And it is lower than it was, by more than a boot and less than two feet, and I am not going to put a figure on it this morning and if anybody asks me for one I will tell them what I have told you, which is that I can see it and I cannot stand behind a number I got off a boot.”

He crouched down and put his heel in where the notch had been and his heel did not find the bottom of it.

“Seven days ago,” he said. “Seven days ago I put a lead line over the face of that wall every nine feet and wrote the number in a book in a shed with the date on it, and the number says the toe is gone along about eleven yards of it in the middle, gone to about two feet of depth, with no bottom holding gravel under the bottom course. Six days ago, at about the seventh bell in the evening, with the sea standing on the crown, that water went out through the middle of it in front of six of us and made a boil the size of a doorway, and nobody was in it.

“And this morning the top of that gravel has come down over about the same eleven yards. Seven days. I said on the sixth evening that it would take less than two winters, and I want about half of that back, and I am not going to take it back, because I have been right about that bar for thirty-one years and two winters is what I would put on it in a room. But I have never seen it do that before and I am not going to stand up here in front of two men and say it cannot.

“Because it is not a catastrophe and nobody is going to believe me about that either, and that is the whole trouble with this thing. Nobody drowned. There is no boat gone. There is a wall with a hole in the foot of it and a bank that used to be a foot and a bit at the top, and that is all, and a shore going does not do its work on the days the sea is doing something else. It does it on the ordinary days. It does it on a good day when there is nothing to look at and everybody is busy. That is the whole of the cruelty of it and I have known that for thirty-one years and I have never had to say it out loud before.”

Nobody at the north end said anything for about a minute, and a boat went up past the point and the man in it did not turn round, because a man looking at gravel is not a thing anybody stops for.

Pell Cawley put his plane down on the garboard he was working and came out into the yard.

“I will tell you the one thing I have got and I have not got a use for it and I am not going to pretend it is more than what it is,” he said. “That hull came up on the ways on the sixth and she is drawing about a thumb deeper at the bow than she did before I touched her, and I have not taken anything off her and I have not put anything on, and I have been building and mending flat boats at this hard for thirty-one years and I do not know what that is. It could be the season. It could be weed. It could be a hundred things and I am not going to guess at one of them in a yard in front of nine people, because I have watched a man on this coast guess at a thing and be right about it for the wrong reason and then be asked to do it again.”

He picked the plane up.

“That is all I have got and I would rather say it than keep it.”

---

The rest of that morning went the way the rest of a good morning goes, and none of it was about the wall, and all of it was on the same ground.

Two hundred and sixty yards of stone with nine courses at the inner end and about two at the outer, and the man who keeps it walked the whole length of the top course on the third of a fifth and put the flat of a bar on every stone he went over, which is what you do to a wall you cannot get under, and it told him that not one stone in that wall had moved, and he said so out loud at the outer end to about four people, and what he said was that the wall is sound and has been sound for nineteen years and always will be, and the two things are true at the same time and everybody who works stone knows they are the same thing.

Six crossings went over the flat between the first bell and the ninth, and the chain went up and came up and went down, and a woman of forty-eight was at her stall with her board turned to the weather and a boy of eleven went over at a penny with a hand-lead in his fist and came back wet to the waist and went over again, and a man of thirty stood at the head of the lane with his back against the frame of a lamp-oil shed, watching the whole of it, and said nothing at all, and went in at the ninth bell and pulled the door to behind him.

And a good day is the day the boats go, and there were four out of the north end before the noon bell and two of them came back in on the flood with a warp over the stern and a man in the bow with his hand flat on the boards, and a flat boat went across the shallow at about the second bell in about a foot of green water over sand with a woman of thirty-six in it and a pole in her hand, and it was not a bar day and it was not a road and it was a flat-bottomed boat in a foot of water going at a walk.

---

The day went on the way a fifth goes, and at about the fourth bell a man of thirty-one came up the lane at the north end on a leg and stood about forty feet off and listened to a man of fifty-eight tell a boat’s builder he did not know something, and did not say a word, and had worked out on the way up that there was going to be a day this month when somebody was going to stand in that yard and ask a man with a cart for a day.

Nobody asked him that day. Nobody asked him that week.

Cleve Gannock came up the lane at about the sixth bell, walking beside a pair of beasts that were yoked to a cart and holding a warp by the middle of it, and he was on his way to the landing, and he was the man who had given a cart, a pair of beasts and an afternoon to a stranger’s wall on the fifth of the eighth tide, in about nine words, unasked, and had said *you are not going to ask me* before anybody had asked him anything, and had been two hours late at a landing because of it and had told the man there it was the beasts, and had not been thanked then and had not been thanked since.

He went up the lane past three people and a boat and a man on a bar and did not stop at any of them, and the whole of what was said to him about the eleven yards of it was nothing at all, and that was the correct end of that and it had been the correct end of it since the sixth of a tide.

And a man of thirty-one stood in a lane with his weight on his good side and worked out, in about four seconds, standing in the cold, that this was the cost, and that the cost was not the wall and it was not the money and it was not even the day.

The guild had said it in four lines and every word of it was right: the season’s money is not in the arch, and it will not be this season.

The one thing on four hundred miles of this coast that could put a number about that wall in front of a body two hundred miles up this coast and make that body have to answer it was glass. And glass is a fire and a year and a person, and the person is whoever stands at the second fire, and the second fire cannot be left once it is let in, and the person at it is a woman of twenty-four who is paid nothing and has not been asked and has not answered a letter of her own, and the year has not started, and the arch behind two feet of brick is cold and has been cold for a season.

Eleven months. That is the price and it is paid in advance and it is paid by her, and it is paid whether or not there is a pane at the end of it, and nobody has asked her, and he was not going to ask her, and a question put to her about keeping it would have been a direction with a question on the end of it, which is a thing he has said out loud in a shed and has not improved on since.

That was not in a room. That was in a lane at the north end of a coast, said by nobody, worked out by a man with a bad leg standing next to a drying yard at about the sixth bell on a good day while a cart went up to a landing, and it was not going to be said out loud because there was no room on this coast to say it in and it was not his to say and it was not her to be told.

At about the ninth bell the water came back over the north end and the gravel that had come down on the inside of the deep went under about a foot and a half of green water, and the wall stood in it, and a cart of deals went over nine hundred yards of shingle and got to the other side, and a man of fifty-eight walked two hundred yards back down a bar that had been a foot and a bit at the top in the middle of it seven days ago and would be a good deal less than that by the turn of the year, and shut a shed with a lead line in a box in it and a book on a bench with a date on it and a number in it that was correct on the day it was taken and was getting further off the truth every day that the gravel went.

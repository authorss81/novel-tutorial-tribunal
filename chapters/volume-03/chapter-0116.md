# Chapter 116: Thirteen Shillings

Gell Roke was on a ladder at the harbour mouth on the sixth of the fifth tide with a pot of paint and a boy of about ten holding the pot, and he was repainting the mark at two feet under on a staff that has been standing in the same place for about four hundred years, and he does it when he feels like it and had felt like it on the fourth.

Jory Vance came down the slip with the returns board under his arm because Bettany Rook had sent him down to ask Gell Roke something and had sent him down for no other reason than that he was the only person in that building who did not know what she wanted to know either.

“Gell,” he said. “She wants to know what happens to the flat on a bad seventh.”

“That is a strange thing to send a boy of twenty-two down a slip for,” said Gell Roke, without turning round, “and I will tell you what happens to it and you can put it up her and you can put it up the council and it will not make one penny of difference, and I have known that for as long as I have been on this coast and nobody ever asked me.”

“Say it anyway.”

---

“On a bad seventh the flat is crossable nine times instead of six,” said Gell Roke. “Six on the first, the second, the third and the fifth, and nine on the fourth, the sixth and the seventh, and I have known that since I was in knee breeches and I have never once seen it written down anywhere except on a song. The reason it takes nine is not that the water is worse. The reason it takes nine is that on those three days the strait is shut, so every cart that has to come round this coast comes over your nine hundred yards of shingle instead of going through, and there is more of it going over and not less.

“And the tariff is a penny a tide, and a tide is a crossing, and it is the same penny on every day of the week including the three days the water is standing up in the middle of it, and the guild has refused twice to write a different price for a day of water, and their reason is good and I have said so to two harbourmasters and a clerk. A tariff that says *dangerous* is a tariff that can be refused, and a man who refuses a tariff is a man you have to argue with on a bad day, and there is nobody at that counter to argue with anybody on a bad day.

“So the price of the danger is exactly nothing. Write it down. I have waited nine years for somebody to write it down.”

Jory Vance looked at the returns board under his arm and then put it down flat on the capping of the sea wall, which he had never done and would not do again.

“Then what does a bad seventh cost,” he said. “That is the question. In money. In a year.”

Gell Roke came down the ladder two rungs, which for a man of seventy-one is a decision.

“Who is it you are asking about.”

“The boy.”

“The one at the lane end. He is eleven and his name is on nobody’s list and I know his name and I am not going to say it on a slip because I have been painting this staff for nineteen years and I do not put names on the water.” He took the brush and wiped it on his thumb. “He goes on five days. Two of the low ones and three of the high ones, because those are the days the cart goes. That is six and six and nine and nine and nine, and so on for a week, and thirty-nine tides at a penny is three shillings and threepence. Fifty-two weeks in a year and it is three shillings and threepence a week whatever we do, because the water does not care what day it is, and eight pounds nine a year is what he costs you and has cost you every year of his life.”

“And on a bad seventh.”

“On a bad seventh the water comes up in the middle of the week as well, and one of his two low days is a nine-day instead of a six-day, so he goes forty-two times a week, which is three shillings and sixpence, which is nine pounds two a year instead of eight pounds nine.” Gell Roke turned round on the ladder and looked at Jory Vance. “You have got a pencil. Three pence a week, fifty-two weeks. Write it down and do not do it in your head, because everybody does it in their head and everybody gets it wrong by a penny.”

Jory Vance wrote it on the edge of the board.

“One hundred and fifty-six pence,” he said. “Thirteen shillings.”

“That is what a bad seventh costs that crossing,” said Gell Roke, “if there is a bad seventh every single week of the year, which there has never been and there never will be, and I want that said in the same breath as the number or somebody will use it in a room in about four months. Thirteen shillings. That is the whole of it. Thirteen shillings a year for a man to stand at the far end of that flat and say how many went over, and I have been up and down this coast for forty years and I will put my hand on it that there has not been a bad seventh every week in any of the four hundred years anybody has kept anything at all.”

“Two pounds,” said Jory Vance.

“What.”

“The wage the council voted is two pounds a year. That is forty shillings. Thirteen shillings is not two pounds.”

“No,” said Gell Roke. “And you have just found the whole of it, and I doubt anybody up that hill is going to like the shape of it.”

He came the rest of the way down and stood on the capping with his hands in his pockets and looked out at the flat, which was dry and would not be for another four hours.

“The guild takes three shillings and threepence a week off a woman who mends nets for a boy who runs a lead over that shingle, and it spends three pounds two a year on a chain and oil and a tarpaulin, and it keeps nine hundred yards of flat it does not mend and does not survey and has never once been asked to mend, and it makes six pounds a year on the whole of it, and that six pounds is the only thing on that road that the guild is proud of and has never once said out loud.” He turned round. “The guild is not making money out of that boy and never has and could not if it tried, and I have been waiting nineteen years for a person to notice that and there has not been one, and now there is a boy of twenty-two who noticed it in about four minutes because I made him do the sum on a sea wall.

“And here is the shape of it and you may carry it up her and you may put it in front of nine men, and it is the whole of what is wrong on that road. The thing on that road that would make the boy safer is a different price on a bad day. That is worth thirteen shillings a year, and if there were a bad seventh every week of every year it would be worth thirteen shillings a year to the households and about the same to the guild, and thirteen shillings is not worth a fight. Thirteen shillings is not worth a season’s bad winter. Thirteen shillings is not worth a council arguing about in public and being wrong in public and having it in a minute book for ever.

“And the thing on that road that is not worth a fight is a man to stand at the far end of it. And that is two pounds. And the council has just voted for the two pounds and cannot pay the two pounds, and I voted against it, and I was right about the money and I was wrong about the only part that mattered, and I would like it recorded that I knew that on the fourth and voted no on the money.”

The boy with the pot had been standing on the stones for about ten minutes with a pot of paint in both hands and had not put it down and had not moved.

“Put it on the wall,” Gell Roke said to him. “That is what it is for.”

The boy put the pot on the wall. It stayed there.

---

“You will go back up there and tell her,” said Jory Vance, “and I am going to ask you a thing first and I am asking it because you are the only man on this coast who has been on that flat for nothing for nineteen years and I have to know the answer and you are the only one who can give it to me.”

“I am not taking two pounds a year to stand at the end of that flat.”

“I did not say you were.”

“You did not have to,” said Gell Roke. “You are going to go back up that hill with a number on a board and you are going to have to tell somebody whose job it is, and there is no somebody, and the first name that comes into your head on a coast like this one is the man who is standing on the stones with a pot of paint. And you want to know before you go, and that is decent of you, and the answer is no, and I will give you the reason for the no, and then it is out of my hands and out of yours and you can put it in front of nine men or not.”

“All right.”

“Because the guild has not paid me one penny in nineteen years and I have kept that staff up to the truth by doing it anyway, and nine men on this coast will never be got to understand why a man of seventy-one ought to be paid two pounds a year to go on doing a thing he has never been paid for and would not stop doing.” He picked the brush up. “If they pay me, then for nineteen years I did it for nothing, and if they find out I did it for nothing, then it is on the staff that it is on the board, and a painted thing that a person put there for love is not the same as a painted thing a person is paid for, and the day somebody needs it to be the second one, it will not be. I would rather be a man who has never been paid and always has, because that is a thing you can say out loud in a room, and in nine years there is not going to be anybody else on this coast who can say it out loud at all.”

Jory Vance wrote that down too. He wrote it on the back of the board where the returns go, in a hand that was not a clerk's, and he did not know why he wrote it, and he did not find out until the third of the fifth tide, when a sentence of his own came back to him off a cart, written down by somebody else, and it was not the sentence he had said.

“Then who is it,” he said.

“That,” said Gell Roke, “is the whole of the thing, and I have thought about it for nine years, and I have got no answer, and neither have you, and the flat is nine hundred yards long and it is not got a keeper and it never has, and there is no person on this coast to be it. So it will be whoever is on the stones. And you will write that down as though it were a rule, and it is not a rule, it is a thing happening, and a rule has a person’s name at the end of it and this has not got anybody’s name at the end of it, and in a year one of those two is going to be in a pane and the other one is not going to be anywhere.”


---

The ninth crossing of the sixth day of the fifth tide was at about the eighth bell and about seventy people were on the shingle, which is more than a crossing that takes nine crossings gets on an ordinary sixth.

Nim Hask went over at the ninth with a hand-lead in his fist and a cart behind him, and the cart was not his and the cart had four carts’ worth of business that day because the strait was shut, and Jory Vance was at the chain with the board and no line on it and wrote nothing, and Corrie Tallow was at the far stones with her slate counting every one that went over, and Corrie Tallow is not paid and will never be paid and was there because she is always there on the ninth.

Gell Roke watched the boy go the length of nine hundred yards of wet shingle with a channel down the middle of it, on the worst day of that week, for the ninth time that day, at the best price of that week.

He came down off the stones with his hands in his pockets and said it out loud, once, to nobody in particular, in the voice of a man who has been painting a number on a post for nineteen years and has just found out what it is for.

“Thirteen shillings,” he said. “That is what it is worth. That is all of it, and it is not worth a fight, and he is still on it nine times, and there is nothing in that anybody can do.”

Nobody heard him except the boy, who was eleven, and who was wet to the waist, and who went home.

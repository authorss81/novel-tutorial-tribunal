# Chapter 148: What Pulling It Costs

The seventh of the ninth tide is a bar day and the flat takes nine, and the sea was over the crown of the bar before the light and the whole two hundred yards of gravel at the north end was under it, and the water owned the bar and did what it does three days a week, and about seventy people were on nine hundred yards of shingle by the fourth bell.

Nobody stood on the stone. It comes round twice a week and the last person to stand on it was a carter of thirty-three on the fourth of the seventh tide, and that is three tides back, and there have been four days since on which the stone was standing there with nobody on it, and this was the other sort of day, and the woman who stands on it does not know this is the sort of day she does not know, and there was not one question anybody could have put there on that shingle and not one person on it would have walked up to try.

There was one thing left to settle and it was the whole of what the thing was, and he had spent two days failing to settle it and had not told anybody that he was failing.

The three of them had each said yes to a page of their own, and the three pages were in a bundle going up the coast road, and the question had come out of a room a mile and a flight of stairs up the hill on the sixth evening from the one person of the three who could not leave her counter, and it had been one sentence and she had said it standing up.

“If it is not revocable it is a licence,” Bettany Rook had said. “And the guild sells licences. And this is not a licence, and if I have put my name to a thing that cannot be pulled then I have put my name to a licence, and I would rather it could be pulled, and I would rather it cost something to pull.”

He had asked her what it would cost to pull and she had said that she did not know and that she was not going to find out by thinking about it in a room, and that if it did not cost anything then anybody could pull it and it would be pulled in a fortnight by a person with a grudge and a bad morning, and that a thing that can be pulled by anybody for nothing is not a charter either. It is a notice.

So the answer to what it costs to pull it had to be worked out by people who would have to pay it, on a coast with nobody in it who could be asked to work anything out, and he had no standing to call anybody and did not call anybody, and it got worked out anyway in about five places over one day because five people on this coast cannot leave a thing alone once they have heard of it.

---

It started at the hard at about the third bell, because a boat’s carpenter of fifty-two was planing a garboard and had a hull on his ways and a slip of his own a long way inside the gravel.

“You have asked me a question and I have got nothing,” said Pell Cawley, without stopping. “I have been sitting on that for a day and a half and I keep arriving at the same place and it is this. A man has to be able to pull it. Not the guild and not the office and not nine men in a room. A man. Any man who goes over that flat. And the minute you say any man, then it is a day, because a carter cannot pull it standing in a lane and a stall-holder cannot pull it between two customers.

“And then it is worse than a day, and this is the part I have not got round. It has to be said out loud, in the open, on this flat, at the head of it, with about seventy people on it, and not one word of it written for him, because a man who pulls a thing by letter is a body, and you have gone a week without a building on the end of this on purpose.

“Which means a man who pulls it has to stand on nine hundred yards of shingle and say why in his own words to every person there, and there is no second door and no clerk to send it to, and whoever he is, he is a person who has said out loud in front of seventy people that three people he may never have spoken to got something wrong.

“That is a day, and it is worse than a day. That is a day and a thing that cannot be put back.”

He put the plane down and picked it up again.

“And I will tell you the part that will do it in about a month and kill it and nobody will notice it happening. You want the day to be a day the person can look at that wall. You cannot do it on a seventh. You cannot do it on a fourth or a sixth either, not properly, and if a man tries it on a bar day he is a man in nine foot of water inside an hour and a quarter. So the day you are asking for is a day he cannot use, and the first time a man works that out he will not use it, and the second time somebody else will not use it, and a month after that nobody on this coast will believe it can be done at all, and it will be a thing that was.”

---

A woman of forty-eight at a stall four hundred yards off said the next part and said it to about nine people who were at the far stones for the ninth crossing and were not the least bit interested.

“The day is not the part that hurts him,” said Corrie Tallow. “I have been at that stall four years and I can tell you what a day is. A day is a cart, or a stall, or a boat, and a man loses it and it is over.

“What hurts him is that he has to stand there and be asked nothing. He stands on that shingle and says why and then about seventy people look at him, and not one of them may say a word back, because they are not the ones who made it and they cannot be, and the whole of what is left for him is seventy faces and no answer.

“I have watched about four people on this coast be refused something in the open and it is the only thing I have ever seen here that costs more than a day. It does not cost the man a day. It costs him the standing he had at the stall, or the boat, or the flat, and he will get it back in about a year if he is lucky and he will feel it every day until he does.

“And the last part, and this is the one I have been turning over since the second of a tide and it is why I said what I said in that lane and I will say it again: he cannot pull it for anybody. He cannot pull it for the people who cannot get over that flat on a bad morning. He cannot pull it on behalf of the eleven households or the boy with the lead or a woman with a handcart. He can pull it for himself, and if he does that then it is his grievance and it is a real one, and it is the only shape the thing has.”

Nobody objected, which on this coast is not a consent and Corrie Tallow had said so out loud three days ago in about nine hundred words, and about four of the people standing there had heard that and one of them had written it on a board in a drawer a mile up a hill.

---

The man on the chain said the other half and said it without turning round, at about the seventh bell, with a cart in the middle of the flat and the ninth crossing about an hour off.

“A revocation stops a thing and does not undo it,” said Hal Bevan. “You know that. You have known it since before you came to this coast and you have known it better than anybody on it, and I have watched you work it out on your own in about four minutes at a counter on the sixth day of a tide four hundred miles behind you, and I am the last man on four hundred miles who ought to be telling you something you have not worked out yourself.

“But I am going to say it anyway, because it is the part that makes the thing real. When a man pulls it, the eleven yards is still going. The water does not know. And the person who pulled it will be the person who said three people who were not together were wrong about a wall, and he will have said it in the open, and he will have been right or wrong about a thing nobody on this coast can prove either way, because the two photographs cannot come out of their room and a man with a lead line has not been out on a fourth.

“So he is going to be a man who was wrong, or a man who was right, in public, with his name on it, on a coast where there is not one door anybody can show it to anybody else in. And the reason that is worth paying a day for is that it is the only kind of record this coast has ever had, and it is not a record anybody will look at. It is a record somebody can be got at.”

---

And at the north end, at about the eighth bell, a woman of thirty-six came out of her own yard with a warp over her shoulder and stopped in the mouth of it, and she had been out in the deep twice that day and had come in past the wall at about the sixth bell and looked at the middle eleven yards of it from a flat bottom on a day when the water owns the bar, which is the only day of the week it can be looked at from that side and is the worst day of the week to be there.

“I am the only one of the three who can get under it,” she said. “So it will be me that gets asked, and I have worked out what I would want before I would do it, and I am telling you both because you will not ask me and I am not going to wait to be asked.

“I would want the day back. A day is a day and I would want it and I have never had one of anybody’s days and I have had eleven years of my own, so I know exactly what one is worth and I am not being brave about it.

“And I would want the eleven yards sounded before I pulled it. Not after. Before. And I would want it sounded by a man with a lead line in a boat of his own on a day the sea is standing on the crown, and I would want the number in his book with his name on it and the date on it, and then I would stand on that shingle and say why, and the number would be on the table in front of seventy people when I said it.

“Without that, it is not a revocation. It is a person being angry in public. And I have been angry in public on this coast twice and one of them was at a counter about a basket, and there is a thing about being angry in public on four hundred miles that nobody tells you and it is that the people who were right about the small thing get asked about the big one for the rest of their lives.”

---

At the turn, in the ordinary way, as it is every seventh, the lamp-oil man at the head of the lane opened his door, came out, leaned on the frame of it with his shoulder, and sang the crossing’s song to about nine people and the water.

It is a tariff in four lines and it is not written down and it is about a hundred years old or thereabouts and it is not a court and it is not an instrument and it settles nothing, and nobody put a figure out of it, and nobody appealed with it, and about nine people on that shingle heard it and had heard it a hundred times before and would hear it a hundred times after, and it was not the subject of anything at all that day.

At about the ninth bell a man of thirty-three, who had been standing at the head of the flat since the fourth bell with his beasts’ heads up and a load of deals behind him waiting for the flat to take the water off, came down about forty yards of shingle on foot and stood four feet off a stranger and said the thing he had been working out for a day and a half and had told nobody, which was that he had heard on the fourth that there was going to be a thing on this coast a person could pull, and that he had thought about it for a day and a half, and that he was not going to do it, and why.

“I spent the only question I have ever had on this coast on a piece of paper,” said Wend Garrow, “and a woman on that stone told me out loud in front of seventy people that I had not spent it on the water, and she was right, and I have not been able to put it anywhere for a month, and there is nowhere on this coast to put a thing that has happened to a man.

“There is now. That is what I have been working out. Not because I want it and not because anybody put me up to it — I have not spoken to a man off a road in my life and I am not speaking to him now. Because I have been in a fortnight where the only door there was a stone with one question on it, twice a week, and a woman standing on it, and that is a thing you can put a thing into. A thing anybody can pull is a thing you can put a thing into, and I have been standing on this flat for a day and a half finding that out, and I am not pulling it.

“Not because I would not be allowed to. Because I would be pulling it about me, and it would be a day, and a day is a cart and a pair of beasts and a landing, and I would stand on this shingle at the turn and say in front of seventy people that three people got a wall wrong, and the one of them that could answer me back is the harbourmaster and she is up that hill and she would be at that counter in about four months, and I would have taken a day off a road that is two days a week for it.

“So I am not going to. And I would like it said out loud, at the head of this flat, by somebody, that a man of thirty-three worked out that he could pull it and decided not to, and that nobody on this coast has to think the thing is not real because of him. Put that in your bundle, and if the clerk in the city cannot read it then he cannot read it, and that will be his.”

He got his beasts’ heads up and went over the flat, and got to the other side in about three quarters of an hour with his beasts used to it, and he did not look back at the stone.

That is the whole of what it costs a person to pull a thing on four hundred miles of this coast, and it is a day and a standing and seventy faces and no answer, and it cannot be done for anybody else, and the day it happens it is a day the sea is over the crown and a wall cannot be looked at from the side that matters.

Which is what a charter is, and not a notice and not a licence.

And a man of thirty-one stood at the near end of nine hundred yards of wet shingle at about the ninth bell with his weight on his good side, and had asked four people a question he had no right to ask and had been answered by all four of them in the open on a bad day, and had not persuaded a single one of them of anything, and had not once said out loud what any of them should do, and had no page for it, and was not going to write one.

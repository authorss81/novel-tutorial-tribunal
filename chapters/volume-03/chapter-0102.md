# Chapter 102: Seven Days and a Wall With One Door

The guild house at Kestrel Reach is a stone building on the harbour side with the custom counter on the ground floor and the tide office over it, and the door has a bell on it that is worked by a wire and not by a hand, so that a person inside can hear a person outside without getting up. The Reach has about nine hundred people in it and the house has four rooms and a loft over the loft, and everything anybody on this coast knows about the water is kept in the upper room, and the upper room is not opened without somebody in it.

That last part is not secrecy and Bettany Rook will tell you so in about nine words, standing up, without being asked, and she will be right about that as she is right about the water.

She is fifty-one. She was the harbourmaster's clerk for nineteen years and she has been the harbourmaster for six, and she is the only person in this building who can be sent for, and she has a hood she wears on the water side of the house because the light off the Reach is bad in the afternoon and has been getting worse for two seasons.

The seventh day of the tide. From the first bell.

Wend Garrow was at the counter before she had the second cup of anything down, with his licence in his hand and a hook in the other one, and he had not slept, and he was not a liar, and he was going to be right about most of it.

“Morning,” she said. “You have brought that licence round again, which I did notice at the second bell yesterday and did not expect twice in three days.”

“You said the first of the tide.”

“I did, and it was the first, and it was a season, and here it is with the days on it, or rather here it is with the days not on it.” She took it without hurrying and looked at it the way she looked at everything, which was not quickly. “Thirty-one shillings.”

“For seven days a week of the tide.”

“For seven days a week of the tide,” she said. “And there is no refund clause, and I have never had one in nineteen years, and I will tell you why in a moment and it is not a lawyer's reason. Where is your cart?”

“In the channel, on the fourth, at the seventh bell, in eleven hundred yards of it, which you will find is four hundred yards more than you told me when I bought it, and I have counted, twice, with my own rope.”

“Yes,” she said. “Four hundred yards. That is correct and it is in the chart and I have told the guild twice that the chart is right and the mark on the bar staff is short by four hundred yards, and nobody has painted it, because to paint it you have to come out on the bar at low water on the second day with a pot, and there is one man on this coast who will do that for the guild and he is seventy-one and he does it when he feels like it.” She put the licence down flat on the counter. “So your cart is at four hundred yards and not at nil, and I am not blaming you for that and it does not pay for your salt boards.”

“I am not asking you to pay for my salt boards,” said Wend Garrow. “I am asking whether a man who buys a season's passage is told which three days of it he cannot use.”

“No,” said Bettany Rook.

“No?”

“He can come and look at the chart,” she said. “Any day of the tide. He can come up to this room and stand in front of that pane and read the fourth off it with his own eyes, and nobody will charge him a penny to read it, because it is a sheet of glass and there is no copying fee on a sheet of glass. It costs him the walk up the hill from the crossing and the day he spends doing it, and that is the whole of what it costs.”

She said it flat, and she was not being hard, and she had said it before, and the man at the back of the room — thirty-one, wet through, a bad leg and a foreign coat — made a small sound and she turned round and looked at him properly for the first time.

“You are the man she went eleven miles for,” she said.

“I am.”

“Then you have heard the answer, and I will say it again in front of the counter, because the answer is right and I would like two people to have it.” She put her hand flat on the licence. “I am asked, twice a tide, roughly, by somebody who has had a bad week: is it not a shame that a body holds the only water. And the answer is yes, it is a shame, and it is also the only reason anybody in this Reach has drowned is one. Last year a boy of fourteen went out on the fourth in a punt with a bad lock and came back. He is alive and he is in the market on a market day and he knows the difference between the water and the channel as well as anybody on this coast. If there were no guild, there would be no guild telling him, and there would be a great many fourteen-year-olds, and every one of them would be finding out where the channel goes, one at a time, on a bad day, with nobody on the bank.”

The System put the licence in front of Ilyan Vester on the seventh day of a tide, at the second bell, at a counter on a harbour, and it was a notice and not a rule about the world.

**A LICENCE OF PASSAGE IS ISSUED BY THE WATER AND PASSAGE GUILD OF KESTREL REACH FOR SEVEN DAYS OF EVERY TIDE. THE SEASON IS PAID FOR ONCE, AT THE FIRST BELL OF THE FIRST OF THE TIDE. THE LICENCE DOES NOT NAME THE DAYS. THE LICENCE IS NOT REFUNDED ON A DAY THE WATER IS ON THE BAR.**
**WHAT IS NOT DECIDED: WHO BURNS THE PASSAGE WHEN THE WATER IS ON THE BAR. THE LICENCE SAYS WHO PAYS FOR IT AND SAYS NOTHING ELSE, AND THE WORD AT THE BOTTOM OF IT IS NOT A NAME.**

She was still talking. She had not stopped and the room had not gone quiet, and Ilyan was standing three feet from a piece of glass on a wall with a charter on it and the leg had the whole of her voice in it and it was the wrong weight again. Forty-four years old, somewhere four hundred miles behind him, and he was thirty-one, and the weight was not his and it had never once been his, and there was no way on any coast to explain a leg.

“You are counting,” she said. “You have been counting since you came in. I do the same thing and I have done it for nineteen years, and there is a habit of it, so I will tell you what you are doing and then I will stop. You have wanted to say a thing and you have been arranging it. Say it.”

“I was going to ask what a passage is,” he said.

“That is a real question,” she said, with a small flat sound that was not quite a laugh, “and it is the ninth one I have had off the road this season, and I will give you the answer, and the answer is a pane of glass on the north wall and it is in the guild's hand and it is the only legible copy of it, and you may read it as often as you like, and there is no charge.”

He read it. He got the first line and he got about four words of the second and then the words stopped meaning anything, the way they do, and there was nothing in the world on the rest of that pane he could take hold of. He stood in front of it for a while with his hand up near it and did not touch it, and he was aware, in a way that was worse than the leg, that he was doing the thing he had done in four months in a city and had told nobody about, and had never once managed in front of a person who did not already know.

He said, “Say the third one again.”

“The third one.”

“The bit after the part about the water being kept.”

She did not say it again. She said, quite pleasantly, and in front of the counter, in a voice pitched to carry about as far as a fishwife:

“You have been on that flat two days and you have not worked out how deep it is,” she said. “The bar staff at the harbour mouth is painted to two feet under. What is the reading on the staff at the second bell this morning, and do not tell me the tide book, tell me the staff.”

He had walked past it. The number was on it. He had looked at the staff and had not read the number and had gone on, because there was no reason on that coast for a man to stop and read a number on a post.

“I did not read it,” he said.

“No,” she said. “Nobody reads it. That is the first thing I have said to a stranger in six years that I have had to be right about.”

She turned back to the counter and gave Wend Garrow three sentences about what the guild would do, and the guild would do nothing, and the three sentences were that the guild would pay the two pounds four for the loan of the wreck gear out of the passage money as it always had, and that the guild would write his loss in the season book so that no other carter bought a licence in the same week without somebody saying a word to him, and that the guild would not pay for the salt boards and could not be made to.

“And I will tell you the last of it in a room with nobody in it,” she said, “because it is the only thing I have got and I would like it said once in daylight. The guild voted a reader at the crossing four years ago. Two pounds a year, out of the crossing's own takings, to sit on the chain and say the fourth out loud to anybody who came. We had him eleven months. His name is Seth Garrick and he sells lamp oil now and he was twenty-six then and he is a good reader and it is not his fault that the crossing took ninety pounds eighteen of tariff that year against a hundred and six the year before, which was a kiln year, and a wage comes out of what the crossing takes, and the crossing took less, and we took the reader off in the eleventh month, and I did not vote against it and I voted for it and I have not voted for it twice.”

“Because the money was the money,” said Wend Garrow.

“Because the money was the money,” she said, “and because a guild that keeps a reader it cannot pay keeps a bad debt, and because I am right about the water, and I have been right about the water for nineteen years, and I have never once been right about anything else, and I would like the difference kept in.” She looked at him again. “You did not read the staff. The staff is free. The chart is free. Everything that would have kept that man out of the channel is free and is on a wall in this room, and the only thing it costs is a day at the crossing, and the only people who cannot find a day are the people who need it, and that is not a trick anybody here is playing. That is the shape of the thing. I have been inside it nineteen years and I have got very good at it and it has never once been a lie.”

The hood on the water side was up. Underneath it her face was the face of a woman in a bad light, and she was fifty-one, and two seasons ago the light off that water had stopped hurting and started going, and she had said nothing about it to anybody in this building, and she had asked one man on this coast a question about it in the ordinary way a person asks, and he had told her the truth, and she had not asked him a second thing.

---

He asked it at the sixth bell, in the yard, with the rain coming off the eaves in ropes, and she had the tide office door open behind her with the light of a lamp on the stone and the pane standing in its frame at the end of the room like a window into a room.

“Read me the part about a bad week,” he said.

“There is no part about a bad week.”

“Read me the part about who holds the passage when the water is over the bar.”

“There is no part about that either, and I will tell you why there is no part about it, and I have told the guild council for eleven years and I have never once been asked again, which is itself a kind of answer.” She put her hand on the door frame. “A charter is a thing that says what a thing is. This charter says what a passage is, and a passage is water a boat may go over and back on, licensed, by the season. That is the whole of what it says. It is a good charter. It is the best charter I have read anywhere and I have been reading them since I was twenty-six. And it does not say what happens on the fourth day, because on the fourth day nothing happens, because the fourth day is not a passage, and the charter is not a place where you write down what you are not going to do.”

“So who holds it when the water comes?”

“The man,” she said. “That is the answer and it is the correct one and I will not improve it for you. The man holds it. He is in his own boat, on his own licence, on his own season, with his own name on the counter at the first bell, and if the water is where the chart says it is then he is the man in it.”

“And if he cannot read the chart.”

“Then he is a man who bought a season's passage and did not come and look at the wall,” said Bettany Rook, “and the guild has no second way of telling him, and I have asked for one for eleven years, and the council will not pay for it, and I am not going to stand in a yard and tell a stranger from a road that the council is wrong about the money when the council is right about the money and I have been on the losing side of that arithmetic for nineteen years.”

The rain came off the eaves. Somewhere down the hill a chain went up, which was the crossing being shut for the night, and it was not the seventh bell, and there was nobody to shut it for except a boy who did it early out of cold.

“All right,” he said. “Then here is what I can tell you, and you are not going to like it and it is not a favour.”

“I have not liked one in six years,” she said. “Go on.”

“Your guild is not doing anything wrong,” he said. “You are right about the strait and you are right about the bar and you are right that the money is the money and I have been in a room for two years listening to a man explain that a thing with a seal on it is a habit, and you would have agreed with every word of it and you would have said it better, and it never once came out of your mouth. That is the first thing and it is true and I am sorry it is not a better one.”

Her face did not move at all, and he understood that she had waited eleven years for somebody to say it to her and was not going to be thanked for it.

“The second thing,” he said, “is that a guild which holds the only safe water on this coast is not a crime. It is a house with one door in it and no other, and every person on this coast is standing in that house tonight, and that is fine right up until the week the door is shut. And there is going to be a week the door is shut, and not because anybody in this building wants it shut, and I would not tell you when it is if I knew it, and you would not tell me, and when it comes there is no second route and no second office and no person on this coast who can read the one you have got, and there is one of you, and she has a hood on because of the light on the water.”

Bettany Rook took the hood off.

She did it slowly, and she did it because he had said *a hood on because of the light on the water* and because a stranger had not said it, and she held it in her hand for a moment and then put it back on.

“When it comes,” she said, “there is a man here who has been doing it nineteen years and knows the water in his hands and does not need the glass, and he is seventy-one and he paints the bar staff when he feels like it, and there is me, and I have two seasons of sight left in this eye and I have not told this guild because the day I tell this guild is the day I am moved to a counter, and I know what the guild would do and it would be right to do it.”

She looked at him for slightly longer than a person needs to.

“Your leg is not the water,” she said. “It is a man of forty-four standing at a gate, and I have watched men walk with another man's work for thirty years and I know what it looks like, and you are not going to be asked about it in this house, and I am not going to talk about it again.”

“All right,” he said.

“If you want the fourth day and the sixth and the seventh, they are painted on a staff at the harbour mouth and the numbers on it are the truth and the chart is the same truth with more words on it, and the staff is two hundred yards that way and the tide is out of it until the turn tomorrow,” said Bettany Rook, “and if you can read a number on a post you can have the whole of this coast in one morning, and I would like to know what you do with it.”

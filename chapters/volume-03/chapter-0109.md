# Chapter 109: Sixteen Carts, and the Line He Copied Off the Sheet

The bar road runs four miles along the coast above the north end and it goes over a sand bar with nine feet of tide over the middle of it in a bad week, and Alma Gannock has kept that crossing for eight years with a chain and a stool and a tarpaulin, and it has never once been shut, and four carts go over it in a good week.

She is eighty-one. Dell Gannock's mother. She knits, and she has a boy of eight with her because his mother is dead and Dell is on the water, and she takes the penny a cart herself on a slate and brings the slate in a basket to the tide office once a quarter, and the tide office writes the number down and does not check it, because there is nothing to check it against.

“There is nobody at the other end of this road to count them,” she said. “Not now. There was a man with a hut two years gone and before him a woman, and that is the whole of the counting on this road and it is me, and it has been me for eight years and there is a penny in it for the guild and a chain I paid for out of my own knitting.”

Ilyan Vester sat down on the stool, which she offered him, and the leg went on the stool with him, and the tarpaulin came up over the bar and the light came off the water and made him put his hand up.

“What happens when the water is not on the bar and the road is not fit?” he asked.

“Nothing,” said Alma Gannock. “Sixteen carts came over that week and I let them and I have done it twice in eight years and I paid the guild out of my own basket for the sixteen, and the road stayed open, and if it had shut then the next four days of that week would have come round the shore and lost a day and a half of a horse apiece, and that is about nine pounds, and I had nine pounds of eggs and knitting in a box that year and now I have a box that is not so full.”

“Why would you pay it out of your own?”

“Because the penny is for the chain and the chain is mine and the man is me,” she said. “That is what a penny a cart is. I have never once asked the guild for a penny of it. Eight years. You may write that down in whatever you have been writing in, and I have said it because you asked me and because I am eighty-one and I do not get asked, and I would like it said once by somebody.”

He asked her how many carts in a bad week and she said sixteen, and he asked her how many in a good week and she said four, and he asked her how many weeks in eight years she would call bad and she said two, and he asked her what the other weeks came to and she said about four hundred and fourteen, and he wrote all four numbers on a page out of a penny exercise book and it took him a moment because of the hand.

Then he said the thing he had come to say, which was not the thing.

“Alma. There is a card on this crossing. It has been there eight years. It is in a box in the guild house and there is a copy in the tide office, and there is a line at the bottom of it with your name at the end of it.”

“That is my husband,” she said. “Ivo. He had that card put in. He did it the first winter, the year the boy was born, and he could not read, and he was very proud of it, and I have never seen the card and I have never wanted to see it.”

“He could not read.”

“He could not read a word of it, and he wrote his name at the bottom of the piece of paper I signed my name on, and he was proud, and it took him two days.” She pulled the shawl round. “He is two years dead. Are you here to tell me something about my husband?”

“No,” said Ilyan Vester.

“All right,” said Alma Gannock, and went on knitting, and did not ask.

---

Wend Garrow found him on the bar road at the second bell and had clearly been walking up from the Reach for an hour and a half with something worked out in his head and no patience left for the walking.

“It was me,” he said.

The road above the crossing is one cart wide and there was a bar coming up behind them and neither of them could go anywhere.

“Say the rest of it,” said Ilyan Vester.

“I filled that card in. Eight years ago, my first season with a cart, working for Ivo Gannock, and he could not write, so I did it, and he stood over me for two days and told me the guild would take the crossing because the guild takes all of them and it is a form of words and a form of words is not a thing I can be hanged for.” Wend Garrow put the hook down on the cart-board, which was a thing of his that Ilyan Vester had not known he owned. “There was a print on the counter in the guild house with the lines on it and I copied them. All of them. I copied every line on the sheet, and I did not know that the sheet was two cards.”

“Two cards.”

“The crossing card and the tide card. They come off the same plate in the same shop and they are printed one under the other, and I never found out until about four minutes ago, and I have known about the bar on the fourth for three weeks and you are the first man I have said it to, and I have been carrying it up and down this road in a cart for three weeks.” He wiped his face with the back of his wrist. “I filled in one card and I copied the lines off a sheet that had a second thing on it, and I put in a name at the end of a line that is not about a crossing.”

“Which line.”

“The one at the bottom.”

“Read it to me.”

“I cannot read it,” said Wend Garrow, “and that is the whole of it and that is why it has been in me for three weeks, because I could not tell anybody what was on it. I told you the words. I can tell you what a line says the way you tell a story. The bottom line is about a week when the water is not on it, and the tide office is not bearing the cost of that week, and the person at the end of the line is the person who is.”

“The cost of a week in which the water is not on it is not borne by the guild.”

“No,” said Wend Garrow. “It is not. And it is not borne by Ivo Gannock, and he is the one who stood over me for two days, and he is two years dead and I filled the card in for him, and I did not know, and the guild clerk who took the card in did not ask me what the bottom line was because there is no bottom line on the crossing card, and I copied it because it was on the sheet.”

“Does the guild know the crossing has been short twice?”

“The guild knows what a woman in a basket says it is,” said Wend Garrow. “There is no second person counting those carts. There is not a person anywhere on that road but her. So whatever she puts in the basket is what happened, and the guild does not know it is short, because a guild cannot know that a thing it is not counting has not been counted.”

---

That is the whole of it, and it took the rest of that day and a good part of the next morning, and none of it is a mystery.

The guild's crossing card and the guild's tide-office card are printed from one plate in one shop in a city two hundred miles up the coast, one under the other, with a rule between them, and the rule is a hair's width and a clerk filling a card in at a counter does not see a rule. A man who cannot read does not see a rule at all. He sees a line and he copies it because he has been told to fill the card in and the line is there. He copies it correctly. It is the wrong line.

The guild is not hiding anything. The guild printed two cards off one plate because the pressman had one plate and two cards off one plate are cheaper than one card off two, and the guild's own return on that crossing for eight years says it was open every week of every year and never short, because the return is a return and a return is made out of the number that comes in the basket.

And the guild's charter does say, in the one sentence that is in everybody's mouth on that road, that the passage-holder bears the passage. It is the sentence that keeps a licence from being a guarantee. It is correct. It is the sentence Bettany Rook has said out loud to strangers for six years.

And that sentence is standing at the end of the bottom line of a card at a bar road, with a name against it, and the name is Alma Gannock, and she is eighty-one, and she has been the person who bears the cost of a week the water is not on it for eight years, and it has cost her thirty-two pence, and it has cost her two hundred and four decisions, and nobody has asked her one question about any of it.

The money is nothing. That is the finding and it took the whole of a day to be sure of it. Two bad weeks in eight years, sixteen carts, a penny a cart, and thirty-two pence between them, and a woman of eighty-one has taken that out of a box that was not so full afterwards. If somebody sat down in a room and looked at the arithmetic alone they would send a letter.

The rest of it is not a number. It is that for eight years a woman has decided, on her own, on a bar road, in bad weather, with a boy of nine in the hut, whether four carts or sixteen go over; that nobody on this coast knows that she is deciding; that if the guild knew, it would put a second person on that road, because a second person is how you stop one person being the whole of a thing; and that she would rather be the whole of the thing, and she has never been asked why, and neither would she be able to say it in one sentence if she were asked today.

Efa Lund said the other thing. She came up the shore road on the second of the tide with a basket on her arm and she had heard from Cleve Gannock, at a shop, in a shop, in three days, and she stood in the middle of the bar road and said:

“You have had it for a day and a half. You have had the worst fact I have heard in eleven years of counting and you have not gone back down there and asked her, and I said on the steps at Slackwater what would happen and I was right about it, and you have done the other thing, and I would like to hear you say why, because I have thought about nothing else for a day and a half and I am not the only one.”

“I am not going to ask her,” said Ilyan Vester.

“Say why, and do not say it kindly, and do not give me the version where it is about her.”

“It is about her, and I am not going to pretend it is not, and I am not going to pretend the other reason is worse,” he said. “Two reasons and neither of them is a good one and the second one is the one I would have used if I had not had a day and a half.

“The first is that if I ask her what the bottom line says, I am putting an eighty-one-year-old woman in front of a guild that has no desk for her, in a season when the passage money is short, over a card that a man filled in honestly and a shop printed honestly and a clerk took in honestly, and nothing will come of it that is worth a day to her, and the day would be mine and not hers and she is the one who would be standing there.

“The second is that I have been told what to do with this once already, on a coast, on the third of a tide, in a net loft, by a man with a lighter with a tarpaulin on her, and what I was told was that consent is a door with a person in it, and that a person in a door can be moved, and that the only way anybody in my trade ever moves a person is by standing in the doorway being awkward. And I have been doing that for four hundred miles and I got away with it once because the man in the doorway had nothing behind him. If I go down that bar road and stand in Alma Gannock's doorway then I will have a page behind me, and it will be a page about a card, and there is no version of that in which she is not moved.”

Efa Lund stood on the bar road and looked at the water for a while.

“That is two reasons,” she said, “and I have been trying all morning to think of a third that is a lie. Go on.”

“There is a third one and it is the worst one and I have had it since the sixth,” he said. “I have found the pattern of it three times now on this coast and the pattern is: a person is standing nearest to a duty, and nobody has written down the duty, and the person has been doing it. Bel Hask. Corrie Tallow. Sabra Trew. And now an old woman on a bar road with a slate and a basket and two shillings and eightpence of her own money in a box. I could put her in a page in a day. I have got a penny exercise book and a hand that will not hold a chalk square and I could write four hundred words about Alma Gannock in an hour, and it would be the fourth time I have done the same thing in four hundred miles, and every single one of the four has cost the person something that nobody put in the book.

“So she does not go in the book. Not this season. And I am writing down that I did not, and what the reason was, and the date, and that it is mine, and I am going to hand it to a woman with a ledger on this coast who is going to read it and tell me that a man off a road is nobody and cannot keep a thing like that. And she will be right, and then I shall have to go and find out how a person on this coast is supposed to be asked, and I do not know, and there is nobody here to teach me, and I have been saying that out loud for nine days to anybody who will stand still.”

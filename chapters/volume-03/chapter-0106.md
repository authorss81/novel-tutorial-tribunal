# Chapter 106: Eleven and Seventeen, and a Boy Who Is Not There

The number got back before he did.

Cleve Gannock came up the shore road on the fifth with a load of what the flat boat had brought over, and he had heard it in a shop, and he had heard it from a man who had heard it at Slackwater from a woman with a book, and by the time Ilyan Vester got off a boat at the Quay at the second bell of the fifth there were two figures on this coast and both of them were true and one of them was his.

He found it out from a carter, on the shingle, in about nine seconds, because carter to carter on this road is the fastest thing on it.

“Seventeen,” said the carter. “Seventeen households over that flat. That is what they say at Slackwater. That is what the girl with the book has got.”

“Seventeen people,” said Ilyan.

“That is what I heard.”

He stood on nine hundred yards of wet shingle with the tide under him and did the thing he has never in his life been able to stop doing, which was to go back over the sentence and find the joint in it. *Eleven households on that road send somebody over that flat.* Two times, in a loft, to nine people and then to thirty-one. It had come out of his mouth in the same words both times, and it had come out of his mouth carrying a claim about households, and somewhere in three days and nine miles of water it had stopped being a claim about households.

The person whose words had been altered was forty-eight years old, sold rope, and was under an awning forty yards away with a board in front of her with two columns of figures in chalk on it that had been washed off twice and put on twice.

She had not been in the room. She was not going to be in the room. She was going to be told.

---

He told her. That was the first thing he did and he did it badly, standing at the side of her stall with the rain coming sideways off the flat, and he got about eight words in before she put her hand flat on the board.

“Seventeen,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Seventeen what.”

“Your eleven is your eleven. Nobody has said it is wrong and nobody is going to say it is wrong from me. There is a woman at Slackwater — Efa Lund, thirty-four, keeps a count of the flat in a book of her own, eleven years, no pay, not asked — and she counts bodies on the sand at the turn instead of households at the doors, and she has got seventeen, and the six she has that you have not got are not a lie and I can give you all six. Three come down the shore road from the east end on market days and go over and come back and you do not know they are there because you are watching your own eleven doors. One of your eleven is two houses and has been two houses since the spring, and it pays for both and counts for one, and there is no column on that flat for half a house. And two are a man and his wife who went round by the road in the winter, found the road cheaper than a boy, and came back in the spring, and they are on that sand every tide of the week and they have never been on your list, because nobody ever told you they had been away.”

Corrie Tallow looked at him for a while.

“So the sentence you have been carrying up and down this coast is not a sentence,” she said. “It is the front of a sentence.”

“Yes.”

“And it came back as the whole of it, in a shop, on a boat, off a man who was not in the room either.”

“Yes.”

“All right.” She picked the chalk up and looked at the board and put it down again without writing anything on it, which was the first time in two days that he had seen her not write. “Then here is what that is and I want it said once and then let go of. A number is not a thing. A number is a thing a person said, and what the person said it for comes off it when it gets out of the room, the way a name does. My eleven is about the tariff. It is about who is paying a penny a tide, and the answer to that is households with somebody to send. Hers is about the sand. It is about how many bodies are on nine hundred yards of shingle at the turn with a channel down the middle. They are both true and they are not the same claim and the difference between them is a difference of *what you would do about it*, and there is no road on this coast that can carry a difference of what you would do about it. It came back in one piece and it is going to sit in somebody's mouth for years.”

“I know. I can say it again. Corrected.”

“No,” said Corrie Tallow. “You can, and you will, and I would like you to, and it will not work, and I want to tell you why before you spend a day on it. You will say *eleven households and seventeen crossings* and both men will hear *seventeen* and one of them will hear *eleven* and the one who hears eleven will be me and the one who hears seventeen will be him, and in four months there will be a difference of six on a board at that counter and the difference of six will be about a tariff, and the only name anybody in this Reach has for that flat is the name of a boy of eleven, and a boy of eleven is the easiest possible thing in the world to make a difference about.”

---

Bel Hask came down the lane about the fourth bell with a net over her shoulder and stopped when she saw the two of them standing there, and she said, “Is he back?”

“He is back,” said Corrie Tallow.

“Then I will say it and then you can tell me what you think.” She set the net down, which nobody on this coast does at the fourth bell. “I have not sent Nim since the second. Three days. The cart went the long way round on the fourth and it came back a day and a half later with about nine pounds of freight on it that I did not earn and a day and a half of a horse I did pay for. So that is nine pounds and a day and a half of a horse, and the boy is three shillings and threepence a week for thirty-nine tides of it, and I have done that sum four times in three days and it comes out the same way every time.”

“You can send him,” said Ilyan.

“I cannot send him and I have been not sending him for three days and I have been telling myself for three days that it is because the week is wet, and it is not because the week is wet.” She wiped her hands on her apron and looked at the flat, which was dry and had been dry since the second bell. “The sum says he is the cheapest thing on this road and the sum is right. It is not the money. It is that he is on that sand nine times a day three days a week and I am forty-four and I mend nets, and when I cannot walk to the end of the lane to see a cart go over I do not know that the cart has gone. A boy is a thing you can send and a boy comes back and says it went, and if he does not come back then something is wrong, and I would rather pay three shillings and threepence a week than find out.”

Nobody said anything for a while, and about four hundred yards off a chain came up and a boy of about twenty-two with a brush in his cuff came along the shingle with a board under his arm, tallying.

Corrie Tallow watched him go past.

“Go on then,” she said. “Go to that counter and ask them to put the days on it. That is your whole errand and you have had it since the first day and you have not been to the counter once.”

“I have not been asked to go.”

“No,” she said, “and that is the last thing I am going to say to you about it, and then I am going to be a woman with a stall. I have been asked four times in four years to go to that counter and be asked the question by somebody who counts. I have gone twice. I am the woman who has been asking four years. If I go up there with a figure, it will not be a figure, it will be me, and they will hear *her*, and they will hear it in a voice that has been asking for four years, and whatever they say next will be a sentence about me and not about the tariff, and I will have spent a day of the only thing I have got on being the woman who asked, and I have been that woman twice and it has cost me two days and both of them came back with nothing at all.”

She hung the net back on the stall pole, and looked at the lane.

“So I am not going. I am going to be here at the sixth bell tomorrow, and the day after, and the day after that, with a board and a stick, and the number that is right is eleven and I am going to go on counting eleven, and one of my eleven is two houses and there is no column on that flat for half a house, and I am not going to stand in a room and be believed. I have been believed twice.”

---

So he went. On the sixth of the tide, at the second bell, with the leg and the hand, a mile up the hill and a flight of stairs into a room with a pane on the wall of it, and he said it to the counter the way you say a thing that has gone wrong and you are not hiding from it: eleven households and seventeen crossings, both of which are true, both of which are mine and one of which is not, and here is the sentence that lost the joint, and here is the woman it belongs to, and she is under an awning and she has not sent anybody to ask her anything and she has refused to come and I am not asking her to come.

Bettany Rook listened to all of it with her hands on the counter and the hood up on the water side, and she did not interrupt once, and when he had finished she looked at him for a moment and then at the board where Jory Vance was working.

“The returns on that tariff are made out of the tally at the chain,” she said, “and the tally is carts and not people, and it has never been people, and it is not going to be people this year. So there is no difference of six on my board and there never will be, and the reason there is no difference of six on my board is that neither figure is a thing this counter counts.”

“So nothing happens.”

“Something happens,” she said, “and it will take about four months and it will be an argument about the seventh day, and it will be about the flat, and it will not be about the tariff, and when it is about the flat it will be about whether the flat should be a road at all, and I have been waiting eleven years for somebody to put that on a table and I have never once wanted it more than I do not want it on the fourth of a tide with the water over the bar.”

She turned round and got a sheet of paper, which is not a pane and which can be crossed out, and she wrote a thing on it in about forty seconds and gave it to him.

> **CROSSING AT WETHER CROSS, THIS TIDE AND THE LAST. TARIFF AS AT THE TALLY: ONE PENNY THE TIDE. NINE HUNDRED YARDS OF FLAT. THE TALLY IS OF CARTS AND NOT OF PERSONS AND HAS ALWAYS BEEN OF CARTS. THE TARIFF IS THE SAME ON EVERY DAY OF THE TIDE, INCLUDING THE DAYS THE WATER IS ON THE BAR, AND THE COUNCIL HAS REFUSED TWICE TO WRITE A DIFFERENT PRICE FOR A DAY OF WATER AND WILL REFUSE AGAIN.**

“That is not the guild's record and I have not entered it as the guild's record and it is not going into the office book, because if it goes into the office book then in twenty years somebody has to fire it into a pane and I have watched a man nearly do that to a pane and I am not doing it to a sheet of paper I wrote standing up on a wet counter with a pen borrowed off a boy of twenty-two.” She squared it up on the counter. “Take it up the road. It is a sheet of paper and a sheet of paper cannot be argued with in a court, and I have known that for nineteen years and it is the first useful thing anybody has written on this coast about that flat since the flat was made.”

He read it. He got the first line and he got about four words of the second and he stood there and held it at a distance and read it out loud to her, slowly, and she watched his face and said nothing whatsoever, and did not look at the hood, and did not help him, and stood at that counter with her hands flat on it while a man of thirty-one read her own sentence back to her one piece at a time.

“That is all of it,” she said, when he had got to the end. “You have read all of it, and it took you about a minute, and you did not miss a word, which means you are not stupid, and if you are not stupid then I would like to know how, because I have watched eleven clerks fail at that counter and one of them is better at it than you.”

And that was all she said, then, and ever, about that.

---

He walked down the hill to the flat with a sheet of paper in his hand and did not know what to do with it, which was the first time in four months that he had carried a document he could not use in either direction.

Behind him, four hundred yards up the shingle, a woman of forty-four was standing at the top of a lane with a net over her shoulder, watching a lane, and a boy of eleven was somewhere on it.

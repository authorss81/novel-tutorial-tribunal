# Chapter 118: The Sentence That Became a Thing

It started on the morning of the third day of the fifth tide at the head of the lane at Wether Cross, with about nine people within twenty feet of the bench and thirty more standing nine hundred yards off watching the water come in, and it was nobody's fault, and by the time it had gone two miles up the coast road it was a different sentence, and the two of those facts were the whole of what happened.

Corrie Tallow was at the end of the bench and Bettany Rook was at the counter door with her arms folded and the hood down because the door was a windbreak and nobody was looking at her, and Belva Cray had come down the lane for the third day running with the register in a case, and Jory Vance had come down off the hill with a board and a brush in his cuff to make the copy fair enough for a woman to take home.

“The two of them are the same list,” said Corrie Tallow.

She said it to Belva Cray. She was not making a point and she was not doing anything clever and she was tired, because it is a long way round a person's head and she had been round it since the second bell two days before.

“They are the same stretch of coast and they are the same list, going back. The wall is only the part of it you can see.”

“They are not the same list,” said Belva Cray, who had checked every line of it herself. “The wall begins with the kiln and the book does not. I have had both of them for two days and there is four hundred years of that book that has no kiln in it at all.”

“Then that is what I meant. Same coast, same list, going back.”

And Jory Vance, with a brush in his cuff, said, “Then the two records agree, and we can put it that way.”

He said it the way a young man says a thing that has just been made easy for him. It is not what she said. It is what a sentence becomes when a person who is copying at a counter takes the part he can use, and it was said in the open, in a yard, to about nine people, and about four of them agreed with it.

---

Bettany Rook said no to writing it and then wrote something else.

She said: “I am not putting that on a sheet of paper in those words, because those words are not true, and I have been in this building six years and I am not going to start.”

“Then tell me the true ones and I will write those.”

“The true ones are that a woman with a stall counted the flat for thirty-one years, and a woman at a school kept a list, and a woman at a glassworks cuts marks on a wall, and not one of the three has ever laid eyes on the other two, and every one of them is about this water, and they do not agree, and there is no arithmetic that will make them agree, because one of them is counting tides and one of them is counting firings of an arch, and those are not the same count and never were.”

“That is the same thing in more words.”

“It is not the same thing. Yours says we know it. Mine says we do not.”

“All right,” said Jory Vance.

She took the sheet and wrote it herself, which took her about a minute, and it is in her hand and not a clerk's hand.

> **THREE ACCOUNTS OF THE SAME STRETCH OF WATER HAVE BEEN COMPARED ON THIS COAST AND NONE OF THEM IS AN INSTRUMENT. ONE COUNTS TIDES AND ONE COUNTS FIRINGS OF AN ARCH AND THOSE ARE NOT THE SAME COUNT. A LIST OF FOUR HUNDRED YEARS OF WEEKS KEPT IN A SCHOOL A HUNDRED AND FORTY MILES UP THIS COAST, AND A ROW OF MARKS CUT ON A KILN WALL AT THORNGLASS, ARE ABOUT THE SAME GROUND AND WERE KEPT BY PEOPLE WHO NEVER MET. ONE OF THEM HAS LOST FOUR DAYS AND THE OTHER HAS NOT, AND NOBODY CAN PUT THE FOUR DAYS BACK. THIS IS ON PAPER. IT IS NOT THE GUILD'S RECORD AND IT IS NOT ON ANY LIST FOR A FIRE. IT MAY BE WRONG AND IT MAY BE TAKEN DOWN.**

She squared it up and gave it to Jory Vance, and then she went up the hill, and Belva Cray went up the hill after her with the register, because the copy had to be checked against the original in a room with a table in it and there is no table at the head of a lane.

There were three people in that room and Corrie Tallow was not one of them. She was forty yards away under her awning with her board turned to the weather and a line of people waiting, and she has been keeping that count since she was seventeen, and what thirty-one years of it has taught her is that a sentence said in a room with a table in it is a different object from the same sentence said on a bench in the open, and that the difference is not in the words.

Jory Vance asked her one question about it, because he was twenty-two and had a brush in his cuff, and Belva Cray answered him, and the answer was a woman's sentence and it came back out of him in his own grammar and she took it without noticing, because she is fifty-one and had been on a road for four days and because he had asked it in a kind way.

By the time the copy was finished and folded, Corrie Tallow’s sentence was in that room in two versions and the one that was on the paper was the one that was false.

---

Seth Garrick carried the sheet up the coast road in a cart at the fourth bell, because he was going that way to buy oil, and it cost him the oil, and he read it twice on the way because he is a man who reads everything.

He put it on the bench at Thornglass with his hand flat on it for about a minute before he said anything at all.

“There is a woman at the end of this piece of paper who says you are one of three accounts of the same water, and she has said it in the guild's own office, and the harbourmaster wrote it out with her own hand.”

Hollis Thrale is sixty-one and has been at the fire for forty-one years and is right about his craft in every particular that can be tested and wrong about one thing, and the one thing is the whole of what a coast is like. He read the sheet twice. He has a pane on the wall of his own shed that the guild has never seen, and it disagrees with the guild's in two columns out of eleven, and he has known that for two years, and there is no instrument on this coast that would settle it in a room, because the only pane anybody will act on is the guild's and the guild has never let it out of that room in nineteen years.

He read it a third time and put it flat on the bench and kept his hand on it.

“Say the rest of it,” he said. “You have not said the rest of it. You have been holding something in your mouth for a minute and I have watched you not say it, and I am sixty-one and I have watched a great many people not say a thing and it is nearly always the thing.”

“There is a woman at the crossing,” said Seth Garrick, “who was in a yard yesterday with about nine people standing round her, and a young man at the counter with a brush in his cuff said out loud that the two records agree, and about four people said aye, and I have not got a way of telling you that it is the wrong words because I have been standing at a post for nineteen years and I am not an authority on a sentence. I am telling you that the harbourmaster would not write it down and that a man with a brush in his cuff said it, and that the two of them were about nine feet apart at the time.”

Hollis Thrale put the sheet down.

“Say that last part again.”

“Which part.”

“The part about a man with a brush in his cuff,” said Hollis Thrale. “Because I have been in this shed for two years with a piece of glass on my own wall that does not agree with the only glass anybody will act on, and in that entire two years the one thing I have wanted is a second record I could put beside it, and you have just told me that this morning a man on this coast said the two records agree, out loud, in a yard, and that four people said aye.”

“That is what I said.”

“That is not a witness, that is a man agreeing with a sentence,” said Hollis Thrale, “and I have not asked for that and I would not know what to do with it.”

---

Then he said the thing that cost.

“Send her up the road and ask her for the book,” he said. “Not a copy. The book. And I will not put it in an arch and I will not put it in a pane, and there is nothing on this coast that could get a hundred and forty miles of paper into a fire, but I will put it on that bench beside mine, and then there will be two records on one bench in one room on this coast for the first time, and I have wanted that for two years, and you may tell the counter where I got it.”

“You have not got it,” said Sabra Trew.

She was standing at the arch with a bar in her hand and had not come in, and she had said it before anybody had spoken to her, and Hollis Thrale turned round and looked at her for a while.

“No,” he said. “I have got a piece of paper with a sentence on it that a man said in a yard, and the sentence is in the same yard as the truth, and I have got about four minutes' work of it and I do not intend to give it back.”

“You said the word *witness* about a piece of paper in a school,” said Sabra Trew, “and I read that book out in a yard on the second of a tide because I was asked to copy a list of weeks, and I would like it said somewhere that nobody asked me to be a witness to anything and I would have said no.”

“You would have said no and you would have been wrong to,” said Hollis Thrale, “because you cut a row of marks for a living and you are the only person on this coast who can lay one list out beside another and say where the two of them stop and start, and I will not stand in a shed at sixty-one and pretend that is not a thing I want. I am going to want it. That is a thing about me and not about you.”

---

The cost of connecting two places is somebody's back and somebody's words, and on this coast it came due on the same day.

The back was his. He went up the coast road with Seth Garrick's cart at the fourth bell and walked back because there was nothing to sit on, and two miles of that road on a leg carrying a gate hand of forty-four's weight out of a city four hundred miles off is about an hour and a quarter of a thing he does not remember afterwards. He came into the yard at Thornglass at about the eighth bell and there was a man of sixty-one at a fire and a woman of twenty-four with a bar and neither of them asked him about it, and nobody on this coast is going to.

The words were a woman of forty-eight's, and she was not in the room, and she was forty yards under an awning with her board turned to the weather, and what had happened to them was that a woman of fifty-one had answered a question kindly in a room with a table in it.

He wrote it down that night in the back of a penny exercise book, on a torn-out page, with the two versions side by side and nothing else on the page. He was never able to find the join. He is quite sure there was no join at all, because the second one was not a copy of the first one and was not a distortion of it either: it was a different sentence, made out of the first one by a person of fifty-one who did not know she was making it, out of a sentence of a woman of forty-eight, by a person of twenty-two who was being useful, and then it had gone up a road in a cart and out of a woman's mouth in a shed, and the woman of forty-eight had not been in that room and would not have believed a word of it.

What she said: *the two of them are the same list, going back.*

What came back to her: *the two records agree.*

He put the page in with two other torn-out pages of the same book and with a sheet of writing paper from a city four hundred miles behind him that has two questions on it in a hand he asked her not to write in.

---

A carter came down the coast road on the fourth day of the fifth tide with an empty crate and asked at the stall whether the woman was in.

“The woman at the crossing told us to say to her that a man off a road has taken her down to the glassworks,” he said, “and that it is agreed at the counter that the two records are the same, and that the man at Thornglass is asking for the book.”

Corrie Tallow stood behind her own board with the chalk in her hand and did not understand one word of it for about four seconds.

“Ask me that again,” she said.

“The two records are the same and the man at the glassworks is asking for the book.”

“That is not what I said,” said Corrie Tallow. “I said it was the same list going back. There is a difference and it is the whole of what has happened. I did not say they were the same. I did not say they agreed. And I would not have used either of those two words in a yard, and I have kept that count for thirty-one years and I know what a man does with a word that says *agree*.”

“It is on a piece of paper,” said the carter. “I read it off the paper. A man at the counter wrote it and a man in a lamp oil shed read it out in a shed at Thornglass and the girl was there and heard it read.”

Corrie Tallow set the chalk against the edge of the board and left it there.

“Then it is on a piece of paper,” she said, “and I said it to a person.”

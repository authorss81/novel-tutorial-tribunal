# Chapter 135: Two Hundred and Sixty Yards of Stone

A good day on the first of the eighth tide and the flat takes six, and the sea has been down since the middle of the night, and at the north end, four hundred yards up the lane from the crossing, the ebb out of that deep runs long and soft and the whole two hundred yards of gravel on the seaward side of the point stands out of it in the low like a floor.

The man standing on the seaward shoulder of that bar at about the third bell was Halse Tarrant, and he is fifty-eight, and he has been on that wall for thirty-one years, and he was one of the hands that laid the first of it.

“It is two hundred and sixty yards,” he said, “and it is laid in courses, and it is nine courses at this end and it goes from about six foot at the inner end to about two courses at the outer, and the outer end stands out into the deep on purpose.”

He said it to nobody in particular. There was a man forty feet off on the wet shingle with his weight on his good side and a hand-lead in his fist that he had picked up out of a pool and had not put down, and Halse Tarrant had not asked him a question and was not going to.

“And what it is for is one thing and it has been one thing since the year it went down and it will be one thing when I am dead. That deep out there is nine feet on a good day and it is a hole the ebb has cut in a gravel bar, and the wall stands along the seaward side of that hole, and the ebb comes out of it sideways because the sea outside is on that side of this shoulder and the two of them are close together where I am standing. And where the ebb and the outside are close the water makes a hole in the gravel. In the year before they laid this it had gone back eleven yards in two winters and it had taken a hut and a boat slip with it, and the slip had belonged to a man called Teel and there is nobody on this coast who was ever taught by him.”

He put his thumb on the top course.

“So they laid the wall along this shoulder to hold the gravel against the ebb, and the guild paid for it and the guild came out of the Reach with eight men in a season and put it down in about seven weeks, and the last course went on nineteen years and eight months ago in the same year they had a thing fired in a shed at Thornglass for the room upstairs of the office at the Reach, and those two things were paid for out of the same season and they are about two miles apart and there is not one person on this coast who has ever put them in the same sentence.”

He looked at the water for a while.

“I have got about four things to say and I am going to say them in the open on a good day and you can do what you want with them.

“The first is that the guild was right. I have watched that ebb every single day since I was twenty-seven and it is every bit as bad as the man who laid the stone said it would be, and if you take this wall out you have got no north end inside three winters, and I have watched the sea try to do it in nineteen years and it has not done it. I am not standing here telling you that anybody was wrong. I am standing here telling you that they were right and it has not been looked at since.”

The flat was doing its six in the distance. A cart went over at about the third bell and there was nothing to see from where he was standing except that it had gone, and a boat was out somewhere beyond the point with a lug sail up and standing along, and at the yard a man was sorting a heap of bait and had not sorted it, and a boat’s carpenter two boats up the lane had a hull up on his ways and was going over it with a plane, and the whole of that was going on the way it goes on about six days in seven, and none of it was about the wall.

“The second is this, and it is the thing I have not got.

“A wall of stone does not stop water. It stops gravel. The water goes where the water goes and the stone is only there to make sure the gravel is where you want it to be when the water goes. So the wall is worth exactly what is behind it, and what is behind it is about two feet of gravel, and those two feet of gravel are the whole of this point, and if the two feet goes then this is a wall standing in the sea about eleven yards from where anything is.

“And the gravel at the foot of the seaward face — the toe, that is what we call it, the toe of the wall — the toe is the part the ebb works on and the toe is the part that goes. And I have not been at the foot of the seaward face of this wall since about the fourth year, because that face looks into that deep and on a good day the deep is nine foot of green water, and on a fourth and a sixth and a seventh the ebb brings the sand out of the channel and packs the middle of the bar flat, and the deep fills, and the face stands out of it and you can stand a flat boat over the top of it. And on those three days the sea outside is standing on the crown of the bar and the wind is on the quarter, and a man out in that deep on a fourth has about an hour and a quarter and then he is sitting in nine foot on the flat, and there is not one man on this coast who will be out on that bank for eleven yards in an hour and a quarter, because it holds about eleven times in twenty and the nine is somebody’s cart in nine foot of water and it would be his fault for being there.

“So that is where the wall is going to go, if it goes, and it is the one part of it I have not laid my hand on in about fifteen years, and I know that, and I have known it for a long time, and I have not got the four things that would let me know anything about it.”

“Which are what.”

“A lead line, a boat, a man who can sound, and a day.”

He said it the way a man says a list he has had in his mouth for years.

“I have got three of them. I have got a lead line — that is the guild’s, that is in a box in that shed, they put it there when they laid the wall and it has hung in that shed ever since and nobody has ever asked for it. I have got a boat, my own, and she has not been out in about a year because a boat that is not going out is a boat that is not rotting. I can sound; I have been sounding that bar since I was nineteen and I can put a number on a depth to a foot inside of forty yards. What I have not got is the day. That is the whole of what I am short of and it is not money and it is not a boat and it is not anybody’s permission, and I have wanted it since about the sixth year and I have not asked anybody, because I am a man who keeps a wall and a wall does not ask for a boat.”

“The third is the one nobody is going to like,” he said, “and I have known it for years and I have not said it out loud before and I am saying it now because you are a stranger and there is no point in a thing like this going to a person who will be here in a year.”

“The water has moved.”

He said it flat and he did not put anything on it.

“That is not a fright and it is not a discovery and it is not anybody’s fault. The line where that ebb works along the outside of this wall is not where it worked when the stone went down. It is outboard of the outer end now, and it has been going outboard for a good many years, and if you stand at the outer end with a boat you can see where it is working, because the water changes over it and you can see it change.

“And I have not got a number for where it worked then. The number anybody on this coast has got is on a wall a mile up the hill in a building with a lock on it, and that number is a morning about three months old, and it is a photograph of a morning that somebody took while they were standing next to a fire, and it is the only instrument anybody will act on and it does not come out of that building and the next one is a year off and there is no money in the arch. The other number is on a wall in a shed two miles along this coast and it is three years old and it is a morning, and it is a different morning, and the man who made that one cannot get to the first one and cannot get the first one to him, and he has not been sure his own is right since he made it.”

“The fourth thing is the smallest and it is the one I want said.”

“I have been on this wall thirty-one years and I have been in charge of it since the fourth, and the guild has never once asked me whether I would like to be the man who keeps it, and I am not complaining, and I am not going to stand here and pretend to you that I have been waiting thirty-one years for somebody to ask me, because I would not have answered yes the first twenty of them, and I have not thought about it once in six years, which is the same as not caring, except that it is not, because I care about the wall and the wall and the man are two different things and I have only ever been sure about one of them.”

The man forty feet off with a hand-lead in his fist had been on this coast forty-four days and had put the lead down about four seconds after he picked it up, and had listened to the whole of that without saying one word, which is the fourth time in ten days that a person on this coast had told him the true thing and gone on with their work.

He had the four things in his head already. A man of sixty-one at a bench two miles along this coast had said them out loud, standing up, as work and not as an argument, to one man in his own shed on the fifth of the sixth tide, and had set out in three sentences what asking for them would come to, and the answer was a year, and the year is whoever stands at that fire, and the woman who stands at that fire is twenty-four and is paid nothing.

He had thought at the time that the glassmaker had a better set of tools than anybody else on the coast and had not got anybody to hold them.

He had a better set of tools than anybody else on this coast standing forty feet off a wall, and there was no way on four hundred miles of this coast to make a man hold them, and there was no standing on this coast that would let him ask, and he had known that for forty-four days.

He said the one thing he had come to say and it was a stupid thing and he knew it was a stupid thing while he was saying it.

“Who do you ask when you want a day.”

Halse Tarrant looked at him.

“Nobody,” he said. “I told you. I have not asked anybody in fifteen years and I am not going to start because a stranger came round the outside of my wall with a lead line in his hand that is not yours. You want to know why not. I will tell you why not, and then you can go away and think about it and I will not be here, because I am here every day of the year at the third bell and there is no way to find me any other day.

“It is not pride and it is not that I would not give you the day if you asked properly, and if you had asked properly I would have given you yesterday and the day before. It is that the last man who asked me for something was a man in a good coat who asked for a year, and the answer to that was a woman’s back for eleven months, and he did not ask her, and he was right not to, and I heard about it and I have thought about it about four times in three years.

“So that is what happens on this coast. A person who wants a thing off a person who has not got it to spare does not ask the person. The person finds out afterwards or does not find out, and either way the person is standing there with it and nobody has said yes to anything. And I have watched that happen four or five times on this coast since you came and I have not liked any of it and I have not been able to do one single thing about any of it, and I am fifty-eight and I am the man who keeps two hundred and sixty yards of stone and there is not one part of that I am allowed to spend on anybody.”

He turned round and went down the seaward face of the wall on the outside, on the courses, the way a man goes down a thing he knows, and the fourth of the ninth course was loose under his boot and he put his weight on the wall instead of on it and went down the way he had come.

At about the fifth bell the man at the yard finished sorting the bait and came down the lane to the crossing with a letter in his hand, and a woman at the fire two miles along this coast put a bar down in the middle of a batch and went back to it, and a man of thirty-one was still standing on the wet shingle at the north end with his weight on his good side, looking at about two feet of gravel that is the whole of the north end, and nothing on this coast was alarmed, and nothing on this coast was threatened, and the ebb went out long and soft and slow and did exactly what it has done every good day for thirty-one years.

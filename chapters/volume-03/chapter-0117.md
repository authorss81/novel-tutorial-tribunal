# Chapter 117: What a Copy Loses

The head of the lane at Wether Cross on the second day of the fifth tide, from the second bell, which is what Corrie Tallow had said, and Corrie Tallow was in the habit of being at that end of the lane from the second bell whatever else was happening on this coast, and there is a bench against the lamp oil shed that Seth Garrick lets anybody sit on, and thirty people were on that shingle by the third bell for no reason any of them could have put into a sentence.

Belva Cray came with a chair and a shawl against the wind off the Reach, and she had a pencil and a school register and a piece of string, and she checked the copy as it was made, line by line, the way a woman checks a thing she is going to swear to in a room.

Corrie Tallow was there with her slate and a stick of chalk and had said she was going to do it, and then had not done it, and had put the slate face down on the bench and said the thing out loud first.

“My hand is not a copying hand,” she said. “I have copied out my own thirty-one years twice and it took me a fortnight both times and I could not read my own a week after. If I do that in chalk I will do it wrong and you will swear to it. She cuts marks for a living and she will do it in an hour and you will be able to see at a glance whether it is right.”

Sabra Trew came down from Thornglass at about half past with a slate and the heel of her hand black with scale, and she had lost a day of the shed and had nobody's leave, and Hollis Thrale had let her go and had not said why, which sat on her all the way down the coast road and which she had turned over about forty times on the way.

“Two lists,” he had said. “And you are the only person on this coast who has cut a row of marks in a hand that somebody else can read.”

---

She did it in marks, because that is what her hand does.

A week that was a week got a stroke. A week that was not there got nothing at all, and the nothing got a space, and the space was the width of a stroke, because Belva Cray made her do that. A reason in a margin got said out loud, once, and Corrie Tallow wrote it on a separate piece of paper because the reasons are not part of the count and a woman from a school a hundred and forty miles away does not need four hundred years of other people's reasons.

It took an hour and a half. The school list is about two hundred leaves in the two hands and both hands run into each other where the older one stops, and about a third of it is a list of weeks and the other two thirds is a list of nothing at all with a few marks in it, and Sabra Trew copied the marks and left the rest and said so out loud at intervals so that everybody knew what she was doing.

Nineteen of the strokes in a row are the first nineteen weeks of a hand that somebody stopped in the middle of and somebody else took up, and the two hands are on the same slate a half inch apart, and the seam is visible, and that is the only place on either slate where you can see the join.

By the third bell the yard had about thirty people in it for no reason any of them could have put in a sentence. Seth Garrick came out of his shed and did the hinge on the chain that he has meant to do since the turn, and worked with his back to them and his head on one side.

Ilyan Vester stood at the end of the bench with his hands behind him and was not reading, because there was nothing on the bench he could read, and had not been able to read a line of any of it for four days, and had worked out on the first afternoon of that week that the woman who was doing the copying was going to put it in marks and not in letters and had been right about it on the first afternoon.

He asked the question he had asked on a wet shingle nine days ago.

“What are the marks for?”

Seth Garrick did not stop working the hinge.

“For counting,” he said.

“For counting what.”

“Crossings,” said the reading man, and then stopped, and put the hammer down, and turned round, and it was a man of thirty with a lamp oil shed and a four-year headache standing four feet away from a bench with a slate on it. “No. No, that is what they are for and that is not the same answer, and you are the second man in nine days to ask me that question in that order, and I have got the same answer ready and I have given it twice already.

“The tally post counts crossings. That is what it is for and that is not what you are asking. I have been counting that post in my head for four years because there is nobody to check me and I have begun to think I have been getting it wrong, and if you stand at that post at a turn with a hammer and a piece of paper you will have the only count of it that has ever been made, and it will be wrong, and it will outlast the post. That is the whole of what I have. What you are asking is why anybody marks a week at all.”

“Why does anybody mark a week.”

“Because the weeks are the count,” said Seth Garrick. “There is nothing else on this coast to count by. The water does not give you a number. A boat gives you a day. A year gives you four hundred weeks, and a week gives you seven, and a week is the only piece of time on this coast that everybody agrees is a piece of time. So the weeks are the count. Every list on four hundred miles of this coast is a list of weeks. The crossing counts crossings and the crossing is a tide and a tide is not a week. A school counts terms. A salt pan counts weeks, because a pan has to be pumped and a pan is a week of pumping. A kiln counts firings, and a firing is a week, and a week is not a batch and never was.”

“Was that your answer in a wet shingle nine days ago.”

“It was,” said Seth Garrick, “and I gave it to you twice, and you are asking it a third time in the wrong order, and I would like to know what you are doing.”

“I am looking at a yard,” said Ilyan Vester.

“All right,” said Seth Garrick, and picked the hammer up and went back to the hinge.

---

Nobody saw the moment. There were thirty people in that yard and about nine of them were within twenty feet of the bench and not one of them saw it, and it took about four seconds, and it happened because two slates were lying on the same bench.

Sabra Trew had her grandmother's list on a slate. She had brought it because Corrie Tallow had asked for the arch's firings and the wall is nineteen of them and one of the nineteen is a hole four days wide, and the grandmother's book that went into the school has four hundred years in it in two hands, and the only person alive who can lay one out and say which hand is which and where the two of them stop and start is the woman who has been copying all morning, and it has taken her eleven days to be able to look at the two side by side and she had not expected to be asked and had not thought about what would happen if she was.

She put it down beside Belva Cray's slate because the bench was full and there was nowhere else to put anything.

The firings are nineteen notches and a four-day hole, and there is no way to write four days in a row of weeks, so her grandmother had written it as a notch, a notch, a smaller notch, a notch, and then a space the width of two, and then on, and that is the whole of it, and Sabra Trew had chalked it that way without thinking about it because it is the way the wall is.

Beside it, forty feet of week-marks in a hand that was not hers.

She looked at the two for about four seconds. She has been in a shed for three years and has cut about nine thousand marks in that time and she can feel the shape of a thing at a distance of a foot the way a person can feel the shape of a room.

Then she went the colour of the wall, and stopped with the chalk about an inch off the slate, and did not move for about ten seconds, and Seth Garrick's hinge made two bangs and there was a gull on the post.

Corrie Tallow said, “Girl. What is it.”

“There is a gap in it,” said Sabra Trew.

“There are about four hundred gaps in it, love, it is a list of four hundred years with things happening in it.”

“No.” She had it up off the bench now and was holding it at arm’s length in both hands the way you hold a plate. “No, there is a hole in that one and there is a hole in this one, and I have been looking at that one every day since the spring and I know it is four days, and I know what made it, and it is not in hers. I have just been down that list for an hour and a quarter and I have got to the part where her hand stops and the next one starts and I have got all the way through and the four days is not in it. It is not in the school book. It was not copied.”

Corrie Tallow put down her chalk.

Belva Cray stood up in her chair. “What do you mean, it was not copied.”

“I mean the wall has four days in it and the book does not.” Sabra Trew had not looked at anybody. She was looking at the two slates. “My grandmother copied it. She copied it twice. She kept one and she sent one to a school in a town a hundred and forty miles up this coast because a school has the only dry locked room on it, and the copy is the one your mistress has had in a pigeonhole for eleven years, and there is no four days in it.

“A four-day hole in a list of weeks is not a mark. It is a smudge. It is a gap between two weeks and a copyist reads a gap between two weeks as nothing, because nothing is what a gap is, and she did not know it was a hole and she did not ask, and she was right not to ask, and that is exactly how it was lost.”

Nobody in the yard said anything.

“It is the only interesting thing in four hundred years,” said Sabra Trew. “That is what I have been frightened of since the spring and I could not tell you why, and it is not the number. It is that if the four days did not get copied then something did not get copied, and nobody knew, and there are two books in the world out of that one wall and both of them are wrong in a different way, and I am the woman who cuts the marks.”

---

It is not what it looks like from outside and Ilyan Vester was in the yard and looked at the two slates about a minute after Sabra Trew had said it.

What he saw was a row of strokes and a row of strokes, one of them about nineteen long and one of them about two hundred, and they are not the same length and cannot be the same list, and nobody was claiming they were. And between the strokes, in both, at the same relative spacing, the same run and the same holes. A coast is a coast and about four hundred years of people on two stretches of it have all been counting the same piece of time the same way, and every one of them has holes in it for the same reason, which is that weeks are not like that. He knew what that was worth. It was worth nothing. Any counting of anything produces the same shape.

And underneath that, in him, without any decision in it, in about half a second and not to be got rid of, there was the other thing.

There was a thing he had spent six years of his life taking apart. Its name was *The Sovereign's Wake*, it was a game, he had been the last man to test it, and he had filed a report on it on the final night and had never once been told by anybody that he was right. It had a map on it that most of its players never opened, and the map was not of anywhere the game let a person walk, and the people who had made the map had built it out of the parts of the world the game did not show you, and had then written a song about it.

He could not have said, standing at that bench, what any of that had to do with four hundred weeks in chalk. He was not going to try. He put his palm flat on the bench and thought about a pair of cold feet, which is what he thought about when he wanted to stop thinking about something in front of people.

Then he asked, in a completely ordinary voice, to a completely ordinary yard:

“Sabra. Does your grandmother’s book have the four days in it.”

“It has the four days in it,” said Sabra Trew. “She wrote it out twice for me when I was about eleven. She said, that is the one you will remember.”

---

The copy was finished at about the fifth bell and Belva Cray checked every line of it against the original herself, and it took her most of an hour, and at the end of it she said the thing that is the finding of the whole two days and said it to about eleven people and one wall.

“I am not going to swear to this in a board room,” she said. “I am going to swear to the column in the back of my own register, in my own hand, for eleven years, and that is the one I will say out loud. And that is not a humble thing to do and I would like it understood that I am not doing it because I am frightened of your coast. I am doing it because your coast has kept four hundred years of weeks and has never once kept a record of days, and I came a hundred and forty miles to find a number and there is nothing whatever on this coast that can turn one of those weeks into so many days, and that is not a sad thing, it is an obvious thing that I had not seen from a hill.”

“Take it home anyway,” said Corrie Tallow. “It is yours, it cost nothing, and you may show it to anybody you like.”

“All right,” said Belva Cray. “And I will put it in with the register and it will not be a record of anything, and I will write that on it in your name so that whoever finds it in a hundred years knows.”

“That is a good thing to write on it and I would not have thought of it,” said Corrie Tallow.

And Sabra Trew washed her slate out in the yard trough and put the stick of chalk on the wall and went up the coast road to Thornglass with her back to the water, and she was an hour late for the second turn of the arch, and Hollis Thrale was standing at the fire with a bar in his hand and one pair of hands and no apprentice, and he did not ask her why, and she did not say.

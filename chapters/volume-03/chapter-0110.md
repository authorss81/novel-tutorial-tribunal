# Chapter 110: The Ninth Tide, and No Name on the Line

The second of the tide there were forty-one people on the flat at Wether Cross and the tide office put a pot of paint out on the counter with nobody's name on it, and on the third there were thirty-eight, and on the fourth the rain came back and Bettany Rook painted the days on the post at the harbour mouth in a bad light with her hood up and the two of them alone, and it took her about an hour, and it is the only work done on that coast in a fortnight that nobody asked anybody to do.

The paint is four white bands and three black ones, thick enough to see from the water, and under them, small, in her own hand, the numbers: one, two, three — bar — five — bar — bar. She could not paint a number to save her life and she wrote them with a brush, which is why they look the way they do, and the seventh has been gone over twice.

The harbourmaster's clerk — Jory Vance, twenty-two — came down the lane on the fifth with a board under his arm and a brush in his cuff and a look of a man who has been told something new and is not certain he agrees with it, and he stopped about thirty feet short of the chain.

“The boy is not on the list,” he said.

“The boy,” said Ilyan Vester, “is Nim Hask, he is eleven, and he has crossed that flat about ten thousand times.”

“He is not on the list of carts that paid.”

“He is not a cart.”

“He is not a person either, not on anything, and I have a chain here and a tariff, and the tariff is a penny a tide and a tide is a crossing, and a crossing is a thing that pays, and he has paid every one of them and he is not on anything, and that is not me being hard, that is me not knowing what to write, and if I do not write it then the turn does not get counted, and if the turn does not get counted then the fourth of a tide does not get counted either, and the whole of the flat does not count.” He turned the board round. He did not have a line for it. “I have been four years on this counter. In four years I have never once had to decide what to do with a person and I have got quite good at the other thing.”

That was all of it, and there was nobody in it to be angry with. There was a young man with a board and no line and a tariff he could not make come out right, and there were about thirty people standing on nine hundred yards of wet shingle watching a boy of eleven try to get a hand-lead under the chain.

“Move the chain, Jory,” said Bettany Rook.

She was on the shingle because she had come down to see the post in daylight for the first time in nine years and had not expected a difficulty, and she had about four minutes and a hood and a bad light.

The chain came up. The boy went through with the lead. The cart went over at the third crossing of the tide, and Jory Vance stood and looked at the board, and then went and got a piece of paper and a pen and sat down on a stone.

“Write it,” she said, standing over him. “A person who guides a cart over this flat on a tide is not a trespasser, and he is not a cart, and he is not a thing the turn does not get counted for. Put that at the top and put the date on it and put my name on it and put it in the day book, not in the returns, and read it back to me before you leave this stone.”

“It will go into a pane in twenty years.”

“Not on its own,” said Bettany Rook, “and that is the second reason and it is the reason I have spent nineteen years getting right. That is paper. If it is ever wrong, somebody takes a pen and draws a line through it and it is wrong and the line is there. And when it goes into a pane in twenty years — and it will, because everything does, and the letting day is a tide — I want it wrong on paper first, and crossed out, with a line, so that the man who fires it has something to see and has a choice about it.”

She stood there about a minute longer.

“Now read it back to me.”

He read it back to her. It took about ninety seconds and it says that a person who guides a cart over Wether Cross on a tide is not a trespasser by that fact alone, and is not counted as a cart, and is counted as a crossing, and is entered in the day book and not in the returns, and it has her name on it, and the fifth of the tide, and the name of a boy of eleven who is not in it because she would not put it in and said why, in writing, on the same paper: *a name on this line would be a name on a line the boy could be found on, and I have spent six years refusing to be that and I am not going to start in a day's writing.*

That cost an afternoon of a young man's day and an hour of a woman's bad light and nothing at all in money. It is in the day book and not in the returns, and there is no name on it, and that is the whole of what this coast has done for a boy of eleven in fifteen days.

The second cost of it was not free and she entered it herself, standing at the counter before she left, in a sentence that Jory Vance wrote down and did not understand until she read it back to him.

“If the flat is counted in persons then somebody has to count the persons, and there is nobody at that end of this road to do it, and I am not sending a child round with a slate, and so it will have to be a person, and a person is a wage, and the wage comes out of what this crossing takes. And this crossing takes ninety pounds eighteen a year. Eighty-one pounds sixteen of that is tallage on carts and goods, taken at a counter by people who do not live on this road. Three pounds two is the chain and the oil and the tarpaulin, and the other nine pounds two of the ninety is not tallage at all: that is eleven households sending somebody over that flat, and eight pounds nine of the nine is one household of the eleven, and the other thirteen shillings is the other ten, and every penny of the nine is somebody going over that flat on their own two feet and not a cart going over it in a box. And the chain comes out of the nine, not out of the eighty-one, because nobody has ever paid for a chain on that flat out of a cart. So the nine less three is six.

“So I have written a line that costs this crossing about two pounds a year in a wage, which leaves four pounds, and the guild runs the only crossing on this road on four pounds a year and has done since the charter was recopied, and I have never told the council that, because they would ask me what I would do about it and I do not know. I have written that down standing at a counter in a bad light with my hood up, and the council will see it in the season and will not like it.”

The fourth of the tide, at Thornglass, Sabra Trew was on the arch with the last batch of the season and there were four turns left in it and one apprentice, and Ilyan Vester was in the shed for about four hours holding an iron bar out of a fire and putting it back, forty times, and nobody asked him to and nobody said thank you and at the end of it his leg was his own.

That was all of it, and it was the first time since the eighteenth of the fifth month, and it was not a gift and it was not a cure and it was the same four hours it had ever been, and he had got it the way he had got it the only other time in his life, which was by standing inside a burden of his own. The paper had been a duty that was not his own, and it had cleared the leg for four hours on a road between a bridge and a pump, and a woman of forty-four with a gate hand's weight had been in it the whole time, and the paper had been the weight and she had not.

The bar was his own work. Nobody's name was on it. It went out of the fire and back forty times and the fourth hour ended and it came back worse, in about a minute, the way it always does, and the weight of it got into his nose in two of its layers with a third under both of them, and under the third of them a room was waiting where it always waited, with the lamp down and the four of them at the table and one of them with her back to him, at the height and the distance it had kept since the third of the second month in a city four hundred miles off. It had not moved. Nobody had sent him looking for it and he was never going to.

Nobody on this coast knew about it. He had told four people in a city four hundred miles off and a fifth who worked it out and said it first, and there was no woman at a gate on a flat four hundred miles off doing a count, and there was nothing here that depended on him standing or not standing, and that was the trade and he had made it with his eyes open at about the second hour.

When the four hours were up Hollis Thrale came and took the bar off him and put him on a crate and looked at him for a while and did not ask and said, “You have a thing you carry and you have just put it down for four hours, and I have been sixty-one for a long time and I know what a man looks like when he has stopped hurting, and I am not going to ask you about it, and I am going to say one thing about it, which is that you carried my bar well and my apprentice says you did not put it down crooked once, and that is a thing about glass and not about you.”

Sabra Trew got him down the step on the fourth of the tide with a hand under his arm and did not comment, and he said thank you, and she said, “You are welcome. It is the step and the step is the step,” and that is the entire conversation and it is correct, and on this coast it is also the second kindest thing anybody did for him in fourteen days and the first kind one was a man with lamp oil and a bed.

The address of a school in a town a hundred and forty miles up this coast was in the back of the exercise book in his coat, written crooked with the heel of his right hand, and it was the fourth of next tide, and four days back he had told a girl of twenty-four in a shed that he would probably do it about the fourth of next tide, and he did not do it. He had a table in front of him and a pot of paint going hard in the shed and eleven months of somebody else's work in his hands, and there was a woman in that shed who had not answered a letter and a woman two miles down the coast who was going blind and a woman in a stall who had counted eleven doors with a stick, and every one of them had a want that was in front of her today, and his was on a piece of paper about four days old.

He wrote down that he had not done it, and the date, and that it was his, and he did not send it to anybody.

The table came into the tide office on the sixth, at the second bell, in a hand that is not a clerk's.

Corrie Tallow had brought it herself, and she had left the stall five mornings running, which is five days, and she had lost the rope custom of one boat's crew, who buy off her on the fourth of the tide and have bought off her for eleven years, and who bought a half-crown's worth on the fourth of this tide off a different shop, and she said that number out loud in the room because she had done it and it is hers and she would like it said.

Bettany Rook read thirty-one years of pencil in about eleven minutes standing at the counter with the hood back, because the hood is for the water side and this is the land side, and the light is bad on the land side too, and she read it the way she reads everything, which was not quickly.

Then she said, “It is right. All of it. The third column in the ninth year of the bad seven and the eighth and the eleventh are better than ours, and ours is printed, and ours was printed in a city two hundred miles up the coast and I have known for nineteen years that it is two hours out and I have never been able to say so in a room without being asked why I have not had it reprinted, and the answer is that I have never had the argument, and the answer to that is that I did not have thirty-one years of pencil.”

“Can it go in the book?” said Corrie Tallow.

“No,” said Bettany Rook, “and I am going to tell you exactly why, and I have been turning it over for four years, and you are not going to like either half of it.

“If your table goes into this office book then in twenty years somebody takes it to a fire, because everything in this book goes to a fire, and it goes into a pane, and it goes in as *the tides of this coast*. And then it is not a stall-holder’s book any more. It is the coast. And in the ninth year you have an eleventh column that is better than ours, and in the fourth year after that somebody is going to come to this counter and say that the flat is crossable eleven times on a bad fourth, and they will be right, and there is no line on your table about a boy, and there is no line on your table about a price, and there will be a pane on that wall with a discrepancy in it that nobody can cross out for ever, and in forty years there will be a young man in this building who looks at that pane and says the old woman was wrong.

“I am not putting a living woman’s pencil into glass while I am still able to read it, and I will not be able to read it in two seasons, and by then I will not be the one asking.”

“So it is not going in?”

“It is going in the other place,” said Bettany Rook, “and I want you to hear the other place said out loud, because I have spent six years watching people not hear it.

“The guild’s book is a fire going to happen. What I am going to do is this. I am going to have your thirty-one years copied into the paper book, in your own hand as far as it will go, dated, with your name at the top of it, and it is going to sit on a shelf in this room, and it is going to be wrong eventually, and a wrong thing on a shelf is a thing a man can take down. And in nine years when somebody comes to the fire with a list, that copy will not be on the list, because it is not the guild’s record and I am not going to make it the guild’s record with my own name on it. And then in a hundred years somebody will come in here and find it, and it will be a copy of a pencil book belonging to a woman who sold rope, and it will be the only piece of paper in this building about this coast that anybody is allowed to say is wrong.

“That is a smaller thing than you asked for. You asked to be in the coast’s memory and what I am giving you is a shelf and a date and my name saying that it is not the record, and I am telling you that it is not the record on purpose, because a thing that is not the record can be wrong and a thing that is the record has to be true, and I have watched the second one win about a hundred times in nineteen years and it is always the same sentence: *you cannot have both, you have to have one or the other, and you have chosen.*”

Corrie Tallow stood at the counter for a while.

“Four days,” she said. “And a half-crown of custom off a crew of eleven years. And the year I get is a shelf.”

“The year you get is a shelf,” said Bettany Rook, “and in about twenty years the man who fires the pane is going to be somewhere on this coast and he is going to be about the age I was, and he is going to be asked to put a discrepancy into glass, and if there is a copy on the shelf with a date and a name on it saying *this is not the record*, he is going to have something in his hands, and if there is not, he has only a pane and his own judgement, and I have met that man. He is me. I was him nineteen years ago and I carried a woman's column to the fire myself and she was sixty-four and it was wrong by a quarter of an hour on two days in a year, and it is in the tide table on the wall of that room this morning and I cannot take it out and I have not been able to in nineteen years, and it was wrong that morning and it is wrong now and it will be wrong in another year when the next one goes into the arch, and I read it every morning on the way in.”

“So it is not going in the record.”

“No,” said Bettany Rook. “It is going in the next place along, and I am the only person on this coast who can put it there, and I have not got a stamp for it, and it is going to be the only thing I have entered in nineteen years that the guild did not ask me for, and I have turned it over twice this morning, and I would like it read out in the room at the second bell tomorrow, in the ordinary way, so that about nine people hear it, and I would like you to stand here while I do it, and then I would like you to go back to your stall.”

---

On the sixth of the tide, at about the ninth crossing, Bel Hask sent her boy over the flat with a hand-lead and stood at the top of the lane and watched him the whole way, which she had not done for eleven days.

Corrie Tallow had the slate on the back of the board in chalk and had gone down to the far stones with it, because the ninth crossing of a day is the only one on that flat worth a slate, and she did not say that out loud either. At the fourth bell she came back up the lane and asked Bel Hask one question, which was whether she was all right, and Bel Hask said she was, and then said the other half of it because somebody finally asked her.

“I have done the sum again and the sum is the same and I am doing it. Three shillings and threepence a week and four days to get the sum out of my own head at night.” She was not looking at the flat. “What I have got for it is that the woman with the slate is at the far stones on the ninth and she counts every one that goes over and she is the length of the flat away and she sees everything, and so if my boy is not on that flat at nine o'clock she will know before he is off it, and I have not had that since he was six, and I am paying three shillings and threepence a week for a thing that is not in the tariff and that nobody is going to put in it.”

“That is a good use of three shillings and threepence,” said Corrie Tallow.

“It is the only use there is,” said Bel Hask. “Do not tell the guild.”

---

The ninth tide of the sixth day was about forty minutes late and about four inches higher than the post said it would be, and the flat went under across the channel first and then in two bays, and the chain went up about two hundred yards short of the water, and about thirty people stood on the shingle at Wether Cross and watched the water come.

Nim Hask came off it with the lead in his fist at the last minute, wet to the waist, and did not say anything about it and did not appear to have enjoyed it.

Ilyan Vester was standing at the tally post, and Jory Vance was at the other end of the chain with the board, and the day book was under Bettany Rook's arm because the day book is not carried about and she had come down to see whether the line was right and had ended up holding the book.

“You will want to know why I am still here,” he said.

“No,” said Bettany Rook. “I have known why you are still here since the second day and there is no version of it that is your business. You are here because that bar road has an old woman on it who has not been asked, and you have found out that being asked is a thing you can do to a person, and you have decided you would rather find out how not to. And you have not managed it, and you have written it down instead, and I have read the sheet of paper, and it is the most honest piece of paper I have read in six years and it is the least use anybody has ever made of one.”

“Your reading of it is better than mine.”

“It is my reading of it. You wrote a page. I have had nineteen years and a counter and a pane on the wall and a hood I cannot take off in the afternoon. That is all the difference there is, and I would like somebody to write down that I said that and did not do him a kindness.”

She shut the day book under her arm and looked at the water coming over the flat and going off it again at the far end, and the channel moving a yard a minute in a way that nobody on this coast can explain and everybody on this coast can tell you about.

“Here is what I am going to say and then I am going to the second bell, and it is the last thing I have got and it is not about you.

“The guild cannot change its own charter. I have told you that and I have not explained why, and it is because the charter is a pane and a pane is a year, and the fifth pane was let go twelve weeks ago without a batch in the arch, and the money for it is not in his hand, and the third of next tide is the last day I could put it in and have it out in a year, and if it is not in his hand by then the next letting day is a year off that one as well. So between now and a letting day a year off there is nothing I can do with that word *charter* except read it. I have said that word to nine hundred people for six years and I have never once been unable to change it, and now I cannot, and it is not because anybody has stopped me. There is nobody to stop me. There is a man in a shed two miles along this coast who is sixty-one and who is standing in front of a cold arch this morning because there is no batch in it for me, and he is not my enemy, and he has never been anybody’s enemy, and he did not do it, and he would have done it for the same money off the council.

“So a rule that cannot be changed for a year is on this coast from today until a letting day a year off, and the tariff is one penny a tide, and the days are on a post in paint, and the paint on the seventh is two coats thick, and the days are not in the charter and never were, and there is a boy on that flat nine times on the worst day of the week at the best price of the week, and I have written one line in a day book about him and I have told the council what it costs, and that is everything I have got, and none of it is the charter.

“I have been asked what the rule is for and I have never been able to answer it and I have had nineteen years.”

She went up the lane. The System put the finding in front of Ilyan Vester on the sixth of a tide, at the ninth crossing, on nine hundred yards of wet shingle with about thirty people on it, and what it gave him was the second thing on this coast that was a record of a thing nobody had entered anywhere in particular.

**THE WATER AND PASSAGE CHARTER OF KESTREL REACH STANDS ON ONE PANE OF SALTGLASS IN THE TIDE OFFICE. THE BURDEN TERM IS NOT ON IT. NO PANE CAN BE MADE TO CARRY IT FOR LESS THAN A YEAR, AND A YEAR IS A PERSON AND A FIRE AND A TIDE THE MAKER DOES NOT CHOOSE. THE GUILD MAY WRITE ON ANYTHING IT LIKES UNTIL A LETTING DAY A YEAR OFF AND NOT ONE WORD OF IT WILL BE THE CHARTER.**
**WHAT IS NOT DECIDED: WHETHER A RULE THAT CANNOT BE CHANGED FOR A YEAR IS A RULE OR A SEASON. THE GUILD'S ANSWER IS THAT A RULE WHICH COULD BE CHANGED IN AN HOUR WOULD NOT BE A RULE. THE HARBOURMASTER'S ANSWER IS THAT SHE HAS GIVEN THE SAME ANSWER FOR NINETEEN YEARS AND HAS NEVER ONCE BEEN THE PERSON WHO HAD TO CHANGE IT.**

He was on the coast fourteen days. He had a bad leg and a left hand of his own that would not take a rope and a token in his pocket that he had not touched and a rendering at the same height and the same distance that he had not gone looking for. He had refused nothing and been refused once, by a man with a lighter, in a room, correctly, in front of thirty-one people, and the refusal had cost eleven pounds of freight and two men's day and four years of a woman who sold rope.

He had put four numbers on a page and had left a woman's name off the bottom of them on purpose and had put his own name there instead and had not taken that off either, and he had not asked a question of an old woman on a bar road, and he had written down that he had not, and a woman of fifty-one in a hood had read that page and told him it was the most honest paper she had read in six years and the least use anybody had ever made of one. A man of thirty who sold lamp oil had given him a bed over his shed because he wanted somebody to stand at a post with him at a turn and count notches onto a piece of paper, there having been nobody on that road to check that count for four years, and he had begun to suspect himself.

He had carried a bar out of a fire forty times and had put down a weight that belonged to a woman at a gate four hundred miles off, for four hours, in a shed, with nobody watching, and had picked it up again. Nobody on this coast knew about the leg and he was not going to tell her and was not going to tell anybody here.

Nobody on this coast had asked him about a household, and he had not mentioned one. What he had come for was a matter in two places, and what he had got was a refusal on a step, a line in a day book with no name on it, a copy of a pencil book on a shelf in a room where it will be found in a hundred years by somebody who does not exist yet, and a count on a piece of paper that will be wrong by some amount nobody will ever be able to name and that nobody will ever be able to check, because a man of thirty will have made it alone, on a turn, with a piece of coal, and it will be the only count of that post that has ever been made, and it will outlast the post. The one person who had asked him anything was a woman selling rope, on a lane, at the fourth bell, and the question was whether she was all right. She had told the truth, and that is the only reason anybody on this coast knows what the boy costs her.

The charter stood on a pane in a room with a hood in it and it could not be changed for a year, and on the sixth day of a tide the water came over the flat and went off it again and the channel moved a yard a minute, and the worst question on that coast for a fortnight had been a question about a person. The question it was going to have to live with for a year was not about a person at all, which was the first time in fifteen days, and it was the plainest one anybody there had ever heard put, and Bettany Rook had put it to him at the ninth crossing without meaning to and had gone up the lane before he could answer it:

What does a body do for a year?

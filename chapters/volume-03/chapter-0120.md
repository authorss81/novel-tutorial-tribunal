# Chapter 120: The Ward of That Road

The post at Wether Cross is inspected at about the turn on the seventh day of a tide, once a year, by a man with a hammer, and the old notches are filled flat with a chisel and beaten level and then the new ones are cut.

The man was Gell Roke, seventy-one, who had come down the slip from the harbour mouth with a pot of paint in one hand and a chisel and a hammer in a bag in the other, and there was a boy of about ten with him to hold the pot, and about thirty people were on the shingle because there is nothing else to do on the seventh and everybody knows it.

They do it in fives. A notch for each crossing, a hole for each ten, a bar cut across the face for each hundred, and the whole of the year of that flat goes into a post about the height of a man in about two hours, and then it stands there until the turn of next year and is beaten flat and cut again.

Seth Garrick had a piece of paper and a stick of coal and had been waiting four years for the day, and he had a bed over his lamp oil shed and a man with a bad leg sleeping in it, and neither of those things had anything to do with the post and both of them had been paid for in the same currency, which is a day of somebody's trade.

They went along it from the stones at the near end. Gell Roke cut and the boy called them out in fives and Seth Garrick wrote them in fives, and twice he lost his place and went back three notches and started again, and the second time Gell Roke waited for him without saying anything about it, which is the greatest courtesy available on that shingle.

Nobody had ever done this. That is the cost of the morning and it is the whole of it: the post had been inspected once a year for as long as there had been a guild at Kestrel Reach and that in the whole of that time there had never once been a second person counting while it was cut, and no figure had ever been written down, and the number that was cut on it went into the wood and stood there and was beaten flat a year later and gone.

“At the bar,” said Gell Roke. “That is a hundred and a bit.”

“Eleven hundred and sixty,” said Seth Garrick.

It was four thousand and sixty-eight. The boy had a good ear and the chalk went down in fives and the holes went in every ten and there is a bar across the face of the post at every hundred, and and about fifty of the notches are on the underside, where the water gets at them, and a man cutting in a hurry cuts those twice, which is one of the places the number is known to be wrong and the only place anybody has been able to name.

It took two hours and eleven minutes and it is the only count of that post that has ever been made and it is the wrong count in at least one place and there is no instrument on this coast that could find out which one.

---

“Four thousand and sixty-eight,” said Seth Garrick, and put the paper down on the flat of the chain post and looked at it, and then said the thing he had been carrying since the second day of the third tide. “I have had that in my head for four years.”

Nobody had asked him to say it and everybody within twenty feet turned round.

“Five thousand,” said Seth Garrick. “Round about. I have had round about five thousand in my head for four years and I have told nobody and I told a man off a road about it in the rain because I had begun to think I was getting it wrong, and I was. I was wrong by about nine hundred. I have been wrong in my head for four years in a shed on my own, at night, with a lamp, and there was nobody on this road to check me and there was never going to be, and I did it anyway because I have been doing it since I was twenty-six and I did not know how to stop.”

He picked the paper up and looked at it again.

“Nine hundred is more than a fifth of it,” he said. “That is not a man being a bit out. And I want it written down that I said it out loud on about thirty people, on the seventh of a tide, with the number on a piece of paper, and that the number on the paper is not mine and mine is on the paper as well, and both of them are now two things instead of one, which is the first improvement I have had in four years and I got it by being wrong in public.”

---

The returns are made out at the counter from the carts that paid, and the tally is of carts and not of persons and has always been of carts, and the two counts have never agreed and the post is not one of the two the returns are made from.

Jory Vance brought the year's return board down to the shingle at about the second bell, on the day, because he had been told to and because it is a thing you can carry, and he stood on the stones with it while Gell Roke cut.

“Four hundred and something,” he said. “That is carts that paid. Three hundred and ninety-one in the season and the rest of it outside, and there is a bad week in there where it is nothing at all.”

“Four thousand and sixty-eight,” said Gell Roke, without stopping.

“Yes.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“No,” said Jory Vance, “and it is not a mistake and I have known that for four years and I have never had anybody to say it to. The post is everybody’s boots. The return is carts. They were never going to agree and nobody ever counted the difference and the difference is every person on this coast who has ever put a foot on that shingle.”

“Which is about nine hundred people,” said Corrie Tallow, from forty yards off, where she was doing what she does at the ninth crossing of every day of the week, which is standing at the far end and counting.

“Nine hundred people in the Reach,” said Jory Vance. “Every one of us goes over that flat at some point in a year. I have gone over it about two hundred times since I was eighteen and I have never once paid a penny for it, because it is not a cart.”

Nobody had ever said that before. The nine hundred people of Kestrel Reach live above about half a mile of water and every one of them crosses a shingle flat to get to a boat, and not one of those crossings has ever been on a piece of paper anywhere on this coast, and the only count of them is a row of notches on a post that has been beaten flat and cut again a dozen times since.

And one boy of eleven is two thousand and twenty-eight of the four thousand and sixty-eight.

---

They nailed a board on the front of the post at about the fourth bell, under the chain, where it is in the way of anybody using the crossing, which is the only place anybody on that coast would put anything.

The board is a plank about two feet by one, and the nails are fourpence, and it took two men and about eleven minutes because Gell Roke had to keep stopping to explain what he was doing to everybody standing round him.

> **THE COUNT OF THIS POST WAS CUT ON THE SEVENTH DAY OF A TIDE AND WRITTEN DOWN FOR THE FIRST TIME.**
> **THE POST: FOUR THOUSAND AND SIXTY-EIGHT.**
> **THE RETURNS FOR THE SAME YEAR, OF CARTS THAT PAID: THREE HUNDRED AND NINETY-ONE IN THE SEASON, AND SOME OUTSIDE IT.**
> **THE FIGURE THAT WAS IN SETH GARRICK'S HEAD FOR FOUR YEARS WAS ABOUT FIVE THOUSAND. HE WAS WRONG BY ABOUT NINE HUNDRED.**
> **A FIGURE CUT ON THIS POST CANNOT BE CHECKED BY ANYBODY. A FIGURE IN THE RETURNS CANNOT BE CHECKED BY ANYBODY. THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN THEM IS EVERYBODY.**
> **THE COUNT IS MADE AT THE TURN, ONCE A YEAR, BY WHOEVER IS HERE.**

And under all of that, in the same hand, in smaller letters, because Gell Roke wrote it while the others were arguing about where to put the board:

> **THAT LAST LINE IS THE ONLY RULE ANYBODY HAS EVER WRITTEN ON THIS COAST ABOUT A MARK AND I AM NOT SURE IT IS A GOOD ONE.**

---

It cost an afternoon of a man of thirty's shop, a day and a half of a woman's slate, fourpence of nails, about two hours of a man of seventy-one's daylight, the oil a man of thirty spent on a cart up the coast road that he did not have to go, and nothing at all in money otherwise, because there is no wage for it and the council voted a wage two pounds a year on the fourth day of the fourth tide and cannot pay it and the vote is in a minute book in four lines and the four lines are the true shape of it.

Seth Garrick shut the shed for the afternoon and did not open it again until the turn, and the people who buy lamp oil on the seventh were told by a boy of nine that the oil man was busy.

Corrie Tallow went down to the far stones four times that day, once for each tide, and stood at the far end of nine hundred yards of shingle and counted every body that went over, for nothing, as she has done for thirty-one years and as she will go on doing now that a board on a post says *by whoever is here.*

And a woman of fifty-one in a hood had four hours of a season's figures dictated to a clerk of twenty-two and everybody in that room counted and nobody asked her one question, and she has not told the guild and she is not going to, and the guild would be right to move her to a counter, and she is fifty-one and it is the fourth week of a season and there is nobody on that coast she can hand any of it to.

---

At about the fifth bell Gell Roke put the chisel in the bag and the hammer in his hand and then stopped, and looked at the post, and then looked along the shingle at the people standing on it, and then at a man of thirty-one standing four feet off with his coat on and a leg that he has been favouring since the third bell.

Nobody had said anything to that man for four hours. He had counted nothing and cut nothing and carried nothing and asked no question and had been useful a half-dozen times in twenty-nine days, and there was no office in this guild, or in any room on this coast, that had ever been given to him or could be, and nobody had asked him to stay and nobody was going to, and his name was not on the board and would not be and could not be.

“Right,” said Gell Roke. “I am seventy-one and I have been cutting that post since before you came off a cart, and there is a hammer in my hand and there is no rule on this coast that says who holds it, and I have not got the pair of hands for a year off and I have never asked anybody for a year off in my life.”

He held it out.

“That is the ward of that road now,” he said. “That is all it is. You cut it in a year and you put the number on the board and you do not let anybody tell you it is a different number. And I am telling you it is not a favour and it is not an honour and there is no word on this coast for what it is, because I have looked for one for four days and there is not one, and if you have a word in a city four hundred miles off I would like to hear it and I will not use it.”

It went about four feet. There was nobody between the old man and him. He was the person nearest.

He took it.

That is the whole of it and it is the whole of this coast, and it is the whole of a thing four hundred miles off that he has spent four months learning the shape of: a duty with nobody in it goes to whoever is standing nearest, and there is no clause on this coast that lets a person put a thing down, and on this coast nobody thought to ask, because asking would have meant admitting that a thing was being handed over, and because he was the person nearest, and because a hammer weighs about a pound and a quarter and it looked like a small thing.

The leg went in about the second swing and went all the way, from the hip down, the way it went, and the hand he could not grip with is the one he got it in, and the weight that arrived in his nose in two of its layers with a third one under both of them was a gate hand of forty-four's out of a city four hundred miles behind him and she was still in it, and the room with the lamp down and the four of them at the table and one of them with her back to him was sitting exactly where it had been sitting since the third of the second month and it did not move.

Gell Roke took the hammer back off him at the fourth swing, without a word and without any expression at all on a face of seventy-one years, and put it in the bag, and closed the bag.

“All right,” he said. “That is enough of that. Boy — get my pot.”

Nobody on that shingle said one word about it, then or afterwards, and about eleven of the thirty-odd people standing there had seen a man of thirty-one go down on a post and get up on a post, and about four of them had worked out what it was, and not one of them said a word to him about it, and that is a thing four hundred miles of this coast does about a man it has no standing over.

---

He stood at the end of the shingle for a long time after it had gone dark enough to see nothing by, and the flat was dry and the chain was up and the board was on the post with two numbers on it and a rule under them in a hand of seventy-one that said the rule might not be a good one.

Here is what he had, on the last night of twenty-nine days on this coast.

He had been put a sentence to a road nine miles by water and had it refused in the open, correctly, in a room, in front of thirty-one people, at a cost of eleven pounds of freight and two men's day and four years of a woman who sold rope. He had a line in a day book with no name on it. He had a copy of a woman's thirty-one years on a shelf in a room where it is not the record, on purpose, and a copy of her pencil in a school a hundred and forty miles up this coast in a register where it is a record of nothing, also on purpose, both of them put there by women who knew exactly what they were doing and have told him so in a sentence each.

He had a sheet of paper in his coat with two accounts of the same water on it, one of which he wrote and one of which came back, and he was never able to find the join between them, and he could not have read either one.

He had a man of sixty-one two miles up the coast holding a pane of his own that disagrees with the guild's in two columns of eleven, and he had offered it to a woman of fifty-one on the fifth day of the fifth tide and been refused, correctly, in a yard, in about nine sentences, and he was not going to carry it to anybody else on this coast, because there is nobody else, and because he had been told by a woman of forty-eight at a crossing in the rain that the only way anybody ever moves a person is by standing in a doorway being awkward, and because he had stood in a doorway once on a road between a bridge and a pump, four hundred miles off, with a piece of paper in his hand and a person in it, and it had cost that person a day and a hedge and it had cost him nothing at all.

And he had a hammer in a bag belonging to a man of seventy-one, and a rule on a board that said the count is made at the turn by whoever is here, and nobody on four hundred miles of this coast has ever been asked whether they would like to be whoever is here.

He had not been asked once. In twenty-nine days on this coast not one person had asked him for anything. He had been given a bed and a meal and a leg's worth of bad light and a bar out of a fire and four turns at a post and a door of a shed and a sheet of paper read out loud to him one piece at a time by a woman with a ledger who did not help him, and every one of those had been given, and not one of them had been asked for, and he understood why, and it took him four weeks to be able to say it to himself in a sentence.

The toll on this coast is a penny a tide. A boy's thirty-nine tides a week at a penny is three shillings and threepence, and there are about nine hundred people on this water, and a man with a bad leg who comes off a cart on a wet road and stays twenty-nine days costs the coast exactly nothing, because nobody has a column for it, and because nobody on this coast has ever written down what a carrier is, and because the one place four hundred miles off where it has been written down is a city where a man was produced three times in four months by people who had a tray and a seal and a duty, and every single time it cost a district a day and a half of a cart and it cost him a day of a leg, and nobody ever asked him either.

The board was on the post. The number was wrong in at least one place and the number in Seth Garrick's head had been wrong by about nine hundred and both of them were on the same board in two different hands, and the difference between the post and the returns was every person on that coast who had ever put a foot on a shingle flat, and one boy of eleven was half of the four thousand and sixty-eight and was still on it nine times that day at a penny a time.

And the arch at Thornglass was cold and would be cold for a year, and the charter on the wall of the tide office could not be touched for a year, and the two panes that did not agree could not be put side by side by anybody because the only instrument on this coast is the guild's and the guild's has not left that room in nineteen years.

And the year had begun on the fourth week of a season, on a coast of four hundred miles, where a rule that cannot be changed for a year is not a rule, and where a man with a cart and a load of lime had said that out loud in the open to nine people on a shingle and had not been answered by one of them, and where a woman of seventy-one had put a hammer into the hand of a stranger and called it the ward of a road, and neither of those two sentences had been answered, and both of them were true.

The question that was left standing on that coast at the end of the twenty-ninth day was not about a boy, and not about a woman of eighty-one, and not about a man off a road with a leg he has been carrying since a city four hundred miles behind him.

It was the one that had been sitting in a trough and a yard and a shed and a bar and a post and a board for four weeks, and it was the plainest anybody on that shingle had ever heard.

It was this. On a coast of four hundred miles there are about nine people holding about nine things in their heads, because there is no column for any of them and no office and no clause and no way of writing one down, and every one of those nine is on its way out.

And the whole apparatus of that coast — a charter, a tariff, a card, a song, a post, a pane, a book in a school, a slate in a basket, a bench in a room with a painted staff on a wall — is a machine for making nine things into one written thing.

It has just been shown, on a wet shingle, by a man of seventy-one, not to work.

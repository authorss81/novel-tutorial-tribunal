# Chapter 122: A Bar Day Is the Only Day the Shallow Is a Road

The flat boat is up above the tide line on her bilge with the garboard up and a canvas over her, and when Sorrel Gannock took the canvas off it on the second of the sixth tide at the second bell, the bottom of the boat was the thing a person looks at first and it is the reason for the whole of her trade.

There is no keel. There is a flat bottom of two and a half inches of larch with a plate of hard wood let into it along the middle line and riveted through, and the plate is a foot and a half wide and about nine feet long, and the wood of it is what has been in the gravel of a bar for eleven years, and it is the part of the boat that the sea has. The garboard is up off the stones and the plate is in the light and you could put your thumb in the grain of it and feel where the sand has got into the wood and stopped being wood.

Sorrel had a file and a block of grease and the collar in her apron, and she was doing the work with her back to the water the way a person does eleven thousand hours of something.

Pell Cawley came down the hard at the third bell with his own cart behind him and a hand bar under his arm, and he is fifty-two and he has a slip at the north end and two boats under him and he has been building and mending flat-bottomed boats on this coast for thirty-one years.

“That collar is going,” he said, and looked at it for a second and a half without touching it.

“It is not going.”

“That is going, and you know the difference between a thing that is worn and a thing that is going, because you have been putting your hand on it for three years.” He crouched and put one thumb under the edge of it. “The wood has gone round it. That is the whole of it. It is not cracked and it is not rotten, it is simply that the plate has worn down into the collar instead of the collar sitting on the plate, and it will hold this season and it will not hold the season after, and the day it lets go it lets go in the middle, over the deepest part, and there is nothing to pull on out there.”

“What do you do when it lets go.”

“You get the rope in your hand and you hold her, and if you have a man on the bank with a rope you have a chance, and if you do not have a man on the bank you have eleven yards and no wind in the right quarter and the tide turning.” He put the hand bar down. “Put a new one on before the fourth.”

“How long does a new one take.”

“Four hours and a half if the weather is on my side and most of a day if it is not, and it is a day of mine either way and I have not got the day, because my own comes up on the sixth and it has to be on the ways by the turn or it is a year on that slip, so you can have the fourth or you can have the fourth of next tide, and I would not leave it to the fourth of next tide if I were you.”

“That is money, then.”

“That is my day. The wood I have and the iron I have and that is the end of what I have.” He sat down on the boat’s rail. “And you have not asked me for money and I am not going to pretend I have put a price on it, so let us have it plain. I do not want your money. If I put a collar on that boat and she comes back in the turn she will be right for another eleven years and I will be sixty-three and I will remember putting it on. That is a better trade than money and I have got about four days of work in me in the next four weeks and I am telling you the truth about which of the two is more worth having.”

---

A carter came up the hard about the fourth bell with an empty cart and a chain over the tail-board and stopped, because a boat with her bottom up is not a thing a man drives past.

“Vanner,” he said. “I have come about the fourth.”

“You have come about the fourth,” said Sorrel.

“I have. I have a load of lime coming up the coast road and it has to be on the far bank before the turn on the fourth and it is coming from the Quay, and the man who is bringing it says he can get it over the flat at the crossing and he has asked me whether I can get him a boat.” He looked at the hull. “Can I get him a boat.”

“You can get him a boat. You cannot get him a cart.”

“That is what I was told and I wanted it off a person rather than off a boy at the Quay.”

“He cannot take a cart over the shallow. Not ever, not any day, not with the best tide of the year, and I have put two in the water in my time and one of them was a cart of a man called Roke who is not a Gannock and who is alive and does not come over it.”

“Why not.”

“Because the middle of that bar is the soft place, and it is soft because the ebb out of the shallow runs through it and takes everything out of it, and the soft place is the only thing on the bar that has ever been deep, and a loaded cart goes straight to it and sits there.” She put the file down. “And it would have to go the other way round anyway to get at the deep part, and the deep part is where the channel is, and the channel on the fourth is under about nine feet and running, and I am not taking a flat boat with a man and a chain into nine feet of that because he has a load of lime on it.”

“Will you take the load.”

“The load goes in the boat. Everything that is not nailed to the shore goes in the boat, and he sits in it and I take the rope out on the ebb and walk her across on the tide going off, and he gets it on the far bank, and I bring him back on the flood.”

“How long is that.”

“Forty minutes over and about half an hour waiting for the water to come back to me, and then forty minutes back if the wind has not got up, and I do it in one day or I do not do it at all, because the boat is not a thing you leave out in that shallow for a week.”

“How much is it.”

“There is a penny a cart at the crossing four hundred yards south of here and the crossing counts what crosses it and not what floats over the top of it, and I have never taken a penny of that penny and I am not going to start, and you may tell your man with the lime.” She got the file up again. “If he wants to know whether the shallow is a road, the answer is yes, on the fourth, and on no other day of the seven, and that is not a thing I can write on anything for you, and it is not a thing anybody can write on anything, and that is the whole of why I say it out loud to you and not in a letter.”

---

Pell Cawley went on up the hard with his hand bar over his shoulder and said the thing on the way past that made the rest of the morning, and he said it the way a man says a thing he has said to four generations of Gannocks and never to a stranger.

“There is no other water,” he said. “That is the whole of this coast and it is worth saying to somebody from a road. There is the Reach, which has four and a half miles of water in it from end to end and a bar of gravel four hundred yards across at the mouth of it, and there is the flat, which is nine hundred yards of shingle and a chain, and there is the road up the coast, which is for carts and not for boats and is eleven miles longer. Three pieces of water and one of them is only a road when the sea is standing on the other one, and that is why you work the fourth and the sixth and the seventh and not the days you feel like it. On a good day the ebb out of that shallow runs long and soft through the middle of it and the middle is the soft place and you are in it up to the garboard in nine feet of a channel with a man in the boat. On the fourth the sea outside is standing up and the ebb is short and hard and it brings the sand out of the channel and packs the middle of the bar flat for the length of it, and the whole two hundred yards is a road for about an hour and a quarter. Then it comes back. And it is not the same bar every fourth, because it is the wind and the sand, and a man who tells you it is a road every week of your life is a man who has been on it forty times and has never once had it go out under him.”

“So I cannot put it in a letter,” said Sorrel.

“You could not put it in a letter if you had a clerk,” said Pell Cawley. “And you could not put it in a pane and I would not put it in a pane.”

---

They got on with it after that, and the collar came off at about the fifth bell, and what was under it was a groove worn into the soft wood of the bottom plate about the depth of a finger, and the plate itself had another two hundredths of an inch to go and no more, and Sorrel looked at the groove for a while and did not say anything, and then set about cutting the new collar out of a length of ash that Pell had left against the wall against the turn.

“You know that it is not for the cart,” she said, half way through, with the knife going.

“It is not for the cart.”

“It is for me.”

“It is for you and it is for the boat, and those have been the same thing in this family for four generations and nobody has ever written either of them down.” He took the knife off her, which he had no right to do and which she let him, and cut the last of the length in about a minute and a half, and put the handle back in her hand. “I have told you the trade and I will tell you the other half of it and then I am going to leave you to it. Eleven years and a man has not once asked your name. Have you noticed.”

“He knows my name.”

“He knows the name on the boat. He has said *the Gannock* to me twice this summer and *your father* once, and he knows perfectly well who is standing in the boat, and it does not matter to him, because he has been getting his lime over since before you were in it and he is going to be getting it over after.” He picked the hand bar up. “I am not saying that to be unkind. I am saying it because you are going to want a place to put it and there is not one, and I have looked, because you asked me nothing and I am a man who looks. There is a bar at the harbour mouth you can paint. There is a post at the crossing with notches in it, and a board on the front of that post now with two numbers on it and a line under them, and I have read that board and I do not understand a word of it and it is not about anybody’s name. There is a day book at the tide office and there is a line in it that says a person is a crossing, and I have been told about that line by a man who was in the yard when it was read out, and I have stood here and worked out that it is the only place on this coast where a person putting their foot over a thing gets written down, and it has got no name on it on purpose, and it is at the crossing four hundred yards south of this hard and it is a different piece of water.” He put the bar on his shoulder. “So there is no place. And I am telling you that there is no place as a man who builds the things, and not as a man who is being kind, and I am going up the hard now because I have a boat coming up on the sixth and I have not got the day.”

He went up the hard.

---

The ash collar went on in about four hours and a quarter, and it went on true, and she planed the top of it flat and let it into the plate and put three rivets through and set them up cold, and she was sitting back on her heels on the stones at about the ninth bell with her hand across the back of her neck when the man with the bad leg came down the hard for the second time that day, and he had been up at the glassworks all morning and had nothing to do there and had come down because the arch is cold and a yard is not.

“You have been sitting in a boat for a day,” he said.

“I have been in a boat for a day,” she said. “It is the same thing and I have not got the wages for either.”

He looked at the collar for a while, and at her hands, and at the flat above the tide line, which on a good day is a mile of shingle with a channel down the middle of it doing a yard a minute and nothing else in the world on it.

“Can I ask you something and will you say no if it is a stupid question.”

“You have been on this coast a month and you have asked me three things today and two of them were not stupid,” said Sorrel Gannock, “so ask it.”

“Tomorrow.”

She waited.

“You said there is one day a week the shallow is a road and six it is not. And you said it cannot be written on anything. And a man on a road four hundred miles from here has spent his life making a thing out of two people saying the same sentence, and he has told me in a yard this morning that the sentence at the top is the only part anybody reads.” He put his hand on the boat’s rail. “So tell me what would have to be true for it to be in a letter, and then I will tell you why it cannot be, and I already know why it cannot be, and I want to hear you say it.”

Sorrel Gannock sat on the stones in the middle of a hard with a new ash collar under her hand and a bad leg standing four feet off her, and she took a moment, and when she answered she answered him exactly and did not soften it and did not put anything on the end of it.

“Two things would have to be true,” she said. “One of them is that somebody could say in a letter *on the fourth, at the lowest of the ebb, the middle of the bar is hard from end to end*, and I have said that sentence to you twice this morning and it is true about eleven times in twenty, and the eleven is not a fixed eleven, it depends how hard the sea has been standing for three days, and the only person on this coast who can put a number on it is a person standing in my boat with her hand on the bottom. And the second is that a person reading it in two hundred miles could come and find out.” She looked at the water. “And that is the one that stops it. The day I put it in a letter, the day there is a paper with a depth on it, is the day a man with a cart comes and says *the paper says the middle is hard* and goes over it, and the paper will be right eleven times in twenty and wrong nine times in twenty and the nine will be somebody’s cart in nine feet of water and it will be my fault because of the paper.” She got up off the stones. “So you write it down in your book if you want to. It is a true thing and it is worth nothing, and it is worth nothing because it is only true while I am in the boat, and there is no paper in the world that can come and find out. That is what I have been trying to say to two men in this family for eleven years and I have never once had the words for it until about a minute ago, so you may put that in your book, and it will be the first thing anybody has written down about me and it will not be a name.”

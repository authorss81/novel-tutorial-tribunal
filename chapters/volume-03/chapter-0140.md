# Chapter 140: The Sixth

The sixth of the eighth tide is a bar day and the flat takes nine, and the westerly had been getting up for two days and came in the night with the rain behind it again, and by the first bell the sea was over the crown of the bar at the north end and the whole two hundred yards of gravel was gone under, and the sea outside was standing on that crown the way it stands on three days a week and no other day, and there is nobody on this coast who is surprised by that and there is nobody who is frightened of it.

A man of thirty-one was on that flat at the first bell with his weight on his good side and had been up the lane at the north end before the light and had come back down, and had not gone into the hut, and had not told anybody anything about that wall, and there is a thing he has to do four miles up that coast and has not done since the second of the seventh tide and he was not going to do it on a sixth, because there is a westerly on that road and a woman of eighty-one in a hut with a boy of eight in it and a day of weather between her and getting back.

She was on the stool with her back to the water and the chain round her knees and the tarpaulin over one arm, and the boy was on a board with a slate and a stick of chalk putting something on it and getting it wrong, and she was not correcting him, and the one thing he had put in front of her in six weeks she had said no to in about four minutes and had not asked him for a reason, and she did not know what was under any of it, and nobody on that road was going to tell her.

That is where she is on this coast. That is where she has been since the second of a tide and she is still there, knitting, about four miles above the north end, and he has been on this coast forty-nine days and has not asked her and has not told her and is not going to, and the whole of that is his and is not going to be settled by anybody who writes a page about it.

Nobody on that coast is owed a page by him for that and nobody has asked for one.

---

Nine crossings on the flat. The chain went up at the second bell and came up and went down all morning and rested an hour between the fifth and the seventh, and about seventy people were on that shingle because it is a sixth and everybody knows where everybody stands on a sixth.

There was no salt in the air and no strange noise and no water anywhere it should not have been, and the man at the crossing said to the man with the bar that it was a good sixth, and the man with the bar said it was a good sixth.

At about the fourth bell the sea at the north end began to do the thing it has done for a hundred years or more, and about nine people at the north end watched it do it and nobody else on this coast did.

---

The training wall stands along the seaward side of that deep. It is two hundred and sixty yards of stone laid in courses, nineteen years and eight months old, nine courses at the inner end and two at the outer, and it is there to hold the gravel of that point against the ebb coming out sideways out of a hole the ebb cut itself, and the danger is real, and the guild knew it was real, and the guild laid the stone about seven weeks with eight men in a season, and it has not failed in nineteen years, and the man who keeps it is fifty-eight and he was one of the hands that laid it.

On a sixth the ebb is short and hard and about an hour and a quarter long, and it comes out of the deep carrying sand, and it puts the sand into the deep, and the middle of the bar packs flat, and the hole that is nine feet on a good day is about a foot and a half of brown water and sand for the length of that ebb, and the whole two hundred yards of gravel is a road.

At about the fifth bell Halse Tarrant walked the wall from the inner end to the outer with a bar in his hand, because that is what he does, and about a hundred and forty yards of that wall had four or five people standing on it and two of them were women and one of them had a basket, and nobody was on it doing anything and everybody on it had been on it before.

At about the sixth bell he was about nine yards along that wall from the inner end, where a driven mark stands about two foot under the ninth course that nobody had looked at in nineteen years, and he had his hand on it because it is the second thing on that wall a man puts a hand on, and he was looking at the water in the deep, which was going down.

It had been going down for about half an hour. That is the ordinary end of the thing. The ebb has done its hour and a quarter and the sand has packed and the bar has been a road and now the flood has come back in behind it and the sand is going out of the deep with the ebb that is running off the shallow side, and the deep is making its depth again, and in about an hour it will be nine foot of green water and there will be no boat within forty yards of it and no way at all to see the bottom.

The man at the hard saw it first. It is Pell Cawley and he is a boat’s carpenter of fifty-two with a slip and two boats under him, and he was standing on a hull with a plane in his hand, and he put the plane down.

“Slee,” he said. “Slee, come here and look at that.”

There were five people on the top course at the outer end and four of them turned round, and none of them went in the water and none of them said anything for about two seconds, and then Halse Tarrant said a thing in the trade’s mouth that four people on this coast have heard said and nobody has ever heard it said about a real thing before.

“That is the water going out through the middle of it.”

That is what it looked like. In the middle of the seaward face, for about eleven yards, the water was not going down the face. It was going out through it, and where it was going out through it the surface of the deep was doing the thing that water does when it is being pulled through a hole about the size of a doorway, and it was making a boil, and a boil is a thing you can see from a hundred yards on a flat calm and there was a westerly on.

Nobody was in it. That is the first thing about it and it is the whole of why it was possible for six people to stand on a wall and look at it for as long as they did.

“It is not the wall failing,” said Halse Tarrant. “Do not anybody say that. The wall is doing what it was built to do and it has been doing it for nineteen years. That is the bed going out from under the bottom course in the middle of eleven yards of it, and I found that yesterday with a lead line in a boat, and I have got it in a book in a shed with the date on it, and there are three people on this coast who knew I went out yesterday and not one of them knew what I went out for.”

He did not say the last part to anybody who had not been there. It came out the way a thing comes out of a man on a wall.

“I have got the number and I have got the date and the number says there is no bottom holding gravel under the bottom course of the middle eleven yards of that face, and that was yesterday, on a good day, when the sea was down and I could see the whole of that face from a boat, all nine courses of it at the inner end and down to two at the outer one. And it is not a photograph. It is not a morning anybody took standing beside a fire. It is a number with a date on it in a book, and I will swear to it in a room in front of anybody who wants me to, and the wall went out from under its own foot at about the seventh bell this evening in front of about six people.”

“And what is the harm in it,” said the boat’s carpenter, who was fifty-two and had been building and mending flat-bottomed boats at that hard for thirty-one years, and who had a slip of his own a long way inside the gravel and had known that for years.

“The harm is not the hole. The hole is not the harm. I want that said in this shed and on that wall and in whatever you tell them at the crossing. The hole is a hole and it is eleven yards and it can be closed with stone in a morning by a man in a boat on a good day, and I will do it, and it will cost me a day that I have not got.

“The harm is the gravel. That wall is worth the two feet of gravel behind it and the two feet of gravel is the whole of that point, and the wall is not holding the gravel up, it is holding it sideways against the ebb, and where the bed has gone out from under the wall the ebb comes through the wall now instead of past it, and the first thing it does on the far side is take gravel, and the second thing it does is go round the end, and the third thing it does is have a wider hole to work on next time, and none of that is a disaster. That is a shore going. It took two winters the last time and it will take less than that this time, because this time the guild is putting stone in a hole instead of taking a wall out, and there is a difference and it is the only good thing anybody can say about it.”

He put his hand flat on the ninth course.

“And the guild was right,” he said. “I want that said too, and by me, and in the open, and not once in the next year by anybody else. The danger was real. The danger is there this evening. They put two hundred and sixty yards of stone on that shoulder in a season because the water had already taken a hut and a slip, and they were right, and I have watched it every day for thirty-one years and it is exactly as bad as the man who laid the stone said it would be, and if you take that wall out you have got no north end in three winters and I would say so to anybody who asked me tonight, including anybody in a building two hundred miles up this coast.”

Nobody said anything for about a minute. The westerly got up another notch, and the boil in eleven yards of it went on doing what it was doing, and the sand went on going out of the deep.

---

At about the eighth bell about four people who had been at the north end came off the wall and down the lane and across the flat, and by the time they were on the shingle the story had four versions in it and none of them was about the gravel, and all four of them were about the hole.

Jory Vance was at the chain at that hour with the board under his arm, and he is twenty-two and four years on the counter and writes down what happened while it is happening, and he made the entry standing up at the chain in the ordinary way, with the sea going off the flat round him.

The System put it in front of Ilyan Vester on the wet shingle at about the ninth bell, on a bar day, at the near end of nine hundred yards of shingle with the water going off it, and what it gave him was the sixth thing on this coast that was a record of a thing somebody had entered nowhere in particular.

---

There are three people on this coast who could do something about the eleven yards of it and not one of them can see the other two, and nobody has asked any of them for anything.

A woman of thirty-six was at the north end with her boat on the hard and a towrope in her hand, and she had been in that deep twice that day in a flat-bottomed boat with a man in it, and she had stood on that wall at the fifth bell, and she had not been there at the seventh, and she was the only person on that coast who had been out in the water over it in a boat and had come back, and she was not asked and she was not going to be told anything about the ninth bell because nobody in that yard was going to think of it.

A woman of fifty-one was a mile and a flight of stairs up the hill from that flat in a room with two panes in oak frames on the north wall, and four people crossed the flat in the evening and one of them came up the lane to the office at about the ninth bell and told her in about a minute and a half, and she listened to the whole of it and then she asked one question and it was *how many feet*, and nobody had measured it in front of anybody, and she was told that, and she said:

“Then that is a number somebody is going to want, and there is not one instrument on this coast that will give it to him except a man in a boat, and the man in the boat has got one with a date on it, and I have wanted that number since about the ninth bell last night and I am not going to send a boy up that lane for it, because that is what this coast does to people, and in about a week it would come back to him from a room and it would be the worst thing that has ever happened to him.” She squared the sheet. “And it will keep until somebody has to move it and there will be a day when that has to be in a room with a thing in it, and I am not going to be the woman who asks for it in a room on a sixth evening, because I would have had it out of him in three days if I had not.”

A man of sixty-one was two miles along that coast at a bench with an order on it under a batch book with a date against it and no figure against it, and a pane three years old on the wall of his own shed, and the eighth column of it said that the gravel stood against the seaward face of that wall to the second course, and at that moment that was the only figure anybody on this coast had in their head about that wall, and it was three years old and the other one was nineteen years and eight months old and the two of them could not be put in one room, and the arch behind two feet of brick was cold.

---

The rendering came up on the man with the bad leg at about the ninth bell on nine hundred yards of wet shingle and it came up the way it comes up, at the height and at the distance it has been at since the third of the second month in a city four hundred miles behind him. The boards, and the table the size of a table a hand could go on, and the lamp with the wick down, and the window with weather on the outside of it that was not this weather, and four people at the table and one of them about forty-four with her back to him. It did not move and he could not look away from it with nine hundred yards of shingle under him, and it cost him what it costs, and the leg went at the same moment, from the hip down, all of it, and did not come back inside two hundred yards and came back the rest of the way worse.

He told nobody then and he told nobody afterwards, and about nine people on that coast have now seen something happen to him on a shingle or in a boat and not one of them has said a word, and that is what four hundred miles of this coast does to a man it has no standing over.

He stood at the near end in the dark at about the ninth bell and worked out what he was going to do, and it took him about four minutes, and it is a decision and it is not a good one and he is going to make it anyway.

He is going to go and ask three people things.

Not to tell them. Not to fetch them, and not to carry a sheet to any of them, and not to write one. He has no standing on this coast and has never had one and there is no way on four hundred miles of it to get one, and he has been produced four times on this coast, every one of them by a thing that was simply nearest to him, and he is not going to be produced a fifth.

He is going to go to the north end and stand in a yard where a woman of thirty-six has a boat and a towrope and eleven years of a thing nobody has ever asked her the name of, and ask her whether she would like to be the one standing there. He is going to walk two miles along the coast to a shed where a man of sixty-one has been at a fire for forty-one years and has never once been asked, and ask him the same. And he is going to go up a hill to a woman of fifty-one who told him on a shingle at four in the afternoon three days ago that she would rather be a coast than a woman, and ask her again, in a room, with the door shut, having been told by a glassmaker that asking a woman to agree to a year is an instruction with a question on the end of it.

He does not know what any of the three will say. He has one guess and he has had it since he watched a man on a shingle say no out loud in front of seventy people about a piece of paper he had made, and the guess is that at least one of the three will say no, and that the no will be right, and that it will be right in the way a thing is right when it is about the person saying it and not about the wall.

He has not got a page for this one and he is not going to write one. A page with a date on it and that it is his is a thing he has done five times in forty-nine days, and every one of the five was a thing he wrote instead of going and standing in a yard, and he has been on the wrong end of the other half of that four times and nobody on this coast has ever said so to him.

And there is a fourth road on this coast that he is not going to go up at all. It is four miles above the north end and there is a hut on it and a woman of eighty-one in the hut who is knitting, and a boy of eight on a board with a slate, and he is not going to knock, and that is because of the second of the seventh tide and a page in his coat, and it is the hardest thing in this paragraph and it is not one of the three, and nobody on this coast knows that he worked out where he was going and left that road off, and nobody is going to be told.

The water came off the flat and left nine hundred yards of it darker than the rest, and the chain came up and went into its sockets, and the ninth crossing went over, and a boy of eleven went over for the ninth time at a penny with a hand-lead in his fist, and nobody on that shingle thought anything about it, including him, and about four people were carrying a story about a boil in a wall, and about nine people were carrying the same story without the boil in it, and a woman of forty-eight at a stall at Wether Cross wrote the ninth down and nothing else, and a man of thirty-one stood at the near end in the dark with his weight on his good side and had not asked anybody for one thing in forty-nine days and was going to ask three of them.

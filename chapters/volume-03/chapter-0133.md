# Chapter 133: Spent

The sixth of the seventh tide is a bar day and the flat takes nine, and the sea came up over the crown of the bar before the light and stayed up all morning, and by the fourth bell there were about seventy people on nine hundred yards of shingle for the same reason there always are: everybody on this coast knows where everybody else will be standing on a sixth.

Hal Bevan had nine crossings to see over and one question to put, and he did the crossings first, because a man does his work, and he put the question at the turn.

The first four went before the fifth bell and there was nothing unusual about any of them. A man went over with four carts of deals and came back for the fifth in about half an hour and would be over and back twice more before the turn. A woman with a handcart of shingle went at the second bell and again at the third and was owed a penny for each and took it. The guild’s own works were not on that flat that morning, because the wall at the far end was finished and there was nothing left on it to do and the men had gone to something else, and a finished wall on a bar day is the only kind of finished there is.

And about halfway up the second crossing, the man with the bar stopped and stood still in the middle of nine hundred yards of wet shingle and looked back at the head of the flat for about four seconds, the way a man looks at a thing he is going to think about at the turn.

He had not done that seven days ago. Nobody on that shingle noticed him do it, and the four seconds cost him a yard and a half of ground and about four minutes, and he had come off about a hundred yards short of the chain and had to walk the cart back up the shingle to make it, and he did not say a word to anybody about it then or afterwards, and the carter said nothing because carters say nothing.

He had the paper in his hand instead of in his pocket. He had not said he would bring it and he had not said he would not. It was folded in four in a dealer’s hand and it was a sheet of nothing that had cost a carter a day and a day’s hire seven days ago, and he held it up at about the quarter to the turn so that the people near the stone could see that there was a thing in it, and then he put it back in his coat while he climbed up.

The stone is four feet of flat stone at the head of the flat and she was already on it, and it is a hundred years old or about that, and there is nothing on it and nothing at the end of any line of it, and the guild has never interfered with it and never will.

He got his two feet on the flat part and looked at her.

“Is there anything in that paper that is wrong about the water.”

He had had it in his mouth for seven days. He had had four others in his mouth for seven days and thrown all four of them away, and this was the one he had kept, and it was the plainest thing he knew how to say.

She looked at him for about two seconds.

“That is a question about the water,” she said.

“It is.”

“Then you get one and you have got one, and nobody may ask you a second and there is not going to be one, and I am not going to make you walk back down nine hundred yards of wet shingle with nothing because you asked it in the wrong words. Nobody has ever been made to walk down that shingle with nothing and nobody is going to start with you on my account. Ask it properly or I cannot answer it, and you have asked it properly.”

And then she answered it, out loud, in front of about seventy people, and she did not hedge and she did not soften it and she did not ask him for anything, and it took about a minute and a half.

“There is nothing in that paper that is wrong about the water.

”The water at the north end on a good day goes out to about four inches at the lowest of the ebb, and about six on the bar, and the bar is a bar and not the channel, and the channel on a good day lies across that shallow and not along it, and a boat that wants to cross it wants the middle of the ebb and a cart that wants the flat wants the end of one and the beginning of the next. On the fourth, the sixth and the seventh the sea stands over the crown of the bar and there is no lowest at the north end at all, because there is no water left in it, and the crossing four hundred yards south of you is the road on those three days and this flat is not.

”That is true, and it has been true since before I came to that counter, and it is the whole of what I can put on this stone this morning. If that paper says what I have just said, then it is right, and I have not got a different figure for you.”

Somebody at the back said the word *four* to somebody else, and the somebody else said the word *feet*, and about nine people on that shingle had a number in their heads that had just been contradicted out loud in the open by the only person on this coast who is allowed to say a number about the water, and about sixty of them had understood the shape of it and not the size.

“And I am going to tell you the one thing I will not do, because you have one question and it is nearly gone,” she said. “I am not going to stand on a stone and say whether the table on a wall a mile up this hill is right about that morning or whether that paper is right about it. I have never in nineteen years had those two things in front of me at once and I cannot do it standing up in the open, and if I tried it I would be guessing about the water, and I do not guess about the water. Ask me again on a fourth or a sixth about the water and I will tell you the water again, and it will be the same, and that is all I have.”

Nobody said anything after that. Nobody asked her a second question. About nine people on that shingle knew exactly what she had said and about sixty had understood the shape of it.

He stood there and worked out that he had just been told, in the open, in front of everybody, by the only person on four hundred miles of this coast who is allowed to say it, that the paper was right about the water.

The paper was right about the water.

That was the whole of what was wrong with his week.

“You did not spend it on the water,” said Bettany Rook.

She said it flat and in the ordinary way, the way a person says a thing about a thing, and she was not unkind and she was not going to be unkind about it, and she went on without waiting for him to answer, which is what a woman who has answered a question on a stone for a hundred years does.

“I am not going to say that is a waste, because you did not ask me that and I would have said no to it. I am going to say the other thing, because nobody may ask you a second question and I would rather you had this than did not. You had a week and you had one question and you spent it on a piece of paper that says the water is doing what the water does, and I have just told you, out loud, in front of about seventy people, that you were right about the water, and that has cost you the only thing you have ever had on this coast and it has bought you a thing you could have got by standing on your own flat with your own eyes on it for a day.”

She got down off the flat stone onto the shingle and did not pick up anything, because there is nothing on it to pick up.

“And that is the whole of what this stone is. It is for the water. It has always been for the water, and it is a hundred years old and it is in nobody’s book, and there is nobody on this coast I could have asked it to and there is not going to be one, and I would not have told you a different answer if there had been. There is no second door. There is one stone and two days a week and me on it, and that is all there is, and you had it.”

He went down off the stone and down the shingle to the chain and put his slate in his hand and carried it for the rest of the day, and he did not say anything to anybody about what he had been told, and about nine people on that flat would have given a great deal to have heard it.

There were seven carts on the flat and the man with the bar took them across in order. A boy of eleven went over for the ninth time at a penny with a hand-lead in his fist. A woman of forty-eight was at the far stones with her slate for the ninth crossing, because the ninth crossing of a bar day is the only one on that flat worth a slate, and she is not paid and never will be and she is always there on the ninth. The tally post stood where it has stood with its chain across the path, and the board was on the front of it with its two numbers and the difference and the line under them, and the fourpence of nails held, and the ninth crossing went over four feet from it and not one man on that flat so much as turned his head at it.

Seth Garrick came out of the oil shed at the head of the lane when the ninth crossing was done and stood with his back on his own door and looked at the flat for a while, and then he said one thing out loud to nobody in particular, which was: “Well.”

And then he shut the door, and that was all he ever said about it to anybody, for the rest of his life.

A man of thirty-one was standing about thirty feet off on the dry part with his weight on his good side. He had heard all of it. He had worked out inside about ninety seconds, standing in the cold, that a man had spent the only thing he had ever been able to spend on this coast on a question that was in the right words about a piece of paper about the water, and had been answered, and the answer had been true, and he was going to be able to use none of it.

He had four pages in a penny exercise book in his coat and there was no place on this coast for a fifth, and he had known there was no place on this coast for a fifth since about the third of a tide and he had not found one.

What he could have said, and did not, and would have been refused if he had, was that a person on a coast with a record nobody can produce has no way at all to put two records side by side, and that this stone is not that way, and that it was never going to be that way, and that a man who spends it on a record has spent it on the one thing on four hundred miles that does not need him.

He did not say it because he has no standing on this coast and has never had one, and because the only two things he has ever been able to give a person here are an argument and a piece of paper, and the argument was refused in a yard in the fifth of a tide and the piece of paper was in the man on the chain’s breast pocket and had cost a carter a day.

So he stood thirty feet off on nine hundred yards of wet shingle and said nothing, and there is not one person on this coast who has ever worked out that he was the man who could have said it.

At the ninth bell the water went off the flat and left nine hundred yards of it darker than the rest, and the chain came up and went into its sockets, and Hal Bevan took the bar in his right hand and a stroke on the slate for the last of the nine and put the slate back in his pocket, and went home up the lane.

He had kept the paper. He never took it out again.

# Chapter 142: Three Places

The first of the ninth tide is a good day and the flat takes six, and the water at the north end goes out long and soft, and the sea was about four foot on that bar at the second bell and still going.

Ilyan Vester went to three places in one day because he had worked out on the seventh, standing in the dark at the near end of nine hundred yards of shingle, that he had fifty-one days on this coast and had been asked for one thing by one person and that this was going to be the only day he got three of them on the same side of the ground.

They are about as far apart as three people on this coast can be. A yard at the north end with a boat in it. A shed two miles along the coast with a bench and a cold arch in it. A room a mile and a flight of stairs up the hill with a lock on the door and two panes in oak frames on the north wall that have not been out of that building in nineteen years.

And not one of the three can see the other two. That is the shape of it and he had known the shape of it for a week and had not been anywhere.

---

The yard first, because it was nearest and because she was in it.

Sorrel Gannock had the boat up on the shingle above the slip with her back to it, doing something to the bottom of it with a short-handled tool, and the drying yard was running and there were two men on a net and neither of them looked up, and a woman of about sixty was going through a basket of bait and talking to nobody in particular at about the speed of a person who has said it before.

“Twice yesterday and once this morning,” she said, before he had said anything. “That is the whole of what I have got and I have not got a number on it and I am not going to give you one.

“I can tell you where that deep is. Not in words. You cannot write down where a deep is, and everybody who has ever tried has written down where it was, which is a different thing and is no use to anybody in a boat. I have got eleven years of it in my hands and in the flat of my own boat and in the place on the bar where I put the pole down when the sand comes up, and none of that is mine to hand over, and if I stood in a room and tried to hand it over I would be doing about four minutes of work and about eleven months of something else and I have not got eleven months, and neither has anybody on this coast.

“So that is what I am holding, and you may have it, and I have just given it to you, and it is a thing you can only have by being in a boat with me on a day when the sea is over the crown. And you cannot have it on a good day, because on a good day it is nine foot of green water over the whole of it and there is nothing to have.”

He stood in the yard and let that sit for a moment.

“You do not have to do anything with it,” he said.

“I know I do not,” she said. “That is the second thing. The first is that I have told you, out loud, in a yard, and two men on a net have heard me, and that is the only place on this coast where a thing like that can be put. Not a room. A room needs a person to stand in it and say they are standing in it, and there is no room on this coast a person like me can stand in, and I am not going to be produced.”

He said nothing for about four seconds, and then he said the thing he had come to say, which he had said once before that week on a shingle in daylight and had got wrong.

“I would like to ask you the next thing as well. I told you yesterday I would come back for it.”

“Then come back for it,” said Sorrel Gannock, “and do not do it in a hurry, because you have got two more to ask and one of them is a man who has been at a fire for forty-one years and the other one is a woman who came down that hill and said no to you in front of forty people, and I will tell you the same thing about both of them and then you can decide what to do with it. Neither of them has been asked for one thing. You are the first person on this coast who has asked either of them for anything, and that is not nothing, and it is also not a reason to hurry, and the hurry is what people do when they have been produced.”

---

Two miles along the coast, and a man of sixty-one at a bench in a shed with the arch behind two feet of brick cold for a season and a year.

Hollis Thrale is deaf on the right side, and it is not an accident of his childhood and it is not an accident of his life: the ear is his father’s, and his father’s father had it, and the kiln at the end wall was put up in the year of a bad seventh by a man who could only work one-handed, which is Hollis Thrale’s grandfather and the reason there is a hearth in that building high enough for a man to be stood at. On the wall of that shed, in a frame he made himself, is a pane the guild has never seen, three years old, with a figure on it for eleven places and every tide of the year.

He had his hands on the frame and he had them on it before Ilyan Vester was through the door, and a woman of twenty-four was at the cold arch with a bar in her hand and did not turn round, because a person comes up that road about twice in a season and she has not got off the fire to be civil to anybody since the spring.

“Sit down,” he said. “You asked me for this in a yard and I am going to say it out loud in my own mouth instead of having it read at you off that wall by the only other person in this shed who can read it, because I have had the two of them in my head for two years and I want it on the record of my mouth and not on hers, and because a man who has had a number in his head for two years ought to be able to say it standing up.”

He said it in four minutes and it was the third and the eighth, because it is always those two. The third is the outer end of the north wall, given as a place and not a figure, two hundred and sixty-one yards from the post at the inner end along the line of the wall, and after the figure it gives a height, which is the top of the inner end where the wall is nine courses and about six foot. The eighth is the toe of the seaward face, also a place: the gravel standing against that face to the second course along the whole of it, and standing to the fifth at the inner end. The guild’s third has two hundred and sixty-four. The guild’s eighth has the fourth course for the whole length and nothing whatever about the inner end.

“I have had the guild’s two in my head in a clerk’s hand for two years,” he said, “and I have had mine on that wall for three, and I cannot get the two of them into a room, and I am not going to be able to, and the reason is not money and it is not spite and it is not that anybody is stopping me. The reason is that the only instrument anybody on this coast will act on is on the north wall of a room a mile up the hill and it has not been out of that room in nineteen years, and it is in an oak frame, and it weighs what a pane weighs, and there is one key and the key is in a pocket on a belt and the belt is on the person, and the person is right about the sea.”

He put his hand flat on the bench, and the bench is where Sabra Trew had laid two slates down side by side on the second of the fifth tide, and he did not know that and Ilyan Vester did and neither of them said so.

“That is what I am holding,” he said. “A photograph of one morning, three years old, and two numbers out of eleven that record a place and not a depth, and the two that would tell anybody whether that wall is still in the right place, and no way on four hundred miles of this coast of getting them beside the other two. And I have never once been up that coast road to the man who keeps it, and I did not know until about a week ago that he was a person, because I have known about that wall for two years and I have only ever known it as a thing in my own head.”

---

A mile and a flight of stairs up the hill, and a room, and a woman of fifty-one at a table with her hood up and a clerk of twenty-two doing the fourth of a season’s figures beside her.

She had sent for him. That was the thing about it and it took him about nine seconds to work out what it meant, and it was not the same as a person being produced, because she had not sent for him, she had sent for nobody, and there was nothing on the coast that would have got a message up that hill except a cart and a man with a reason.

“You have been to two places today and I can tell you which two by the dust on your coat and I am not going to make you say it,” said Bettany Rook. “Shut the door. Not because of the room, because of the stair, and because I have six weeks of your coat in my eye and I would like one conversation on this coast with the door shut, and it may well be the last one, and I have not had it yet.”

Then she said the whole of it, standing up, without sitting down and without asking him for anything.

“I hold two panes of glass in oak frames on the north wall of this room. The older one is nineteen years and eight months old and it is the water and passage charter and it is the only legible copy of it on four hundred miles and it was made by a woman called Mare who is dead, and her name is inside the lid of the batch book two miles along the coast because that is how you keep a name. The newer one is the guild’s tide table and it is about three months old and it is three months of a woman of twenty-four who is paid nothing, standing at a fire.

“They are a mile and a flight of stairs up a hill from nine hundred yards of shingle that a cart goes over six times a day, and I have never once in nineteen years taken either of them off that wall and carried them anywhere, and I am not going to start on a good morning when nobody has asked me to, and the reason I am not going to start is not that anybody asked me not to. It is that a record of a moving thing is a working instrument and instruments do not travel, and the man who has one is the only man who can use it, and the guild said that to a man in a lane in four lines last week and every part of it was right and I have written it down as being right.

“I hold a table of tides that I can read out of this room to a man on a bar day in about ninety seconds and get him over nine hundred yards of shingle with a load on, and I have never been wrong about that water and I am not going to be. And I hold two panes that agree with each other and disagree with a man two miles along this coast in two columns out of eleven, and I cannot put the three of them in one place, and I am the harbourmaster of Kestrel Reach and I have not been able to do it in two years and neither has anybody else, and nobody has ever asked me to try, because a man in a good coat asked me once for a year, two winters back, and the answer to it was eleven months of the back of a woman of twenty-four, and he did not ask her, and he was right not to ask her.

“And on the sixth evening a man stood at that counter and told me about the middle of that wall and I asked him how many feet and he said nobody had measured it in front of anybody. I have wanted that figure ever since. I am not going to go and get it, and I have written down why in a place where nobody will read it, and the reason is that there is a way of getting a number out of a person on this coast and the way is a boy up a lane, and I have watched what that does to a boy about four times in nineteen years and I have never once done it myself and I am not starting on a sixth evening with a stranger watching me not do it.”

She put her hand flat on the table.

“That is what I am holding, and you may have it, and I have just given it to you, and it is worth exactly nothing, and I am telling you it is worth nothing so that you do not go and find out.”

---

He went down the hill at about the seventh bell and up the coast road to the crossing and stood at the near end of a flat that takes six, and the cart went over at about the eighth and came back, and the light was going, and he worked out the thing that had been in the back of his head for four days and let it be said out loud, once, to nobody, on nine hundred yards of dry shingle.

Three people had each told him a true thing in about four minutes each, in three places, in three different trades, and every part of every one of them had been right, and there was not one thing in the three that he could carry to either of the other two.

Not because of him. Because of a custom about four hundred years old that is in nobody’s book and has the shape of the reason a man will not sign for a woman: where a thing on a road is a person, the account of it belongs to that person and has to be given by them, in their own mouth, and every word of it. And because there is a room a mile up the hill with a lock on it, and the two figures that would settle the wall are in that room, in glass, in nineteen-year-old frames, and a record of a moving thing does not travel. And because the only thing that could put three people’s accounts of one wall into a thing anybody could act on is a pane, and a pane is a person and a fire and a year, and the person is a woman of twenty-four who is paid nothing and has not been asked, and the year has not started, and the arch is cold behind two feet of brick.

And a fourth road on this coast went up past the north end to a hut four miles above it, where a woman of eighty-one was sitting on a stool with her back to the water and the chain round her knees, knitting, with a boy of eight on a board with a slate putting something on it and getting it wrong, and nobody on that road was going to tell her what was under any of it.

He had worked out on the seventh where he was going, and he had gone to three places, and he was not going up that one, and no person on this coast knew that he had left it off, and he was not going to tell anybody that either.

At about the ninth bell a man of thirty was at the head of the lane with his back on the door of a lamp-oil shed, watching the water come in, and a boy of eleven went over for the sixth time at a penny with a hand-lead in his fist, and a woman of forty-eight wrote it on a slate on her knee under an awning and was paid nothing for it and had never once said so.

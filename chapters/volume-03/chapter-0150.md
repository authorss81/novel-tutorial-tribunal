# Chapter 150: Beneath the Weeks

The second of the tenth tide is a good day and the flat takes six, and Ilyan Vester had been on that coast fifty-nine days, and he walked two miles up the coast road at about the second bell with the weight on his good side and got to a shed at about the half past.

It took him about an hour and a quarter, and it had taken him an hour and a quarter on the third of the ninth tide going the other way with nothing in his coat he had not had before, and the leg had been his own for about four hours on the fourth of the third tide in this building and had not been his own on any day since that one, and it was not his own now, and he told nobody on the road and nobody in the shed.

The arch at the end wall was behind two feet of brick and was cold and had been cold for a season and would be cold for a year, and the guild’s own order was where it had been since a cart brought it up behind a load of oil, under the batch book, with a clerk’s date on it and nothing at all under the date, and it would be under that book at the turn of this day as well.

The wall behind the bench is the one thing in that shed that has never been moved and cannot be. It is four hundred years of weeks. A week is the only piece of time on four hundred miles of this coast that everybody agrees is a piece of time, and so every list on this coast is a list of weeks, and a week is cut as a stroke and a week that is not there is a space the width of a stroke, and the marks go the length of that wall in a single row at about the height of a man’s chest, and there are about four hundred years of them, and there are holes in it that nobody has ever counted.

Two people made copies. The woman who cut most of them is dead eleven years and copied the list out twice in letters, and one of those copies went a hundred and forty miles up this coast in a bundle to a school and is in a press in a room with a dry lock on it, and the other was the one the dead woman’s granddaughter used to work from, and the granddaughter is the woman standing at the second fire with a bar in her hand.

And at the bottom of that wall, below the row of weeks, at about the height of a knee, where the brick is hardest to reach and the mortar is oldest, there is a second run of marks that is not weeks.

“Nobody has touched that in my lifetime and nobody asked me about it until about a minute ago,” said Sabra Trew. “I have been in this shed every day since I was eleven years old.”

---

He could not read it. He had said so out loud on this coast twice in five weeks and it had been remarked on by nobody, and he said it now at the bench, to a man of sixty-one and a woman at a fire, and neither of them showed the slightest sign of having heard anything unusual, and a man of sixty-one cannot hear a voice on his right and had not heard the whole of the sentence anyway.

“Put your hand on it and tell me what is under your hand,” he said. “Not what it says. What is under your hand. I want the arrangement. I want to know where the marks are and how big the gaps are and what is a row and what is a column and how many rows there are and what the shape of the outside of it is, and I will not be able to tell you what any of it is for and I am not asking you to tell me that.”

She put the bar in the fire and came and stood in front of the wall and put the flat of her hand on the brick about two feet along, and moved it, and stopped, and moved it again.

“It is one row,” she said. “The whole of the weeks is one row. That is four hundred years of them going that way and it is not a grid and it is not a list of columns, it is a line, and about nine people on this coast have stood where you are standing and looked at that wall and every one of them has counted it as a line.

“The other thing is not a line. It is in blocks. And the blocks are not square. And they are not even. The first block has a border on two sides and not on the other two, which is a thing you do when you are marking the edge of something and running out of wall, and the columns in it are not all the same width — one of them is about half the width of the one beside it, and it is empty below a point, so the block has a step in it. There are nine columns. I have counted them about a hundred times because I cut marks and I count things.

“And at the end of it there is a row of about eleven things that are not strokes at all. They are little marks, and every one of them is different, and they are all the same size, and they are in a straight row with even spaces between them, and that is the one thing on this wall that looks like somebody meant it. Everything else on that wall is a count. That is a set.”

“Who cut them.”

“My grandmother cut the set,” said Sabra Trew. “I know because she was the one who told me to keep off the bottom of the wall and she never told me why, and I asked her once, when I was about fifteen, and she gave me an answer I have said out loud to about four people in twenty-four years and I have never once been able to get any further with it.

“She said: that is not mine. I found it. I have been finding it since I was a girl. I have never worked it out and I am not going to guess at it in a wall.”

Nobody said anything for about a minute, and the light came off the water outside the door and went along the row of weeks on that wall and did not stop at any of the gaps, because gaps in a wall do not interrupt light.

---

Then the rendering came up on him, in a shed, with his hand flat on about two feet of brick, and it came up the way it comes up, at the height and at the distance it has been at since the third of the second month in a city four hundred miles behind him: the boards, and the table the size of a table a hand could go on, and the lamp with the wick down, and the window with weather on the outside of it that was not this weather, and four people at the table and one of them about forty-four with her back to him.

It did not move. He could not look away from it with two feet of brick under his hand, and it cost him what it costs, and the leg went at the same moment from the hip down, all of it, and did not come back inside the length of a shed and came back the rest of the way worse, and he put his weight against the bench and stood there for about four minutes and did not say anything about it, and a man of fifty-eight two hundred yards down that coast has never been told and a man of sixty-one in this room has not noticed and a woman at the fire has her back to both of them.

Nobody was told, then or afterwards, and about nine people on this coast have now watched something happen to him on a road, on a shingle or in a boat, and not one of them has ever mentioned it.

And then the System put something in front of him that it had not been asked for, and that was the first time in his life that anything in it had recognised anything at all.

It did not translate the wall. It has never translated a wall, and it did not begin on the second of the tenth tide. What it did was recognise, and it gave him one line, and the line was not a rendering of a record and it was not a notice and it was not anything he had entered anywhere, and there was no block quote in it and no second line under it, and it did not end in what is not decided because there was nothing in it that was a decision.

The line was a shape. It was nine columns of uneven width with a border on two sides, and a step in it, and then a row of eleven marks that were all the same size and all different, and it was a shape he had seen ten thousand times in a building four hundred miles behind him in a life that had ended a very long way back from here, and it was a test grid, and he knew it the way a man knows a door in his own house in the dark.

And at the end of it, standing on its own with nothing attached to it, a signature.

**NK-3317.**

He could not read. He had not read it. It arrived, and it was two letters, a dash and four figures he had never once seen on this coast and would not have known from a wall if somebody had cut it into one, and he knew it anyway, because a man does not have to read a thing he has been looking at for years.

Nadia Kestrel had signed nine hundred test cases in a building with a bad coffee machine and a column of dates beside her name, and the signature at the bottom of every one of them had been her own two letters and a number, and it had been the thing she was proudest of and the thing she had been shouted at for, and once, a long way back, she had told him that a build was a thing you could hold a person up with and a signature was the only part of a build that could not be argued with, and that if you took the signatures off a build it stopped being evidence and went back to being an opinion.

He had filed more of them under her than under anybody, including himself, and had been shouted at for it more than once, and the last time she had shouted at him about it was over a grid he had got wrong on purpose to find out what a system would do with a person who got it wrong on purpose.

The System said nothing else. It did not say whose the signature was. It did not say where the grid came from, or what a grid was, or what a set of eleven marks the same size and all different was for, or why somebody four hundred years ago had cut it in marks on the wall of a working shed because a stroke is the only mark anybody on that coast had. It recognised a signature and it stopped, the way it stops, and the thing it recognised was about four hundred years old and had been under a bench in a shed two miles along a coast for as long as anybody alive had been able to remember, and nobody had ever asked it what it was for, because the woman who had found it had told the only person who ever asked her that she was not going to guess at it in a wall.

The name of it came up in his head about four seconds later and he did not say it out loud and it went nowhere and it is not a picture of anywhere a person could walk.

It was *The Sovereign’s Wake*. It came back to him in the same words he had once had about it standing at a bench in a yard, because there was no other way to have it: a game, the last man to test it, a report filed on the final night that had gone up to a machine in that building and had never been merged, and four hundred years of telling anybody that he was right. And something had begun, a long time afterwards, to move words out of it, and out of a few hundred thousand other places, and he had never known that, and it was not about him. That was four hundred years ago on this coast, by the reckoning of a woman who is dead, who found the marks at the bottom of that wall and never worked them out and told her granddaughter so.

And he could not have said, standing at a bench with his hand on brick, what any of that had to do with nine uneven columns and a row of eleven marks.

“You have gone a colour,” said Hollis Thrale, from the bench, without turning round, and then, after a moment: “That is not about the fire. Do not tell me it is about the fire. I have been at a fire forty-one years and I can tell the difference between a man who is tired and a man who has found something out about himself, and I have had two of the second in this shed in twenty years and I did not have a good time with either of them, and I am not going to ask.”

“All right,” said Ilyan Vester.

“That is the correct answer and it is the only one I have ever wanted from anybody, and I am not going to ask you again tonight, and if you ever want to tell me you know where the fire is.”

---

The question was asked at about the sixth bell, by a woman of twenty-four standing in front of a wall with her hand flat on it, and it was asked in the ordinary way and in an ordinary voice and nobody had set it up.

She had been quiet for about ten minutes, which is a long time at a second fire, and then she said:

“If that is a copy of something, then somebody made it.”

Nobody answered her, and she went on, because she was not asking anybody.

“And the making of it is the whole of what I want. Not what it is. I have not got a use for what it is and there is nobody on this coast who could tell me and I have stopped wanting to know in a shed.

“This coast has people in it who can say no. That is the thing I have never been able to account for and I have been trying to account for it since I was about eleven years old and I am the woman who cuts the marks in this building, so I have had a good deal of time to notice it. There is a stone at the head of the flat and a woman stands on it two days a week and she answers about the water and nobody may ask her a second, and nobody has ever written down that she does it, and nobody has ever asked her how she came to be allowed to.

“A hundred years, near enough. And there are four hundred years of weeks on that wall and every single one of them has a gap in it where a week was not kept, and a gap in a list of weeks is not a mark, it is nothing, and I have spent three years being the one person on this coast who can lay two lists beside each other and say where each of them stops, and in the whole of that there is no column anywhere for a thing that was not kept.

“So a coast where a person can say no and a coast where a thing can be left out without anybody noticing are the same coast, and they have been the same coast for a hundred years, and it has never once occurred to me before this evening that that is a thing a person would have had to put in.

“If that is a copy of somewhere, then somebody stood in front of the somewhere and wrote down the bit about saying no, and then somebody here cut it into a wall in marks because a stroke is what you have. And I would like to know who was standing there. That is all. I have got a fire going and I have got nine uneven columns and a row of eleven things and a grandmother who told me she was not going to guess, and I would like to know who was standing there.”

The shed was quiet, and a man of sixty-one at a bench with his hand on a frame did not turn round, and a man of thirty-one with his weight against a bench had nothing at all to give her and gave her nothing.

And nothing on that coast answered it. Not the System, which had recognised a signature four hundred years old and had said nothing about what it was for. Not a man of sixty-one at a bench. Not a man of thirty-one. Not the four hundred years of weeks on the wall behind her, which is a thing that cannot answer anything at all, and which has a gap in it about every nine years where a week was not kept, and which will be there on that wall in a hundred years with the same gaps in it.

---

At about the ninth bell he went down the coast road and across the flat and the light was going and the chain was up and the sixth crossing was in the middle of nine hundred yards of shingle with the water off it.

A bundle of three sheets of paper had gone up that road two hundred miles on the seventh of the ninth tide, in a cart, with nothing else in it. It is not sealed. It is not in a locked room. It has three women’s names on it and his own at the foot of the third sheet in his own hand, and it is a thing any clerk in that building can ask for and be handed and copy and carry to a man on a shore he has never stood on. He knew all of that on the day he tied the string round it and he knows it now, and it will be just as true on the day a man he will never meet asks for it, and there is no arrangement of this in which it is not.

A penny exercise book was in the inside pocket of his coat with six torn-out pages in it, and the sixth of them had a date on it and the words that it was his and a reason on it, and the reason on it was the one he could have put his hand up to in a room, and it was not the reason, and he had put the page back in with the other five, and that was the whole of what had happened to him in fifty-nine days.

The right of refusal is not restored. It was not restored on this coast and it is not restored by a piece of paper about a wall, and whether it ever was is a question about four hundred years and about a quarter of a sentence that somebody entered a long time ago, which is four hundred miles behind him and not on this coast, and nobody on this coast is going to answer it and nothing on this coast was offered to anybody as its answer.

And a woman of twenty-four went back to a second fire in a shed two miles along a coast and put a bar into it and stood at it, because a second fire cannot be walked away from once it is let in, and she stands at it about six days in seven, and there is a pane on the wall above a bench, three years old, whose third column gives the outer end of that wall as a place, two hundred and sixty-one yards from the post at the inner end, and whose eighth column says the toe stands to the second course for the whole length and to the fifth at the inner end, and there is a pane in a room a mile up this hill whose third column says two hundred and sixty-four and whose eighth says the fourth course for the whole length and does not give the inner end at all, and there is a row of four hundred years of weeks along a wall with gaps in it where nothing was kept, and there are nine uneven columns and a row of eleven marks at the bottom of it and a man of thirty-one who knows what they are and has told nobody and is going to walk down a coast road tomorrow.

The sixth crossing went over. A boy of eleven went over at a penny with a hand-lead in his fist, and a woman of forty-eight wrote it on a slate on her knee under her awning at Wether Cross and was paid nothing and had never once said so, and the chain came up and went into its sockets, and the sea was down on that bar and going out long and soft over about two hundred yards of gravel, the way it does four days in seven and not on the other three, and nobody on that flat thought anything about any of it.

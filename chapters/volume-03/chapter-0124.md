# Chapter 124: What a Fitter Will Not Say

The fourth of the sixth tide is a bar day and the strait is shut, and the sea outside the Reach is standing up in a westerly that came up in the night and has been getting up all morning, and by the third bell the water at the north end was doing the thing it does on the three days a week and on no other day, which is that it goes up the flat and the gravel bar with it and stands over the whole of it and then goes out again, hard and short, with about nine feet of it in the sea outside and nothing between that and the crown of the bar.

Sorrel Gannock took the flat boat off the hard at the fourth bell with Vanner’s lime in her and the towrope coiled in the bow and Pell Cawley’s new ash collar in the bottom, and she put the man off on the bank with the rope and the man was an ordinary carter with an ordinary hand and he had done this before and did it well.

The man with the bad leg went in it because there was nowhere else to be on a bar day. Dell was on the flat with two crossings and would be there until the turn, and there is nothing on nine hundred yards of shingle to talk to, and Cleve was in the yard, and the sea was standing up.

So he sat in the stern of a flat-bottomed boat with a man’s knees against his back and had the ebb go under him at about the eighth bell, and it did what it does.

The soft place in the middle of the bar is two hundred yards of nothing and about nine feet deep and it is the only deep water on the whole of it, and it is a hole the ebb has cut, and on a good day the water in it moves and a flat-bottomed boat goes straight into it and stays. On a bar day the ebb is about an hour and a quarter and it is hard, and it comes out of the hole carrying sand, and it puts the sand in the hole, and for the length of that ebb the hole is not a hole.

That is the whole of it, and it is why the three days a week that nobody will work are the three days a week this boat works, and it is not a rule and it is not a lane and it is a hole filling up with sand for about an hour and a quarter while the wind is in the right quarter.

He put his hand flat on the bottom of the boat at the crown and could feel the sand come under it, in grains, in a wave of them, the way you can feel rain coming on a roof.

And the sea outside was up over the bar at the harbour mouth four hundred yards south of them and the wind was in the west and the standing was coming and there was a smell of weed on it, and about two hundred yards off the north point the whole of the west horizon went white for about a second and a half, and then it did not, and nobody in the boat said anything about it.

The rendering came up on him the way it does on a moving deck. The boards, and the table the size of a table a hand could go on, and the lamp with the wick down, and the window with weather on the outside of it that was not this weather, and four people at the table and one of them about forty-four with her back to him. It was at the height and the distance it had been at since the third of the second month in a city four hundred miles off. It did not move. He could not look away from it with a boat under him and a man’s knees against his back, and it cost him what it costs, and the cold place in his hip did the six seconds it does and took the hand out of his left, and by the time it was over they were across and the boat’s bottom was grinding on hard stuff.

Sorrel put the bow up on the far bank and let the man out with his lime, and took the rope in and coiled it in the bow again, in eleven seconds, without hurry, because there was about half an hour of water to wait for.

“It was hard all the way,” she said. “Not hard the whole of it. Hard from the crown to the far bank. The near hundred yards was soft at the north side and there is a place there about a boat’s length across that will never be hard on the fourth, and I went round it, and I have gone round it eleven years and I will go round it when I am sixty.”

---

The cart’s wheel came off at about the second bell on the shingle above the flat, and it came off the way a wheel comes off, suddenly and all at once and about four seconds late, and the sound of it went a long way over the shingle.

There were about seventy people on the flat on a fourth and the water was up over the channel and in both bays, and nobody saw it, because the only people who saw it were the two in the water and the three men in the yard, and the man in the yard who came out first was a man of fifty-eight with hoop iron in a cart behind him and a bundle of bar steel on the top of it, and he had come up the coast road in the morning with a load and had stopped at the north end to get a collar cut, and he came out of Cawley’s shed at the second bell with a piece of ash in his hand and was across forty feet of hard before the cart had stopped.

He is Ostre Brann and he is fifty-eight and he is a wheelwright and he has his yard two Reaches down this coast, which is nine miles and a bad road, and he is up here twice a season with iron.

Nobody said anything for about ten seconds. The carter got down and looked at it. The rim had come off the nave at the two o’clock and three o’clock and the wheel had gone about a quarter turn and had not gone over, and the cart was standing on three corners on nine hundred yards of shingle with the sea coming in on both sides of it and a chain two hundred yards short of the water.

Brann got down and put the bar steel on the ground and went at it with his hands and said one word, twice, and the word was *sprung*.

“Where do you want me,” said the carter.

“Down there is no good and down there is going to be water in an hour and a bit,” said Brann. “Bring it up the hard and put the disc off it and stand it up, and do not put that iron on the shingle, that iron has been on my cart for two days.” He was already taking the felloes out of his pocket and spreading them on his knee. “Who is this cart off.”

“Mine. It is the lime cart.”

“Whose lime.”

“Ren Tubb’s. It is the third load of the week and it goes up the coast road this afternoon and if it is not up there tonight it is not up there until the seventh, and the seventh is a bar day, and that is the last load before the turn.”

“All right,” said Brann.

---

He did it on a bench in Cawley’s yard in about an hour, in the wind, with the ash collar Cawley had cut for the boat on the bench beside him, and it is the smallest possible physical thing and it takes nine minutes once you know it.

You take a length of line and you lay it across the rim of a wheel at four places, and you see where it touches. A sound wheel touches at four places and the four places are the same distance apart. A wheel that has been sprung and has not been mended since touches at one place and stands off at another, and you can see the gap with your thumb, and you cannot argue with a thumb.

He did it twice, once with the disc on and once with it off, and then he did the other thing, which is put the felloe ends against a flat surface and feel for a crush, and there was one, on a pair of them, about a third of the way along, and the wood had opened about the thickness of a thumbnail and had closed again in a wedge shape, and the shingle was in it.

“Shingle in the wood,” said Brann. “That is four or five stones in the felloe ends, and they are not in there from the road, they are not from the yard, they are from your own flat. Somebody has been over that flat loaded, and going over loaded, and hitting, and hitting on the same place more than once, and every time it hits it closes the wood a little and opens it a little. That wheel is not broken. It is sprung, and it has been sprung for a long time, and it has been sprung for a long time while it has been going over your flat nine times a day in the worst of it, and I would like you to have that in front of you now, because you did not know it and you were doing that with it and you would have gone on doing it.”

---

“A repair is not a date,” said Brann, and he said it to a man four feet off who had not asked him anything, and he said it in the tone of a man who answers that question eleven times a season.

“On the nav — the nave. There is a piece let into it and pegged, and the pegs are standing proud and they are old, and that is the only thing on this wheel that anybody put on it on purpose. It is not mine. The plate is not mine, the pegs are not mine, and the man who put it in is dead or he is out of my trade, and the plate is a good piece of work and whoever did it knew what he was about. That tells you a wheel once came in with a crack in it and a man mended it properly. It does not tell you when, because a man mends a wheel when the money is there and a man does not put a date on the mend.”

“And the mark on the hoop,” said the man with the bad leg. “There is a punch in the iron.”

“There is. And I can tell you whose it is, and it is not mine, and it is a crossed hammer with a nick out of the left arm of it, and that is Ness Tarn’s punch, and Ness has been dead nine years and his forge is a shed with a hole in the roof.” Brann put his thumb on the mark. “Now listen to what a mark is. A mark says who bought the iron. It does not say when the wheel was built, because a wheel keeps its hoop, and a hoop keeps its punch, and a man who buys a new rim off the same smith gets the same mark. It does not say when the wheel went into the water, because the iron does not know anything about water. It says a smith’s name and that is all it says, and a smith’s name is the only thing on this coast that does not change hands and it is worth nothing to you, and I have watched a man in my yard look at a punch mark on a wheel and say *that is the spring* and it is not the spring, it is a hammer.”

“Say what would tell you the spring.”

“Nothing on this wheel,” said Brann. “There is no mark on this wheel that tells you a week. If you brought me the wheel and a lead line and a man in a boat and a day, I could put a line in that channel and tell you what the water is doing to it this week, and that would be a fact about this week and it would tell you nothing whatever about the morning your father was in it, and the two of those are not the same kind of knowing and there is no way on this coast to get from one to the other. You cannot get a date off a wheel. You can get a condition off a wheel and a condition is not a season.”

---

The carter came up off the shingle with the wheel barrowed back into the cart and the disc back on it and the iron bar steel tied back on the top, and he was an ordinary man of thirty-five called Sarl and he had been on that road since he was seventeen, and he was not a mystery and he was not going to be told anything.

“That is going to hold,” he said.

“It is going to hold to the turn,” said Brann. “Do not come down that flat again today with it and I will put a felloes in it tonight and it will be a wheel for another two years and if you hit something with it in that time I will not be surprised and you will not be able to say I did not tell you.”

“All right.”

“You will not be able to say I did not tell you,” said Brann, “and I have been trying to get that sentence into carters for thirty-one years and it has never once been worth anything, and I say it every time anyway, and you may tell the man with the leg that I said it, and he can put it in whatever he is writing about, because it is the only thing in this yard that has a thing in it that nobody can check.”

He went up the road with the iron and got three miles up the coast road before the rain came in off the water and turned the road, and he was two Reaches from his own yard at the turn and it cost him the rest of the afternoon.

The cart went up the coast road at about the fifth bell with a sprung wheel and a bar of iron in it and a load of lime that was a day late, and nothing else on that coast came of it, and by the sixth bell the man who had watched a thumb find a gap in a rim was standing at the top of the lane at Wether Cross with a leg that had gone from the hip down at the second bell on a moving deck and had come back about a minute later and come back worse, and he told nobody, and is not going to.

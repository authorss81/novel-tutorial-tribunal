# Chapter 119: The Fourth Week of a Season

The fourth week of a season is when the quarter goes up, and the quarter is four weeks of passage money written in a hand that has not changed since before Bettany Rook came here, and it is read by the guild's own office in a city two hundred miles up the coast and by nobody else at all.

There were nine sheets of it and about two hundred and eighty figures on them, and on the fourth of the fifth tide she came into the tide office at the first bell and put them on the counter in a stack, and Jory Vance was already there with a brush in his cuff because the quarter goes up on the fourth and it always has.

Then she stood on the other side of the counter and did not open the first one.

“Right,” she said. “There is a thing to say and then there are four hours of work, and I would be obliged if you let me get to the work first.”

“All right.”

“I have two seasons of sight in this eye and I have had them since the spring, and I have told nobody in this building, and I am telling you because you are the only one who can do the other half of this and I am not going to stand in front of nine people and be asked questions about it.” She put her hand flat on the top sheet. “I can read a big number on a good day. I cannot read a small one. I have been reading them for six years by leaning, and leaning is not a thing a person can do for four hours, and I did it for three on the second of the tide and I could not do the last page, and I did it anyway, and it took me until the ninth bell and I do not know what I put on it.”

Jory Vance looked at the stack for a moment.

“Then I will read them to you and you will say the figures and I will write them.”

“No. You will not do that, because you do not know my shorthand and there are eleven things in it that are not money, and if you write a number from my mouth in your own hand then in a year a clerk two hundred miles up this coast is going to read a figure I did not mean.” She drew the stack across the counter to her. “I will read them and you will write them. It will take four hours. If I lose a line you will not know, and I shall know, and I shall tell you, and we shall do the line again. And you will not look at me while I am reading, and you will not help me, and I want that in front of us now rather than in the middle of the fourth hour.”

“All right,” said Jory Vance, and got a fresh pen, and did not look at her, and it was four hours and about forty minutes.

The room had four people in it and nine or ten came and went over the four hours, and nobody asked her one question, and about half of them counted. Nobody on this coast can work out how she does it. A woman of fifty-one reading a column of two hundred figures and saying the odd one out loud and the even one silently, and getting to the bottom of a page and going back up it, and starting a page again, is not a thing that is about her eyes. It looks exactly like a thing that is about her hands.

And at about the seventh bell her hands were not doing it.

“Stop,” she said. “Give me the pen for a minute.”

“You have the pen.”

“Then stop reading and do not look at me,” said Bettany Rook, “and go and stand at the north wall for about a minute, and when you come back we will do the ninth sheet, which is the last one, and we will do it before the office fills up, because I would rather be slow in front of four people than fast in front of nine.”

---

The order for the fifth pane came back up the counter that same afternoon on the season's own paper, with the date struck out in a clerk's hand and no figure against it at all.

She read it in about four seconds, standing up, and then she stood with it for about as long as a person stands with a thing.

“Right,” she said. “Then the year is a year off and it is settled, and I did not settle it and the council did not settle it and nobody in this building asked the council about it, and there is a pane on that wall that cannot be changed until a letting day a year off that I have now got written down in a clerk's hand in the middle of the word *ordered*, and it will be read by a man in a city in about a month, and he will not know what it means either.”

“Do you want it entered?”

“No,” said Bettany Rook. “If it is entered it is a thing this office has decided about, and this office has not decided about anything. If it is not entered it is a piece of paper about a piece of glass, and in a year a man in a shed will put a batch in an arch and the council will be told afterwards if anybody tells it.” She squared the sheet up. “Put it in the drawer with the other orders and write the date on the outside of the drawer, because I am going to forget, and because the day I forget is the day the fifth pane does not happen at all, and that is a worse sentence than the one in the charter.”

---

He put it to her in the yard at the sixth bell, in about four minutes, and it is the only thing he did on this coast that was entirely his own idea and entirely useless, and he did it because he had been carrying it since the sixth day of the third tide and because a woman of fifty-one had stood at a counter on the first day of a tide and told him what was on a wall in her own building and had never once asked him for anything in return.

“There is a pane at Thornglass,” he said. “Not the guild's. His own. He made it three years ago and hung it on the wall of his own shed because yours does not come out of the room, and he and the guild's were fired about sixteen years apart, and there are eleven courses on both of them and the same brass and the same hand on the ruling, and two columns out of the eleven do not agree. The third and the eighth. The two that record a place and not a depth.”

“He told you that.”

“He told me that. He has not told anybody else on this coast. He has had it on his wall for about two years and he cannot get at yours to put them side by side, and he cannot be sure his own is right, because a channel moves, and he has been out on the bar twice this year with a boat and a line and he knows which of them he believes and there is no instrument here that would say so in a room.”

Bettany Rook did not answer for some time.

“No,” she said.

“I have not finished.”

“You have finished, and I have listened to all of it, and the answer is no, and I am going to give you the four reasons and then you are going to go and do something else for the rest of the day.

“First. His is three years old and mine is three months old, and yours — the charter — is nineteen years and eight months, and all three of them are photographs. A pane is a man standing beside an arch on one morning with the weather he got. The third and the eighth columns record a place and a place that has moved is a place that has moved since somebody wrote it down, and that is not a disagreement between two men. It is the channel doing what the channel does. If you could put them side by side in a room tomorrow it would tell you what the water was doing in two mornings and it would not tell you which of the two men is right, and I have been on that water for nineteen years and I will tell you the answer to that question in about four words and the answer is *both*.”

“You do not think it matters.”

“I think it matters enormously and it cannot be settled, and those are two different things and the council in that room yesterday proved it for four hours with about eleven shillings, and they were not wrong.” She turned round. “Second. The guild's people are right about the water in their hands. That is the whole of the guild and it is a good thing to be. There is nobody on this coast who has been in the channel and been wrong and come back, and I have never had to argue that with anybody and I am not going to start with a man off a road about a column of glass.

“Third. The moment I take this to the council, I am a woman saying the guild's own record is bad. And a record that is bad is a record that has to be replaced, and a replacement is a year, and the charter is already a year and the tide table will be a year behind before the arch is cold again. And I have a year of sight left in this eye. So the arithmetic of that is that I spend my last year of reading putting a thing in front of a council that cannot act on it until the water has moved again and made the question a different question.”

“Which brings me to the last one.”

“It brings me to the last one and here is the last one, and then I am going inside, and it is this: this office is the only office on this coast that anybody can be answered in, and if I go into a room and say the record is bad, then in about four months the guild will have got a perfectly good reason to put the harbourmaster of Kestrel Reach at a counter for the remainder of her sight, and they will be right to, and I will be the woman who gave them the reason.” She stopped. “There are about nine hundred people on this water and every one of them comes to a person. Not to a bench and not to a court and not to a guild. To a person. I am that person and I have been it for six years and I have never once been sure it was a good idea and I have never once had an alternative, and I will not hand in the alternative in a yard to a man with a bad leg because he has been three weeks on this coast and has been careful with me.”

He had nothing for that and it took him the best part of ten seconds to find nothing for it and those seconds were the worst of the whole three weeks.

“You are right,” he said. “And there is one more thing I have been carrying since the first day and I am going to say it now because you have just refused me twice and I would rather be refused on something I chose than go on holding it.”

“Say it.”

“The guild holds the only legible copy of the charter because it pays for the only copy of the charter.” He said it flat and he said it to a person and not to a room. “Not because it built it. Not because it holds it. Because it is the customer. And the customer owns the copy, and no living soul on this coast can ask that a copy of anything be made, and everybody who has needed one in three hundred years has been told that a pane cannot be copied.”

The yard was quiet. Inside the building a pen was going on a sheet and it did not stop.

“That is not mine and it is not yours either,” said Bettany Rook. “You have been on this road three weeks. Whoever taught you to say that has not been on it. Go and stand at the far end of something.”

He went and stood at the far end of the flat for about an hour, with a leg that had a whole of her voice in it out of a city four hundred miles off, and did not get it back for about a minute, and then got it back worse, and nobody on that coast was told and nobody on that coast is going to be.

---

Danno Ray had a cart at the Quay at about the seventh bell and a load of lime that was not going anywhere for two hours, and he came down the lane and put the beasts in against the wall of the lamp oil shed and stood with his back on the stones where he can see the whole of the flat, which is what he does now when he has a minute. Then he said it to nobody, in a voice that carried about as far as a man carrying a voice carries, and there were nine people there.

He said: “I have been on that road thirty-one years and I have hauled over the low road at the top of this coast in the turn of the year in nine different years and I have worked out the three days out of seven when the water owns the bar and I have never once written it down and I never will, and the reason is not that I do not know.

“Here is why it cannot be put anywhere. You cannot put it in the charter, and the charter is a pane and a pane is a year and the year is not ours. You cannot put it in a song because a song is a thing people agree on and about half of them would get it wrong, and that is not a thing you build, it is a thing that happens to you. You cannot put it in a book, because a book goes. You cannot put it in a return, because a return is a number and there is no column for it. And you cannot put it in a man, because a man is seventy-one and paints a staff when he feels like it and has never been paid a penny of it in nineteen years.

“So it sits in about nine heads on this coast and nowhere else, and every one of those nine heads is on its way out. I am forty-five. That is the whole of the difficulty and there is no office for it.

“A rule that cannot be changed for a year is not a rule. It is a season. And a season is a thing that happens to you and a rule is a thing that a person can be told, and the guild has got one of them and calls it the other one, and I say that as a man who has been under both.”

Corrie Tallow was forty yards off under her awning with the chalkboard face out to the weather, and had not looked up, because a woman of forty-eight on this coast has learned in four years exactly how far a sentence like that carries and what it costs to be the woman who answers one. Bettany Rook was up the lane with a stack of nine sheets and about four hours of work still in them and had not heard a word of it. Seth Garrick was at his shed door with a hammer in his hand. There were nine people on that shingle and not one of them answered him, and one of them was a man of thirty-one who had spent three weeks finding out that the hardest thing about this coast is not a rule and not a season and not a pane, and is a sentence said out loud in the open on a day when nine people happen to be standing there.

---

The System put the order back in front of Ilyan Vester at about the ninth bell of the fifth day of the fifth tide, on the far side of nine hundred yards of dry shingle, and what it gave him was the third thing on this coast that was a record of a thing nobody had entered anywhere in particular.

**THE GUILD'S WATER AND PASSAGE CHARTER IS ONE PANE IN THE TIDE OFFICE AT KESTREL REACH AND IT IS NINETEEN YEARS AND EIGHT MONTHS OLD. THE GUILD'S OWN TIDE TABLE IS THE OTHER PANE AND IS ABOUT THREE MONTHS OLD. A PANE IS A PICTURE OF ONE MORNING TAKEN BY A MAN STANDING BESIDE AN ARCH, AND A CHANNEL MOVES, AND WHAT IS OLD ON THIS COAST IS THE RECORD AND NOT THE HARBOURMASTER. THE ORDER FOR A FIFTH PANE CAME BACK UP THE COUNTER THIS WEEK WITH ITS DATE STRUCK OUT AND NO FIGURE AGAINST IT, AND IT WAS ENTERED IN A DRAWER AND NOT IN A MINUTE BOOK, AND NOBODY HAD ASKED THE COUNCIL FOR EITHER.**
**WHAT IS NOT DECIDED: WHETHER A RULE THAT CANNOT BE CHANGED FOR A YEAR IS A RULE OR A SEASON. IT WAS SAID OUT LOUD ON A SHINGLE BY A MAN WITH A CART AND NOBODY ANSWERED HIM.**

The water came off the flat at about the eighth bell and went away again and left the nine hundred yards of it darker than the rest, and the chain stayed where it was, and he stood on the far side of it for about a quarter of an hour after the nine people had gone, on a leg that was carrying a hand of forty-four's weight out of a city four hundred miles behind him, and did not get it back for about a minute, and then got it back worse, and told nobody, and is not going to.

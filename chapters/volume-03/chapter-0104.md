# Chapter 104: Three Hours and Nine Miles of Water

Dell Gannock was forty-two and he was the eldest of three brothers and the only one of them who had a boat with a wheel, and he took a stranger across the Reach on the second of the tide for fourpence and a bag of tobacco he does not smoke, and he put the bag in the forepeak afterwards where it was still going to be in twenty years.

There are four generations of the Gannocks on this water. There are three of them on it now. Dell has the wheel. Sorrel, thirty-six, has the flat boat and the towrope and takes people across the shallow on the fourth and sixth and seventh when nobody who knows better will go. Cleve, thirty-one, is a dealer in what the flat boat brings back and does not go on the water at all, and has a house with a good roof on it, and the whole coast knows about that roof and none of them says anything in front of his mother.

Ilyan gave Dell Gannock eleven shillings of the money he had and got fourpence of it back and a tin cup of something hot out of a can with the fire built into the side of it, and sat down on a thwart that was not there for sitting on.

“Nine miles,” said Dell Gannock, “and three hours if the tide allows and not one minute more than that, and I will tell you the three things that will kill you on this water, and none of them is the water. The first is the turn in the middle of it, which comes on without any warning you can feel from where you are sitting, and she leans about two inches to port and stays there for about four minutes. The second is the cold under the third strake, which is not cold. The third is that you will want to go to the rail, and there is no rail on this side, because there has never been anything to lean on there, and the reason there has never been anything to lean on there is that nothing on this water has ever come back needing to be leaned on.”

“Yes,” said Ilyan.

“You have said that to three people this week and I am the third and I have not asked you a single question about it,” said Dell Gannock, and put the wheel over, and did not ask.

The leg went in about the second of the three hours and it went in the way it went in, from the hip down, and the low water came up into his nose in two layers — the day over the night, and the flat over the water — and under both of them came the third, which was a room with boards and a table the size of a table a hand could go on and a lamp with the wick down and a window with weather on the outside of it that is not this weather, and four people at the table, one of them about forty-four with her back to him.

The room was at the same height and the same distance it had been at since the third of the second month in a city four hundred miles off. He had not gone looking for it and he was not going to, and on a moving deck on a channel he did not know he was not going to be able to look away from it, and it sat where it had always sat and did not move and cost him what it cost.

Half an hour on he got his left hand round a line without meaning to and it went, because the base of the left thumb is a scar and has been for four years, and the line came half a hand's length through his fist and he had it in about a second and the coil went over the side and Dell Gannock took it out of the water without a word and coiled it and put it down by the bitt, and gave him a length of manila to hold instead, which is the right thing to hold, and which is not a rope.

“You cannot grip,” said Dell Gannock, at about the third of the three hours.

“No.”

“That is the fourth thing then, and I will not ask that either.” He put his thumb on the manila. “My mother keeps the crossing on the bar road above the north end, and she has kept it eight years, and she is eighty-one, and it is a good crossing and it is not on any sheet anybody in the guild has, and you may tell the guild I said that and I do not care.”

---

Slackwater Reach is two Reaches down the water and it is poorer, and it is not a village, it is about four hundred people in low houses along a creek, and it has its own assembly of thirty-one seats that meets in a net loft on the first and the third of every tide, and it has had one since before the guild's charter was recopied, and it does not call itself anything but the assembly and it does not print anything and nobody in it has ever sent a page to anybody.

The thing that strikes a man from a city about Slackwater is not the poverty. It is that the argument is *old*. In four hundred miles he had heard a district say that a thing it could not pay for it would do itself about forty times. He had not once heard a body ask, before anything else, what a thing was going to cost in days.

“Eleven households,” he said, to about nine of them, in the loft, on the first bell of the third.

He had it on two sheets of a penny exercise book that Corrie Tallow had torn out and given him, and they were folded in with a sheet of writing paper from a city that had a question on it in one hand, and he carried the two of them in the same pocket and had not thought about that.

“Eleven households on that road send somebody over that flat. One of them sends him every crossable tide of five days, which is thirty-nine tides at a penny, which is three shillings and threepence a week and eight pounds nine a year. The other ten are threepence a week between them. It is thirteen times, and I did not know that was the number anybody was after until I had it written down on the back of a stall board.”

He said it in the same words both times, which was a habit from a city and which he noticed about halfway through the second telling and did not stop for.

Nobody argued with him about the number of tides. That was the thing he was not ready for. Nobody in that room had any opinion at all about the arithmetic, and two of them checked it, and out loud, and it was right.

“It is the same penny on every day of the tide,” he said. “The fourth, the sixth and the seventh are charged the same as the others, and on those three days the flat is crossable nine times instead of six because every cart has to come round, and so the days the water is worst are charged exactly what the days it is best are charged, and the guild will not write the word *dangerous* on a tariff, and I have been told the reason and the reason is that a tariff that says dangerous is a tariff that can be refused.”

Somebody at the back said, “Aye. And a man who refuses a tariff is a man you have to argue with on a bad day. That is a good reason. I have never heard it said out loud before.”

---

It took him two days to find out that the number was the whole of it, and he found that out from a woman who came in at the back of the second telling and stood there until the end and then said one thing.

Efa Lund is thirty-four and keeps the shore's own count of what crosses the flat and the creek and has kept it eleven years in a book of her own. She is not a clerk and is paid nothing and was not asked.

“Seventeen,” she said.

“Eleven is what the woman who keeps the stall has written down.”

“Yes, and I have not said it is wrong,” said Efa Lund. “I have got seventeen and she has got eleven and both of us are counting the same water and we are counting it for different ends.”

“There is one flat.”

“There is one flat and there are two ends of it,” she said. “She is counting the households that have to send somebody across it, because the tariff is a penny a tide and the question is who is paying the penny. And I am counting the people who cross it, because the question where I come from is how many bodies are on that sand at the turn, and I have wanted to know that since I was a girl in my mother's house and my mother did the same and got no pay for it either. And there are six more of them than she has and not one of the six is a lie. Three come down the shore road from the east end on market days and go over and come back, and she does not know they are there because she is watching her own eleven doors and I am watching the sand. And one of her eleven is two houses and has been two houses since the spring, and it pays for both and counts for one, and there is no column on that flat for half a house. And two of the six are a man and his wife who went round by the road in the winter, found the road cheaper than a boy, and came back in the spring, and they are on that sand every tide of the week and they have never been on her list, because nobody ever told her they had been away.”

“So it is eleven and it is seventeen.”

“It is eleven households and seventeen people’s worth of crossing and they are not the same claim and you will not be able to hear the difference when you say it in a room, and you have said it twice in a room today, and I have been in this loft a long time and there are only two kinds of trouble and the other kind is a number that comes back wrong.” She shut her book. “I am not saying you have done anything. I am saying that the sentence you are carrying is about households, and if it comes back here as seventeen then in four months somebody at that counter will look at a return and see a difference and be pleased about it, and that is how a rate stops being a rate and becomes a thing people are caught out on.”

He wrote it down. He wrote it on the back of the torn-out page, under the count, in the space left over, which was a space of about two inches and was not enough.

---

Pether Voke was fifty-four and he had been a ferryman's mate for thirty-one years and he spoke for the Slackwater assembly, and he was the speaker because the assembly picks its speaker for a year and he had been picked four times and had said he would not stand again after the third and had stood again because nobody else would, which everybody in the loft knew and nobody said.

He sat at the front with his hands on the edge of a trestle and he did not interrupt once, and when Ilyan Vester had finished the second telling he let about a minute go by, and then he said:

“Right. Two things, and I will tell you both now so that you can plan your day around them.

“The first is that you are right. All of it. I have got no quarrel with the arithmetic and neither has anybody in this room and we have all been thinking about the same tariff for about nine years and nobody could put the two ends of it on one board, and you have done it in an afternoon, and I would like that said where people can hear it.

“The second is that I have to take it to the assembly on the third, and you have to say it again in front of thirty-one people who did not hear you in a loft with nine in it, and then I have to say whether we are in it or we are not. And that is not mine to decide. I have been the speaker four times and I have a good hand for a room and I have used it, and I am telling you that I do not know, and I have not decided, and I will not pretend to you that I have, because you are the first person from a road in four hundred miles who has stood in this loft and it would be cheap of me to tell you what you want to hear about your own proposal in order to get a whole day of it out of you.”

He put his hand flat on the trestle.

“You want the flat and the strait in one matter,” he said. “That is what all of it was for, and I can see the shape of it and it is a good shape, and I have been the shape of it before. Here is what it costs. The moment this assembly is a party to anything, it is served. Serving is by hand, up a coast road, and a serving is a day of somebody’s time and a day of a cart, and the somebody is me, because I am the only person in this Reach who can be served and read it to the assembly without being laughed at. And I have been served four times in thirty-one years and I can tell you exactly what happens on the four occasions. The first one we paid. The second one we paid. The third one a woman of this assembly went out and got her hand crushed on a bollard at the water stairs carrying the papers, and the papers went into the creek, and we had them sworn again out of her own memory, which we were allowed to do and did, and the hand took nine days and she is here and it is in the book. The fourth one we paid and got nothing for it in return and I have never worked out why.”

He stopped, and it was not for effect, and then he said the last of it standing up, because he was a man who said the last of a thing on his feet.

“Four times in thirty-one years is not a rate,” he said. “It is a habit, and a habit of this Reach is that when a body somewhere puts us in a matter, the price is a day off a boat, and the boat is mine, and I have four or five days in a good week and a boat that is holed above the waterline if I lay her up one day longer than I mean to, and I will be fifty-five in the spring.”

He sat back down.

“So I have not decided,” said Pether Voke, “and I will say that I have not decided in front of the assembly, in the open, with the whole of the room, and that nobody is to say that anybody was talked out of anything, and that if the answer is no then the answer is no and I will be the one who says it and not you and not the man from the road, and I would like that written on whatever it is you people write on.”

---

At the end, on the steps, Efa Lund had a word with him, and she was the only one who came out.

“Your toll man,” she said. “At your own crossing, on the fourth of the tide, in the channel. Dell Gannock's mother. Alma. Eighty-one. She has taken that toll eight years and there is a form at that gate with a line on it and her name at the end of the line, and the man who filled that form in could not read, and everybody knows it, and nobody has ever gone and asked her one question about it, including me, and I have walked past that gate four times a week for eleven years.”

“What is on the form?”

“That is exactly the question,” said Efa Lund. “Nobody knows. It is a piece of paper in a box in a guild house and there is a copy in the tide office and neither of them is anywhere anybody in this Reach could be got to look, and I have wanted to know for about two years, and I have never once asked, because she is eighty-one and it works.”

“Why does it work?”

“Because four carts go over that bar road on a good week and it has never been shut and nobody has ever gone round it,” said Efa Lund. “If you go and ask her, one of two things happens, and I cannot tell you which. Either it is all in order, and you have spent a day of your own on a piece of paper, and she has had a strange day for nothing. Or it is not in order, and you have put an eighty-one-year-old woman in front of a guild that does not have a desk for her, and you will not be able to undo it, and I am not going to be the one who did it to her.”

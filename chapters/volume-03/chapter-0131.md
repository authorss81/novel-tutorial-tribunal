# Chapter 131: The One Question

The fourth of the seventh tide is a bar day and the flat takes nine, and the sea outside has been standing up since the middle of the night behind a westerly that came up the coast with the rain still in it, and by the fourth bell the water was over the crown of the bar and the near end of that flat had a channel in it with perhaps a cart’s width of good dry ground either side and about a foot of running water in the middle of the channel, which on a good day is a mile of shingle with a dog on it.

Wend Garrow had his load at the head of the flat by the second bell. He had it there because on a bar day there is no other road for it, and there is more of it, not less: every cart on this coast that has to come round this coast comes over nine hundred yards of shingle instead of going up above the harbour by a road a loaded cart does not care for, and by the fourth bell there were seven carts on that flat and a cart at the head of it with stone on it that had been on the wrong side of the water since the sixth of a tide five days ago.

Two men were on the line because that is what a fourth is for. Nine crossings go, one after another, from the first bell to the ninth, and the chain goes up and comes down nine times, and between the fifth and the seventh on an ordinary sixth the chain rests an hour while a man eats.

The guild’s own works were finishing at the far end of that water, four men on the last of the nine yards of sea wall, and the stone for it had gone over on the third of that tide and it had been two days since, and a man who lays a wall knows that a wall does not wait and so the stone had come on the second ebb and not the first and the two days were on the far side and everybody who had hired that stone had stopped looking at their own watch.

And the tally post was there at the crossing with its chain up, and the board was on the front of it, and it is a bar day and there were seven carts on that flat and every one of them was more urgent than a board.

At the turn Wend Garrow went up the shingle to the head of the flat and put his beasts’ heads up about a foot and waited, because the water was coming over the bar and was going to keep coming for another hour and a half, and everybody on that coast knows what a fourth does and none of them will stand on a stone early.

The stone is four feet of flat stone at the head of the flat where the lane comes down onto the shingle. It is flat. It has been flat for a hundred years or about that. Nobody on this coast knows who cut it or why, and there is nothing on it, and it has no chain and no mark and no number and nobody’s name anywhere near it, and it is the only thing on four hundred miles of this coast that a person can stand on and be answered.

She was already on it.

She is Bettany Rook and she is fifty-one and she is the harbourmaster of Kestrel Reach, and she has been in that office nineteen years and six of them in the chair, and she came down the hill about half an hour before the turn on the fourth and on the sixth, which is how anybody on that flat knows at all, and stood on the stone and waited with her hands behind her.

How long she has done that, and how long she means to go on doing it, and whether anybody in a building a mile and a flight of stairs up the hill from that crossing knows she does it, are three things that nobody standing on that shingle has ever asked her about, and he had been on this coast forty days and had not asked her either.

Wend Garrow put the cart in and got up on the stones and stood on the flat part with his hand on the beasts and looked at her.

He said it the way a man says a thing he has said to himself about two hundred times.

“What is the water going to do.”

And she answered him, out loud, in front of about seventy people, and she did not hedge, and she did not ask him for anything, and nobody on that shingle had to lean.

“Take your cart at the near stones and not in the channel. You will lose nothing and the stones are better going than the sand has any business being in a draught.

”The ebb that will carry you comes off the far side and not the near one, and you will know it when the water at the far stones starts to go brown, and that will be about the quarter past the turn, and you want to be on the middle of it and not the start of it. At the start of it the sand is coming up and the channel is still full of the last tide, and a cart that gets on the start of an ebb on this flat is a cart that stops, and everybody on this shingle has watched that happen to somebody.

”You will have the best part of two hours of it. You will be over in about three quarters of an hour if your beasts are used to a flat and about an hour if they are not, and you will be on dry shingle at the far side at the end of it and the chain will be behind you and nobody will have said one word to you.

”That is true tomorrow. And it is true the day after. And on the sixth the water will be back over the crown of the bar and you will not be going, and the fourth of next tide you can go and it will be a longer one than this one, because the wind will have had three days to get up. Take the middle of that one as well and do not listen to anybody who tells you different, because I will be standing on this stone and I will say it again.”

Nobody asked her a second question. There was not time and there was not anybody who would have been allowed to, and the custom is about a hundred years old and is written down nowhere, and the guild has never interfered with it and never will, and about nine people on this coast have worked out in the last year that the song and this are two customs and not one.

That was the whole of it and it took about two minutes, and after it there was a gap on that shingle of the kind there always is after a thing like that, and into the gap, from the far stones, about forty yards off, a woman of forty-eight said one sentence at half the volume she used to say things at.

“He is going to go,” said Corrie Tallow, to nobody. “I have watched that one look at a bar for a day and a half and I have watched him not ask anybody anything in twenty-four years and I would give a year of the stall to have heard him ask that.”

Hal Bevan was on the chain at the near end and had heard the whole of it, and Seth Garrick was at the head of the lane where the oil shed is and had come out and stood with his back on his own door, and Sorrel Gannock had come up off the hard four hundred yards down the flat with a coil of rope on her shoulder to see what the shouting was about, and went back down when she found out.

And then Wend Garrow said thank you.

He said it out loud, on the shingle, in front of seventy people, in the ordinary voice, and it took about a second and a half.

He said it because he had never said it to anybody in his life and there had never been a place to say it in, and because a man in a lamp-oil shed had told him about this stone when he had no reason to, and because a day and a half of his own and two men paid for a day and a day late with a wall had come out of it, and because the water was going to go brown at the far stones in about a quarter of an hour and after that nothing on that coast would remember any of it.

Nine people heard him say it clearly. About four of them found somewhere else to look. Nobody stopped him and nobody told him not to and nobody said it was not done.

Ilyan Vester was standing about thirty feet off with his weight on his good side and he had been standing about thirty feet off for most of a month, and when the carter said thank you he worked out something that took him a moment to get to and that he did not put anywhere afterwards.

There was nowhere on this coast to put what had just happened.

Not as a decision. Nobody had decided anything. It was simply that there was no form for it and no second door and nobody to take it to, and the one man on that shingle who had been done a wrong by a piece of paper could not put it anywhere at all, and had said it out loud on the shingle instead, and had been listened to by nobody who would act on it, and the whole of that was not anybody’s fault and was on the ground at ten in the morning with the sea over the bar.

He had four pages in a penny exercise book in his coat with a date on them and that they were his, and no reason on any of the four, and he had been carrying the fourth one since a walk up a coast road. He could not have put this on a fifth if he had wanted to. There was no fifth page that would hold a thing that was not his, that was not a record, and that was about the water, and the water is the one thing on four hundred miles of this coast that has never once been written down, because it does not stay put long enough to be worth the trouble.

Then the leg went.

It went at the turn, at the moment the man said thank you, and it went the way it goes — from the hip down, all of it, in about four seconds — and he stayed exactly where he was on nine hundred yards of wet shingle because moving was worse, and it stayed gone for the best part of two hundred yards of standing still and came back about a minute after the carter had got up onto the stones, worse, the way it comes back.

About seventy people on that flat and not one of them said a word to him about it, then or afterwards, and four hundred miles of this coast does that to a man it has no standing over, and that was the fourth time since the fourth of the sixth tide, on a bar day, in front of seventy people, and it was going to keep on doing it.

He worked out afterwards that a true answer on that stone was worth, to him, exactly the difference between a day and a half of a man and two men paid for a day and a cart of stone standing on the wrong side of nine hundred yards of shingle, and to the twenty-nine other people on that flat it was worth a very good morning’s watching, and to the woman standing on the stone it was worth whatever the water was going to do, which she had already said and which was the whole of what anybody had come for.

He could not work out what it had been worth to her, and he did not know how to ask, and that was the first thing on this coast that had put him in a place he could not get out of by doing his job in it.

At the fifth bell the water at the far stones went brown, and the cart went out on the ebb with the beasts’ heads up and a man on each side of the boards and its driver standing on the tail-board with a bar, and it was over in about fifty minutes, and the chain came up behind it, and the man on the chain did not say anything to it at all.

The load got to the wall at the north end at about the ninth bell, a day and a half later than the man who had hired it had reckoned, and nobody on this coast added that up either.

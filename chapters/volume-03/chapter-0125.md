# Chapter 125: A Pane Is a Person, a Fire, and a Year

The arch at Thornglass is cold on the fifth of the sixth tide and it will be cold for a year, and behind it there is two feet of brick, and the only fire in that shed on the fifth of a tide is the second one, which is a hole in the end wall with an iron plate in front of it and a mouth you can get your head into, and it is where the small work goes: a weight, a stop, a lens, a pane of window glass for a man who has a house with a hole in it.

Sabra Trew was scraping the floor of it out with a bar and a piece of flat iron, and she does the second fire on her own for about six days in seven, and the reason she does it on her own is that the second fire cannot be left once it has been let in. If it is banked for an hour the glass in it goes cloudy and cloudy is not a thing you can mend. So somebody stands at it.

She is twenty-four and she is in her third year and she is paid nothing, and her back is the shape of a person who has been leaning over a low fire for three years, and she has been like that since about the middle of her second year and it is not going to get better in a fourth.

The man with the bad leg came up the coast road at the second bell because there was nothing else to do with a day, and he had been at that arch for four hours on the fourth of the third tide carrying a bar out of a fire and putting it back forty times, and his leg had been his own for the length of it, and he had never once mentioned that to anybody on this coast and was not going to start.

He came in and sat down on a crate by the warm wall and she looked at him and said nothing about it, which is what everybody in that shed does about that leg, which is not a kindness and is a habit.

---

She told him the price because he had been in the yard when the old man said the words *a person, a fire, and a year* to a man with a good coat, and because she had been the person in that sentence for three years and nobody had ever asked her what it was.

“A pane is eleven months,” she said. “You put a batch in on a letting day a year before, and the glass is turned about forty times over the eleven months, and every turn is a fire and a half and somebody standing beside it with their hands on iron, and at the end of it there is one day the glass will come out of the arch without being broken by the change in the air. About one a year will take a pane that size. You find it a year ahead the way you find a day the tide is lowest — by a rule, and then by the sea coming in and not agreeing with the rule.”

“How many are there in a year.”

“One that will take a pane that size. One. And it is chosen by the tide and not by the man, and if the money is not in the arch on the right day then there is no batch and the next one is a year after it.” She set the bar down and got up off her heels, which took her a moment. “So there is a price for it and the price is a person for eleven months, and the person is whoever is here, and there is one apprentice in this shed and I am twenty-four and I have been here three years and I have been paid nothing for three years.”

“That is the whole of the trade.”

“That is the whole of the trade and I have never once said it out loud to anybody and you are a man off a road.” She said it without any weight on it, the way a person says the price of a thing in a shop. “And here is the part I have not said to anybody, so I will say it to you because you cannot do anything with it. The guild has paid it twice already and got a pane twice and I was not the one who paid either of those, and both of them are on a wall in a room a mile up the hill from here and I have never been up it. The tide table on that wall is three months old and it came out of this arch three months ago and three months of it is three months of me with my hands on that bar. The charter is nineteen years and eight months old and it came out of the arch when I was five years old, and the woman who stood at that fire for it was called Mare and she is dead, and her name is inside the lid of the batch book because that is how you keep a name, and it is the only name in that book that is not mine and I put it there myself when I came.”

“And the fifth one.”

“The fifth one is the one they ordered, and there is no batch in my arch for it, and there has not been a batch since the spring.” She looked at the cold arch through the brick. “And if it came in the winter with the money in the same post, then the year would start in the winter with me in the arch on the first of it, and it would be eleven months of my back before anybody in that guild house knew whether there was going to be a pane at the end of it. And that is how it works, and it is not anybody being cruel, and it has been done to me twice already, and I have been paid nothing for it, and the paying is a separate matter and does not go the same way as the year.”

Hollis Thrale had come in at the yard door some time before that and had been standing on the other side of the second fire, on his deaf side, and had not turned round for any of it, because he cannot hear a voice on his right and has not been able to since he was a boy, and it is not a thing that happened to him in one place: his father could not, and his father’s father could not, and the kiln was built in the year of the bad seventh by a man who worked one-handed because of it, and nobody in that family has ever been asked about it, and the whole of the family knows.

She had not once turned round while she was saying it, and she had raised her voice by about a third on the last three sentences without appearing to notice she was doing it, which is what you do after eleven years.

---

The old man put the bar down on the anvil and came and stood at the end of the bench, and the bench is a plank about twenty feet long with a low fire under one end of it and it is where everything in that shed is written, and what was on it that afternoon was his own.

His own pane’s notes, in his hand, going back years, in a hand that is not ruled and is not in columns because a man does not rule his own. The batch book, closed. The yard book, closed. And Bettany Rook’s sheet, which had come up the coast road in a cart with a man buying lamp oil, and which he had read three times and had not given back and had said out loud that he had about four minutes’ work of it and did not intend to return it.

“No,” he said. “There is nothing of theirs in this room and there is nothing of mine either that a body in the guild house will ever act on, and that is the whole of the difference between this bench and that room and it is not a small thing and it is not a large one. On that wall, a mile up the hill, there is a thing that came out of this arch and nobody may go and look at it. On this bench there is a thing that comes out of this arch and nobody can take it away. Both of those are the same record and only one of them is a record.”

“The sheet,” said the man with the bad leg.

“The sheet is the fourth time in a month that a person has carried a true sentence from one end of this coast to the other and had it come back with a word in it that nobody said, and I am not going to tell you whose, because it is a woman’s and she is not in this room and it is not my business to put her in it.”

“That is fair.”

“It is more than fair, it is the only rule in that shed.” He put his hand flat on the bench. “Now. You have come up that road twice in three days and you have not once asked me for anything, and every man in this shed has noticed that, and I have noticed it, and I am going to work out what you would have to ask me for, out loud, because I have got to know whether I would say yes or no before you ask, and because you will ask, and because a man who does not know what he would say is a man who gets himself into a position.”

---

He said it at the bench, standing up, and he said it as work, not as an argument.

“If I am to settle the third and eighth columns, here is what a man would want. He would want to know what the water did in those two places, and not what somebody wrote down about it, and not what a photograph of one morning says.” He held up a hand and put one finger up against each thing in turn. “A lead line. A boat. A man who can sound. And a day — a day when the sea is doing whatever it is going to do that week, and not a day somebody remembers. Four things. That is a measurement. It does not last, it does not go on a wall, it does not come out brown in twenty years, and in four months it is a thing in a man’s notebook that he swears to in a room if anybody ever wants him to. And it is the only instrument on this coast that is not a pane, and it is the only one of the two of them that will still be true in ten years, because a channel that has moved will move again and a line is a line.”

“And to ask for it.”

“To ask for it, I write to that guild.” He did not raise his voice; he did not need to and he was used to not needing to. “I write and I say: I have a disagreement with a table on your wall in two columns and I will put a boat and a lead line and a day against it and I will give you the numbers, and I will not put them in glass. And then they will want to know what I want in return, because that is what a guild does with a letter, and there is one thing they can give me, and it is a year. It is the only thing they can give anybody, and it is a year of whoever stands at that fire, and it would not be me. It would be the girl, because I am sixty-one and I would not be let out of my own arch on my own.”

“So the asking is a year.”

“The asking is a year, and the year is not mine to spend, and I have known that since about half past eight this morning and I have been standing here since the second bell working out how to ask for four things that cost a boat and a day, and I worked it out at about the eleventh bell, and here is what I worked out.” He put both hands on the bench. “I cannot ask. Not this season and not the next one unless the fifth pane comes back with a figure against it, and it came back with its date struck out and no figure at all, and a man who writes to a guild in the middle of a season where the passage money is short is a man who has asked for a year and has been given a season, and I have watched a man of sixty do that at about forty and he did not have a good decade out of the other side of it.”

“So you will not write.”

“I will not write to them about the third and eighth columns,” said Hollis Thrale, “and I want it understood that I have not refused to measure the water. I have refused to buy the measurement with a girl’s back, and there is a difference and I have not found the wording for it in forty-one years.”

---

He went back to the bench and picked up the bar and turned it over, and the afternoon went on the way that shed goes on, which is nobody saying anything for about an hour and then somebody saying one sentence.

Then Sabra Trew said the other half of it, out loud, on the fire side, where he could hear her.

“I have a letter,” she said, “in a box, from a works two hundred miles up this coast, and they want me in the spring, and I have not answered it, and I have told him there is a letter and he has said nothing, and that is all of it and it is not going to be any more than that, so you can stop looking at the back of my head for a reason.”

“All right,” said Hollis Thrale.

“That is the whole of what has not been answered. I stand at this fire when you are not, and if I am two hundred miles off in the turn then this fire is not let in for a year, or it is let in by a man of sixty-one on his own, and both of those are worse than what happens now, and I would rather be here in the fire.” She set the bar against the wall. “That is the reason. It is not a deep one. I have made it sound like one and it is not, and I would like it written down that it is a fire and not a decision about my life, because I have watched a man make it into a decision about his life and that is how he got where he is.”

Nobody wrote it down.

And on the fifth of the sixth tide, at the second fire in a shed two miles along a coast, a man of sixty-one stood at a bench with his own record on it and a guild’s record on a wall a mile up the hill that cannot come out of its room, and worked out for the fourth time in a month what he would have to ask for, and did not ask, and put the bar down, and the arch stayed cold, and the cost of the next pane would be paid in advance by a woman of twenty-four who had not been asked and had not said so and had just said so about something else.

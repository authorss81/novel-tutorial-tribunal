# Chapter 111: The Press

The school stands at the top of a green bank with a yard in front of it about the size of a good room, and on the third day of the fourth tide there was a cart in that yard with eleven barrels of pan salt in it and the cart had been standing there since the second bell because the driver had come for a load of lime and the school had none to give him and would not have any before the turn.

He was about thirty-four and he had a coat on him that had been somebody else's, and he was eating an apple off the tail-board, and Belva Cray came out of the schoolroom door to find out whether he was going to stay the night.

“Two hours,” he said. “I have a load of lime at the top road and a boy with a limp and a road that is going to take me all of tomorrow to get back down, and I am not staying in a school.”

“That is fair,” said Belva Cray. “Can you tell me something about the road, though, since you are complaining about it.”

He looked at her properly for the first time.

“That is a strange thing to ask a carter in a schoolyard.”

“It is the only thing I have wanted to know in four years and I have asked four carters and got three different answers and one of them was a song.” She put her back against the door frame. “The low road. How many weeks a year is the low road not passable for a wheel.”

He chewed for a moment.

“Once,” he said. “The turn of the year, and it is not a week you can call a week, it is a week and a bit, and it is the same nine or ten days every year and you can put your hand on it. There is soft ground under Sallow Bight where the salt pans have been pumped for ninety years and the water stands in the pans in the winter and the whole of it goes to soup when it rains, and there is no stone in it and there never was. I have come at it with a loaded cart in the turn of four different years and I have got round twice, and both of those times was because I went up over the top at Nether Head, and the top is eleven miles longer and it is a climb the whole way, and I have done it in one day twice and it has nearly finished me both times. If I went down in it I would be in the water to the axles before I had done a mile and I would not be the first man to have found that out.”

“And in the other fifty weeks?”

“The low road is the best road on this coast and I could take a loaded cart over it in my sleep,” said the carter, “which is the reason nobody has ever thought to write down when it is not, because it is not interesting. It is only interesting for those nine days, and in those nine days a woman teaching eleven children cannot get her scholars down to the school at Nether Head and back, and that is the whole of the trouble and I have said the whole of it.”

He got up on the tail-board and put his weight on it to test it.

“You are the mistress.”

“I am.”

“Then you have my advice and it is worth what it costs, which is nothing, because you have already thought of it. In the turn, your school does not open for about nine days, and not because anybody is ill. Nobody has ever put that on anything.”

---

Belva Cray is fifty-one and has kept that school for eleven years, in a room with a stove in it and a slate roof and forty feet of wall, and the eleven families that keep the school pay nine pounds between them at the turn of the year, and the school teaches twenty-six children in three rooms and the third room is the coal store with the door taken off.

The board met in the village at the last turn and voted, nine families to two, to send the children down the coast to the school at Culler Head from the turn of next year, on the ground that the school at the top is eleven miles from a school with a wall and a stove and twenty-six children is eleven miles from a stove that does not draw.

She had been mistress eleven years and had a figure in her head and no number. Nine days, she had said at the meeting. She had said it twice and one man had said that a week was a week and another had said that the coast road was not passable for a fortnight in the turn and they had both been talking about the same nine days and neither of them had a figure either.

A feeling does not stop a board. That is not a thing anybody has to be told.

She went back into the schoolroom and stood in front of the press.

The press is a shelf of pigeonholes behind the door, four of them, and it has been in that room longer than she has, and it is the school’s own record and it is the only record the school has ever had. The subscription list is in the first hole. The registers are in the second, forty of them, one a year, going back past her. The third hole has the letters, and there are a great many of them, and they are mostly about a roof.

She had been in that room eleven years and she had never once gone into the fourth hole in front of a child, because the fourth hole is where the things go that nobody has claimed, and a school is a place where a great many things go that nobody has claimed. There is a bundle of old primers in it. There is a slate with the frame off. There is a hat.

The fourth hole has a book in it that she has known about for eleven years and has never once read all the way through.

She had read enough of it. It is written twice, in two hands, in order, and the two hands do not agree, and the years in it go back a long way further than that school has been in that building, and the second hand is not a copy of the first. It is the same list done again, and where they differ, the person who did the second one has put a small mark in the margin, and where they agree she has put nothing.

It is a list of weeks, and nothing else. One line to a week, and a line that is a week has a mark at the head of it, and a line that is not a week has a reason written against it, and there are a great many reasons.

She had never been able to put a year to the first page. She had tried twice. She had asked a man in the town who deals in old paper and books and had got a laugh out of him and a second look, and the second look had been longer than the laugh, and he had asked her three questions about the ink and had not offered her a name for it, and had said that a thing like that was not a thing a person could date, it was a thing a person could only guess at, and had told her to go home.

She had gone home. She had gone home about four times in four years.

---

In the last ninety years of it — she had counted back that far twice and got the same answer, which was the only check she had — there are thirty-one weeks with a reason against them that says, in four different hands, that the low road could not be crossed.

Thirty-one times in ninety years.

That is the number she cannot use. It counts weeks and she needs days, and there is nothing on this coast, in this room, or in her own head, that will take one of those things and give her the other. A bad week is one day or nine. She has had both, and they are the same word, and about four of them in ten are nothing at all.

And the last thirty-one of the ninety are all in her own mistress's hand, and her own mistress is dead six years, and had said nothing about the book to her in eleven years of standing in that room with it in the wall behind her.

She shut the hole and stood with her hand flat on it.

“There is no sense in you,” she said to the book, which is a thing she had said to it perhaps forty times in eleven years and had never once got an answer to.

---

The carter went at about the fourth bell and the yard was empty and she went up to the room above the school, where she keeps her own things, and she took four days' notice off the board of the school in the village with a piece of chalk, and she wrote a note to the new mistress who was coming, and she went down to the press again and took the book out of the fourth hole and put it inside her coat against her chest, where it is about the size of a good armful and heavier than it looks.

It came out of the hole for the first time in eleven years and it left a clean rectangle of unfaded distemper in the dust.

That is what the going costs and the list is not complete. Eleven shillings, because the road is a road and roads are money, and the eleven shillings were in a box under her bed and they were the money for a new desk, and a desk is not a want, it is a thing a school looks like it needs. Four days on the road, and the four days are four days. And there is a boy of eleven in the village who will ask her where she has gone and she will have to tell him a thing that is not a lie and is not the truth, and she will have to say it twice, once to him and once to his mother, and both times it will be about a book.

And the new mistress comes on the fourth day of the tide after next and will find a clean rectangle of unfaded distemper in the dust behind the door and no note explaining it, because Belva Cray wrote the note about the school and not about the book, and a person does not put a book in a note to a stranger.

She went down the bank to the low road with the book under her coat and the wind coming off the water in the way it does for nine days in the turn of the year, and the low road was soft at Sallow Bight to within about forty yards of the crossing, and she could see where it had been soft in the year before, because the ruts from the year before were still in it, under the water, and the water was not deep.

Forty ruts. A year ago, in the turn, with a hundred people going up and down this road, and somebody had crossed it forty times, and it had been under, and they had gone across anyway.

That was the number she needed and it was the wrong shape, and it was the first thing in eleven years that anyone had put in front of her that was any use at all, and it was no use at all.

Forty crossings of soft ground by people who knew it was soft. Not forty days. Forty times, by nine carters and a good many carts, and she could not have told you from the ruts whether one of them had been in the water for an hour or a cart had gone over at speed and thrown a wheel, and neither could anybody else, and the water had been over the top of them all the same.

She stood in the middle of the low road at the top of the bank with a book under her coat and forty ruts going away from her in both directions, and what she had in her head was that she had spent four years asking a board for a figure and there were forty of them lying in the mud two hundred yards below her house, and that whoever put them there had not counted them either, and had not intended to, and would have been surprised to be asked.

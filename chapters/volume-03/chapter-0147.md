# Chapter 147: Three Texts

The sixth of the ninth tide is a bar day and the flat takes nine, and the sea was over the crown of the bar before the light, and about seventy people were on nine hundred yards of shingle by the fourth bell for the same reason there always are.

He went to the flat first, at the head of it, at about the second bell, and found Hal Bevan on the chain and asked him for nothing.

That is the whole of what happened between those two men that morning and it is worth about a hundred words, because it is the first time in nineteen years that the man on the chain of that crossing has been told what was going to be done in his name by anybody, and he was told it by a stranger with no standing on the road who was not asking him for a decision.

“I am not going to ask the flat again,” Ilyan Vester said. “I asked it once, by accident, and a woman of forty-eight said no in front of nine people and she was right, and I am not going back to it in the same words, and I would like you to know that before anybody else does.”

“All right,” said Hal Bevan, and went on with the fourth crossing.

Then he waited until the sixth, at about the turn, on a bar day with the sea standing up over the bar, with about nine crossings gone and three to go and a cart of deals in the middle of the flat with nowhere to be.

“You will want to know what I think of it,” he said, without turning round. “So I will tell you, and I will tell you it is not worth much, because I do not think I have anything that counts as a thought about it. I have got a thing I have carried for nineteen years and it is that I decide who gets the tide and I do it with a slate and there is no rule anywhere on this coast that says I do it, and I have never written it down and I am not going to now.

“You have gone and asked three people. Two of them have never been asked for anything and one of them has been the only person a person can be answered by for six years, and the three of them have never been in a room, and they are not going to be, and whatever it is you have made is going to be three things and not one thing, and that is going to sound clever to a man in a city and it is not clever, it is just what there is.

“I have read four sentences off the post out there once a year for nineteen years,” he said, “and the count is the same every year because a man puts a notch in for every crossing, and a man does not have to understand the crossing to put the notch in, and that is the whole of what a number is. You have got three people who were not together and they have each said a thing, and no one of them has to understand the other two, and if you put them side by side then somebody, somewhere, four hundred miles off, is going to read the page and see one thing where there are three, and he will not be doing it on purpose. That is what a page does. A page is one page.”

“I am not going to put them side by side,” said Ilyan Vester.

“Good,” said Hal Bevan. “Then you have understood more about this coast in a fortnight than a man in a good coat understands in a year, and I have got no more, and that is nine crossings.”

---

The yard at the north end, at about the eighth bell, and a woman of thirty-six standing at the mouth of it with a warp over her shoulder who had been out in the deep twice that day.

He did not ask her in front of anybody. That was the first thing he did and she noticed it and he did not pretend he had not.

“I am asking you for something I have no right to ask you for and I am going to ask it anyway, and I am going to ask it once, and you can say no and I will not come back with it in different words.”

“Yes.”

“Let what you said to me on the seventh be a thing that goes in a record. Your words. Not mine and not a clerk’s. And it will be a record about eleven yards of that wall and about a boat and about a day, and it will be read by somebody, and I cannot stop that and I would not stop it.”

“No,” said Sorrel Gannock.

She said it fast and she did not stop there, and Ilyan Vester stood in a yard and did not say one word for about a minute and a half while a man with a boat went past behind them.

“Not because of you. I want that said first and I want it said in the open, because there are four people in this yard and two of them have stopped.

“I said no because you said *a record*, and a record on this coast is a thing with everybody’s business in it, and the two things in it that came from me this week were said in a yard standing up with a man of fifty on the other side of me and two men on a net forty feet off, and that is the only place a thing of mine has ever been said that anybody could act on, and I said it because you asked me a question and I am not going to be a woman who gave a thing to a page.

“And you said three people. And I have not met either of the other two. One of them is a man of sixty-one who has never once in forty-one years been asked whether he would like to be the one standing there, and one of them is the harbourmaster, and I have been under her counter about nine times in twenty-four years and I have said *how much to the far stones* and *is the tide in* and that is the whole of what has ever gone between us.

“If I put my words in a page with theirs, then there is a page. And a page is one page. And in about four months a man in a city is going to read it and see three people who were together about a wall, and we were not together, and none of the three of us has seen the other two, and I have not agreed to be with anybody, and I would not be able to say afterwards that I had.”

“That is right,” said Ilyan Vester. “I am not going to argue with it, and I am not going to come back with it in different words, and I am going to tell you what I am going to do instead and you can say no to that as well.”

“Tell me.”

“Not a page with three people on it. Three texts. Yours is a page of its own and it is yours and it goes in on its own and it is not inside anybody else’s and it does not come before anybody else’s and it does not come after. It says the day you said it and the place you said it in and that you were alone when you said it. And it does not say what the other two said, and it does not say their numbers, and it is not going to be read next to theirs, because I cannot stop a clerk reading a page and I can stop the two of them being on the same page.

“The other two are the same. Three sheets. Three names at three separate places. And nothing at the top of any of them that says the three of them are about the same water, because nobody has asked the three of you that and I am not going to ask you.”

Sorrel Gannock stood in her own yard for about half a minute.

“The third thing is not going to ask me,” she said. “I am going to say the third thing myself or I am not saying it.”

“All right.”

“A page of its own is not a record of nothing. Everybody on this coast thinks a page of its own is a record of nothing, because a woman forty miles up the coast took a copy of a list of weeks home in a bundle and wrote on it that it is a record of nothing, and she was right about that copy and that copy is a record of nothing.

“Mine will not be a record of nothing, because it will have a day on it and a place on it and my name on it, and it will say what a person in a boat can be shown and what she will not put a number on, and a number somebody can act on has to come off a page or it is a man talking in a yard for a fortnight.

“But if I am on a page on my own, then I am a person who has said something in a record, and I have not answered a letter in four years and I have done a thing for eleven years that nobody on this coast has asked me the name of, and I would rather have it on a page on its own than in a man’s mouth in a room for the rest of my life, and I have been eleven years waiting for somebody to put me somewhere and I did not know that was the shape of it until about a minute ago.”

“Yes,” she said. “To a page of its own. Not to that. I will say it again this afternoon in the same words and you may put that down as the second time, because I do not want a thing of mine in a record on the strength of a man saying it in a yard once.”

---

The bench at Thornglass took him about two minutes, because the man at it had said on the third of the ninth tide that he had wanted to be asked for two years and would say yes again to being asked.

“Yes,” said Hollis Thrale. “And I am going to tell you what mine is going to be and I have thought about it since you went down that road.

“Mine is not a number. Mine is the third column and the eighth column and the fact that I cannot tell anybody which of two photographs is right, and the fact that for two years I have had a figure in my head about a wall and have not walked down a road to give it to the man who keeps the wall, and that if the two of them ever do come out of that room and go in a boat together then somebody is going to have to say which morning is the morning, and nobody can, and I am not going to be asked to and I am not going to pretend I could.

“You are going to write on it that the outer end has come back three yards. I am not going to have that on my page. That is your arithmetic and not mine and it is a thing a man does in a room. What is mine is that the third column says two hundred and sixty-one and the guild’s says two hundred and sixty-four, and I have had both of them for two years, and the water moved, and I do not know which one the water left.”

“Yes,” said Ilyan Vester. “That is better than mine.”

“It is not better. It is more honest and it is worth less, and you are going to find that out about this in about four days and I have been finding it out for two years.”

Then he asked the woman at the fire, and it took about nine seconds and four words, and she did not turn round.

“I am going to ask you for one thing and it is not about the wall and it is not about glass.”

“Say it.”

“Keep the three pages. Not to do anything to them. To hold them, and to know where each of the three of them stops and starts, because there is nobody else on this coast who can put two lists side by side and say where one of them runs out, and everybody who has tried has come away with a sentence made out of both and been wrong.”

“Yes,” said Sabra Trew, to the fire.

And that was the whole of it. He did not ask her what it involved and she did not ask him what it was for, and neither of them has said one word about it since.

---

The room a mile and a flight of stairs up the hill, at about the sixth bell, with the door shut, which is the second time in sixty days that anybody has had a conversation in that room with the door shut for its own sake.

“Put it in,” said Bettany Rook, before he had said it. “I have been waiting four days for somebody to ask me that and I am not going to make a man who has walked a mile and a half say a sentence twice.

“And I am going to say what goes on it and what does not, and I have had four days and I have not asked anybody.

“What goes on it: a duty, and my name on it, and not an office. And four lines from the guild, word for word, with the fourth line underlined, because the fourth line is the one that costs them nothing and it is the only sentence anybody in that building has written to this coast in about two years that was not about a sum, and it belongs in a record of this more than anything anybody will say about the wall.

“What does not go on it: a number. I have not been on that wall and I have not sounded it and I have not got a figure and I am not going to be handed one and put my name under it. If somebody sounds it, and somebody will if anybody ever gives them a day, then it will be his number with his name on it in his own book with the date on it, and it will be somewhere in these three sheets, and it will be his.

“And I am going to be at a counter for the rest of the working life of a woman of fifty-one because of this, and I have told you that once and I am not going to tell you again, and you are not to put that in either, because that is mine and it is not a fact about a wall.”

Then the two of them made the thing, and it took most of the evening.

He wrote it out longhand on three leaves torn out of the back of the penny exercise book he carries in his coat, which took him the better part of an hour and a half and used up nine sheets of paper and all of the ink he had brought with him, and he folded the three separately and put a string round them and wrote the date on the outside of it and nothing else, because he wanted it to be a thing a person could pick up and read and not a thing that had been handled.

The top of the first sheet said what the first sheet was and when and where and that the person was alone. The top of the second said the same. The top of the third said the same. There was no sheet on top of the three of them. There was no sentence at the top of the three of them, and it took him about a minute and a half every time he started to write one and about a minute and a half to stop.

He had done that before, in a net loft, in the sixth of a tide, in about nine minutes, and it had held a tide and cost a carter of thirty-three a day and a day’s hire, and every word of it had been out of one of three true accounts, and the three had been in three different places, and nobody had been asked to agree to anything, and it was legal.

That is the thing he has been wrong about for a month and he had it exactly backwards until the third of a tide in a yard at Wether Cross when a man with a hammer told him he was asking in the wrong order, and the whole of the trade is the separation and not the joining, and there is no other trade.

At about the ninth bell, at the near end of nine hundred yards of wet shingle with the water going off it, the System put the record in front of him, and what it gave him was the three sheets and nothing that joined them.

**THREE ACCOUNTS OF ELEVEN YARDS OF THE NORTH WALL. THE THREE WERE GIVEN IN THREE PLACES ON THREE DAYS BY THREE PEOPLE WHO WERE NOT TOGETHER. NOBODY HAS ASKED THE THREE OF THEM TO AGREE, AND THE THREE ACCOUNTS DO NOT AGREE.**
**WHAT IS NOT DECIDED: WHETHER THREE ACCOUNTS OF ONE WALL ARE ONE MATTER OR THREE.**

He read it and it told him what he already knew and it certified nothing and it joined nothing, and he had wanted it to do one of those two things and had not said so to anybody.

Then he put his name at the bottom of the third sheet, and under his own name he wrote that he had asked the three and that two of them had said yes to a page of their own on the first asking and one of them had said no to a page with anybody else on it and had said yes afterwards of her own accord in the same words, and that he was the man who put the three sheets in a bundle and that he was not a party to any of the three of them, and had no standing on this coast, and had no office, and had asked the three of them for one thing each and had been given three.

And the bundle went up the coast road in a cart in the morning with a load of oil, two hundred miles, to an office with a printer in it, and it is not locked and it is not sealed and it is a bundle of paper with three names on it and his name at the bottom, and anybody in that building can send for it and read it and take a copy of it and send the copy to a man on a coast he has never walked.

He worked out what the fee was, standing in the wet at the near end of that flat, in about nine seconds, and it was not a thing he had known when he started, and he said it out loud once, to nobody, on a bar day, and then did not say it again that week or the week after.

That is the broker’s fee. Somebody can send for the broker’s own paper.

# Chapter 132: What a Man Has Left

A good day on the fifth of the seventh tide and the flat takes six, and the sea outside has come down off the bar since the middle of the night, and the ebb out of the Reach runs long and soft and the whole of that flat is a mile of wet shingle with a channel in it doing a yard a minute and nothing else in the world on it.

Hal Bevan put the chain up at the second bell and the six crossings went over one after another from there to the turn, and he stood in the middle of that flat with a bar in his hand the way he has stood on it for nineteen years and he did not watch any of them go.

In the inside breast pocket of his coat, folded in four, was a sheet of paper in a dealer’s hand. It had been in that pocket since the fourth bell of the third of a tide, when a man who sells eels had read it out twice in a lodging-house kitchen and written it out again and given a copy to the man who puts the chain up, and it had been in that pocket for nine days, and it had been in that pocket through a good deal of weather.

Six days ago it had stopped a cart. The sixth of the sixth tide, at the third bell, a load of dressed stone had stood on the near side for a day and a day’s hire and two men on it and had gone up the coast road the long way instead, and the man who had hired it had told four people up the road that a man on a chain had held the tide and given a reason, and the reason had been that paper against a table on a wall a mile up the hill, and that the two of them could not be put in front of one another, and there was not going to be a room.

He had not taken the paper out since the second day. He can read it. Reading it a third time does not do anything at all.

There is one question a person may put to Bettany Rook on this coast. Not in a room. On four feet of flat stone at the head of that flat, on the fourth and on the sixth, at the turn, in the open, one question, and one answer, and nobody may ask a second, and nobody has ever asked it twice in the same week, and it is about a hundred years old and it is written down nowhere.

The fourth was yesterday. The sixth is tomorrow. He had been on this flat nineteen years and he had never spent one, and he had never told anybody that he had one, and there was nobody on four hundred miles of this coast he could have told.
---

He priced it three times that day and he did it while working, and nobody asked him what he was doing and nobody stood near him while he did it, and that is how a thing gets worked out on a coast.

The first one was the water. He could ask her what the water at the north end is going to do on the fourth of next tide and she would tell him, in the open, in front of everybody, and it would be true.

And he already knew.

He knew it to about a yard, which is not a figure and is not a number and cannot be put down, and he had had it since he was about nineteen and he had never once been wrong with it in a way that cost anybody a cart. He had watched that channel come out of the low and lie along the flat and go back into the low eleven times in twenty years. He did not need a woman to tell him where the water was going. He needed a woman to tell him something he could not get by standing on nine hundred yards of shingle and looking at it, and there was exactly one thing he could not get by looking at it, and he had known what it was since the third of the sixth tide.

The paper said the water at the north end was under six inches at the lowest and that a cart went over and came off dry.

The table on the north wall of the tide office said four feet at the lowest for the same morning.

Six inches and four feet is three foot six, and one of those two numbers is wrong, and he did not know which, and nobody on four hundred miles of this coast knew which, and he had spent six days finding that out for himself at the cost of one day of one carter’s hire and a great deal of his own.

The second one was the table. He could ask her whether the guild’s own table is right about the north end.

And that is not a question about the water. It is a question about a thing somebody made in a shed and paid for, and this stone is for the water, and everybody on that flat knows it is for the water, and about nine of them know it because a woman with a net stood on it in a different year and was refused in front of sixty people and was right to be refused and the reason she was given was the water and not the money and not the guild.

He would get one sentence. It would be true, and it would not be unkind, and it would be over in about four seconds, and it would cost him the only thing he has got, and there is no way on this coast to get it back, because a person does not get a second question and does not get a different day.

The third one was nothing at all, which is not a question and costs nothing and leaves the paper in his pocket for the rest of his life, and which meant that the only reason a cart was ever held off that flat would remain a sheet of paper that a stranger wrote in a net loft, and that he would be the man who held it, and that he would not know.

---

Dell Gannock came off the water about the fourth bell with the flat in about two feet of it and two crossings done and the pole over his shoulder, and stopped at the chain and put his hand on the post the way a man does.

“That is a good one,” he said. “That is a good day and you can feel it in the channel.”

“It is a good day.”

“You can go over it loaded, today.”

“I know I can go over it loaded, today,” said Hal Bevan, “and I knew that before you came off and you knew that before you went on, and that is the trouble with this flat and it always has been.”

Dell Gannock laughed at that and went up the shingle, and he was right about the water, and he was forty-two, and he could not have told anybody what a man could not get by looking.

Cleve Gannock came down the lane at about the fifth bell because one of his carts was on the third crossing and he wanted to know whether it would be off by the seventh, which is a thing a dealer does and is right to do, and he stood about ten feet off with his arms folded for a while and then went back up.

And a carter with a load of deals, who was nobody in particular and was on the flat for his own reasons, put his beasts’ heads up and said, without being asked and in the ordinary way, as a man says a thing while he is getting his breath:

“I would not spend it on that.”

“What would you spend it on.”

“I would not spend it at all,” said the carter. “You have had that since the sixth and you have not got a figure out of it and neither has anybody else, and if you go up there and ask her about a thing you have already got, you will come down that hill with nothing and a stone will have gone somewhere it does not come back from. There is only ever one of them in a life, and I am forty and I have not spent mine, and it is not mine either, and I have never once wanted to.”

He got his cart over inside the hour and went up the lane and was gone, and he had not been talking about the water and neither had Bevan.

---

At the far stones, with her slate on her knee, Corrie Tallow said the other half of it, and she said it to a man standing four feet off with a bad leg and she did not say it to Hal Bevan, and he heard all of it and did not move.

“He is going to ask her tomorrow,” she said. “I have been counting that flat in persons for thirty-one years and I have asked on that stone once and I will tell you what I got, which was that she does not do numbers, she does the water. And I have wanted to ask her where the ninth crossing of a bad sixth goes, because that is the only number on this coast anybody would pay for, for about eleven years. And I am not going to, and it is not pride, it is that a ninth crossing is a number and she does not do numbers, and if I stand on that stone and ask her a number then in about a year there will be a paper with a number on it and a man with a cart will say *the paper says* and go over it, and the nine that is wrong will be somebody in nine feet of water, and it will be my fault because of the paper.”

She turned a page in the exercise book she pays for out of the stall.

“So if you are asking her about a piece of paper tomorrow, then I hope you have got your money’s worth out of six days of thinking about it, because I have watched four men in my life spend a thing like that and get a straight answer and not be able to use it, and every one of them did it for exactly your reason, and not one of them did it for a better reason afterwards.”

---

The man with the bad leg was standing about thirty feet off and had been standing about thirty feet off for most of a month.

The paper had been in that man’s breast pocket for nine days and the man with the bad leg knew every word of it without having read it, and he had heard the whole of the hold told twice in a room with the door open, and he could not have told anybody how he knew.

And the thing that had changed on this coast since the third of the sixth tide was nine words, given at the top of a lane, by a man with a bar in his hand, to a man who had made the paper and did not know that the paper had been used.

*The next one of these that comes up this coast I am going to bring it to you before I use it.*

So Hal Bevan came down the shingle at about the sixth bell, when the six crossings were done and the water was going off the flat, and stopped four feet off, and did not make a thing of it.

“I am going to ask her something tomorrow at the turn and I am telling you what it is, because I said I would, and because you made the thing in it and I would rather you heard it in my mouth than worked it out of what I do with it tomorrow morning.”

“I am not going to tell you it is the right thing to spend it on.”

“I know you are not. You have not got the standing and neither have I, and I would not take it off a man who has been on this coast a month.” Bevan put the bar down on the shingle. “And I am not going to tell you it is the wrong thing either, because you are the only man on this coast who has been able to tell me one water from three and you have not been able to do it in nine days and neither could I and neither could the woman on the stone, and if anybody in this building wants to say that is the guild’s fault then the guild has not been out in a boat.”

“What is the question.”

“I am going to ask her whether there is anything in that paper that is wrong about the water,” said Hal Bevan. “And I have had six days to think of a better one and there is not one, and I want you to hear it from me first because if you hear it from me first then at least one man on this coast knows exactly what I spent it on.”

“That is a question about the water,” said Ilyan Vester.

“It is a question about a piece of paper about the water.”

“Yes,” he said. “Those are two different things and I do not have anything that turns one into the other, and if I had it I would not be standing thirty feet off with nothing to do about it.”

Bevan looked at him for a while.

“No,” he said. “You would not.”

And he picked the bar up and went up the shingle.

---

He had the stone on the sixth and he had it in his pocket and he did not know what was on it, because it is a slate and it is a piece of soft grey stone about the size of two hands and it has been in that pocket for nineteen years and it has a stroke in it for the first cart and a stroke for the second and a mark on the fourth for the tide, and there is no rule anywhere on four hundred miles of this coast that says the man on the chain holds the tide.

He took it out at the seventh bell and looked at it and put it back, and it was a slate, and it was the only thing he had that anybody on this coast would act on, and it told him who was going over first on a tide, and it had nothing whatever to say to him about a piece of paper in a dealer’s hand.

The channel was doing a yard a minute down the middle of a mile of shingle and there was nothing in the world on it, and he went up to the crossing and got his coat off the post and went home, and the day was a good day and the flat took six and he had spent nothing and had decided everything.

# Chapter 128: What a Pane Is For

It is a good day on the first of the seventh tide and the flat takes six, and a letter came up the coast road in a cart with a load of oil, and it was read out to a man who could not read it in a yard at the head of a lane by a man of thirty who reads everything and cannot read quickly.

Seth Garrick read it twice, standing up, with the sheet in both hands, and then he looked at the man who had asked him to and said, “That one is not a roof.”

“Read it again.”

“No, I have got it.” He held it out. “I am not reading it twice because I have not got it. I have got it exactly and it is four lines and I will say it and you can stop me if I get a word wrong.

“The woman at the school a hundred and forty miles up this coast says that the book is in the back of her register and not in the press, because a press is a thing twenty-six children go into. It is in the back of the register and there is a sheet laid in front of it in another woman’s hand and the sheet says, in Corrie Tallow’s name, that it is a record of nothing and that it may be shown to anybody. She has shut the hole in the press and put the fourth pigeonhole back the way it was and there is nothing in it but a hat and a slate with the frame off, and she says she is sorry about the letter and she is not sorry about the book, and she says that whoever told her to send it a hundred and forty miles has cost her four days and eleven shillings and the money for a desk, and she has got what she came for and it is not that.”

“She is right,” said Ilyan Vester.

“She is right about all of it and I have never met her,” said Seth Garrick, “and that is the part I cannot get round. Two women a hundred and forty miles from here know more about a list of weeks than anybody on this coast does and one of them has sworn to a column she wrote herself and the other one has washed a slate out in a trough, and between them they have got the only interesting thing in four hundred years and it is in a book in a schoolroom in letters, and there is not one mark in it, and there is not going to be, because a schoolroom is a schoolroom and nobody cuts marks in a schoolroom and there never was anybody there who would know what a mark was for.”

He folded it and put it inside the coat.

“They are four hundred years of a kiln wall,” he said, “and it was copied twice out of marks, and the copy that got to the school lost the only thing in it that was worth copying, and the copy that is left is in letters and says on its face that it is nothing. So there is one of the two left and it is in the wrong medium in the wrong place with the wrong woman, and it will be a drawer in a school a hundred and forty miles off for about two hundred years, and nobody on this coast will ever know what is in it, and neither will anybody a hundred and forty miles up it. That is not a mystery and there is nothing to chase. It is a thing that got lost because the two people who had it did not know it was worth keeping and I know why they did not know, because it is the same reason as everything else. Do you want the rest of that now or at the glassworks.”

“At the glassworks,” he said.

---

At Thornglass there was an order on the bench, and it had come up the coast road in the same cart as the letter, and it is a sheet of the guild’s season’s own paper and it is a pane of the guild’s water and passage table, to be fired and delivered in the turn, and there is a date against it and no figure against it.

Hollis Thrale had it under the batch book, where it had been since the second bell.

“You have had that since the second bell,” said the man with the bad leg.

“I have had that since the second bell and I have not put it in the arch and I am not going to and I want to say why in one sentence so that you can go and say to whoever you like that the glassmaker turned it down.” He did not take his hand off the bench. “Because a pane is a year, and the year is whoever stands at that fire, and I am sixty-one and I would not be let out of my own arch on my own, and so the year is her. And I have got an order with a date on it and no money on it, and a man does not open a fire for a year on a date. That is the whole of it and it is the fifth time I have said that sentence in a month to about five different people and the fifth time I have been asked the question as though I had not answered it.”

“It is not the same order,” the man said. “That one was the charter’s burden. This one is the tide table again.”

“It is the same order with a different thing in it,” said Hollis Thrale, “and it is the same fire, and the fire does not know what is going into it. That is the whole of the trade and nobody has ever believed it.”

---

It went on into the afternoon and it went on the way that shed goes on, with the second fire let in at about the second bell and a woman standing at it, and the argument was not about the fifth pane or the order on the bench. It was about a different one, and it had been going on for two years, and the man with the bad leg had been the only other person who had ever been told about it.

Hollis Thrale wants a third pane made. Not for the guild. For himself, in his own shed, on his own wall, of the same water, fired this year, by his own hand, with his apprentice at the fire, and it is to go beside the one he made three years ago and it is to be the third.

“A third one settles it,” he said. “That is the whole of my argument and I have had it for two years and it is the argument of a man who is right about glass. One of them is nineteen years and eight months old and belongs to a room I cannot get into. One of them is three years old and is on this wall and is mine and I would swear to mine. And there is no way on four hundred miles of this coast to put those two side by side in one room, because the only instrument anybody will act on is theirs and it does not come out of that room. So I make a third. And then there are three, and two of them are mine, and the two that are mine were made four years apart by the same man with the same hand on the ruling and the same brass, and if the guild’s disagrees with both of them then the guild’s is a photograph of a morning and the channel has moved, and that is not a thing a person can argue with. That is what I have been trying to do for two years and it has not cost me anything yet because I have not been able to pay for it.”

“You have not been able to pay for it because the money is not there,” said Sabra Trew, from the fire.

“I have not been able to pay for it because the money is not there,” he agreed, “and if it were there tomorrow I would put it in the arch on the first letting day and I would not sleep for the eleven months and I would be sixty-two when it came out and it would be the best piece of glass I have ever made and it would be on this wall with a date on it, and the two of you would be here to see it go in.”

She put the bar down and turned round from the fire and wiped her hand across her face with the back of her wrist, which left a grey streak from the hairline to the jaw, and she said the thing that was going to be said, and she said it standing up, and it was the only time in three years she had put the bar down in the middle of a second fire and she put it down because she was going to say it properly.

“No,” she said. “A third one does not settle it and it cannot, and you know that, and I have watched you not know that for two years and I have never said so because you are sixty-one and it is your bench and your fire and I am paid nothing and it is not my place to tell the man who stands at it what he may want.”

“Do not stop for that,” said Hollis Thrale.

“I am not stopping for that. I am going to say a thing and then I am going to stand at this fire.” She did not sit down. “You cannot settle a disagreement between two photographs by making a third one. That is all there is. Two of them and a third is three photographs, and every one of them is a morning, and you have taught me that better than anybody and you taught it me and then you went and forgot it. A photograph is not a witness. It does not answer anything. It says what a channel was doing once, on a day, to a man who was standing beside an arch, and a channel moves, and if you fire a third one this year you will have three mornings and a channel that has moved three times and no more truth than you have now, and in forty years there will be three panes on a wall in a shed and a man in a room four hundred miles off with one on his wall, and the difference between the two of them will be exactly what it is now, except that you will have paid a year for it.”

“Then what is it for,” said Hollis Thrale.

“It is for being a record of a morning, and that is all it is ever for, and it is a very good thing to be and it is not an instrument and it never was and it is not going to be.” She turned back to the fire. “And you do not need a photograph, you need a number, and a number on this coast is not made in an arch. A number is made with a lead line and a boat and a man who can sound, and a day when the sea is doing whatever it is going to do, and it does not last and it does not go on a wall and in four months it is a thing in a man’s book that he will swear to in a room if anybody ever wants him to. That is what you have wanted for two years and it has cost you nothing and you have not had it because you have been going about asking for the wrong thing, and I have watched you ask for the wrong thing for two years and I have never once said so, because when I came here you told me the second fire cannot be left and I have been at it since about the fourth hour of my first week.”

Nobody said anything for about a minute and a half, and the fire made the noise it makes.

“Say the last of it again,” said Hollis Thrale.

“No.”

“You have said it once and I have asked you for it twice in my life and I am not going to be a man who is asked a third time and expects a different answer.” He turned round. “You are wrong. Not about the photograph — you are right about the photograph, and you have been right about it since you came into this shed and I have been wrong about it for two years. Wrong about what there is instead. You have given me four things you cannot do in a building. And I have asked for four things I can do, and I can pay for, and a woman of twenty-four is not part of them.”

“That is fine,” said Sabra Trew.

---

The man with the bad leg had got about as far as *she is right* and had been sitting on the crate by the warm wall with it in his mouth and had not said it, and had been working instead on why the guild had never let the panes out of that room, and had arrived, as he arrived four times a month now, at a body.

“They will not let the first one out of the building because it is theirs and it is the only one and it is the charter,” he said. “And they will not let the second out of the building either because the same is true of it, and that is the whole of the difficulty, and it is a difficulty about two people keeping two things.”

“It is a difficulty about a pane and about a fire,” said Hollis Thrale, “and you have had it backwards for a month and you may go on having it backwards. If the guild will not bring the charter out of that room then no third pane on this wall will fetch it out of that room, and you are standing in my shed telling me the trouble is a building. The trouble is that a woman of twenty-four is the only person on this coast who could stand at that fire for eleven months and the guild has not noticed that and neither have you.”

“You are right,” said Ilyan Vester, “and I had it wrong a minute ago and I was about to say so, and I have been wrong about the same shape of thing twice on this coast and both times I put it in a building, and I would like it noticed that I have noticed.”

“Noticed by me,” said Hollis Thrale, “and not by anybody else on this coast, and it makes no difference whatever.”

He picked the order up out from under the batch book and turned it round so that it lay on the bench face up, with the date on it and nothing against it.

“This is a pane of water and passage table to be fired and delivered in the turn,” he said. “There is a date on it. There is no money on it. It came up the coast road in a cart with a load of oil and it has been on that bench since the second bell and it is going to be on that bench at the turn, and when the season’s money comes round and there is money against it, then it goes into the arch, and it will be a year, and she will stand at that fire for it.” He looked at the arch through two feet of brick. “And the year has been paid for three times on this coast already and paid for in advance every time, and the fourth time would be paid for in advance by the same person, in the same eleven months, on a bench in the same shed, and nobody has asked her, and I am not going to ask her, because I know what she will say and I would be asking a woman of twenty-four to agree to a year in a bad season for a thing that has no money against it, and that is not a question. That is an instruction with a question on the end of it.”

He put the order back under the batch book, and the arch stayed cold, and the second fire went on being let in at the second bell and stood at by one person, as it is six days in seven, for nothing, in a shed two miles along a coast.

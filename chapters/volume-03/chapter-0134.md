# Chapter 134: The Two Days a Week

The seventh of the seventh tide is a bar day and the strait is shut, and the water has been on the crown of the bar since before the light and it is not coming off it, and there is more of it on that shingle this morning than there is on any road in four hundred miles, and not one cart of it is going anywhere else, and on a seventh the water owns the bar the way it owns everything else that day.

Nobody has ever given the seventh a name that means anything. It is the day the water owns the bar, and that is all it is, and it comes round about seven times in a season and there is nothing anybody can do about it and nobody on this coast has ever once tried.

---

There was a man on the staff at the harbour mouth at about the eighth bell, and a boy of about ten was holding his pot for him, and his hammer was in a bag on the ground because he had not wanted to carry it up the slip in that hand.

Gell Roke is seventy-one and he paints that staff two feet under, and the staff itself has stood where it stands for about four hundred years. What makes it a job and not a hobby is the water. A mark two feet under can only be put on when the sea at the harbour mouth is standing up over the two-foot mark, and that happens once a week and not on six days, and on the other six that paint goes on a mark nobody can see. He has never set that out for anybody, nobody has ever thought to ask him, and he comes back and does it about twice a season according to how he feels.

He had a coat on the mark from about four years ago and it had gone thin at the two-foot line, and he was putting a second one on, and the pot had to go up under there to reach it.

“Hold it up,” he told the boy. “Not down. There is no arm in a man that holds a pot down.”

The boy held it up.

The mark under the water is as wide as the mark above it and has been for nineteen years, and what it marks is a long way from where it is, and somewhere under two hundred people on this coast have worked that out for themselves at some time, and it has never once come out of one of their mouths, and nobody said it this morning either, because it is a bar day and seventy people were on that flat and every one of them had a load.

---

The chain went up and came down on that flat nine times, and then it lay still for an hour between the fifth and the seventh, and a seventh is the day it lies still, and the eighth crossing of the nine is the one the man with the bar has to hurry over, and that is how a seventh is made and it is not anybody being cruel.

Corrie Tallow was out at the far end with her slate, because a ninth crossing is the only crossing on that flat anybody has ever thought of putting in pencil, and nobody pays her for it and nobody is going to start, and she is there on every ninth without fail. She had counted forty-one crossings that day and about half of them had been people on foot, and a boy of eleven went over at a penny with a hand-lead in his fist and nobody on that shingle thought anything of it, him included.

The tally post was at the crossing with its chain up and the board on the front of it, and the fourpence of nails holding as they have held, and the ninth crossing went over about four feet from it and nobody looked at it. It is inspected at about the turn on a seventh, once a year, by a man with a hammer, and it had been inspected in the turn of the year and it would not be inspected again until the turn of the year after, and there is nobody on this coast who has any business at that post on a seventh morning and nobody had.

Danno Ray came over on the seventh crossing with a load of lime and two men on and he was forty-five and he had been on the high road since he was a boy, and he got his cart off the flat and turned the beasts round and came back along the near side where the going is firm, because he was not a fool about a seventh.

He stopped at the chain about two hundred yards down from the crossing and stood there a while with his hands in his sleeves, and a boy of twenty-two with a board under his arm came down the shingle to see whether he wanted the tide held.

“I do not want anything,” said Danno Ray. “I am standing here because there is nothing to stand anywhere else. I have been waiting since the second bell and it is now the seventh and my lime is on this side of nine hundred yards of shingle and there is a stone man on this road that you can ask one question on, and there is a song at the stone this afternoon that a carter can hear, and there is a number cut on that post that a man counts with a hammer once a year, and there is a board on the front of it. And I have been on that flat with two men on since the second bell, and I have worked out that a rule that cannot be changed for a year is not a rule, it is a season.”

Nobody answered him.

The boy of twenty-two with the board did not answer him. Two carters standing at the near end with their beasts did not answer him. A man who was on his fourth crossing that day and had nine hundred yards of shingle under him and a good load did not answer him, because he had heard it once before, in the fifth of a tide, from the same man, standing in the same place with his back to a wall, and about nine people had been standing there then and not one of them had said a word, and nothing has happened on this coast since.

“It is not the rule I am complaining about,” Danno Ray said, to nobody, and got his beasts up the shingle and went to the hard. “You have had that crossing a hundred years. I am complaining about the season. There is a difference and one of them can be got in a letter and the other one cannot, and I have not worked out yet which is which, and I will work it out, and I will not be the only one who has.”

He was not the only one who had. There were about nine people on this coast who had that sentence in them and had had it for some time and had not said it out loud, and two of them were within forty feet of him, and nobody has ever worked out which two they were and neither of them knows, and it is not a thing that needs working out before it can be said.

---

Seth Garrick sang at the turn, on the stone, as he does.

Four lines, and a tariff, and it belongs to the seventh and to nobody else, and there is no copy of it anywhere, on a board or off one, and about nine hundred yards of wet shingle had it this morning the way it has everything, and four or five people came in on the chorus, which they do.

The stone was bare behind him. Nobody stood on it that day and nobody could have, and there is nobody on this coast who has ever explained to the guild or to anybody else why that is, and the guild has never asked.

---

The man it landed on was the man on the chain.

Hal Bevan had put the chain up at the second bell and had been on that flat for eleven hours and had nine crossings in him and a slate in his pocket and nothing in his coat but a bar and a folded sheet of paper in a dealer’s hand that he had not taken out since the sixth of a tide and never took out again.

He had spent his question on the sixth. He had got a true answer, out loud, in the open, in front of about seventy people, and the answer had been good, and it had been about the water, and he had known it would be about the water for six days before he spent it, and he had spent it anyway, and it had cost him the only thing he had ever had on this coast and it had bought him nothing at all, and he had not been angry about that at the time and he was not angry about it now.

What he had on him instead was the fact of it. A man of fifty-four who has held a chain for nineteen years and has never once not known where the going was had been told, by the only person on four hundred miles who is allowed to say it, in the open, that he had been right about the water.

That was all. And it was not nothing. He thought about it about twice a day for the rest of that year and once after that, and he never said it out loud to anybody, and about four people on that coast worked out that he had spent the stone’s question and never said so.

A carter came down the shingle at about the eighth bell who was not going anywhere in particular, which on a seventh is a thing a carter does.

“When is the next one,” he said.

“Which next one.”

“The stone. When is she on it next.”

“The fourth of the eighth,” said Hal Bevan. “Four days. And then the sixth of that same tide, and then the fourth of the ninth, and it goes like that and it has gone like that for about a hundred years, and there is no more to it than that.”

“What about the sixth.”

“The sixth is there. And the fourth is there. And the first, the second, the third and the fifth are not, and the seventh is not either.”

“So there are two in a week.”

“There are two in a week,” said Hal Bevan, “and the seventh is not one of them, and the seventh is the day the water has the bar, and there is nothing at all. That is the whole of it. I have never seen anybody get a day out of this coast and I have not had one out of it in nineteen years and I am not expecting one, and I would say the same to anybody who asked me tomorrow morning, which is exactly the same answer, and it will be the same answer on the second.”

The carter stood about a minute.

“You are telling me a man gets two days a week.”

“I am telling you there are two days a week when the stone is there and a woman on it, and about four hundred thousand days a week when it is not, and I have never once heard anybody on this coast work that out in a sentence until just now, and I have not said it to be clever. I said it because you asked me and because it is the truth and I am too old to be doing sums in my head about a thing like that in the cold.”

“Nobody is going to be able to use that,” said the carter.

“No,” said Hal Bevan. “It has got a stone in it and no more. It is the only thing on this coast I have ever been able to say that is true and does not cost anybody a day, and I have had nineteen years of the other sort and I am not complaining.”

---

The water went off the flat at about the ninth bell in a hard westerly and took three quarters of an hour to do it, and by the time it had gone the chain was up and the posts were in their sockets and the staff at the harbour mouth had a second coat on it at two feet under and the boy of about ten had gone home with an empty pot.

A man of thirty-one was standing at the head of the flat in the dark on nine hundred yards of dry shingle with his weight on his good side, letting the sea go off it, and about seventy people had been on that shingle that day and not one of them had asked him for anything, and he had been on this coast forty-three days.

He had a page in his coat with a date on it and that it was his and no reason on it, and there was no reason on the page for four pages now, and he had never once been able to decide whether that was a discipline or a habit, and it was a habit.

He had also worked out, somewhere in the middle of the afternoon, standing at the head of a flat with nothing to do, that a man on this coast had two days a week with a woman on a stone and five days a week with nothing, and that the five was not a punishment and was not anybody’s doing, and that the whole of the working life of every person within four hundred miles of that shingle is arranged around two days a week and a question about the water, and that not one of them has ever been asked for a single thing in return for it, and that this is the only arrangement on this coast with no form in it and no counterparty and nobody to complain to and nobody who can be got at.

And then Gell Roke came past him at the harbour mouth going home with the hammer in the bag and the boy with the empty pot, and neither of them said a word to him, and neither of them ever did, and he walked up the lane to the hard and got his leg to the top of it and put his weight on the good side and stood there for about a minute, and the leg had been his own for four hours once, on the fourth of the third tide, at a fire two miles along this coast, and had not been his own since.

# Chapter 103: A Penny a Tide, and the Cost of a Day at the Counter

The turn was at the sixth bell on the first of the new tide and the market was on the quay below the harbour, and the board with the day's prices on it was nailed to the end of the fish counter, and Ilyan Vester stood in front of it for a while and understood the shape of it and nothing in it, and a boy of about eleven read it out to him twice, and the second time was slower, and the woman behind the counter looked at him over the boy's head for a length of time and then said nothing at all and went back to her fish.

That was the first it cost him on this coast, and it cost him about a penny, and nobody charged him for it.

Corrie Tallow was at the crossing. She was under her awning with the board turned back round and the chalk side out, and the flat was drying from the middle out, and there were about nine hundred yards of wet shingle between the two stones and the far stones, and the water was standing in the channel down the middle of it in a long grey rope.

“I have been standing here since the second bell,” she said, “watching a boy of eleven earn three shillings and threepence a week and I have counted every one of his tides on the back of this board in chalk, and I have been at it two days, and I am not going to tell you what I found because you have not asked yet.”

“Then tell me,” he said.

She told him.

The tariff at Wether Cross is a penny a tide. It is written on a board in the guild's hand and nailed to the tally post where the chain comes across, and it is the same penny on every day of the tide, and it has been the same penny since before she started the stall.

“The flat is crossable six times a day on the first, second, third and fifth,” she said, “and nine times on the fourth, sixth and seventh, because on those three days the water stands up in the middle of the day while the strait is shut and every cart that has to get round comes over the flat, so there is more work and not less.”

“So the days the water is worst are charged the same as the days it is best.”

“They are the same penny,” said Corrie Tallow, “and I have asked about that. They have told me they will not write a different price for a dangerous day because a tariff that says *danger* is a tariff that can be refused, and because a man who refuses a tariff is a man who has to be argued with, and they have not got anybody at that counter to argue with anybody on a bad day.” She wiped the chalk off her thumb. “So on the fourth, the sixth and the seventh, Nim is sent nine times instead of six, at a penny, and the reason there is more of him is that there is more danger, and the price of the danger is exactly nothing.”

Corrie Tallow turned the board over and there was a second board behind it, thinner, and on the back of it, in her own hand, in a chalk that had been washed off twice and put on again twice, there were two columns of figures and a lot of ticks.

“That is my count and it took me two afternoons and I did it with a stick, standing in the lane, going down the doors, and I am not going to tell you whose doors. Eleven households on that road send somebody over this flat. I know it is eleven. Ten of them send a child on one tide, or two tides, or none in a week when the week is wet, and one of them sends him on five days a week, and it is two of the low days and three of the high ones, because those are the days the cart goes over.”

“Which is yours.”

“It is not mine, it is a boy of eleven, and his mother mends nets and her name is Bel Hask and she is forty-four and she is the best hand with a net on this coast.” She went and turned the board back. “He makes thirty-nine tides a week at a penny. Three shillings and threepence. Eight pounds nine a year, and in a bad week when the seventh stands up as well it is forty-eight and he gets a fourth shilling.”

“And the ten?”

“The ten are threepence a week between them. That is the commonest figure on this road, threepence, and three shillings and threepence is thirteen times threepence, and I did not know that was the number I was after until I had it written down, and then I sat on my own step for a bit.”

The chain came up about forty yards below them. A cart came off the flat and up the shingle with the near horse's head up and a boy of eleven walking at the head of it with a hand-lead in his fist, and the boy was not hurrying and was not slow either, and he had the walk of a person who is going to do this again in about forty minutes.

“He is not yours?” said Ilyan.

“He is not mine, and he is not the guild's, and there is no keeper of this flat and there has never been one, and the guild says the flat needs nothing done to it, and the flat does not need anything done to it, and that is true, and it is also the whole of why he is there.” She looked down the shingle. “His mother wants him off it. She has wanted him off it since the spring. She cannot pay three shillings and threepence a week out of what a net makes. But she has a cart, and the cart has to come round, and it is eleven miles the other way by the shore road when the strait is shut, and eleven miles is a day and a half of a horse, and a boy at a penny the tide is cheaper than a day and a half of a horse, and that is the entire arithmetic of his mother's life and she is right about it and she hates it.”

---

They went up to the counter at the tide office about the second bell, and there were six people in the room and the pane on the north wall with the light coming off the Reach into it, and the guild's tariff sheet was on the counter, and Corrie Tallow put it in front of him.

He read the first line of it. He got the penny. He did not get the rest.

“Say the second one again,” he said.

“It is one penny a tide,” said Corrie Tallow, “and I have read it to you twice.”

She did not say it a third time. She put her thumb on the paper to keep it still against the sea coming under the door, and she looked at the top of it for about as long as a person looks at a thing they have decided not to say, and then she picked it up and gave it back and said, “Never mind. It is one penny a tide and it is the same penny on every day and you have got it.”

Behind the counter a young man of about twenty-two was copying a column of figures onto a board, and he had the watercolour brush of a clerk and the ink cuffs of a tallyman and the expression of a man who has been four years in a room and still checks everything twice, and he had a piece of paper with about thirty names on it, and Corrie Tallow looked at it and said, “That is the guides.”

“No,” said the young man.

“It is the guides, because I have asked for it four years.”

“It is the carts that paid,” said Jory Vance, twenty-two, of the tide office, “and there is a difference, and I have been told to write the difference down and I have written it down, and you may read it, and it is a piece of paper, not a pane, and you may have a copy for nothing because it is not the guild's record, it is mine.” He pushed it across. “Eleven households, and thirty-one tides of tolls down the side of it, and the Hask boy's name is not on there because he does not pay for a cart.”

“He is the only person on this road who comes over the flat every tide in it.”

“Yes,” said Jory Vance. “And when I asked his mother for a cart she said her boy was the cart.”

---

The other thing she wanted, the thing she had wanted for four years, she said in the road outside with the rain starting again, and she said it in about nine sentences because she had said it four times before and never once got past the second one.

“I have kept the tides of this coast in a book since I was seventeen years old,” she said. “Thirty-one years. Every day of every tide, six a day, and the fourth, the sixth and the seventh, nine, and what the water does, and when it turns, and what the wind does to it, and what happened in the year of the bad seventh, and I have got it in pencil because I have never once had money for anything else and a book of the exercise kind is a penny.

“It is the only one on this road that is right. Their table is printed in a city and it is two hours out by the time it gets to the Reach and it is two hours out in the wrong direction at Slackwater, which I have not been to since I was nineteen, and I am told it is four hours out there, and I do not believe that and I cannot prove it because I have not been.

“I want it in their book. Not to be paid for, I do not want paying for it, I want it *in* it. I want a person at Slackwater to be able to come up to a counter and read what I wrote in the spring of a year they will not remember. I have asked four times. I have got the same four times and it is not a lawyer's answer, and I will tell you the answer because you are a stranger and you have not got any authority at all on this coast and I would like to give it to somebody who cannot do anything with it.”

“What is the answer?”

“They will not fire a hand into a pane,” said Corrie Tallow. “That is the answer. They keep their water on glass because paper goes. A pane does not go, a pane lasts about twenty years and then you fire a new one, and that is the only record this coast has of where the channel goes. And you cannot take a mistake out of glass. If my figures were wrong in the fourth of some tide, and somebody fired them in, and then a boat went on the bank in the seventh of the next tide because of what I wrote in the fourth, then there is a pane on the wall of that building with a woman's pencil in it and no way of taking it out and no way of saying whose fault it was, and they will not do that, and they are right, and I have got thirty-one years in a penny exercise book that answers to a wet sleeve.”

She put her hand flat on the awning pole.

“So that is all of it and it is very small. The only complete record of the water on this coast is in a thing that cannot be argued with, and my thirty-one years are in a thing that cannot be argued with either, and I could put mine into theirs if I stood here a year, and standing here a year is what it costs, and I will be fifty in the spring and I have a stall.”

She turned round and got her stall's board up and did not say anything else about it, and he stood in the rain by a pole and worked out that he had walked eleven miles up a coast road to be told that a question of money on this coast is not a question of money, it is a question of what a record is made of, and that the answer had been sitting in a building he had been in for six hours with a pane on the wall of it.

---

“There is one more thing and then I will leave you alone,” he said. “You said a matter in one place about a strait is not enough.”

“I did say it.”

“Why.”

“Because the crossing is a road and the strait is water, and the guild is right about the water and cannot do one thing about the road, and the tariff is on the road.” She wiped her board. “And because the second place is not the guild, it is Slackwater, and at Slackwater the people think a passage is a day, and here the people think a passage is a penny, and both of them are right and you cannot make one place into the other one, and you certainly cannot make the other one agree by asking it nicely in a room.”

“How is it from here?”

“Nine miles by water if the tide allows it and three hours and not one minute more than that on any tide, and I have not been in twenty-nine years.” She looked at the flat. “You will want to go on the second or the fifth, and not on the fourth, and if you ask anybody here they will tell you the fourth is the one to ask about, and that is because the fourth is the one that is going to happen to you if you are wrong.”

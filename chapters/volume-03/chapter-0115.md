# Chapter 115: What Comes Back

Belva Cray came over the road head on the seventh of the fourth tide at about the first bell with a bundle under her coat the size of a good armful, and she had been four days on the road and had done it with two changes of cart and the eleven shillings that were in a box under a bed, which was the money for a desk, and there is no such thing as a school that needs a desk and everybody in that trade knows it and buys one anyway.

She came down the lane behind a carter who had been paid at the road head and had not been asked to come any further, and she was the only person on that lane who had come from anywhere, and a dozen of them saw her go past and worked out in about four seconds that she was not from the coast.

She stopped at the tally post, which nobody stops at.

“Does that do anything?” she said.

“It counts,” said Seth Garrick, from the door of the lamp-oil shed, where he had been watching a boy of eleven come off the flat with a hand-lead. “Nobody knows what for. I have been counting it in my head for four years and I have nobody to check me.”

“That is a poor answer to a stranger.”

“It is the only one there is,” said Seth Garrick, “and you will find that out about this coast inside two days and it does not improve.”

---

Bettany Rook read the letter at the counter on the fifth, standing up, with the hood back because the counter is on the land side, and it is four lines and it is in a hand she has not seen before, and it is signed with two initials and no name.

> *I am sorry to write to a school. There is a book in your press that came from a glassworks two miles along this coast from a crossing called Wether Cross. The woman who sent the second copy of it to you has been dead eleven years. The woman who made the copy is her granddaughter and she is twenty-four and there is a letter of her own that she has not answered.*
> *I have no way of reading it and there is nobody on this coast who can read it for me, and the wall it was cut from is two miles from where I am writing this, and nobody has been able to put the two together, and the woman who sent the book has been dead eleven years and the woman who copied it is twenty-four and has a letter of her own she has not answered.*
> *If anybody can come and look at it, they should. It costs four days and eleven shillings and I am told that is what it costs, and I am sorry about the order of those two things.*
> *I. V.*

Nobody had ever written a letter to that school that was not about a roof.

The new mistress had opened it on the fifth, in an empty room, and had been able to answer nothing in it, and had carried it nine miles down the coast to Culler Head to a woman who keeps the district's business, and had written two lines on the back of it, which said that the book was a book and the school did not know what it was and would be glad to be told and would rather somebody came.

---

So Belva Cray came, and she waited at the counter from the second bell with a bundle under her coat and eleven shillings gone and four days gone and thirty years of wanting, and she set the bundle down on the counter, and Bettany Rook did not touch it for about a minute.

“Forty-one weeks,” said Belva Cray.

“Was it.”

“Thirty-one. In ninety years. I have counted it twice and got the same and I am not going to say forty-one again.” She had it out of the bundle by then, and it is the size of a good armful and there are two hands in it going back a long way and the second one is not a copy of the first. “It came to us because a school is the only building on this coast with a room in it that is dry and has a lock on it and outlives a person, and that is the whole reason and there is no other. I have had it eleven years in a pigeonhole behind a door and I have never once read it all the way through, because I could not date the first page, and because I was a woman of forty asking questions about old paper and there is nobody to ask.”

“What do you want from it.”

“The number of days a year the low road is under and my school does not open,” said Belva Cray. “I have said nine days at a board meeting twice and a man said it was a fortnight and another said a week was a week, and all three of us were talking about the same nine or ten days and not one of us had a figure. A feeling does not stop a board. Nine families out of eleven have voted to send twenty-six children down the coast to a school with a wall and a stove, and I have until the turn of next year to stop them, and I am the woman who will have to stand in that room and say a number and swear to it. And I have got thirty-one weeks in a book that is older than the school, and I cannot turn thirty-one weeks into days, and I will not stand in a room and say a week is a day.”

Bettany Rook squared the licence sheet on the counter and left her hand on it.

“I have been at that counter six years,” she said. “There is a pane on the north wall upstairs with every tide in it since before I came, and a bar staff two hundred yards down that road that is painted, and a slate that goes once a quarter to this counter in a basket off a bar road four miles along this coast, by a woman of eighty-one who takes her penny a cart out of the same basket and has never been asked for either. And there is not one thing on four hundred miles of this coast that will take a week and give me back days.”

“So I have come a hundred and forty miles for nothing.”

“You have come a hundred and forty miles and spent eleven shillings on a desk,” said Bettany Rook, “and I am not going to be kind about it, because you would not want me to be. There is one thing I will tell you and then I will send for Seth Garrick, because he reads.

“The thing you want is not a thing anybody on this coast has ever kept, and that is the whole of your answer. There is no record of days anywhere on this coast. There is a song up your road that says nine and a man called Danno Ray who has been up it in a cart for nineteen years and says the number is three in a dry turn and eleven in a wet one, and that a hundred years of people up that coast have gone on believing the ground and gone on using the song. There is not one written thing about that road and there never has been, and the reason there is not is that the road is under for a different number of days every year and a record that does not come out the same twice is not worth the keeping to anybody who has ever had to keep one.”

---

They got Corrie Tallow, because Bettany Rook sent a boy down the lane and the boy was back in nine minutes, and Corrie Tallow came up with her slate and her chalk under her arm and did not look pleased about it.

“All right,” she said. “Give it here.”

They walked down to the crossing, because it is eleven people who can hear it and the room holds four, and Corrie Tallow read the school book out under the awning at Wether Cross for an hour and a half to anybody who came, and Belva Cray sat on the end of the bench Seth Garrick lets people sit on and listened to the whole of it, and it is a list of weeks.

That is what came back. Four hundred years of it, and nobody on that coast able to say where it begins, and nobody on this one either; and in the end nobody on it but a man of thirty-one with a coat on and a bench at the far end of it, listening to every week of it go out of a stranger's mouth in the open, and who could not have read one line of it off the page if it had been put in his hands, which is a thing about him that four people on this coast know and about nine more have worked out and not one of whom has said a word.

A week to a line. A mark at the head of a line that is a week. A reason in the margin against a line that is not a week. And the reasons, where they are written at all, are the reasons a coast has for stopping: a bad seventh, water over the low ground, a death, a bad year, somebody ill, once a sentence about a roof.

And Corrie Tallow read out the whole of it and Belva Cray listened and at the end of about two hours of it said:

“Then it is no use to me at all.”

“It is no use to you at all,” said Corrie Tallow.

---

The light had gone flat and Bettany Rook had gone off up the hill to the office with the last of the copying and had not said she was going, and Belva Cray stood up off the bench with her hands in her coat and said the thing she had not said for four days and two changes of cart and eleven shillings.

“There is one more thing and it is the whole of what I have and you will not like it, because it is not the book. It is me.

“I have kept that school eleven years. There are forty-one registers in that press and one of them is a register for every year, and in the top corner of every page of every one of them there is a column for the days the school did not open, and I have written in it myself, in my own hand, every day, for eleven years, and I have never once looked at the bottom of that column, because I thought the old book was a better hand than mine and I was ashamed of wanting the number badly enough to go and get it out of a thing I had made myself.”

Corrie Tallow stood quite still.

“You have eleven years of it in your own hand.”

“Eleven years, and about nine days a year, and I have never added it up because adding it up requires a quiet evening and I have had about nine of those in eleven years.” Belva Cray put her hands down at her sides. “And I have gone a hundred and forty miles to be told that the coast has never kept a record of days, which I could have told anybody, and I would have been right, and I would have been right in a room and it would have been worth nothing, because a rightness with no number in it is not a rightness, it is a mood.”

She picked the bundle up off the step.

“I am not going to say that the trip was wasted, because I have got a thing out of it that I did not have, and it is not the thing I came for. It is that I know now what everybody on this coast knows and did not know I knew, and the knowing is that the coast has no record of days anywhere on it, not at a crossing, not at a bar, not at a school, and the reason is not that people are careless. The reason is that a day is a thing that is only ever true once and everybody on this coast has understood that a long way before I did, and it is the only sensible thing anybody up my coast has ever done, and it has cost every one of us a figure we can stand in a room and swear to.”

Corrie Tallow looked at her for a moment.

“Right,” she said. “Then I am going to copy it.”

“No. There is no need. I have got what I came for and it was not in your country.”

“There is a need,” said Corrie Tallow, “and I am the one who is going to do it, and I am not going to argue about it. You have come a hundred and forty miles with a book under your coat and you are not going home off this coast with nothing in your hands, and if I do not copy it then I have spent this morning listening to about four hundred years of a stranger's coast and got nothing, and I would have been the woman who got nothing, and there is enough of that on this road already.

“Tomorrow, in the yard, from the second bell, and you can read it and check me and take it home with you, and there is no charge for it because you cannot charge for it and because I am not doing it for you.”

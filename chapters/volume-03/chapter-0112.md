# Chapter 112: The Margin of Four

The council of the water and passage guild of Kestrel Reach is nine men and it had not sat since the turn before last, and it sat on the fourth of the fourth tide because the fourth is a bar day and the strait is shut and every cart on this coast that has to come round comes over nine hundred yards of shingle in front of the guild house, so that a body of nine which meets on a day it cannot travel is a body nobody can be got hold of on.

That is not in the charter. It is in the practice, and the practice is about four hundred years old, and it is the only piece of administration on this coast that has ever improved anything.

Bettany Rook put the book on the table with the page turned to a place, and did not sit down, which she has not done at that table in six years, and Jory Vance, who is twenty-two and has been four years on the counter, stood at the wall with his hands behind him and did not know what was coming and would know by the end of it.

“What is on this page is mine,” she said. “It went in on the fifth of the tide before last. I told the office I would bring it to you myself and I have not brought it to you in nine months, and that is on me and not on the office. I am going to take about eleven minutes and then I am going to sit down, and you will do what you like with it.”

She read it out. It is nine lines. It says that a person who guides a cart over Wether Cross on a tide is not a trespasser by that fact alone and is not counted as a cart and is counted as a crossing, and that it is entered in the day book and not in the returns, and that it has her name on it, and that it has no name on it about the boy, on purpose, and she read that reason out too, because she had written it and she was not going to be a person who reads six lines of a thing and keeps the seventh.

Then she read out the cost, which is on the same page in her own hand, and there is one number in it and the number is two pounds.

“It will cost that crossing about two pounds a year in a wage,” she said. “That is the whole of what I am asking you. I did not bring this in until I could stand up in this room and say the number out loud, and I can say it now, and I would like it written down that I said it before anybody else said anything at all.”

Out on the shingle a cart came off the flat and the chain went up and somebody shouted at the boy to bring it down again, and the room did not stop for it.

Two seats at that table had wanted this money spent for about a year. The rest of them had wanted it spent in the sense of wanting somebody else to be the one who said no, which is not a thing anybody will admit to a table and which is what nine people are on a coast most of the time.

Nobody spoke for a while. Then Dunn Pike, fifty-eight, who has two boats and the best berth at this quay, said that if a person counted as a crossing then somebody had to stand at the far end of that flat and count, and that this was not a thing to be argued about.

Nell Crake, forty-one, who keeps a chandlery on the quay and is the only woman on the council and has been for six years, said that it was a good thing and cheap, and that the guild had been getting the counting done for nothing for four years by an accident of a boy being cheaper than a wage.

Gell Roke, seventy-one, who paints the bar staff at the harbour mouth, said nothing at all for a long time.

“Where does the money come from,” said the man who owns four carts, who is thirty-one and has never once pretended not to understand a question.

“Out of the crossing,” said Bettany Rook.

“Then what does the crossing take,” said Gell Roke.

She had known that question was coming since the second bell of the day before. She had got here at the second bell and had the words ready in the order she wanted them, and she has never in her life been at a counter or a table and had a number go out of her mouth in the wrong order.

She said: “The crossing took ninety pounds eighteen last year.”

“Of which how much is carts.”

“Eighty-one pounds sixteen is tallage on carts and goods, taken at a counter in this building by people who do not live on that road.”

“So the road comes to nine pounds two,” said Gell Roke, “and what is the nine pounds two for.”

“Eleven households send somebody over that flat. The chain and the oil and the tarpaulin come out of that and not out of the tallage, because nobody has ever paid for a chain on that flat out of a cart. Three pounds two. The other households’ crossings are worth thirteen shillings between them, and eight pounds nine of it is one household of the eleven, which is a boy of eleven who goes over nine hundred yards of shingle thirty-nine times a week at a penny. So the road gives the guild six pounds, the guild spends three pounds two keeping a chain across a flat it does not maintain, the margin is four, and the wage is two.”

She got to the end of it. There was no sound in the room at all.

“I have never told this council that number,” she said. “I have kept that crossing for six years and I have never once put that four pounds in front of you, and I am not going to stand here and be praised for saying it out loud in the worst week of the season. The passage money is short. You all know that. I am telling you the margin of a road that makes four pounds, and the wage is two, and if you vote for it you will have voted for a wage out of a margin of four. I have wanted it for eleven years and I have never brought it, because I did not want to be the woman who brought it.”

“All right,” said Gell Roke.

---

Then he said the thing, and it is the reason the vote went five to four and not nine to nothing, and it is the reason four people at that table were right and the five were not.

He said, first, the money. He said it without heat and he said it the way a man does sums on a paint tin for a long time. Two pounds out of four leaves two, and the two is what a bad year eats, and this has been a bad year, and the passage money is short, and a council that votes a wage it has to withdraw in the winter is a council that has told the road it does not matter what happens on it. That was the argument the four used, and it closed, and the cooper and the fish factor used it and neither of them had anything to put on top of it.

And then Gell Roke said the other thing, and he said it in nine or ten words and did not look at Bettany Rook while he said it.

“I have painted that staff for the guild for nineteen years,” he said. “Nobody has paid me one penny of it and I have never asked and I am not asking now, and I would like this table to notice that I am the only man at it being asked to vote against paying a stranger two pounds a year for standing at a post. I have not got an argument that will carry in a room, and I am voting no, and I would like it written down that I know exactly what I sound like.”

Nobody wrote anything down.

“It is not a good reason,” he said. “It is nineteen years of a man who does not want to be told what he has been doing for nothing. The money is the reason. I would not have voted no on the money alone, because the money is a bad year and a bad year is not a reason to leave a boy on a flat by himself three days a week. So it is the money and it is me, and I have said both, and I would rather the table had only the one.”

The vote was five to four. Dunn Pike, Nell Crake, the man of four carts, the ship’s carpenter and a boy of twenty-three who had one seat because his uncle died in the spring voted for the wage. Gell Roke, the cooper, the fish factor and a man of sixty-eight who had not voted for anything in nine years voted against it.

The five were not wrong. There was nobody on that flat on a bad sixth with a chain across it and nobody at the far end, and a line in a day book saying a person was a crossing was a line somebody would have to stand behind.

The four were right, because the money is the money, and the money was three pounds two short that season, and four people at that table could add.

Bettany Rook sat down at last. The finding came out of the room about four minutes later from the man of sixty-eight, who had said nothing for the whole hour, and he said it in the voice of a person asking where a thing is.

“Roa,” she said. “I am sorry. What is it.”

“The guild’s charter,” said the retired man. “Can it be changed this year.”

“No,” said Bettany Rook. “It cannot be changed for a year, and the year is not mine to shorten.”

“Then what are we voting about,” he said.

“A wage,” said Bettany Rook.

Nobody answered that, and nobody had to, and that was the whole of the afternoon. It went into the minute book in four lines, and the four lines are the true shape of it: the crossing is to be counted in persons; a person is to be paid two pounds a year out of what the crossing takes; the season is short; and the money is not there.

The one thing nobody at that table knew, including the woman who had been harbourmaster for six years, was that the money for the fifth pane had been due at Thornglass the day before and had not come — because there was no pane on the order that anybody at that table had ever seen, and because a rule that cannot be changed for a year is not a thing a council is asked about in a season, and because nobody had thought to ask.

---

The System put the vote in front of Ilyan Vester at the fourth bell, on the fourth day of a tide, on the step outside a room with a bell on its door worked by a wire and not by a hand, and it was a record of a thing entered in a minute book, and a minute book is the only durable thing on this coast.

**THE COUNCIL OF THE WATER AND PASSAGE GUILD OF KESTREL REACH IS NINE. IT VOTED FIVE TO FOUR THAT A PERSON BE PAID TWO POUNDS A YEAR OUT OF WHAT THE CROSSING OF WETHER CROSS TAKES, TO COUNT CROSSINGS IN PERSONS AND NOT IN CARTS. THE CROSSING TOOKS NINETY POUNDS EIGHTEEN. EIGHTY-ONE POUNDS SIXTEEN OF IT IS TALLAGE TAKEN AT A COUNTER BY PEOPLE WHO DO NOT LIVE ON THAT ROAD. THE MARGIN ON THE ROAD IS FOUR POUNDS. THE WAGE IS TWO. THE SEASON IS SHORT.**
**WHAT IS NOT DECIDED: WHETHER A BODY THAT VOTES A WAGE IT CANNOT PAY HAS VOTED AT ALL, AND WHETHER THE FOUR WHO VOTED AGAINST KNEW WHAT THE ROAD TAKES AND THE FIVE DID NOT.**

He was standing on the step with the door shut behind him and the wire going over its pulley, and it had come to him there and not in the room, because the room had one person in it who could not read and about eight who could and would not have read it if it had been put in their hands.

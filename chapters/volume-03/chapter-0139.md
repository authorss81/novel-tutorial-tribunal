# Chapter 139: A Day Given Away

It is a good day on the fifth of the eighth tide and the flat takes six, and the water at the north end goes out long and soft on a good day, and at about the eighth bell the lowest of it is four hours and a bit off the turn and about two hours after the ebb has finished doing its work on the gravel.

Halse Tarrant got his boat off the top of the bank at the first bell and did not launch her until the sixth, because there is no point in a boat being in the water for two hours doing nothing, and he had a lead line and a book and a piece of chalk and a boat of his own that had not been out in about a year and was sound.

The four things were a lead line, a boat, a man who can sound, and a day, and he had had three of them since about the sixth year and had not asked anybody for the fourth, and on the fifth of the eighth tide somebody gave him the fourth and nobody thanked the man for it.

---

Cleve Gannock came down the lane at about the second bell with a pair of beasts yoked and a cart behind them and a rope’s end in his hand, and he is thirty-one and he does not go on the water and he deals in what the flat boat brings in, and he has a house up above the yard with a good roof on it that four generations of this coast have known about.

“I am going to the landing,” he said. “I have got two hours before the turn and then the water is off the boat and whatever is in the boat stays in the boat till the next good day, and the beasts are by the day and the cart is by the day and I have paid for both of them whatever happens to me, so it is mine either way and it does not matter what I do with the morning.”

“I have a boat and a line and I can sound,” said Halse Tarrant, “and I have not had a day since the fourth year.”

Halse Tarrant did not ask him for it. That is the whole of what happened and it is worth being exact about, because it is the shape of every single thing on this coast: the man who had the day and the man who needed the day were standing in a lane four hundred yards from a crossing and neither of them asked the other for anything, and the day was given away in about nine words by a man who was on his way somewhere.

“You are not going to ask me,” said Cleve Gannock.

“No.”

“Right.” He took the rope’s end out of his hand and looked up the lane at the boat. “Then I will tell you what I have got, because I would rather say it than have you work it out of me. I have a cart and a pair of beasts and a day, and the cart and the beasts are going to the landing with or without me, and the only thing my day buys is me standing at a landing being useful to a man at the Quay, and he does not need me until about the ninth bell. So take the day. That is all of it, and you are not to say anything about it, and if anybody in this yard starts on about it I will tell them it was the beasts that were late.”

He took the cart up the lane to the landing and was two hours late and told the man at the Quay it was the beasts.

Nobody said thank you. Not the man with the bad leg, who was standing in the lane, and not the man in the boat, and not the three people at the yard, and the man who gave the day away was not thanked then or afterwards and told a man at a landing two hours later that it was the beasts that were late, and that was the end of it and it was the correct end of it.

---

A man of thirty-one was standing in that lane at about the third bell and had heard all of it and had worked out inside about eleven seconds what he was supposed to do next.

He could not refuse it.

He could have said to a man of thirty-one in a lane that he should not give away a day’s hire for a stranger’s wall, and he would have been wrong, because he has no standing on this coast and has never had one, and because a man who is not from this coast does not get to decide what a Gannock’s day is worth to a Gannock, and because he has been here forty-eight days and has never once been asked what he thinks and has never once been owed an opinion by anybody, and a thing he is not owed is not a thing he is entitled to hand out.

He could have said that the man on the boat should not spend a day somebody else lost on a question nobody asked him about.

He could have said that the answer would be a number, and that a number is the right instrument, and that the right instrument is the whole of what this coast has needed for two years.

He said none of it. He has no standing to accept it either, and there is no form of any kind on four hundred miles of this coast on which a man with a bad leg from a city four hundred miles behind him could write *a day was given*, and there is no one to write it to, and there is no one to write it for, and being the person standing nearest is not a qualification and has never been a qualification and cost a woman of eighty-one nothing and a clerk of twenty-two nothing and a man of thirty-one the best part of a day four times.

What he did was take out the penny exercise book from the inside pocket of his coat, and turn to a fresh leaf, and write four lines.

*On the fifth of the eighth tide, at the second bell, at the head of the lane at the north end, Cleve Gannock gave a day to Halse Tarrant. It was his cart, his pair of beasts and his own day, and he was two hours late at a landing because of it and told the man there it was the beasts. Nobody asked him for it and nobody thanked him for it.*

*It is mine to write down and it is not mine to decide.*

The date went on it, and that it was his. And there was no reason on the page, and there had been no reason on the page for five pages now, and he noticed himself not putting one and did not stop, and it was a habit.

He put the book back and went and sat on the bank above the boat with his weight on his good side and watched a man of fifty-eight with a lead line go out into four feet of green water at the eighth bell.

---

The line went over the seaward face at the inner end first, because that is the order you do it in, and the man in the boat sounded it every nine feet along the whole two hundred and sixty yards, and wrote nothing down for about an hour and a half and then started writing, and the boat came in at about the ninth bell with the man in it and the lead line coiled on the thwarts and the book shut.

Halse Tarrant sat down on the bank above his own boat and did not say anything for about ten minutes, which is not his way, and then he said it, to the three people in the lane and the man on the bank, and it came out in the trade’s mouth and it was about eleven yards long.

“The toe of the seaward face is gone,” he said, “along about eleven yards of it, and it is gone in the middle, not at the ends, and it is gone to about two feet of depth. There is no bottom holding gravel under the bottom course of that wall for eleven yards. There is stone in courses and then there is the sea.

“And I have got that in a book in a shed with the date on it and the number in it, and I will swear to that book in a room in front of anybody who wants me to, for as long as I am alive, and that is the whole of what it is worth, and it is worth a good deal more than a stake with a number on it, and it is worth exactly nothing at all tonight.”

He shut the book.

“And here is why, and I am going to say it once and then I am going to feed the beasts, because I have not eaten since the second bell. Nobody on this coast will do anything about a number in a book. There is a table on a wall a mile up that hill that is the only instrument anybody will act on and it does not come out of that building, and there is a pane on a wall in a shed two miles along this coast that is about the same wall and is three years old and disagrees with the first one in two columns out of eleven, and the two columns are the ones that would settle this, and the man who made the second one cannot get at the first one and the first one does not come out of its room.

“So I have got a number and there are two photographs that do not agree, and one of the photographs is nineteen years and eight months old and the other one is three years old, and my number is from today, and today is the best day anybody has had on that wall in about fifteen years, and none of the three of them is going to travel to meet either of the others, and I have not got anybody to carry it and I have not got a cart, because the cart went to the landing two hours late and I am not going to say that out loud twice in one day.”

---

The post came up the coast road in the afternoon and Seth Garrick read it out in the lane at the head of it, standing with his back on his own door, and he read it twice because he reads everything twice and cannot read quickly, and the first one was the guild’s own office and the second one was a letter with a seal on it from a city four hundred miles off.

The guild’s was four lines and it was correct.

*That a record of a moving thing is a working instrument. That instruments do not travel, and that a man who has one is the only man who can use it, and that a body in an office two hundred miles from a wall cannot act on a number taken from a boat at the north end.*

*That the guild will sound that wall at its own cost and in its own season, and that the season’s money is not in the arch, and that it will not be this season, and that the guild does not send a copy of a working instrument to a person who has one.*

*That the guild is not in dispute that the wall is nineteen years and eight months old and that it is to be walked.*

“That is four sentences of the truest thing anybody has said to me in a year,” said Halse Tarrant. “And it is a refusal, and every part of it is right, and the guild is right about the instruments and they are right about the season and they are right that they will do it at their own cost when the money is in, and I want it written down that I have not got one single thing to say against any part of that, and I am not going to argue with a guild about the one thing they know best.”

“And the fourth line,” said Seth Garrick. “They want to be walked. That is not a letter that costs them anything and that is the first sentence anybody in that building has written to this coast in about two years that was not about a sum.”

The second letter was short and it had a seal on it and it came from a city four hundred miles behind this one and it was from a woman who is a magistrate and who was out-voted in a room and has not been out-voted since and is not going to be.

It asked two questions. It asked what body stood behind the crossing at Wether Cross, and it asked what instrument governed the counting of it, and it said that both questions had been answered in the return for the last quarter and that both answers were the same answer, and that the same answer had been given for eight years.

And it said one other thing, and the other thing was a request, and the request was that a person ought to be able to be told no by the harbourmaster’s office in a place where the refusal was written down and could be shown to somebody else, and that she did not care in the least what the answer was and only wanted it to be somewhere.

A woman of forty-eight at a stall at Wether Cross read it once and gave it back.

“We have not got that and we are not going to have it,” she said, “and I am not going to be unkind about it to a stranger four hundred miles off, because she has asked for it in the only way anybody ever asks for anything that is not money, and because she is right, and because a body two hundred miles off that is asking a coast to have a box for refusals in is a body that has not noticed what this coast does with a person who says no to a question she was never asked. It has got a stone. Four feet of it, a hundred years old, and she answers about the water, and nobody may ask a second, and that is the only door anybody on this coast has ever had and it is not a door anybody can write a refusal on. And we are not building one, and I would say so to her office as well as to her and I would not put a name at the end of it.”

Nobody wrote anything down, and the letter went up the coast road to the house it came from, and nobody on this coast was any nicer or any kinder about a magistrate than she had been about them, and nothing about it softened.

---

In the same post, in the same cart, in the same hand as the guild’s own four lines, was the guild’s quarter.

It had gone up the coast road in the fourth week of a season, and it had been read by a building two hundred miles off and by nobody else, and it carried a line for a person to be paid two pounds a year out of what the crossing takes, to count the crossings in persons and not in carts, and that line had had no name on it since the fourth of the fourth tide, and the office had read it and had done one of the two things it can do and it had left it.

It was not an oversight. The guild’s own quarter carries that line every quarter, and the clerk who reads it two hundred miles off sees a line with a wage on it and no person on it, and he has two choices and neither of them is a good one, and this quarter he chose the one that writes nothing.

Which means that the vote of a council of nine sits in a book in a building two hundred miles up this coast for a year and does nothing at all.

It will not be reopened. Nobody in that building has asked the office here to fill it in and nobody here has written and asked. In about a month the next quarter will go up in a cart on the coast road and a clerk will read a line with a wage on it and no person on it, and he will leave it, and the next one after that he will leave it too, and by the turn of the year the paper with the vote in it will be in a drawer with last year’s, and a year is a long enough time that everybody who was in the room will have found something else to be doing.

And a man of thirty, who is the only person on this coast that line is for, has a lamp-oil shed at the head of the lane and counted that post in his head for four years and got it wrong by about nine hundred and said so out loud in front of thirty people, and he has not asked for it, and nobody is going to ask him, and nobody in that building two hundred miles off is ever going to know that there was a man on this coast the wage was voted for, because the line is blank and the quarter does not say.

That is what a blank does. It does not stop a thing and it does not refuse one. Nobody on this coast is to be asked about it and nobody is going to be asked about it, and in about a year somebody in that office will go looking for the minute that voted it, and will find that the minute says a person is to be paid, and will not be able to find the person, and will put the minute back.

Seth Garrick shut the door of the oil shed behind him and did not say anything, and nobody asked him, and nobody is going to.

At about the ninth bell the water came back over the north end and a man of fifty-eight shut a book with a number in it and went to feed a pair of beasts that belonged to somebody else, and a man of thirty-one was sitting on a bank above a boat with a leg that had not been his own since the fourth of the third tide and a page in his coat with a name on it and nothing at the end of the name.

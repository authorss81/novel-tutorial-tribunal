# Chapter 201: The Road That Is Not Where It Began

The road is nine days long from the last stile on the Shelf and the three of them came to the end of it on the second day of the fiftieth week, and the basin in front of them was red, and low, and had a floor that went out further than the eye would carry in the heat.

Nobody had sent for them. That is the whole of how it happened and it is worth saying at the top because everything else in this basin goes the same way: a road is nine days long, a man heard a thing at one end of it, and the man who heard it was not the man who acted, and there was a whole season in between and no form for asking what happened in it.

Tarin Keel put the board down on the flat ground at the head of the last rise and got a boot on it so the wind could not have it, and the board was a plank with figures on it in his own hand and a strip of red rag tied to one corner, and he had made the figures himself, twice, and he had thrown the first set away.

“It set out at the stile on the bearing I have here,” he said. “It arrives four miles east of that and about a mile and a half south, and I have walked it both ways, and I have measured the last two miles twice from the Kettle Stone and the two measurements agree with each other and neither of them agrees with the stile.”

“Then something moved,” said Ilyan Vester.

“Something moved and then moved back, or it was never there, or there are two roads and the one you walk is the one that gets walked.” Tarin took his boot off the board. “I have been in a country for nineteen years where the answer to a road that is not where it was is that nobody has measured it in a long time. I have measured it. I am not going to tell you which of the three it is, because I do not know, and a man who says he knows when he does not know is the reason roads go wrong.”

Nobody argued with him about it. The road ran south off the flat and dropped into a valley of red clay with a line of scrub in the bottom of it, and the scrub was dead except in four places, and the four places were where something came up out of the ground that was not a root.

“It crosses the ridge,” said Ewan Troke.

He had been sitting on a stone at the side of the road since before the three of them came over the last rise, and he was twenty-nine and he was a chainman of the water-seat at Sallowby, and he had come up the Shelf road four days behind them with a case of papers and a threshing drum that had been at a smith’s on the Shelf for a man who wanted it back.

Nobody sent him and nobody knew he was coming. He had heard about them at a market on the Shelf and had made up his own mind about the road and had walked the other way round to be useful at both ends, and he said so in about eleven words when he had said good afternoon, and then he had gone back to his stone.

“What ridge,” said Tarin Keel.

“The marl.” Ewan Troke got up off it and put his thumb south. “Four miles that way, running with the water, and it is about a quarter of a mile wide and it is the only ground in this basin that a house will stand on above the flood line. The whole of Hollow Sallow is on the mud below the north end of it. Everything above the water in this basin is on that ridge, and I have four hundred and ten people in that village and about ninety houses and eleven of them are on the low side of it.”

He stopped there and did not say the rest of it, and it took about half a minute for the three of them to work out that he had stopped on purpose, because he had a chain on his belt and a habit of laying a thing down before he said it.

“There is a word in two deeds,” he said. “Both of them use it and both of them define it, and the definitions are not the same, and I have got a copy of the page where the definitions are and I have not been able to make the two of them agree for eleven years and I have asked twice and I got told it was not a thing anybody could ask about.”

He put the case down on the flat ground and undid the strap and took out a sheet of oiled paper, and the sheet was a copy, and the copy was in a hand that was not his, and he had made the copy himself on the third day of the week before last with a rule and a flat stone and a piece of lead, and he said all of that before anybody asked him for any of it.

---

Here is what was on the sheet, and the sheet is a copy of one page of the chain-book of the water-seat of Sallowby, and the chain-book itself is in a locked press in Sallowby in a building with eleven seats in it, and this is what a man of twenty-nine wrote out in the open with a rule and a flat stone and a piece of lead.

> THE MARL RIDGE, FROM THE SLUICE STONE AT HOLLOW SALLOW TO THE KETTLE STONE AT COLDBECK, LIES IN A STRIP CALLED THE COMMON IN EVERY DEED THAT MENTIONS IT, AND NO DEED ON THIS SHEET SAYS WHOSE.
> THE ROAD FROM THE SALLOW FLATS TO THE SHELF CROSSES THAT STRIP AT ELEVEN CHAINS FROM THE NORTH END, AND THE CROSSING IS BY A CAUSEWAY OF STONE AND GRAVEL PUT IN BY PERSONS UNNAMED.
> THE WORD COMMON IS DEFINED IN THE MARGIN OF EACH DEED AND NOT IN THE BODY OF EITHER, AND THE TWO MARGINS WERE WRITTEN BY TWO HANDS, AND THE OLDER OF THEM IS EIGHTY-TWO YEARS OLD AND THE NEWER IS NINE TURNS OLD.
> ENTRY OF THE SECOND, FOURTH LINE: THE CROSSING IS NOT BY RIGHT AND IS NOT BY GIFT, AND WAS FOUND SO WHEN THE MAN CAME TO LOOK AT IT.

Nobody read that out loud on the road. It was read out twice in a room over the next two days, by two different people, and the two readings differed in one word, and the word was right, and the difference was found in a margin, and neither of the two people who read it out knew that they had differed until the third person in the room asked each of them the same question separately.

“What is the margin on the first,” said Sera Quill.

“There are two,” said Ewan Troke. “One is the salt-pan year, and the word is set out the way it is set out in a covenant of the pans, which is that a thing is common to every house that stands on it and every beast may go over it and no beast may be stopped. That is the old one. That has been in the pan books since before the first shaking, and every house on the ridge holds by it, and eleven of them are on the low side and they have never once been able to make a beast go any other way.”

“And the second.”

“The second is nine turns old, and a turn is a year in this basin and the count runs from the first shaking, which was the big one, twelve years back, and it is in the hand of a man in Sallowby who is not a lawyer and did not say he was one, and it says that a thing called common in a deed of this basin is ground held of the water-seat and worked by the Anner, and that no house stands on it and no beast goes over it, and the reason the last part is in the wording is that somebody asked.” He stopped. “Somebody asked, in the ninth turn, and the ninth turn is three years back, and I do not know who asked and there is no note of it anywhere in this basin and I have looked for one for two of those three years.”

The sun was coming down onto the west end of the ridge and the ridge was the colour of a dirty coin, and about two miles off there was a pan of standing water with a board drawn through it and a man walking behind the board, and neither of them turned round to look at four people on a road four miles from anywhere.

“Eighty-two years old,” said Ewan Troke, following nobody’s thought. “That is the old margin. Pan books at Coldbeck go back further than that. My father’s father kept one and I have his boards and they are in my mother’s house because there is nowhere in Sallowby to keep a board.”

He said that last part without any weight on it at all, and it was the first thing anybody had said in four days about where a record was kept rather than what a record said, and nobody remarked on it then and there is not going to be a remark on it later.

---

The four of them came down off the flat at about the time the light goes and got as far as a fold of ground above the dry bed, which is where a person who has walked that whole road stops, and the three of them had said the thing they had come to say somewhere around the fourth day and had not improved on it since, and it was worth putting down in the three mouths it came out of.

“A date,” said Sera Quill. “One date, agreed to by everybody, so that there is one first day of the year in this basin.”

“A document,” said Ilyan Vester. “Four pages. One dam, one height, one day, and the party that carries it.”

“And out of the basin before the grain year turns,” said Tarin Keel, “because I have a road to be on and a woman of twenty-eight here with a licence that has a term on it, and neither of those things improves by sitting in a valley.”

“No advocate,” said Sera Quill. “I have said it on nine days of road and I am saying it here so that there is no part of the next four weeks anybody can be told later on was arranged.”

“I am not a party to anything and I am not going to be,” said Ilyan Vester, “and if anybody in this basin asks me what I am, the answer is that I am a man who writes down what people say in their own words and keeps the parts apart, and it is a worse answer than a lawyer’s and it is the only one I have.”

He said that in the ordinary way and he did not say the other half of it, which is that he could not have read the four pages if he had drafted them, and which is the reason the four pages are going to be somebody else’s and is the reason he had come nine days to be the least useful person in a valley.

They put up two tents and the wind came off the dry bed at about the closing of the light, and it was cold out of the sun and there was no water in the bed and there was a sound of boards being drawn through something a long way off.

Ilyan Vester took out a penny exercise book and a stub of lead and wrote the day on the page, because that is a thing he does on a day and has done on every day of two hundred and one of them, and the day in this basin is not a day of the Shelf and the number is not a number of the Shelf.

*ONE HUNDRED AND FORTY-NINE DAYS SINCE THE SECOND SHAKING.*

He looked at it for a moment and then he wrote under it, in the same hand and the same pencil, that the Shelf kept its own count and that the two numbers were not the same number and that he was going to keep them apart in this book because a man who mixes them is a man who will one day be asked a question about a year and will not be able to say which of the two he was being asked.

He did not say any of that out loud, and neither Sera Quill nor Tarin Keel asked him what the page was, and they had been walking nine days with a man who keeps a book and neither of them had asked him what was in it once.

Then the man with the board came over the top of the ridge on his way down, because that is the way the pans are worked and the fold of ground is between him and his own house, and he stopped, and looked at four people and two tents, and said good evening, and waited, in the manner of a man who has decided to be spoken to.

“Evening,” said Ewan Troke. “That is the water.”

“Good,” said the man with the board. “There is a lot of it about.”

He put the board down against a stone and took out a stone of his own and looked at the sky and worked something out of it, and it took him no time at all, and then he took his board up and went on down the far side, and he did not know that four people had come nine days to look at his valley and he would not have cared and there is no reason on earth why he should have.

Ewan Troke got his case shut and the strap over it.

“There is nothing wrong with your four pages,” he said. “I have read them, both of the deeds, and the margin, and the chain-book, and the pages that are in the press, and there is not one thing in your four pages that is not true. I have been trying for eleven years to find the thing in them that is not true and there is not one.”

“That is a very good result,” said Tarin Keel.

“It is not a result at all. It is a finding, and the finding is worse than a bad page.” He shifted the case to his other hand. “There is nobody in this basin who can be in the room when they are read.”

Nobody asked him what he meant and he did not wait to be asked.

“There is a room in Sallowby with eleven seats in it and about four hundred people in this basin and none of the four hundred has ever been in that room, and the ones who have been in it are the same five or six men who have been in it every year since the first shaking, and they are not all in it at once because one of them is at Coldbeck and one of them is dead.” He got a hand out and put it flat on the case, which is a thing he does, and there is no reason on earth why a chainman should do that. “Your four pages are true. And to say them out loud in that room there has to be somebody in the room who has got a reason to be in the room on that day, and there is not one, and I have tried to get one for eleven years and I have not got one.”

The light went off the ridge and the valley came up out of the dark underneath them, and somewhere down there a woman was going to open a gate and shut it again, and neither of those facts was going to be in anybody’s four pages.

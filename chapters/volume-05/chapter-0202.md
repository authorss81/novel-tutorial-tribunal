# Chapter 202: Fifty Saltings, And A Count Nobody Keeps

The pans above Coldbeck are on a shelf of flat ground where the red clay has been dug out to about the depth of a person’s shin, and there are eleven of them, and each of them is about the length of two carts, and the water in them is the colour of weak tea and does not move when the wind moves.

At the second light on the third day of the fiftieth week there was a man drawing a board through the fourth pan, and he was sixty-one, and he had been drawing a board through salt water for thirty years, and his name was Fen Tregerran and nobody in this basin had ever asked him a single question.

He was not waiting for anybody. He was working, and the three of them came up the bank because the bank is the only way up and he had a kettle and they had a bladder, and that is the whole of the reason the three of them were ever in the same field.

“You will want the head of it,” he said, without turning round. “Everybody who comes up here wants the head of it and nobody ever comes twice.”

The head of it is a board about five feet long with notches cut in the upper edge, one notch a day, and beside it on a flat stone there are two heaps of stones, a large one and a small one, and the small one holds seven and the large one holds a thousand six hundred and thirty-nine, and he counts the large one in tens because counting stones one at a time is a thing a man of sixty-one does not do twice in a day.

“A hundred and forty-nine notches,” he said. “And I will tell you what the stones are for before you ask me, because the last four of you asked me and then asked me that. The stones are for the pans, not for the days. Eleven pans, so eleven stones a day, and a man who has not turned up on a day has had his stones carried forward and the small heap is short, and you can see the small heap from the bank.”

He put the board down on the near side of the fourth pan and came and sat on the flat stone, which is the thing a man of sixty-one does instead of standing.

“You are not from the Seat,” he said.

“No.”

“You are not from the Pit.”

“No.”

“Then there is nobody here you have come to see, and you have come up a bank for water, and the water is there.” He nodded at the bladder. “And I am going to tell you about the notches, because I have been telling people about the notches for thirty years and about nine of them stayed, and the other hundred and forty went to the Sallow with my name on a heap of stones, and there is nobody in this basin who knows that the count has been out for a hundred and fifty days.”

Nobody said anything, and none of them had come up the bank for the count, and that is exactly how it happened.

---

The day-count is not his. It is the water-book’s, and the water-book is the Anner’s, and it was begun at the second shaking by the man who kept the water-book then, who was not a scholar and did not pretend to be. The year-count above it is older than that and it is in the same book, and it was put there on a door frame by a man in the first year, and his eight is four short of the true eight, and he knew it, and he put it right in his own hand in the front of the book, in a better hand than the rest of it because he was frightened of it, and he died eleven years ago without anybody else in this basin knowing that the years in that book had been numbered wrong from the first to the eighth and had been corrected once and never since.

“That is the first thing anybody in this basin can be shown to be wrong about,” Fen Tregerran said. “One man, one door frame, eight years, and he said so himself in the front of his own book, in a better hand than the rest of it because he was frightened of it. Nine of us know that. Nine of us, and about four hundred people, and the four hundred are not interested.”

“Why are you one of the nine,” said Sera Quill.

“Because I keep a count too.” He turned the board over on his knee. “And it is not a day count and it is not a turn count either. It is pans. Eleven pans, and a salting is finished when the eleventh pan has given up its salt, and a salting is a drying, and a drying is as long as the weather makes it and no two are the same length.”

“And how long is a salting.”

“That,” said Fen Tregerran, “is the thing nobody in this basin has ever been able to tell me.”

He let that sit for about as long as it takes to draw a board through a pan and back.

“Not because nobody knows. Because there is no answer to give. A dry year, a salting is about seven weeks and there are not many of those. A wet year, a salting runs eleven weeks, and I have got that board too, and it is the board my mother sold a pig for.” He put the board down and picked up a stone and turned it over. “There is no length. You cannot get from my count to that book in the pit office, because my count has no length in it. It is a shape. It is fifty shapes a year, more or less, and the shapes do not start in the same place twice, and there is no way to put a shape and a number on the same page.”

Sera Quill had been sitting on the bank with her hands in her lap for about four minutes, and this is the thing she is for, and she asked it in nine words and no more, and it is the only question anybody in this basin was asked that day.

“Which of the two do your boards date.”

Fen Tregerran looked at her for a while.

“That is not a hard one,” he said. “That is the first question anybody has asked me in eleven years and it is the only one there is.”

He got up and went to the head of the fourth pan and came back with a second board, thinner than the first, and it was grey with salt and had been out in the weather a long time, and he did not offer it and she did not take it and he held it out flat on his palm for about four seconds and then put it away, which is a thing a man does when he has decided to answer a question and not to prove anything.

“Salt-pan year,” he said. “Always. Every board I have got is the same. I was asked once, nine years ago, by a man from the Seat who wanted a deed dated, and I said I would not put my boards against a turn count, and he said it was only a formality, and I said it was not a formality, it was a different number, and he wrote that down and I have not seen him since and I do not know what he did with it.”

“Who was he.”

“I do not know his name and I would not have known it.” Fen Tregerran picked his board up. “He was not a lawyer. Nobody in this basin is a lawyer. There is a man in Sallowby who writes deeds for people and he is careful and he is not one, and he will copy anything you bring him and he will not tell you what it means, and that is the arrangement and everybody knows it and everybody is afraid of it.”

---

The thing that made it a finding and not a conversation is that it was said in front of four people who could have contradicted it, and none of them did, and the reason none of them could is the plainest thing about the Red Basin.

The salt-pan year is the only count in it that is not about land.

There is no salt-pan year on any deed in this basin. There is no day out of that water-book on any board at Coldbeck. Every household in Hollow Sallow has an interest in what the water-book’s day count says about a boundary and not one of the four hundred and ten people in that village has any interest whatever in how long a drying takes, and that is the only reason a man of sixty-one could stand on a bank in the second light and say out loud, to four strangers, that his life’s work has no length in it and cannot be made to have one.

The pan count is the one clock in this basin that nobody has ever claimed.

“You have known it eleven years,” said Ilyan Vester.

“I have known it since the wet one, and that was the sixth winter I had boards for, so I have known it longer than that and I have had a copy of the water-book’s first page since the second winter, and I have not taken it up a hill in nine years, because every time I have gone down that road somebody has told me that it was not the salt’s business.”

He said it without any bitterness in it at all, and then he did the thing he had come to do, which was draw the board through the fourth pan and back again and set it on the near side to dry, and he did it the way he has done it every day for thirty years, and the sound of it was the loudest thing on the shelf.

Sera Quill wrote nothing down. She had a book in her coat and she did not take it out, and Ilyan Vester noticed that she did not and did not say anything about it, and it was the first time in nine days he had seen her leave a thing unwritten on purpose.

A hundredweight of that salt comes to the pans at a pound and fourpence and the pans above Coldbeck are the only place in this basin that a person can count a thing by and be sure of it, and about a hundredweight a week goes down the bank on a barrow in the season, and a hundredweight is twenty-four shillings, and twenty-four shillings fifty-two times is sixty-two pounds and eight, and the man who does the barrow gets five pounds and four of it and he is seventy-one, and his name is on no document anywhere, and nobody in this volume has met him and nobody in this volume will.

The day was said once that morning, in Fen Tregerran’s own mouth, while he was putting the board down, and it was the hundred and fiftieth out of the water-book, and it was the second time in two days that a number out of that water-book had been said out loud to a stranger, and both times it had been in answer to a question and not as a thing anybody was giving anybody.

Nobody asked him anything else, and the three of them went down the bank with a bladder of brine-water each and did not go up it again that day, and the hundred and forty-nine notches stayed where they were, with the large heap of stones beside them at a thousand six hundred and thirty-nine, which is eleven times a hundred and forty-nine, and thirty years of that is the whole of a man’s life in a heap, and it is not a record of anything anybody in this basin can use, and it is the only one of the three counts in this valley that is true.

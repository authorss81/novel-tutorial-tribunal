# Chapter 222: Nobody Names A House

There are two houses in Hollow Sallow with new doors on them and there is no mark on either of them, and one of the two is a house that was a barn in the year the marks were cut, and the other is a house that was a house the whole time and had its door replaced in the winter before the water came, and there is nobody alive in that lane who was ever told to cut a mark on a door.

The under-manager of the Anner came down the hill at the second light on the second day of that week for the second time in three days, and he did not come into the lane, he stood at the top of it, and he had a reason for coming and the reason was arithmetic and he gave it in his own mouth in about a minute.

“The Anner will pay for a thing that is in a list,” said Jory Vellan. “That is not a threat and it is not a condition, it is how a body of eleven men buys anything at all. There is no form in this basin for a body of eleven men to pay four hundred and ten people money for nineteen houses, and there is one form for a body of eleven men to pay for work, and the work has to be on a list, and the list has to say where the work is.”

“Then we list them,” said Tarin Keel.

“No,” said the under-manager. “I have been in this basin nineteen years and I have watched two things about lists and I will tell you both of them and then I will go up the hill. A list of houses is not a list of roofs. A list of houses is a list of people. And the day there is a list of people in it, the next house that goes up in this lane is a house that is not on the list, and the house after that one is a house that somebody will want on the list, and you will have a lane in about four years in which the two ends of it are argued about on the grounds of a piece of paper and neither end of it is a person.”

He put his hand out flat, at nothing, the way a man does over water.

“I have got two doors down there with no mark on them and I noticed them coming down the hill and I have said nothing about them until this second. I am not going to say one word about them again. I am telling you what a list of houses turns into and then I am going to hold a dam.”

---

The question went into the lane that morning the way questions go into lanes, which is that everybody talks about it at once and about nine people are talking within a hundred yards of each other and nobody is talking to anybody in particular.

The strongest argument for naming them was a woman of about thirty-one with two children and a house at the ninth door, and she made it without raising her voice, and her argument was that if a house is not on a paper then a house cannot be argued for, and that she has been argued with for eleven years by a man with a book about a strip of ground four miles north of here and she has never once been on a paper that anybody could produce.

The strongest argument against was a man of seventy-four who was born in the second house up the lane, and he made it in thirteen words while he was putting a lid on a bucket of tar, and he had a bucket of tar in his hand all of that week and nobody ever asked him about the eleven marks and nobody ever asked him this either, and the answer came out of him the way water comes out of a crack.

“They are not houses,” he said. “They are the ones with the tar on them.”

And then, because about four people had gone quiet, he said the rest of it, and it is the only arithmetic any man of seventy-four in that lane has ever offered anybody.

“Eleven marks. I have watched this lane since I was born and I can tell you how many times a roof has gone up at the bottom end of it and I can tell you it is more than eleven and I can tell you it is fewer than a number I would like to say out loud, and every one of them was somebody putting a roof on a house for his own reasons, and not one of them was a decision about a line. That is what a line is. It is a mark somebody cut once. It is not a thing that goes on being true because it was true in the year the water came up nine feet in a night.”

---

The argument was not settled that day and it was not going to be settled that day, and the reason it could not be settled was found at about the fourth hour at the ninth door, and it had nothing whatever to do with marks.

The sheet went into Coll Ferren's hands at about the third hour, and he was asked to do it because he had walked it and because he is the only man in that lane who knows every door in the bottom half of it, and he did not ask why. He stood at each one and read each answer out loud and said whose it was, and that is the third time a thing in this basin has been read out loud in that lane by a person it was about and not by a clerk, and the first two were the third line of the order by a woman with a barrow and the whole of the order by a clerk who had no authority to read it in a road.

He got to the ninth and stopped.

“This is not hers,” he said.

It was the ledgers, nineteen years of them, in the top. It belonged to the house on the other side of the lane, which is the house with the new door, and the woman at the ninth house read the answer and said that she had never had ledgers in her life and that her name was not on any and that whoever it was should come and take them back off the list.

And that is the whole of what was found in the second half of that week, and it took four hours and about two hundred yards of lane, and it is this. A list of nineteen answers with no addresses on it is not a list of anybody. It is nineteen opinions, and the moment a person writes an address against one of them, the address becomes the answer, and a person can be argued with about a thing they did not say, in their own doorway, about their own house, by a man holding a piece of paper.

The woman of about forty did not alter the sheet. She put a stroke through the ninth answer and wrote nothing against it and did not take the answer off, and she said out loud, in about eleven words, that she was not going to take a person's speech off a piece of paper because she got it wrong once, and that if a thing cannot survive being got wrong then it was not what anybody said, it was what somebody meant.

And that was the whole of the case for the settlement not naming the houses, and nobody had made it, and it was made by a woman of about forty at a door.

---

It went up to Sallowby at the closing of the water on the back of a leaf and it went up the hill in the hands of a girl of seventeen who was asked to carry it and said yes before she was asked twice, and she is the grocer's daughter and she has been in that lane since she was born and she read a four-line order out to a neighbour with one eye on the fifth day of the fifty-first week and asked the first question anybody in the Red Basin has ever asked about a document, and she has not asked another one since and she is not going to.

On the table in the Seat's one room that evening there were two sheets and one of them was a hundred and seventy-one pounds of arithmetic that everybody agreed with, and the other of them was a question with no name on it.

The question was four words long and it went on the table in a clerk's fair hand because there is nobody else in that room who writes, and it was this.

ARE THE HOUSES NAMED.

Nobody answered it that evening. Nobody answered it the next day. On the third day, a man of thirty-four who had lost a house to about two feet of the Sallow said that a man who has lost a house is not a house, and that changed the shape of the paper, and the clerk of the Seat wrote it down and said that he would want it written that he had said the paper was wrong before anybody had told him it was wrong, and he is forty-four and he has been in that room nine years and he keeps an index of indexes and there is no column in it for being wrong first.

At the end of the second day of that week the woman of about forty washed the pen, which she does at the end of a week and did on the second day because there was ink on the table and nobody else in that room washes a pen, and she put it upside down on a board and turned the board over, and the two sheets were on the table, and one of them had nineteen strokes on it and the other of them had four words on it, and neither of them had a date on it and neither of them had a heading on it and neither of them was going to get one, and the room had a document on its wall in a press with a key in a coat and about a hundred and seventy-one pounds of flood outside it and no remedy on the wall at all.

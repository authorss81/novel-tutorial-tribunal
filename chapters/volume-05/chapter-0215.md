# Chapter 215: Eleven Feet, Held Exactly

The Anner dam is a mile and a half above where the clay comes out of the ground and four miles above the village, and it is held up by four men and a barrow and a great deal of arguing that happened before any of them were born, and on the second day of the fifty-second week there were four men on it and a barrow and about two hundred tons of clay and rubble standing on it.

The order says the Anner shall hold the dam on the Anner water to eleven feet above its own sill, and keep the same, until the slow is down, and the Anner is the party bearing the order and is answerable for the height.

That is the whole of the instruction. It is one dam, one height and one party, and it is the same eleven feet that has been in a bond in a box under a floor in Sallowby since before the first shaking, and the two documents say the same number and they were written by two men about twelve years apart and one of them is dead.

“Eleven feet,” said Ilyan Vester, standing where the water came up to the clay and stopped.

“Eleven feet,” said Jory Vellan. “And I will tell you the exact state of it, because I have been asked for a figure twice this week and once of those times it was by a man who could not read and did not say so.” He did not look up. “It is at eleven feet and an inch at the low end and ten feet and eleven inches at the high end, and that is on purpose, and it took a man with a barrow and a string and most of a morning, and the paper says eleven feet and the paper does not say where along the dam, and so I have made it eleven feet all the way. It has been exactly eleven feet every day since the first of the week and it will be exactly eleven feet tonight, and I have not slept more than about three hours a night doing it, and I am not complaining, and you may all stop looking at my hands.”

“Where does the material come from,” said Tarin Keel.

“Forty loads of clay at ninepence the load, and a hundred and forty of rubble at threepence, and that is three pounds and five out of the Anner’s own book, and it has been three pounds and five out of that book every year for eleven years and it has never once been in a bill to the Seat because the Seat has not paid anything since the fourth year and the material has been in a habit.” He turned the barrow over and started down the slope. “The clay is a mile and a half up this bank. The rubble comes off the flats in winter. The four men are on the Anner wage, which is ten shillings a week, and a man is on that fifty weeks in a year, which is twenty-five pounds a year, and four of them for a week is forty shillings, which is two pounds, so the whole week's labour of the four men who are holding your eleven feet costs about twice what a year of material costs, and neither figure is in a document and both of them are mine.”

---

They went up to the office in the middle of the morning because the wind came off the flats and there was nothing to be said on a dam that could not be said better with the stove going, and the office is a shed with a stove in it and ochre on the floor of it that has been worked into the boards so far down that it is now part of the boards, and nobody in that building has ever cleaned it and it is not dirt, it is a floor.

“Your bond,” said Ilyan Vester, “says eleven feet above its own sill and that Hollow Sallow is not to be flooded by the Anner, and that the Anner is not answerable for any flooding not held by that height. All three of those are what the order says.”

“All three of those are what the order says,” said Jory Vellan, “and the first of them is what I have been holding for eleven years and the third of them is the only line in that document that has ever been of any use to anybody, and you will forgive me if I say that without being asked.”

“It is under about an inch of ink.”

“It is under about an inch of ink, and it is void, and it was void in the year after it was lodged, and the man who wrote that at the back was right, and there is nobody in this basin to say he was wrong.” He put both hands flat on the boards. “A thing that is void and unread is not a protection. It is an absence. My father put forty pounds in a box to say that if the Anner held eleven feet then Hollow Sallow would not be flooded, and the Anner has held eleven feet every day for eleven years, and Hollow Sallow is about to be under two feet of water in a lane, and there is no money in this valley that has been spent on this village by anybody because the forty pounds has not gone anywhere, and every load of clay and every load of rubble and every week of the four men has come out of a pit that sells its ochre seven pounds a ton dearer in Sallowby than at its own mouth, and nobody has ever said that sentence out loud, and I have said it to you once, on the sixth day of the fiftieth week, in a room, and a chainman wrote it down a week later because a man of thirty-one said he could not hold four figures and a hand at once.”

Nobody said anything about that. Ewan Troke, who was standing by the stove with his case shut, put his hand on the case and left it there.

---

“Then what is the height for,” said Ilyan Vester.

That was the wrong question and he knew it was the wrong question about half a second after he had asked it, and he asked it anyway, because he had asked it, and because a man who has been in a valley thirteen days and has been asked nothing for thirteen days will take a question that is going badly if it is the only one on the table.

“The height is for the road,” said Jory Vellan.

That is what he had said in 205, unasked, and it is the sentence that is in a chain-book in a room in Sallowby in the only handwriting in this basin that has ever gone into a book on anybody's own initiative, and it is four sentences long, and each of the four is somebody's business, and he had said it to four strangers in a room and had not known that one of them would take it away and put it in a book.

“But that is what it is for,” he went on, “and that is not what I have been asked. You have asked me what it is for and I have answered you and the answer is a road and a market and seven thousand seven hundred pounds a year, and that answer is worth nothing to me at four in the morning with a barrow on a wet bank, because I did not build the road and I do not get the seven thousand seven hundred. I get told a number.” He said the next part at the same volume as the rest of it, which is a thing he does. “I have been given a number and never a reason.”

Nine words. He had said them to a room in an office in a shed with ochre in the floor, and he had said them the way a man says a figure he has given a great many times, and then he picked the barrow up and went out into the wind.

---

He was right. That is the whole of it and it took the rest of the day to be sure of it and it was not comfortable.

A dam is not held by a decision. A dam is held by a number that everybody can repeat, and a number that everybody can repeat is the only thing a body of sixty men can work to at four in the morning in the wet. He had done it that way for eleven years and it had worked eleven times, and the four pages on the wall in Sallowby were going to be the eleventh of those and the first one anybody had ever written down, and he was going to hold it exactly, and he was not going to think about the lane.

And a thing held exactly is a thing held to a figure. And a figure is not a decision. Nobody in this valley has ever asked him what the height is for, and the one person who came close to asking him on the sixth day of the fiftieth week did not ask him, and he volunteered it, and nobody wrote it down for a week. The Anner was going to hold the height because the order told it to and because a void bond told it to and because there is no other kind of holding, and the doing of it was going to be done correctly, and correctly is the whole of the danger.

He said that last part himself, to the four of them, at the door of the shed, and then he went back to the clay.

“The Anner is the only body in this basin with men enough to hold a dam,” he said. “A man with men enough to hold a dam is not looking at eleven houses. That is not a fault. That is the arithmetic.”

---

The bag was still in the gap in the wall. That was how it was known, because the gap is about the size of a person and nothing else in this basin goes into it, and the man with the bag was not due for six weeks, and the second matter had gone in on the fifth day of the last week and the answer to it, if it was an answer, would be about nine days off, and the water does not wait nine days and has never in the history of this basin waited nine days for anything.

The four men went down to the dam at the second light on the third of the week and the clay went on in the same order it has gone on in for eleven years, and eleven feet was to the sill, and the sill was under the mud, and the four men were the same four they had been all week and one of them had a father at the Anner who was asleep in a shed four miles away because he had been on a dam since the second light.

# Chapter 229: What The First Year Costs

The first roof came off in the first week of the fifty-fourth week's second day, and it came off the second house from the bottom of the lane, and it was the one whose door was on its hinge and whose salt had been out of the plaster in three places, and the man who took it off was fifty-six and he had a ladder on his shoulder and he did it in about four days and took four days longer than a roof on dry ground, and he did not price that and was not asked to.

The timber came up the lane on the backs of about eleven people over two days, because a cart cannot go up that lane, and nobody wrote that down, and the only man in this basin who wrote it down wrote it in his own book at the fifth hour of the second of those days because counting things up a hill is what he is paid for, and there are about nineteen days of it in a year and none of them are paid days.

The first wall to be looked at was looked at on the fourth of that week by a man of about forty who was asked to look at it because a man of thirty-four had said in a lane that somebody had to, and it took about nine minutes and the finding was that two of the houses in the bottom half of that lane are not going to stand through a winter and one of them is going to have to come down and be put up again, and nobody in Hollow Sallow knew that before the fourth of that week and about six people have been saying since the spring of last year that they thought as much.

There is no figure on that in the paper and there is not going to be one, and the man who looked at the wall said in about nine words that a man who comes to look at a wall is paid by the hour and that his hour is a shilling and that he has never in his life asked a village to pay his hour and is not starting with this one, and a man of fifty-two in ochre dust said that the Anner would pay the hour, and the man would not say yes, and the man of fifty-two did not press it, and about nine people in that lane noticed that he had offered and had been refused and that he had not been offended, and that this was the first time in three weeks that anybody had seen anybody refuse the Anner and the Anner let it stand.

---

Tarin Keel did the sum on the second of that week at the top of the bank where anybody could hear it, because that is what he is for and because about forty people wanted to know what the paper was going to cost, and nobody in that valley had ever been told a figure for anything that was about to happen to them.

“Nineteen roofs at nine pounds is forty-one thousand and forty pence,” he said. “The whole wage, nine shillings a week for the sixteen weeks the water runs, is a hundred and forty-four shillings, which is one thousand seven hundred and twenty-eight pence. Sixteen weeks of seven days is a hundred and twelve days, and a penny a day is a hundred and twelve pence. Forty-one thousand and forty, plus one thousand seven hundred and twenty-eight, plus one hundred and twelve, is forty-two thousand eight hundred and eighty pence, which is a hundred and seventy-eight pounds, thirteen shillings and fourpence a year, for as long as the roofs want doing.”

“That's the year,” said somebody.

“The year is the year,” said Tarin Keel. “Here is the rest of it and here is the whole of it and there is nothing after it. The gate is a frame at four pounds ten and an oak at one pound eight, and one thousand and eighty pence and three hundred and thirty-six pence is one thousand four hundred and sixteen pence, which is five pounds eighteen. The three years that are owed to the woman who opens the gate is ten pounds sixteen, which is two thousand five hundred and ninety-two pence. One thousand four hundred and sixteen and two thousand five hundred and ninety-two is four thousand and eight pence, which is sixteen pounds and fourteen shillings, once.”

He put his boot on the mud and did not do it for a while.

“Forty-two thousand eight hundred and eighty and four thousand and eight is forty-six thousand eight hundred and eighty-eight pence, which is a hundred and ninety-five pounds, seven shillings and fourpence, and that is the first year of this paper with everything in it that is owed in it. And the flood of nineteen roofs was a hundred and seventy-one pounds, which is forty-one thousand and forty pence. Forty-six thousand eight hundred and eighty-eight, less forty-one thousand and forty, is five thousand eight hundred and forty-eight pence. That is twenty-four pounds, seven shillings and fourpence.”

Nobody in that lane said anything at all and about forty people were standing on a bank in the mud.

“So the settlement of this valley costs more than the flood did,” said Tarin Keel. “Twenty-four pounds, seven shillings and fourpence more, in the first year, and I have got the working on a board in my own book and anybody can look at it. And a hair under one part in eight of the whole thing is not roofs. It is a gate, and three years of arrears to a woman of thirty-eight, and a hundred and twelve pennies to a girl of seventeen, and that is what a settlement costs when somebody has been in a valley for eleven years and has not been paid in full for three of them.”

Then he did the last one, because he is the man who does the last one, and he did it out loud and slowly and he said the number before he said the working so that the lane would have the number first.

“The other way to say twenty-four pounds, seven shillings and fourpence is that the whole of what a woman of thirty-eight is paid in a year is seven pounds and four shillings. Seven pounds four is one thousand seven hundred and twenty-eight pence. Five thousand eight hundred and forty-eight divided by one thousand seven hundred and twenty-eight is three and a bit over three, and the bit is six hundred and sixty-four pence, and a hundred and eight pence is a paid week. So the paper costs three years and about six weeks more than the only wage anybody in that village has ever been paid.”

Nobody in that lane said anything to that either and about nine people looked at the bottom of it, where a man of thirty-four was carrying a board up to a roof that had no roof on it.

---

The one thing that did not happen that week is the whole of the last day of it.

Hollis Nance put it on the table in about eleven words at the end of the second of that week and it was not argued with by anybody, and what he said was this.

“The bench is seized of the matter. It said so in two paragraphs and it said it in its own time and it is not going to hear anything else about this basin this year. If I take that paper nine days up a road it costs five pounds and six, which is a pound for entry and four pounds for a day at the chamber and nine days' board at eightpence, and it comes back as two paragraphs. And if I do not take it, the paper is a paper of this valley and it stands or falls on the people in it, and nobody in Orison is going to be able to say in a year that a bench of four somewhere said so about it, and nobody in this valley is going to be able to say in a year that anybody anywhere said anything about it.”

“Nobody in this valley was going to say that anyway,” said the under-manager.

“No,” said Hollis Nance. “And that is the first good reason for anything anybody in this room has done in a month, and I have been waiting nine days for one and I have not told anybody about it because it looked like cowardice until a man of fifty-two said it out loud without being asked.”

And the sheet stands on the wall of the Seat's one room with eleven hands on it and one copy down the hill in a clerk's hand, and no paper of this basin has gone nine days up a road in about a month, and the five pounds and six was not spent twice, and about four people in that room noticed that the second time that money was not spent was the first time in nine years that a clerk of that seat had found a reason not to spend it, and that the reason had been given to him by a mine.

At the end of the second day of that week a girl of seventeen was paid seven pennies for seven days the water ran, in a jar, at the closing of the water, in the road, out loud, in front of about nine people, and she counted them twice and put the lid back on, and said that the second hand of the water-book was now a job and not a favour, and that she was going to write the day at the top of every page in it from now on whether anybody liked it or not, and about four people said they liked it and nobody said they did not.

# Chapter 205: A Bond Keeps A Thing True By Costing Somebody Money

The water-seat of Sallowby is one room over a shuttered shop with eleven seats in it, and eight of the eleven have never had anybody in them, and the four that have are the same four and three of those four are the same three men, and the room is nine feet by fourteen and there is a table down the middle of it and a press in the corner that is locked and a hearth that has not been lit since before the first shaking.

The clerk is Hollis Nance and he is forty-four and he stood up when they came in, which nobody asked him to do.

“I am not a Bench clerk,” he said, “I am not a recordwright, and I am not an advocate, and I am the only person in this room who is anybody at all, and if you have come about the ninth turn of anything then you are in the wrong room and the right room is nine days away and it costs five pounds and six to take a paper there and bring it back, which is a pound for entry and four for a day and six shillings for a rider’s board, and I have said all of that in nine words because in all the years I have stood in this room I have found that people who are told the price at the beginning argue about the price at the end and people who are told it at the end have already made a plan.”

Nobody had come about the ninth turn of anything and Ewan Troke said so, and the clerk sat down.

“Then you have come about the bond,” said Hollis Nance, “because there is nothing else in this basin anybody comes about, and if it is the bond then it has been in that press since the first winter after the first shaking and I have brought it out once, for a man from the Seat, about six years ago, and he read the front of it and put it back, and I have thought about it every working day since and I have never done anything about it, and you may as well have it on the table now as have it in my head.”

---

Here is the bond, and it is on two skins because every deed in this basin is on two skins, and the two skins of this one were sewn together by a man in the room below this room, twelve years ago, and the seam is in it and the seam does not show because the bond is folded.

> A BOND OF FORTY POUNDS LODGED WITH THE WATER-SEAT OF SALLOWBY IN THE ELEVENTH SALTING OF THE FORTIETH YEAR, BEING THE THIRD SALTING AFTER THE FIRST SHAKING.
> THAT THE ANNER HOLD THE DAM ON THE ANNER WATER TO A HEIGHT OF ELEVEN FEET ABOVE ITS OWN SILL AND KEEP THE SAME, AND THAT HOLLOW SALLOW NOT BE FLOODED BY THE ANNER, AND THE ANNER NOT BE ANSWERABLE FOR ANY FLOODING NOT HELD BY THAT HEIGHT.
> THE NAME AT THE FOOT IS ALDIS VELLAN, WHO LODGED IT HIMSELF IN THE ROOM BELOW THIS ONE AND WHO IS DEAD ELEVEN YEARS.
> THIS BOND IS OF NO EFFECT AFTER THE DEATH OF THE PERSON WHO LODGED IT, WHICH IS WRITTEN IN AT THE BACK IN A DIFFERENT HAND, AND WHICH HAS BEEN READ ONCE.

The forty pounds is in a box under the floor in the room below and the box is a strong-box and the strong-box is the Seat’s and the forty pounds has not gone anywhere since the day it went in.

Hollis Nance put the last line on the table face up, which nobody had asked him to do either, and then he sat down again.

“It is void,” he said. “It has been void since the year after the first shaking, because the man who wrote that at the back wrote it correctly and there is nobody in this basin to say that he was wrong, and I am a clerk and not a lawyer and a clerk who says a bond is not void is a clerk who has read a document. I have said that sentence out loud in this room about four times in the whole of the time I have been in it and three of them were to men who came to find out what the money is doing and none of them came back.”

“Then why is it in the press,” said Ilyan Vester.

“Because it is a bond and a bond goes in the press.” He looked at it without touching it. “That is the whole of it and it has been the whole of it for twelve years. Nobody comes for a bond. A bond is the thing you have got so that you do not have to go and look. And the four people who have been in this room in nine years have all come about water and have all been glad to hear that there is a bond, because a bond is forty points in a box and forty points is cheaper than nine carts a day on a high road for a hundred and forty days a year.”

The bond is forty pounds, and a hundred and forty loads of rubble at threepence is one pound fifteen and forty loads of clay at ninepence is one pound ten, and the two together are three pounds and five, and that is the whole of what the dam costs in a year to somebody who buys the material.

A man at the Anner is on ten shillings a week for fifty weeks a year, which is twenty-five pounds a year, and there are about four hundred men in the working years and about sixty in the off years, and Jory Vellan said all of those figures out in the room without being asked for any of them, and he said them standing at the end of the table with his hands in the pockets of a coat that has ochre in the seams of it, and he said them the way a man says figures he has said to himself for nineteen years.

---

The mud on the second skin was not dried. That was the thing about the bond, and nobody had mentioned it, and Hollis Nance had the sense to be embarrassed about it.

“Twelve years,” he said. “The first shaking came in a wet year. Everything we wrote that year has been damp and none of it has ever been properly dry and there is no fire in this room and there has not been a fire in this room since before the first shaking, and so the ink has run down the middle of the second line and the foot of the first, and the words about not being answerable are under about an inch of it, and the fourth line is in a different hand and a different ink and is as clear as anything anybody has ever written in this room.”

Nobody in the room said anything for about as long as it takes to put a chair back at a table.

“Then the bond may not say what we think it says,” said Tarin Keel.

“The bond says what it says on the four lines, or as much of the four lines as a man can get at,” said Hollis Nance. “I have read them. What I have never known is what the four lines were about to say when they were written, and there is nobody left in this basin who was in the room when they were written except one man, and he is the man whose name is at the foot of it.”

And there is a thing about a room with eleven seats in it and about an under-manager of nineteen years standing at the end of a table, and what it is, is that for about two minutes in Chapter 205 of this volume nobody said anything, and both of them knew why.

---

“You have been keeping it for eleven years,” Ilyan Vester said. “After your father died.”

“After my father died,” said Jory Vellan, “the dam was to be left. That is what the bond said, or that is what the bond said until the ink went, and I read what was on it in an afternoon and I was twenty-one and I had four men and a barrow and a hundred and forty loads of rubble a year to find, and I have not left it once.”

“Four men and a barrow.”

“Four men and a barrow in the wet years and two of them in a dry one.” He said it without pride and without apology, in the tone of a man giving a figure he has given a great many times. “Forty loads of clay at ninepence and a hundred and forty of rubble at threepence is three pounds and five, and the clay is a mile and a half up the Anner and the rubble is brought down off the flats in winter, and the four men are on the Anner wage, which is ten shillings a week, and a man is on that fifty weeks a year, which is twenty-five pounds, and the three pounds and five of the material is about a fortnight of one of them.”

“And the Seat has not paid the Seat’s half of the material.”

“The Seat has not paid anything since the fourth year,” said Jory Vellan, “and the fourth year is eight years ago, and there is nobody in the Seat who owes it to me and nobody in the Seat who knows that it is not being paid, because the material has not been in a bill since the fourth year. It has been in a habit.”

“Your father’s bond says the Seat has forty pounds of mine in the box.”

“My father’s bond is void,” said Jory Vellan, “and it has been void for eleven years, and I have not taken the forty points out of the box, and I have never once in eleven years thought about the forty points.”

“It does not say the Seat has forty pounds of yours in the box,” said Ilyan Vester. “It says a bond of forty pounds was lodged. That is a different sentence.”

Nobody argued with him about that and nobody agreed with him either, and it went in the notebook with the time on it, and it is the first thing anybody in this room had done in twelve years that was not about water.

---

The last hour of that day was spent on a heap of clay and rubble by the dam, in the light, with a knot of them there, and a man tipped a barrow out of it and it went down the slope in a long red heap and the ochre in it came up through the clay and made a yellow streak about four feet long where it stopped, and Jory Vellan went over to it and looked at it and then went back to the table and did a sum, out loud, because he was asked to and because there is nothing else to do with a morning.

“Eleven hundred tons a year out of the Anner,” he said. “Ochre and salt together, and the salt is the half that sells at the pans and the ochre is the half that sells here. Four pounds a ton at the pit’s mouth. Eleven at Sallowby. There is no more to it than that and no one of those three figures is anybody’s opinion.”

“Nobody has ever said them in a room,” said Sera Quill.

“Nobody has ever been in a room with all three of them in it,” said Jory Vellan. “That is the whole of the difficulty in this basin and it took you nine days to walk here and I have been here nineteen years. The Seat hears the eleven because the eleven is what Sallowby gets. The Anner hears the four because the four is what the mouth gets. Nobody in between ever hears the seven, and the seven is the whole of what the Anner is and it is not anybody’s profit, it is the road, and the road is a hundred and forty days a year and the road is what the dam is for and the dam is what the four pounds a ton is for and nobody has ever written that sentence down because it is four sentences and each of them is somebody’s business and none of them is anybody’s document.”

He wrote it on the back of his own hand, which he is not supposed to do, and then he put the hand flat on the table and left it there.

“Seven pounds a ton,” he said. “Eleven hundred tons. Seven thousand seven hundred pounds a year. That is what the road between the pit’s mouth and this market is worth, and that is the only number in this basin anybody should be arguing about, and neither the Anner nor the Seat has ever said it out loud, and I have known it since about my third year here, and I have never said it because I could not think of a room it was any use in.”

Nobody wrote it down. That is on the record and it is the whole of the account, and Ewan Troke wrote it down about a week later in a chain-book of his own because he was asked to by a man of thirty-one who said he was not able to hold four figures and a hand at once, and the chain-book is the Seat’s, and Ewan Troke is the Seat’s, and that is the first thing in eleven years in this basin that has gone into a book on anybody’s own initiative, and it is not in a press and it is not sewn and there is only one of it.

The four men and the barrow went down to the dam at the second light and the four men were not asked and had been at it in the morning, and the clay and the rubble went on the same barrow in the same order they have gone on for eleven years, and the eleven feet was to the sill and the sill was under the mud, and the whole of it was in the state it was in yesterday, and that is a thing that is true and is not going to be reported to anybody until somebody in this valley has an instrument for reporting it, which is about four days off and is a page and a penny and a seal.

# Chapter 214: Asking Eleven People

The first day of the fifty-second week was a working day in Sallowby and the Seat’s one room had a table down the middle of it with nine things on it, and the nine things had been on it since the second light, and every one of them was a thing a man had put there on purpose, and there was no form in this basin for any of them.

A sheet of paper. A second sheet. A stub of lead. A length of twine. A pot of tar with a brush in it. A rule. The Seat’s water-book, brought out of its drawer and put down with the spine toward the man at the head of the table. A sheet with the chamber’s second ruling copied on it in Ewan Troke’s flat hand. And a lamp, which nobody had lit and which was not on the list, and which was in that room because the hearth has not been lit since before the first shaking and eleven people cannot be asked anything in a dark room.

Hollis Nance came in at the second light, looked at the nine things for about as long as it takes to fold four sheets into eight, and said the thing he says.

“I am not a Bench clerk,” he said. “I am not a recordwright, and I am not an advocate, and there is no instrument in this room that makes eleven seats decide anything, and I have said all of that in nine words in the first week of this business and I am saying it again today because I have put a bag in the gap in that wall that I paid for out of my own money and I would like it on the record that I know exactly what I am.”

He put his hand on the water-book.

“Now tell me what you want asked, and then tell me what happens if the answer is no.”

---

They spent two hours on it and the two hours produced a list of eleven people who could be asked and a list of everything that had to happen first, and the second list was longer than the first by a factor that nobody could turn into anything.

Nobody in this party can compel a person. That is not a rule anybody in this basin has ever broken and not a rule anybody in this basin has ever heard of, and it is the plainest fact about the four of them: they could ask, and they could wait, and they could not make anybody come. There is no form at the Seat for asking a person of Hollow Sallow a question that matters, because a question that matters has to be answered on paper, and a paper has to be kept, and a thing that is kept in this basin is a thing somebody can take away eleven years later and use, and the woman who keeps the sluice has not been in this room in eleven years for exactly that reason and has said so.

And the cost of asking eleven people was not a fee. It was three days.

“You send for one,” said Garrow Lee, from the end of the table, “and then you wait, because a person in that lane is either at a gate or on a bank or under a roof taking water off a floor, and then you send for the second, and it is two days before you have two, and then somebody has to write down what the two of them said, and the writing is a penny a page for the woman over the chandler’s shop, and the reading is free because nobody in Sallowby charges anybody for reading anything. So it is three days. It has always been three days. I said so on the seventh day of the last week and I was told it was not a figure.”

“It is a figure,” said Ilyan Vester. “It is a figure that is in nobody’s minute.”

“That is what I am telling you. Three days is a number and it is not a thing anybody can put in a minute and take out again. A pound is a figure. Five pounds and six is a figure and I have said it out loud twice. Three days is three days of eleven people not being where they otherwise were, and there is no column in this basin for a person not being where they are, and I have been carrying that since the first year I went at those marks and it is too heavy, and you have all been in this valley thirteen days and it is the heaviest thing in it.”

Nobody put it in a minute. He wrote it in a penny exercise book, on the basin side, under the day, in his own hand, and the day was one hundred and sixty-two.

---

The rest of the morning was the part that could not be done, and it was done carefully, and it took an hour and a half, and it is the ordinary machinery of a basin in which a question has to be asked of both of two parties who have never been in a room.

The Anner would not answer for the village, and said so in the shortest sentence anybody said that day, at the end of the second hour, and it came from a man of fifty-two standing at the end of the table with ochre in the seams of his coat. The village would not answer for the Anner, and nobody had asked it to and nobody was going to. The Seat had eleven seats in it and eight of them had never had anybody in them and the four that were used were the same four and three of those four were the same three men, and the seat could not make eleven seats decide anything, because a thing that makes eleven seats decide anything is an instrument, and the Seat has a press and a hand-book and a gap in a wall and no instrument, and the whole of what a clerk of a Seat can do is write down what somebody says in front of him and keep it, and that is what he had done with a four-line order and it was still on the wall.

“Then it is three days against a river,” said Tarin Keel.

“It is three days against a river,” said Ilyan Vester, “and the river is two feet in the lane and rising, and there is no version of the three days in which eleven people are standing on dry ground on the third of them, and I am going to spend them anyway, because the only two things in this valley that have ever been made to anybody are a mark on a doorpost and a page on a wall, and both of them were made without asking anybody, and the second one happened seven days ago and is on a wall in Sallowby, and the first one is under tar on a post at the bottom of this hill.”

He said the last part to the table and not to the lane, and afterwards he wrote it in the book, and the book said only this, in his own hand, and no more:

*THREE DAYS. DAY ONE SPENT. THE WATER IS TWO FEET AND RISING. I HAVE ASKED NOBODY ANYTHING AND I AM GOING TO ASK ELEVEN PEOPLE TOMORROW, WHICH IS THE SAME AS SAYING I AM NOT GOING TO, BECAUSE TOMORROW I WILL BE AT A DAM.*

---

Thirteen days is how long he had been in the valley, and there was a thing in the book about it in his own hand, and it is worth putting down because it is the only honest sentence he put on that side of the page that week.

Nobody in this basin has asked him a single question. Not one person in about four hundred and ten, and about forty in Sallowby, and sixty at the Anner, and four people in a room with eleven seats, has asked him where he is from or what he is or what he is carrying or what year it was, and he has been here thirteen days and has got used to it, and he wrote down that he had got used to it and that this was not a relief, and the reason it was not a relief is that a man who is not asked anything is a man nobody is waiting on, and a man nobody is waiting on is a man who cannot be held to the thing he came for, and he came nine days up a road for a thing with a date on it.

He did not finish that thought in the book and he did not finish it out loud, and there is no page in that book with the rest of it on it, and the new question on the other side of the shelf is not the same question and was not raised that day by anybody at all.

---

The lane got the order read to it at the closing of the water, and it got it read by a clerk of a Seat who has no authority to read it in a lane and did it anyway, and the nine people from the morning carried eleven chairs down the hill because there were not enough chairs in the Seat of Sallowby for eleven people and nobody had thought about chairs until the bottom of the lane.

He read all four lines, including the head, which is a day out of a water-book, and he read the third line last, and he read it exactly as it is on the wall in Sallowby, and he did not read it better than the girl of seventeen had read it six days before and he did not read it worse.

Nobody signed anything. That is the whole of what came of the first day of asking eleven people: a clerk stood in a lane at the closing of the water and read four lines out loud to about forty people, and not one of them signed, and not one of them said they agreed, and about eleven of them said nothing at all, and eleven of those eleven were the eleven whose houses are on the marks, and not one of them would say whether it was the water or the mark that was wrong.

“It is the second thing I have done this month that I have no authority to do,” said Hollis Nance, afterwards, on the bank, to nobody in particular. “The first was a bag off a shelf and there was a sheet folded into it and I read all four of the sheets and they were about this basin. This is worse than that. That was other people’s paper and it had come to me from nine days off. This is our paper, and it is on our wall, and I have just read it out in a lane to people it does not name, and there is nobody in this valley I can hand it to, and there is nobody in this valley I could hand it to if there were.”

He said it standing up, with the copy still in his hand, and then he looked at it, and then he went up the hill to Sallowby with it, and about a quarter of an hour later he came back down with it and put it on the doorpost of the third house from the top, and it was not the same copy and it was a copy of a copy in a hand that was not Wenna Skell’s and not anybody’s, because the Seat cannot make anybody buy a page and a penny a page is a penny and the Seat does not have one.

The last thing that happened on that bank on the first day of the fifty-second week was a piece of paper coming off a wall in a room in Sallowby and going back up onto it, and it went up crooked, and the woman of about forty who washes the pen and turns the board over at the end of a week did not turn the board over that night because it was not the end of a week, and she looked at the paper instead, for about as long as it takes to grind ink, and then she washed the pen.

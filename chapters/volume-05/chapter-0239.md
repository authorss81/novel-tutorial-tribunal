# Chapter 239: What A Road Costs When There Is No Figure For It

There is no figure for that road in any book in this basin. There is a road in chalk on the side of a cart at Sallowby, four miles of it with a bend at the second ford and a market at the end of it, and there are about nine men who put it there, and not one of them has a figure for it, and the man who would have had a figure for it was asleep behind a cart shed at Hollow Sallow on the second of that week because he had been on a dam until the middle of the night, and what was in his head stayed in his head until about the sixth hour of the fifth.

Nobody asked him for it. He came down the hill at the sixth hour on his own account, and about seven people were on the bank at the top of the lane because about seven people are always on that bank, and he put a rule down on the mud, which is a thing he does, and he did the sum out loud, and he printed the working, because four people in this valley have learned in about a month that a man who counts carts will show you how he got there.

---

Here is the whole of it and there are two figures in it and one of them is four hundred miles old.

“The tonnage of this trade in the eighth year is one thousand and forty tons, and it is not our figure, it is theirs. It is on a return that came down inside a bag with nine printed sheets on it and it has been in a building nine days away for four years, and the price at the mouth is four pounds a ton and the price at the market is eleven pounds a ton, and it is printed that way and not the way we say it.”

“A thousand and forty tons,” said the woman of thirty-eight, from the top of the lane. “About eleven hundred, and sixty of them unaccounted for, and a man in a room has already said he is not going to be the one who says where sixty tons went.”

“One thousand and forty tons, and I am going to use their figure and not ours, and I am going to say why out loud, because it is the only interesting thing in this sum. Eleven hundred is what nine men have been saying in a yard for nineteen years. One thousand and forty is what one man wrote down in a room four years ago. A number somebody said is worth nothing by the second day. A number somebody wrote down is worth something for about as long as the paper lasts, and I would rather subtract from a number that was written down by a stranger than from a number that my own yard has been saying since before I could count.”

He put the rule flat on the mud and did it in the order he always does it, which is the number first and the working after, so that the lane has the number before it has the argument.

“A thousand and forty tons at two hundred and forty pence to the pound is two hundred and forty-nine thousand six hundred pence to the pound. Four pounds a ton at the mouth is four times two hundred and forty-nine thousand six hundred, which is nine hundred and ninety-eight thousand four hundred pence, which is four thousand one hundred and sixty pounds. Eleven pounds a ton at the market is eleven times two hundred and forty-nine thousand six hundred, which is two million seven hundred and forty-five thousand six hundred pence, which is eleven thousand four hundred and forty pounds. And the seven pounds between them is seven times two hundred and forty-nine thousand six hundred, which is one million seven hundred and forty-seven thousand two hundred pence, which is seven thousand two hundred and eighty pounds.”

Not one person in that lane had heard either of those two numbers before, and about four of them wrote them down on the backs of their hands.

“Now the paper on the wall,” said Tarin Keel. “Nineteen roofs at nine pounds, and nine pounds is two thousand one hundred and sixty pence, and two thousand one hundred and sixty times nineteen is forty-one thousand and forty. The whole wage, nine shillings a week for sixteen weeks, is a hundred and forty-four shillings, which is one thousand seven hundred and twenty-eight pence. Sixteen weeks of seven days is a hundred and twelve days, and a penny a day for a second hand on the water-book is a hundred and twelve pence. Forty-one thousand and forty, plus one thousand seven hundred and twenty-eight, plus a hundred and twelve, is forty-two thousand eight hundred and eighty pence, which is a hundred and seventy-eight pounds, thirteen shillings and fourpence a year.”

“And the road?” said the man of forty-two from the cart yard, who had put the road in chalk and who had come up the hill for it.

Nobody in that lane had a figure for the road. That is what the fifth day of the fifty-fifth week came to, and it took about four minutes, and it arrived at a figure the way figures arrive in this basin, which is that somebody says a number out loud and everybody else is pleased.

“The mouth figure,” said Tarin Keel. “That is what nine men priced it at, and every one of them will tell you the same and not one of them can say why. Four thousand one hundred and sixty pounds a year. And I am going to tell you why and then you can all be as angry with me as you like. Nine men in a yard priced a road at the only figure in that yard anybody could name, and the only figure in that yard is what a ton is worth before anybody carries it. And a road is not a carrying. The seven pounds a ton is the carrying. The four pounds a ton is the trade. And a road is the ground itself, and ground is paid for out of what the thing on the ground is worth before anybody moves it.”

He let that sit for about as long as it takes to get a boot off.

“So the road is four thousand one hundred and sixty a year, and the settlement is forty-two thousand eight hundred and eighty pence a year, and one thousand and forty tons at the mouth is nine hundred and ninety-eight thousand four hundred pence a year. Forty-two thousand eight hundred and eighty, plus nine hundred and ninety-eight thousand four hundred, is one million forty-one thousand two hundred and eighty pence. Two hundred and forty pence to the pound goes into that four thousand three hundred and thirty-eight times, which is one million forty-one thousand one hundred and twenty pence, and there is a hundred and sixty pence over, and a hundred and sixty pence is thirteen shillings and fourpence. So the settlement of this valley and a second road come to four thousand three hundred and thirty-eight pounds, thirteen shillings and fourpence a year.”

He stopped there and put his boot in the mud.

“And the whole of what the Anner takes out of this basin in a year is what a ton is worth at the mouth, which is nine hundred and ninety-eight thousand four hundred pence, which is four thousand one hundred and sixty pounds. And the sum is over the whole by one million forty-one thousand two hundred and eighty less nine hundred and ninety-eight thousand four hundred, which is forty-two thousand eight hundred and eighty pence.”

Nobody said anything for a while, and the river was not in it, and the lane is above the river.

“Which is a hundred and seventy-eight pounds, thirteen shillings and fourpence,” said Tarin Keel. “Which is the settlement. The whole of the excess over the whole of the trade is the settlement, to the penny, and the settlement is the first claim on that seven thousand two hundred and eighty and the road is the second, and the second one is bigger than the money, and the two of them are the same money counted once, and I have been sitting in a shed for a night and a day with that in my head and I could not make it come out any other way.”

---

Somebody asked him what a road would have to come in at, and he did that one too, and he did it because the four of them in this basin had learned to ask what the largest is.

“Nine hundred and ninety-eight thousand four hundred, less forty-two thousand eight hundred and eighty, is nine hundred and fifty-five thousand five hundred and twenty pence. Two hundred and forty goes into that three thousand nine hundred and eighty-one times, which is nine hundred and fifty-five thousand four hundred and forty pence, and there are eighty pence over, and eighty pence is six shillings and eightpence. So a second road would have to come in under three thousand nine hundred and eighty-one pounds, six shillings and eightpence a year, and leave the settlement standing, and the nine men came in at four thousand one hundred and sixty, and they came in at four thousand one hundred and sixty because four thousand one hundred and sixty is the only figure in that yard anybody could name.”

He looked at the seven people on the bank for about as long as it takes to turn a cup round on a board.

“That is a hundred and seventy-eight pounds and thirteen shillings and fourpence over the top of it, which is the settlement again, and it is over the top of it by exactly the thing it is supposed to be paying for, and I am going to say the rest of it once and then I am going to do something else for the rest of the day. There is nothing behind that difference. It is not a figure for a thing. It is the same number twice, and one of the two times is real and the other one is a road drawn in chalk, and I am not going to be the one who says what that means, and I would ask whoever is minded to decide it not to be me in a room with a table in it.”

Nobody in that lane asked him for more of it, and about seven people have been saying since that the sum says it, and what it says is that the two are the same money, and nobody has said it in the room with eleven seats in it, where the Seat keeps no minutes of its own meetings, which is a thing this record has to say and will have to keep saying.

---

He took a board out of a cart shed behind two houses on the second half of the fifth of that week and chalked on it, in a hand of his own, the sum and the two numbers and the ceiling, and he propped it against the outside of the shed where the wet comes off the ridge onto it about four times a winter, and it is going to go in about a month and he knows that and did it anyway.

And a man of thirty-one was in the cart shed at about the seventh hour on the fifth of that week, and he opened a penny exercise book with two halves in it, and on the basin side of it, at the head of a page, he wrote one hundred and eighty-seven days since the second shaking, and that is the fourteenth figure of that kind he has put at the head of a page in this valley, and that is a count of figures and not a count of anything else, and this is the hundred and eighty-seventh day, and the three days between the last figure and this one are not in the book and are not anywhere else.

The second line under it is the reason, and the reason is that the figure he has just subtracted from was written four hundred miles off in a turn four years back, and a turn cannot be placed in this basin by anybody living, and so the only date this valley can put on the whole of it is the morning it was said out loud on a bank, and he did not put that morning on it, because a man may put a day on his own act and may not put one on another person's speech, and what he did this evening was write a sum down.

And on the other side of that book nothing was opened. The carrying is off nothing. The second notice of five shillings and fourpence is not paid. The claim of forty-five pounds two shillings and eightpence to a carter of fifty-one with a boy eleven miles off is not producible and he is right. The letter of a clerk of thirty-four is in a bundle on a shelf in a room in Sallowby with a knife-cut end on the string. And about four hundred and eighty-five pounds a year on nine thousand seven hundred falls due at the close of a grain year in a county four hundred miles from this valley and the district of Ryefold is at nothing, and it does not stop, and it was not mentioned once in a valley that has just worked out what a second road costs.

---

The board was still against the outside of the cart shed on the sixth of that week and about seven people walked past it on their way up the lane, and two of the seven stopped, and one of the two read it from end to end and neither of the two said what it was for, and nobody in Hollow Sallow has asked what it is for, and the sheet on the wall of the water-seat of Sallowby is a hundred and seventy-eight pounds, thirteen shillings and fourpence a year and has not been touched since the seventh of the fifty-third week, and a girl of seventeen was paid six pennies that week in a jar at the closing of the water.

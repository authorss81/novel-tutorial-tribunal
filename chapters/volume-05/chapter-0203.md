# Chapter 203: Two Halves Of One Deed

Sallowby has four hundred people in it, one market day a week, and a street of about the length of a lane at Ryefold with a chandler at one end and a saddler at the other and nothing in between that is not a house with a yard behind it.

The room Wenna Skell works in is over the chandler’s shop and it has one window, a shelf of wax, a stool, a flat board on the sill, and a press against the wall with a lock on it that is not the only lock in this basin that nobody has a key to and is the only one in this basin that is locked against her.

She was thirty-three. She had been copying for eleven years and she had copied about six hundred and forty deeds in that time, of which about ninety were for the Anner line, and she could tell you both numbers without looking at anything, which is the sort of thing a person says about their own work and about nothing else.

“A penny a page,” she said, before anybody had asked, because they were standing in the doorway and she was at the board with a pen in her hand. “Threepence for a sealed copy, and the seal is wax and clay and a woman at the desk in Sallowby who charges me two for the wax and does not know that I put it in. A penny for an ordinary copy, fourpence from anybody but the Anner, and I will tell you why the Anner is a penny if you have got the time, and I have, because there is nothing on this board until the noon light.”

She put the pen down.

“The Anner is a penny because the Anner pays in full and in advance and has never once asked for a second copy. Four hundred pages of the Anner a year, about, and every one of them a penny, and about four hundred pages of everybody else at fourpence, and there is a reason in that and the reason is the same reason as the price and I will not make you wait for it.”

She waited about four seconds, and then she told them, because nobody had asked her anything in about a year.

“Because the Anner sends for them in the same hand every time. One man, since the first shaking, writes out what he wants and sends it and it comes back copied and right. I have never had an Anner page wrong. I have had every other page in this basin wrong at some point in eleven years, and I have had about forty pages wrong twice.” She picked the pen up again. “So the penny is not a discount. The penny is what a page is worth when a person has told me what is on it and I can believe them. And the fourpence is what a page is worth when I have to stand in a room and find out.”

Ewan Troke put the case down on the floor, and the case had been down on the floor for a quarter of an hour, and he had been looking at the press with the lock on it the entire time.

“Then we will want two of those,” he said.

“A shilling for two pages, and I will want the second one in a week, and I am saying that now because the price before the fact is the only price there is and I have been burned.”

---

Here is what was on the flat board on the sill, and it is two pieces of paper and they are in the same hand and the hand is hers, and the two pieces are the two halves of one deed, and neither half is a copy of the other and the other half of each is about nine miles away in a chest under a floor.

> FIRST HALF, COPIED BY W. SKELL IN THE ELEVENTH SALTING OF THE FORTIETH YEAR, FROM AN ORIGINAL SEWN FIRST HALF, HELD IN THE PRESS AT SALLOWBY:
> THE MARL RIDGE, FROM THE SLUICE STONE AT HOLLOW SALLOW TO THE KETTLE STONE AT COLDBECK, IS COMMON GROUND, AND THE CROSSING OF THE ROAD IS BY RIGHT AND NOT BY GIFT, AND COMMON IS SET OUT IN THE MARGIN AS FOLLOWS —
> SECOND HALF, COPIED BY W. SKELL IN THE NINTH TURN, THE FOURTH DAY, THREE YEARS BACK, FROM AN ORIGINAL SEWN SECOND HALF, HELD IN A CHEST UNDER THE FLOOR AT COLDBECK:
> THE MARL RIDGE, FROM THE SLUICE STONE AT HOLLOW SALLOW TO THE KETTLE STONE AT COLDBECK, IS COMMON GROUND, AND THE CROSSING OF THE ROAD IS BY RIGHT AND NOT BY GIFT, AND COMMON IS SET OUT IN THE MARGIN AS FOLLOWS —

The two pieces agreed in every word down to the dash. They had been written about eight years apart by two different men in two different rooms, and each man had finished his own sentence and stopped at a dash and turned his skin over, and the seam is in both of them and the two seams do not meet, because one of them was written at Sallowby and the other at Coldbeck and the two towns are not the same distance apart by that line.

“That is the whole of it,” said Wenna Skell. “That is what a deed of this basin is. It is two pieces of skin sewn together and the sewing is done by the man drawing it, and the half he does is the half he can see from where he is standing.”

She said it in the ordinary way, in her own mouth, with a pen in her hand, and she had no idea at all that she had said the thing that four people had come nine days to find out.

“Which means,” she went on, “that a person in Hollow Sallow with the first half has not got a deed. A person at Coldbeck with the second half has not got a deed. A person with one half of a deed and a man who can read has got a piece of paper and an argument, and there is nothing in this basin that will settle an argument out of a piece of paper, because the thing that settles an argument about a deed is the other half of the deed.”

Nobody in that room said anything for about as long as it takes to heat ink.

“Nobody has ever asked me to bring the two halves together,” she said. “In eleven years. Not once, and I have been in this room the whole of eleven years, and there is one man in this basin who would benefit by my doing it and he has not asked me either, and you may draw whatever you like out of that.”

---

The second half was in a chest under the floor at Coldbeck, and the chest was in a house, and the house belonged to a family that had held the ground on that side of the water since before the first shaking, and the family was not in Sallowby and had no reason to be in Sallowby and did not come to the market twice a year like everybody else.

That took a day and a half and it is not the end of this volume and it is not going to be the end of anything, and what it produced was Ewan Troke saying, at a door, to a woman of about seventy, that there was a man in Sallowby who could read the two halves and would not charge her for it, and the woman asking what a man would want, and Ewan Troke not having an answer to that and saying so, and the woman then saying that her grandfather had asked the same question in the first year and had been given the same absence of an answer and had let it go, and that she was not going to be the one to start it.

Nobody in this record has recorded what was said in the house and it is not in this chapter and it is not going to be in any of the next forty, and the reason is that the woman of seventy was talking to a chainman about her grandfather and neither of them was talking to us.

The first half was in the press in the room they were standing in, and the press was not locked against Wenna Skell because the press is hers, she bought it with her own money in her fourth year, and it is a strong-room in the sense that a locked press is a strong-room and in no other sense at all, and the reason anybody calls it a strong-room is that there is one at the pit and the one at the Seat and a woman of thirty-three with a press is a third thing and gets the same word.

The man who came in about the second light on the following day was not sent for and was not produced and had walked in from the Anner because he was in Sallowby anyway.

He was fifty-two and he had been under-manager at the Anner for nineteen years and his name was Jory Vellan, and he stood in the doorway of a room over a chandler’s shop and looked at the two halves on the board and did not come in, and he read the first line of each from where he stood, which is all he needed and more than he intended.

“Allie,” he said. “You have got the old one and the new one on the same board.”

“I have got the two halves on the same board,” said Wenna Skell. “They are not two deeds and they never were and a person who told you they were two deeds told you a thing that made his own case easier.”

There is a thing a man does with a pause.

“Then the Anner has been holding half a deed for nine turns,” he said. “Which is not a thing I am going to say in a room, because it is not wrong either, and the other half is in a chest under a floor in a house at Coldbeck and it has been there since before the Anner was a company, and the Anner did not know that when it took the ninth-turn half, and the man who drew it did know, and he is at Coldbeck still and he is sixty-eight and he drew it for the Anner and he was paid by the Anner.”

Nobody said anything about that for about four seconds, and then Wenna Skell said the thing that is the reason this chapter is in the volume.

“Then it is not two deeds and it is not one,” she said. “It is two people, each holding one half, and each of them is certain, and neither of them is a liar, and the thing that is wrong is the seam.”

She put the pen down flat.

“I have been copying for eleven years and I have copied about six hundred and forty of them and about ninety of them were this one, and I can tell you that nobody in this basin has ever lost a deed. Not one. A woman loses the bag, a man loses the house, a house burns, and out it comes at the seat, and it is there, in a press or a chest or a sleeve, because people in this basin do not throw deeds out. What they do is this.” She looked at the board. “They stop bringing the halves to each other. And then there are two halves and two people and a seam, and the seam is where the truth is, and neither of the two people can get to the seam, and that is not a loss. That is a thing that two people have done to each other without knowing.”

She said that with her hand flat on the board, and it is the closest thing to a sentence about this volume anybody in this basin has said in the whole of the first ten days of it, and nobody in the room told her that she had said it, and the reason nobody told her is that in Sallowby nobody tells Wenna Skell anything, because she already knows it and is waiting for a fee.

---

He said what he had come for, which was that there was a name on the ninth-turn half.

“There is a name on it,” said Jory Vellan. “It is a woman of twenty-nine and she has been dead four years, which is the eighth turn, and the half is dated the fourth day of the ninth, and she was my wife and it is not her hand.”

“You brought me that half three years ago,” said Wenna Skell, “and you brought it me with the name on it and I copied it with the name on it, and I have copied it four times since for other people, and I never asked you about the name because you were not the one who wrote it, and a man who does not write a deed is not the man to be asked about a name in one.”

“I am the man to be asked about a name in one,” said Jory Vellan. “It is my wife’s name and I watched her die and she has been dead four years and the half was drawn three, and I have not laid eyes on that deed since the day it came into this room, and there is a word at the foot of it under her name and I have never asked what the word is, because I have read it about nine times and I have never once asked anybody to read it out.”

“Which word.”

“The one that means she signed it,” said Jory Vellan, “and she did not, and I have had that piece of paper in a press in a room over a chandler’s shop for three years and I have not asked a scrivener of thirty-three what the word at the foot of it is, and you may make what you like of that either.”

Sera Quill asked the question, and she asked it in nine words and no more, and it was the only thing she said in that room that was not a recordwright’s sentence, and the room did not know it and neither did the three people who had come nine days for a different thing.

“I am not going to say which half is the deed,” she said. “Either of you may have it. I will read it and I will not say.”

That is the whole of what a recordwright of a public record said in eleven years of this volume, and she was right, and Ilyan Vester wrote it down in a penny exercise book on the second of the day, in his own hand, with the hour on it, and he did not put the next sentence under it.

She would not say which half was the deed because saying so would be a finding, and a finding is a thing a recordwright makes about a record she is not a party to, and the first thing that happens when a recordwright becomes a party to a record is that the record cannot be used against the people in it, and there are about four hundred and ten people in a village two miles from a chandler’s shop whose only protection on a wet night is a piece of paper they have never been able to read all the way through.

---

At the noon light Wenna Skell started a copy for a man of Sallowby who wanted a will made, and it was four pages, and she said the price before she began, and he said he would think about it, and she said the price again the same, and he said he would think about it again, and she went back to the board.

There is a woman two streets down who has owed her eleven pence for a copy of a will since the spring before last and who says every market day that she will pay it on this market, and Wenna Skell has never once said no to her and has never once written the eleven pence down, and the reason she has not written it down is that a thing written down in a book in Sallowby is a thing somebody can take away, and she has been in this room eleven years and she has watched what happens to things people write down, and she has eleven pence in her head and it is the only figure in the Red Basin that she is completely certain of.

# Chapter 224: The Wage That Comes Every Week

The three years that are owed to a woman of thirty-eight were paid in one week, in an envelope, at the closing of the water, on the fourth day of the fifty-third week, and it took about ten minutes and there was nobody in the lane but the two of them and about four children and a man of thirty-one who was not asked to be there and was not asked to leave.

She counted it on the sill of her own house and she counted it twice, and the sill is the third from the top and is the highest of the eleven, and her house had two feet of the Sallow in the bottom room on the third night of the week before last and has salt in the plaster in one place now and a dry floor and a bed.

“Ten pounds sixteen,” said Ilma Treen. “Three years at four and six for sixteen weeks. Sixteen times fifty-four pence is eight hundred and sixty-four pence, and three times that is two thousand five hundred and ninety-two pence, and that is ten pounds and sixteen shillings, and it is the first money anybody in this lane has handed me in eleven years and I am not going to say thank you for it in the road.”

Nobody said anything to that either.

What she said about the wage was not about the arrears and it is the whole of what the fourth day of that week was for, and she said it standing in her own doorway with the envelope in her hand, and she is not a lawyer and there is no lawyer in this basin and has not been one since before the first shaking.

“It is not the four and six,” she said. “I want that written down somewhere, because a man has spent three days putting four and six on a piece of paper as though that were the trouble. Nine shillings a week for the sixteen weeks the Sallow runs, and nothing the rest of the year, and I have had that for eleven years. Four and six of it is paid and four and six of it is not, and I have known that every week of eleven years and I have never once gone without, because the pit does not pay late and the pit is the half that comes.”

“And the half that is not the pit,” said Tarin Keel, “is the half you cannot plan on.”

“It is the half I plan on,” said Ilma Treen. “That is what I want said and it is the whole of what I have learned in eleven years at about four in the morning on a morning in the wet and in the wrong week of them. I do not need more money. I need the money I have already been given to arrive on the day it is due, and it has not arrived on the day it was due three times in three years, and the three times were all in the wet years, and I have never asked anybody about it and I am not going to now.”

---

The second hand on the water-book cost a penny a day and it was named on the fifth of that week by the person who was going to keep it, and she named it herself and she was seventeen and she is the grocer's daughter and she read a four-line order out to a neighbour with one eye in the fifty-first week and asked the first question anybody in the Red Basin has ever asked about a document, and she had not asked another one since and she said that was deliberate.

“A penny,” said Elsa Quint. “Not a penny a week, and not a penny a day of the year. A penny a day the water runs, and the water runs sixteen weeks and that is a hundred and twelve pennies, and a hundred and twelve pennies is nine shillings and fourpence, and I have worked it out twice and I have had it checked once by a man who counts carts.”

The room did what a room does when a girl of seventeen does arithmetic in it, which is that about four people look at each other, and one of them — a woman of about forty who washes the pen — said the thing that made it stick.

“That is the first money anybody in this basin has ever paid anybody for looking at water,” she said. “Nine shillings and fourpence a year, and the woman who opens the gate is paid a hundred and forty-four shillings a year, and nine shillings and fourpence is about a fifteenth of that, and it is the first figure on this paper that somebody has set for herself out loud, and I am going to write it down in the order she said it and I am not going to improve it.”

The finding underneath it is not about the girl and it is not about the penny, and it was said by a man of forty-four with an index of indexes in front of him, and it is the reason the line is in the paper at all.

“The water-book has four hands in it,” said Hollis Nance. “I have been in this room nine years and I have never once had a hand in that book. The oldest is the mill hand and the mill hand has been the same hand for about a hundred years. There is a hand of the Seat's and there is my predecessor's and there is one of a man of twenty-two with a list of drains. Not one of the four has ever stood at the frame at Hollow Sallow and watched the water come up to it.”

He put his hand flat on the table, which he does.

“If four people in a room write down what a river did, and not one of them is standing at the thing, then what the book has is not what the river did. It is what those four were told. And on the third night of the week before last, at an hour nobody wrote down, the mill hand and my hand disagree about that frame by about two hours, and both of us were in the room, and neither of us went up the hill, and the book has kept both of them for eleven days and neither of us has looked at it, and the difference between those two hours is the whole of what happened in that lane.”

Nobody in that room had looked at it. He was going to, and he said so, and said that he was going to do it in the ordinary way, which is that he would take the book down four miles himself and stand at the frame and write what he saw and the hour, in a fifth hand, in the middle of the page, and that this was the fourth thing in eleven days he had done that he had no authority to do and that the sentence had not got any shorter.

---

The nineteen roofs came off in the order a woman of about sixty named in a lane with her hand out flat over the ground, and not in the order anybody would have chosen, and the order is in the paper in four lines and the four lines cost nothing and about nine people argued about them for two days.

Bed. Boards. Salt out of the plaster. Roof before the frost.

And the sentence that went with the four lines, which is the under-manager's, and he said it on the fifth of the week at the top of the lane with his hands in his pockets and did not improve on it.

“That order is the order of about four hundred and ten people who have been in a wet house for eight days, and not one of them has thought about a roof since the second day.”

Brann Slee came over the bank at the second light on the sixth of the week and said the figure again to a different set of people, because he had said it twice already and he was not going to say it a third time for nothing, and then he did the thing that put a stop to about an hour of talk.

“Nine pounds is nine pounds,” he said, “and I have said it three times now and I am not going to say it a fourth time, and the reason I will not break it into pieces is not stubbornness, it is that a roof is a roof, and if you put a roof into battens and thatch and nails then you have three men in this lane each able to take a third of it and none of them able to stand on a wet one. You want it done before the frost. Then find me a man who can stand on a wet one and pay him nine pounds and let the rest of the lane carry the timber.”

He went back up the hill and the timber was carried by about eleven people out of about four hundred and ten, over two days, up a lane that a cart cannot go, and nobody wrote that down, and Tarin Keel wrote it down in his own book at about the fifth hour of the second of those days, and his own book is not a public record and he keeps it because that is his trade.

And at the closing of the water on the sixth day of that week, four miles below all of it, a woman of thirty-eight came up out of the Sallow with a barrow and turned up the lane instead of going straight on, because the short way was still under, and she has been the sluice for eleven years and she did it every evening, and on that evening she was carrying a barrow of stone and she had been paid a wage in full and on the day, and it is the first time in eleven years that those two facts have been true on the same evening.

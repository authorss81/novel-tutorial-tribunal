# Chapter 235: A Record Perfectly Kept

The second thing in the bag came out of the bag on the first day of the fifty-fifth week, and it came out because the bag was on a shelf in a room with a press in it and a clerk of forty-four went to put the sheets in a drawer and found it, and it was at the bottom of the bag, under nine printed sheets, folded twice, and it had been under them since the sixth day of the last week.

The man with the bag had not pointed at it. He was not asked about it. He is a man with a bag and he is in about nine rooms a week and he is paid at the Seat of Orison and not by this valley, and he takes what is in the gap and he has never once told anybody what he has taken, and a clerk of forty-four said afterwards that a man in that trade who points at a thing in his own bag is a man who has been asked to.

It was two paragraphs and a head, in a clerk's hand, and it was dated, and the date is in a turn and a day of a year this basin cannot place, and it is not in either of the two calendars that anybody in this valley keeps.

“It is the answer,” said Hollis Nance, reading the first line standing up, which is a thing he does. “It is the answer to the matter. It is dated in the eleventh year. It is nine days old at the door and it has been in this room two days and nobody in this valley has seen it, and the bench said in the fifty-second week that it was seized of the matter and would consider it in its own time, and it has considered it in its own time, and it has come back, and it is two paragraphs, and I want the room to hear both of them.”

He read them out.

They were true. They were consistent with the three rulings in the abstract, and they were consistent with the four pages on the wall, and they were consistent with the sheet in eleven hands underneath them, and not one of them is inconsistent with anything, and every single thing in them was said by somebody who was right about the thing they were speaking of.

And the whole of them is wrong about this place.

Nobody in that room had to be told why, and nobody in that room was told why, because it was found in the fifty-first week in a room in this building by a recordwright of twenty-eight who said that the man in Orison who signs the referrals on everything has not been in the Red Basin since before the first shaking, and that a record can be perfectly kept and still be wrong about a place, and that the reason is that nobody who kept it was ever in it.

That is not a discovery. It is the same finding for the second time, and the second time it is duller and worse, and the woman of about forty who washes the pen said so out loud in about eleven words: “We found that out a month ago and we have all been extremely careful with it since, and it has not improved, and I would like it recorded that being careful with a true thing is not the same as being right about it.”

---

The third of the four lines under that heading had a figure in it, and a return quotes its own file, and the figure came out of the return that was folded twice at the bottom of the bag, and it is the only figure anybody anywhere has ever written down about what this valley trades.

“It is a return of the tonnage and the prices of the trade of the Sallow basin for the eighth year,” said Hollis Nance, “which is four years back, and the tonnage is one thousand and forty tons, and the figure at the mouth is four pounds a ton and the figure at the market is eleven pounds a ton, and the difference of seven pounds a ton is what the road is worth in a year, and it is seven thousand two hundred and eighty pounds.”

He put the paper down and looked at it for a while.

“Seven thousand two hundred and eighty,” he said. “And I have been in this room since before the water and there is no figure of that in it, and I have never had a copy of it, and I did not know there was one, and it has been in a building four hundred miles off for four years, and it is the number the whole of this valley has been arguing about, and we have been arguing about it out loud for a month using a figure of about eleven hundred tons that four people made up between them in a lane.”

“A thousand and forty and about eleven hundred,” said Wenna Skell. “Sixty tons.”

“Sixty tons,” said Hollis Nance, “at seven pounds a ton is four hundred and twenty pounds a year, which is one hundred thousand eight hundred pence out of one million seven hundred and forty-seven thousand two hundred pence, and that is five and seven-tenths in the hundred, because one hundred thousand eight hundred times a hundred is ten million eighty thousand and ten million eighty thousand divided by one million seven hundred and forty-seven thousand two hundred is five and seven-tenths, and I am not going to be the one who says where sixty tons went, because I have been in this room since before the water and it was not me.”

---

And the under-manager came up the hill on the second half of that day, which he does twice a week and did not have to, and he had been told about the four lines by a carter at a gate and not by a clerk, and what he said in the room with eleven seats in it was about the second line and not about the first.

“Forty-one out and nine back,” said Jory Vellan. “Nine years. And there is a mine at the top of this hill that has put a hundred and seventy-eight pounds, thirteen shillings and fourpence a year on a village it had never spent a pound on in nineteen years, and it is going to be asked again in a year, and it is going to answer, and it is going to have to do that with a sheet on a wall in a market town that says what it pays and what for.”

He put his hand flat on the table, and Jory Vellan does that, and Hollis Nance does that, and neither of them knew the other did it the first time and both of them knew the second time and neither of them said so.

“So the mine is now a body with a standing interest in a number,” he said. “Not the height. I want that said, because the room will get it wrong by the second sentence. Not the height. The wage, and the gate, and the roofs, and a penny a day to a girl of seventeen. And the number that is in the sheet is a number the mine is now paying every year to keep true, and a number that a body pays every year to keep true is a number that body is frightened of losing.”

“Is that a complaint against the sheet?” said the woman of about forty.

“It is not a complaint about that sheet,” said the under-manager, “and I have sat with it for an hour to find out whether it is, and it is not, and I would like that written down in the order it was said in.”

---

And the man of thirty-one took the return out of the room and carried it four miles up a hill and put it on a bench in a cart shed behind two houses, and he opened a penny exercise book with two halves in it that he had shut on the third day of the last week and told himself in his own hand that the next figure in it would be a hundred and ninety-something in the first week of a month he cannot name.

It is one hundred and eighty-three days since the second shaking on the first day of the fifty-fifth week, and one hundred and seventy-eight was the last one, and the four in between are not in the book and are not anywhere else, and he did not write them and has not said so to anybody.

He wrote the hundred and eighty-three at the top of the page, and the second line under it is the reason, and the reason is a return.

He wrote: *A DAY GOES ON A THING WHEN IT ARRIVES. A DAY DOES NOT GO ON A THING WHEN IT WAS DONE, AND THERE IS NO COLUMN ANYWHERE IN THIS VALLEY FOR WHEN IT WAS DONE, AND FOUR HUNDRED MILES OFF THERE IS A TURN AND A DAY AND NOBODY IN THIS VALLEY CAN PLACE A TURN.*

And he did not write the Shelf half open, because the Shelf half has not been opened once in six weeks and there are six debts on that side of it and none of them is this.

---

The thing that was found in that room on the first day of the fifty-fifth week was said by a woman of about thirty-eight at about the sixth hour, in a lane, in the ordinary way, and she was not asked and she was not setting out to say anything, and she was talking about the sixty tons.

“Nobody has ever written down what this valley trades,” she said. “Nineteen years, and about eleven hundred tons, and every one of those numbers was somebody remembering. And there is a thing that is worse than that and I have been trying to say it to somebody for two days.” She put her hand on the gate. “There are things in this valley that are true in every room and are not on any page. Not hidden. Not kept back. Just not on. Everybody knows them and no paper has them, and that is not anybody's fault, and I do not know what it is, and it is the only thing I have ever wanted to put on a piece of paper.”

“Somebody will have a name for it,” said the woman of about forty.

“There are about nine of them in this basin,” said Ilma Treen, “and I have counted them twice and I am not counting them a third time, and one of them is a height.”

Nobody in that lane asked her which nine, and she did not say, and about four people in that lane have been thinking about it since.

---

The girl of seventeen found one of the nine on the evening of the first day of the fifty-fifth week, in her own book, in her own hand, and it was a number she wrote herself and nobody asked her for.

She had written it off the frame at Hollow Sallow on the fourth day of that week at the closing of the water, in the way a person writes a thing down when they are standing in front of it, and it is a figure of the water against the frame with nothing to do with any order, and there are four hands in the hundred-year-old book and the four hands are the Seat's and the mill's and a man of twenty-two's and a predecessor's, and not one of the four has ever stood at that frame and watched the water come up to it, and the second hand on that book is now paid a penny a day and is the girl's own, and what the second hand is for is to write down what she is told.

So she wrote it in her own book instead, and then she sat with it, and what she had found out is that there is nowhere in this basin to put a number a person wrote themselves.

The water-book has four columns and they belong to the four hands. A deed has a room and a copy and a press. A settlement has a wall and a copy down a hill in one hand and a man of thirty-four who reads it out. A figure she wrote off a frame at the closing of the water on a night when nobody had asked her for it has got a page in a girl's own book in a cart shed, and the cart shed is behind two houses and nobody keeps a book in a cart shed.

She wrote a second line under it and the second line is the finding, and she is seventeen and it is the first thing she has ever written down about the ordinary way this basin works, and it is: *NOBODY HAS ASKED ME FOR THIS AND I DO NOT KNOW WHERE IT GOES AND I AM NOT GOING TO ASK.*

The second hand of the water-book was kept in two hands that week, and a third book was begun, and the first thing in it was a number that did not go anywhere.

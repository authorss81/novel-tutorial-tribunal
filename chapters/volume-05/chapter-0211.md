# Chapter 211: The Slow Is Down

The causeway at Hollow Sallow is a hundred and forty years old and it is stone and gravel put in by persons unnamed, and on the fifth day of the fifty-first week it stood dry for the first time since the second shaking, and a man of about forty was standing in the middle of it with a crowbar, looking at it as though it had done him a personal injury.

He had not put a cart over it in five days and was not going to, and the nine carts a day were on the high road above him with their loads covered, and he was paid by the Anner, and the Anner had shut this road under an order that named a dam and a height and did not mention him.

“You are standing on it,” said Ilyan Vester.

“I am standing on it because I have stood on it for twenty years.” He put the crowbar down on its end. “It is the only road between the pit’s mouth and that market that does not add a morning. Five days I have been going round. Five days each way is ten days out of a hundred and forty, and there are nine of us, and the Anner is paying all of it, and the Anner would rather not, and they have said so out loud in a room, which is more than most of them do.” He picked the crowbar up again. “You are the man whose four pages did that.”

“Yes.”

“Then I have got nothing to say to you that I have not said to the crowbar.”

---

The slow had been down six days. That is the word this basin uses for the Sallow when it is up, and it had come up out of the flats on the evening of the sixth day of the fiftieth week and it had not gone back, and on the sixth night of it the water in the bed below the sluice was the highest anybody could remember and there was about two feet of it standing in the lane.

The copy of the four pages was on the doorpost of the third house from the top, which is Ilma Treen’s, and it had been there three days, and it had been rained on twice and it had not gone soft, because the Anner copies everything on a penny a page and the Anner does not do a thing twice.

A girl of seventeen was standing in the lane with a barrow’s handle under her arm, and she had been sent to read it out to a neighbour who cannot see at forty and has not got the use of the other eye, and she was doing it in a voice her father had told her to use, which is a voice people use for the dead and for documents.

She read the head, which is a day out of a water-book. She read the first line, which is the Anner and eleven feet above its own sill. She read the second, which is the causeway and the high road. She read the fourth, which is where it is to be read and who keeps the copy. And she came to the third line and stopped on it, because it is the shortest line on the page and it is the one that is hardest to say out loud in a lane.

“That eleven feet be the height,” she said, “and that the eleven houses of Hollow Sallow be below it, and that no person of Hollow Sallow be asked to leave any house by this order.”

She said it to the end of the lane, where about eleven people were standing, and nobody said anything, and the girl waited, and then she said the thing that is the finding of this chapter in her own mouth, and she said it the way she said the rest of it, in her father's voice for documents.

“It says how high the water is,” she said. “It does not say high as from where.”

---

Nobody in that lane could have said where eleven feet was measured from, and the girl had been in it since she was born and had never once asked, and she had put her hand flat on the doorpost the way you do with a thing that is about to be rained on.

The woman of about forty who washes the pen came up the hill from Sallowby an hour later, having been sent for by the girl, and found the two of them in the Seat’s one room, standing in front of four pages on a wall.

“It is on a doorpost in your lane,” said Ilyan Vester, to the clerk, “and it has been read out by a person of seventeen who has never been in this room and never will be. And she has asked the only question anybody in this basin has asked about a document, and it is the sill, and it is the same question the chamber at Orison asked nine years ago and nobody in this basin ever heard it ask it.”

Hollis Nance had the abstract of three rulings out on the table and the second one face up, and it had been on his table for three days and he had not known until about a minute ago that he had anything to do with it on the fifth day of the week.

“Read the third line of mine,” he said, “and then read the second line of that, and say them both out loud in this room, and do not let anybody tell you they are about different things.”

Ewan Troke read the first. The order named a dam, a height and a day. It said *to eleven feet above its own sill*, and it said *that eleven feet be the height, and that the eleven houses of Hollow Sallow be below it*, and the second eleven feet had nothing under it at all.

He read the second. The chamber of plenty, in its third turn and nine years back, upon the petition of a grocer of Coldbeck, had said that a height marked upon a doorpost is not a measurement, and that no order may name a height without naming the sill it is taken from.

Then he stopped, and put his finger on the paper, and waited for somebody else to do it, and nobody did, and he did it himself, out loud, in the flat voice he reads things in.

“The order names a sill for the dam,” he said. “The dam’s own. And the third line does not name one for the houses, and the eleven feet in the third line is the dam’s number, and the houses are four miles down a river on a bed that nobody has ever put a level on, and the chamber said nine years ago that you have to say which one, and the four pages do not say, and they are the first instrument this basin has ever had.”

Nobody in the room said anything for about as long as it takes to grind ink.

“Then it is void,” said a voice from the door, and it was not anybody’s voice that had been in the room before that morning.

“It is not void,” said Jory Vellan. He had his coat on and ochre in the seams of it and he was not going to take the coat off, and he had come to the room because a carter had told him at the pit’s mouth that morning that a man was in Sallowby saying a thing about a sill. “The second ruling is about a height marked on a doorpost. That is what it says. It is about eleven marks a man cut with a string in a wet year. It does not say that a height named in an order is void, and if it did, half of every deed drawn in this basin before the first shaking would be void this morning and there is nobody in this room who would throw them out.” He put his hand flat on the table, which is a thing a man does in this basin. “It says you have to name the sill. Name the sill. That is a page and a half hour and there are eleven seats in this room and I have used four of them in nine years.”

---

It took until the noon light to find out that there was nothing to find out, and that was the finding.

There is no form for asking a chamber whether its own order breaks its own ruling. The first ruling, nine years old and never in this basin, says the Seat is competent and does not need the nine days and does not need the seal. The second says a height wants a sill. The third says both halves of a deed are required before the Seat enters any matter of ground, and about forty-one lines of a list of drains and a chain-book page and four pages on a wall are not a matter of ground and nobody has ever made them one. All three of them are about this basin. None of them has ever been in this room except since the second of the week, and two of them are now about four pages on a wall in this room, and neither of the two has been asked whether it breaches the other.

Hollis Nance said that out loud, in his own mouth, standing at the end of his own table, and he said it the way a clerk says a thing he has checked twice.

“I have four hundred and eleven pounds in the year in a book in a drawer,” he said, “and none of the three of those is in that book, and I have paid for sixty-six sheets at a time twice a year for nine years, and a man comes and takes whatever is in the gap, and he is paid at Orison and not by us, and he has never once told me what he has taken, and I have never once asked him, and I had four hundred and eleven pounds’ worth of not asking and I did not spend it.”

He got the pen out and then stopped with it over the paper.

“It is five pounds and six,” he said. “Entry a pound, the day four, and nine days’ board at eightpence. Two of them is ten pounds and twelve, because a pound is two hundred and forty pence and one of them is twelve hundred and seventy-two, and twice that is two thousand five hundred and forty-four, and that is ten pounds and a hundred and forty-four pence, and a hundred and forty-four pence is twelve shillings and nothing over. That is the whole of what a basin this size can find out about itself.”

“Who is paying it,” said Ilyan Vester.

“I am,” said Hollis Nance. “Not the Seat, because the Seat has no column for it, and the column that would hold it is the one for things the Seat does not do, and I am not going to open a column in my own index on the sixth day of a week about a question nobody in Sallowby asked me. And I would like that written down in whatever you are writing, and I would like it written down as what it is and not as what anybody is generous about.”

Nobody wrote it down.

---

He put a sheet in his own hand into a bag and tied it, and the sheet had the third line of the four pages copied on one side of it and the second ruling copied on the other, and the day on the outside of the bag in the same number that was in the water-book, because the bag is going into the gap in the wall and the man with the bag does not read paper and cannot be asked what day it is.

“The first one is not come back,” he said. “It went in on the third and I have no book of what goes in the gap, because there is no book of what goes in the gap, and I have had nine years to make one and I have not, so I cannot tell you for a fact what I sent them, and I can tell you it was on the third and it has not come back and a thing that has not come back has not been refused, and that is worse.”

He stopped before the string was tied.

“If you want a number on it, read it off to me,” he said. “I cannot use a day out of a grain year, because a grain year does not exist in this basin and a deed that arrives in Orison with a grain year on it is a deed that arrives about nothing. Read me the number out of your own book and I shall put it on the outside, and the man with the bag will carry a day that this valley has actually been living, and if anybody in Orison wants to know how old the thing is they will have to come and stand in the lane and see it.”

So Ilyan Vester took out a penny exercise book and a stub of lead, and he did not say the number to a page. He said it out loud in a room with five people in it, and he said what it counted, and this was the first time in about a year that he had told anybody what was at the top of the page.

“One hundred and fifty-nine,” he said. “Days out of the water-book, from the second shaking, and the water-book is the Anner’s and it has been kept in four hands, and it is not a day of a grain year and it does not convert into one and there is no office in this basin that can convert it, so if anybody at the other end of that bag reads a day on the outside of it, that day is a day you could stand in a lane and look at.”

Hollis Nance wrote it on the outside of the bag in a small hard hand and tied the string with a knife-cut end, because that is how the two that came before it were tied, and then he put the bag in the gap in the wall, and the gap is about the size of a person, and nothing in the room said anything about a bag going into a wall.

---

The last hour of the fifth day of the fifty-first week went back up the hill and along the bank, and the water was two feet in the lane and moving, and a woman of thirty-eight came up out of it with a barrow of stone and put the barrow down before she said anything, which is what a person does when there are people on a bank she is working.

“Two feet,” she said.

“It was a foot and a bit on the fourth,” said Ewan Troke.

“It was a foot and a bit on the fourth and it is two feet now and it has been six days and six nights, and I have been the sluice for eleven years and I have not seen it do that in any of the six, and I am not frightened of it, and I am telling you the number because you are the only three people in this valley who have written a number down since the fourth.”

That is the whole of what the order did on the ground in its first six days. It shut a causeway that a hundred and forty years of persons unnamed had put in, and it turned nine carts a day onto a high road that adds a morning and a half each way for about a hundred and forty days, and it held eleven feet above a sill four miles up a river with four men and a barrow, and it named a dam, a height, a day and a party, and it did not name the person the water is going to arrive on, and the only person in that village who had read the four pages at all was a girl of seventeen with a barrow's handle under her arm who had been sent to read it out to a neighbour with one eye.

On the causeway, at about the time the light went, the man of forty put the crowbar in the shed and went up onto the high road, where nine carts were standing in the wet with the horses’ heads down, and ten days out of a hundred and forty was what the Anner had bought, and the Anner would rather not, and the four men and the barrow went down to the dam at the second light and the clay went on in the same order it has gone on in for eleven years.

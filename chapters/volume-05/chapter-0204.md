# Chapter 204: The Sluice, And The Half Wage

The Sallow comes into the Hollow at Hollow Sallow and it is nine feet from the sill of the sluice down to the bed, and in the sixteen weeks a year when there is anything in it, it comes down all at once and not gradually, and a man who has seen it twice does not need to be told about the third.

The frame is oak and it is about twice the height of a man and it has been in the same place since before the first shaking, and the gate in it is new, or near enough, and there is a new frame behind the new gate, and that is the whole of what anybody has spent on this structure in the last three years.

There is one woman on the bank and she had a barrow of stone beside her when the three of them came up, and she put the barrow down first, which is the thing a person does when somebody comes onto a bank they are working, and then she came and looked at them.

“Ilma Treen,” said Ewan Troke. “This is the sluice.”

“I know it is the sluice,” she said. “I have been the sluice for eleven years.”

Nobody in that party had asked Ewan Troke to bring anybody anywhere, and he had done it anyway, on the way, without explaining, and it is on the record that a person who takes a stranger to a person on a day of his own is not the same as a person who is produced and there is a difference and it is not a small one and it is the reason the three of them got anything out of this basin in five days.

---

“Nine shillings a week,” she said, before she had been asked anything, “in the sixteen weeks. The Anner gives me four and six of it. The Seat gives me four and six of it and has not given me four and six of it for three years and I have not had a wage that came every week since the turn before.”

“How much of it,” said Ilyan Vester.

“That is the whole of what I have ever said about it and I have said it to a handful of men in three years and every one of them was a man who wanted me to say it louder.” She put a hand on the frame. “Sixteen weeks at four and six is three pounds and twelve, and three years of it is ten pounds and sixteen, and if you want the whole of it, it is seven pounds and four a year, and the four that I have come to expect is the half the Anner gives me on the first day of the week in an envelope and the half the Seat has not given me since the turn before is the other half and I have not chased it and I am not going to, because the only way you chase the Seat is in a room, and I have never in my life been in that room, and the women in this village who have been in that room are two, and one of them is seventy-six and the other one is the wife of a man on the Seat, and there is nobody else.”

“What is the Seat’s half spent on,” said Tarin Keel.

She looked at him. It was the first time she had looked at any of them properly, and it took about three seconds.

“That,” she said, “is a question.” And then she stopped, and Tarin Keel did not say anything, and about four seconds went past with nobody in them, and Ewan Troke shifted the case on his shoulder and did not fill it, which he is good at.

“No,” she said. “The Seat’s half is not spent on anything. The Seat has not had the money. That is the thing you would find if you looked, and nobody has looked, because looking means a book and a room, and I have never been in the room.”

“Then you are owed ten pounds sixteen and the Seat does not have it,” said Tarin Keel.

“The Seat has four hundred and eleven pounds in the year,” said Ilma Treen, “and I know that because I have heard it said in a kitchen, and the four hundred and eleven is not my figure and it is nobody’s figure and I am not going to be a woman who quotes a kitchen. My figure is four shillings and sixpence a week for sixteen weeks, and it is in a book in a room in Sallowby in a hand that is not mine, and if a person goes and gets that book out then they can have my figure and not the kitchen.”

---

The book was in the room in Sallowby, and it was brought to the bank in the afternoon because she would not go and get it and would not ask for it to be brought and Ewan Troke went and got it and brought it in the flat of his hand, and nobody in this record will say that was the first of anything.

It is a water-book. It is a day-book of the Sallow kept at the Seat for as long as there has been a Seat, and the entries are in four or five hands over a hundred years, and about four of the lines in it are a hundred and fifty years old and are in copperplate, and the last of the entries before that was in a hand about forty years old that had nothing to do with the water-book at all and had been brought out of a press for this and put back again.

The sluice is in it four times in a hundred years, and one of the four is forty years old and the other three are in the last three years, and all three of those are eleven days short of the day in the standing order.

“You have opened it early,” said Ilyan Vester, “three years running, eleven days before the day in the order.”

“I have opened it early eleven years running and the order is three years old,” said Ilma Treen, “and the order was made by a man who has not been down here since, and it says a day and the day is the day the pans are turned at Coldbeck, and the pans are a month behind us in a wet year, and I have never once been able to know in advance which kind of year it is going to be.”

“Which is what he meant,” said Tarin Keel.

“No,” she said, and she said it fast. “There is no what he meant. There is a day and I open on the day. That is the whole of it and it is not an argument.”

---

Nobody could read the book except two of them, and it was read out, and the two who read it read it differently on one line and neither of them noticed and a third person caught it and it was the only moment in five days in which anybody in this party said something that made another party member stop.

“The last entry is a fortnight back and it is in a hand that is not the water-book hand,” said Sera Quill. “It is a mill hand. It says that the sluice was standing open on the ninth and that the water was in the Hollow and that nobody came.”

“That is my hand and my mill,” said Ilma Treen. “I have a mill on the Anner water and I write on the Seat’s book because the Seat cannot make me buy a book. I write what I have to write on it and the Seat has never once asked me what I am writing on it.”

The three other entries in the book were in a different hand and older and they all said the same thing, which was that the sluice had been opened before the day, and the oldest of them is forty years back and has the word again at the end of it and nothing else, and the hand on that one is the under-manager’s own and he was twenty when he wrote it.

“It was not unusual,” said Ewan Troke. “I would not have told you it was unusual if I had not read it in front of you. The Seat’s order has named a day since before the first shaking, and the sluice has been opened early since before the first shaking, and the word again at the end of that page is the under-manager’s own word and he was twenty when he wrote it, and he has never told anybody in this basin that he wrote it, and I found it out in the last half hour standing on a wet bank.”

Nobody asked the obvious question for the better part of a minute, which is not long.

“Why,” said Ilyan Vester.

Nobody answered him, and it was Ilma Treen who answered him a good while after that and she did it while moving stone, which she did not stop doing, because she had come up the bank to move a barrow of stone and four people had arrived on it and she was not going to leave a barrow of stone standing in the sun for a conversation.

“The Anner’s road comes down off the ridge,” she said, “and it crosses the causeway where the Sallow goes out south, and about nine carts a day go over that causeway in the season, and the water goes over the causeway too, and if the sluice is standing when the carts are on it then the causeway is not there any more.”

She set the stone down.

“Nine years back I lost a causeway, in one day, in a wet year, and I could not tell you now which day it was and I have never once needed to,” she said, “and there was a cart on it and a mule on the cart and nobody in either. And the Anner shut the road for nine days and the Anner lost the week and said so in four rooms, and the Seat said the sluice was open and that was true, and the Seat said nothing else, and I have been the sluice for eleven years and eleven days early in every one of them, and the first four of the eleven there was no order at all, and I have never said this out loud to anybody who was not the man who lost the mule.”

“Then why eleven days,” said Tarin Keel.

“Because eleven days is what it takes them to get nine carts off the causeway and onto the high road, and the high road adds a morning and a half each way, and the Anner does it because I asked them once, in the first year, and I did not ask them properly, I asked, and they did it.” She moved another stone. “And they never cost it to me. It was their week. It was their carts. It was their road. And I have been getting paid four shillings and sixpence a week for sixteen weeks and holding the water in the Hollow for four more weeks than anybody agreed to pay me for, and if you want to make that into a thing I have stolen, you may, and I will listen, because I have not heard it said out loud before and I would like to know which of us is right.”

---

The question nobody had asked her, which was not asked by the man who had come nine days for a different thing, was asked by a man who had been counting carts on roads for nineteen years and who asked it in about nine words and got an answer to it.

“How many days a year are you paid for.”

“Sixteen,” said Ilma Treen.

“And how many days a year do you work.”

She stopped moving stone.

“A hundred and forty days a year, and I have been paid for a hundred and twelve of them, and I have never once done a hundred and forty and been paid for a hundred and forty, and I would like that written down somewhere, and I do not know who in this basin writes things down.”

Ilyan Vester took out a penny exercise book and a stub of lead and sat down on the sill of the sluice, which is wet, and which he did not notice until about four lines later, and he wrote the day at the top of the page in his own hand, and the day in this basin is a day out of a water-book and not a day of a grain year, and the number that went on the page is one hundred and fifty-two, and it is the third time in five days that he has put a number of that kind at the top of a page in this valley, and it is the first time he has put it in front of anybody.

He did not tell her what the number was. He had never told anybody in two hundred and one days what the number at the top of the page was, and there was no reason on earth why he was going to start in a village four miles from a chandler’s shop.

“Twenty-eight days,” he said, to the page and not to her, and then he said it again to her, because it is his own arithmetic and he does it out loud, and it is the habit of a man who has spent a year in a room where nobody could check him and has decided that the checking is the point. “A hundred and forty days and a hundred and twelve paid is twenty-eight days, and twenty-eight days is four weeks. At four and six the week, and a wage being a week’s wage, four weeks is eighteen shillings exactly and there is nothing over, and eighteen shillings is what the Seat has not paid her for holding the water in this Hollow for a hundred and forty days a year.”

Ilma Treen looked at him for a while and then at the gate and then back at him.

“Say that again,” she said, “and do not say it to a page.”

He said it again. He said it to her.

The sun was well down and the Sallow had about a foot and a half in it, which is nothing, and the water in the Hollow was moving at the speed of a man walking, and about two hundred yards off there was a woman on the bank above the mill with a barrow going down toward the water, and the mill had a wheel on it about as big as a cartwheel and the wheel was not turning and would not turn until there was more water in the Sallow, and the two of them are paid out of the same four hundred and eleven pounds a year, and the four hundred and eleven is a figure that came out of a kitchen in Sallowby and that nobody in Hollow Sallow can produce out loud, and the book in Ewan Troke’s flat hand says which half of what and does not say what the Seat has not got.

Then Ilma Treen went down the bank to open the gate for the last time that day, and she did it in the ordinary way, at the closing of the water, with a barrow of stone in the other hand, and she did not look back at the four of them to see whether they were watching, because in eleven years of doing it there has never been anybody on that bank at the closing of the water to look at.

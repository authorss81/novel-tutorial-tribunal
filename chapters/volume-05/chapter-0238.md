# Chapter 238: A Cart Is Not Where It Was

On the second day of the fifty-fifth week a carter went over the shoulder with a load of ochre, and he did it because the customer was four days short of Orison and the shoulder is two days shorter for that one place, and he had known the road was on the map since before the water and had never had a reason to try it, and this week he had a reason, and the reason was good.

He was back in the yard at the second light on the fourth of that week and he was a day and a half late on his rounds and he had lost about a hundred and twenty miles of high road that the Anner pays for, and nobody in the basin knew any of that on the second of that week, and on the fourth of that week four people knew all of it and about four hundred could not have said who was the first.

He was not the first. The first was a carter in a yard at Sallowby who asked a question at about the sixth hour on the second, out loud, in the ordinary way, and the question was why anybody had ever gone round by the high road at all.

---

The second line of the four-page order on the wall of the water-seat of Sallowby reads as follows, and it is the second line and it is one sentence and it is about a road.

“That the causeway at Hollow Sallow be shut for so long as the sluice stand, and that about nine carts a day go by the high road while it is shut, and that the Anner bear the whole of the cost of the high road in every week of the closure.”

It has been on that wall since the hundred and fifty-fifth day. It was the line that shut a causeway that a hundred and forty years of persons unnamed had put in, and it is the line that turned nine carts a day out of the flat and up onto the ridge, and it is the line that the mine has been paying for every day since in the only way it pays for anything, which is by doing a thing it would rather not do.

Nobody in Hollow Sallow had read it. That is the sentence this week turns on and it has to be said plainly: about four hundred and ten people had lived above that line for a fortnight and not one of them had read it, and the reason none of them had read it is that it is on a wall in a market town, and the reason it is on a wall in a market town is that it is the only document this basin has produced in about a year, and a document that a village has to read all the way through before it can know what has been done to it is not a document a village can act on.

A woman of about forty read it out loud on the fourth of that week, in a lane, at the closing of the water, to about seven people, and it was the first time in her life she had read it and she had been in that room for nine years.

She read it because a carter had asked her a question about a cart.

“He came down off the ridge with an empty cart on him and asked me why the Anner's carts go round the long way when the causeway is dry at both ends,” she said, “and I said because the water is on the flats, and he said the water is not on that part of the flats today, and I said I did not know, and he said that is all right, and he went up the hill. And I went up the hill behind him about a quarter of an hour later, because I wanted to know what a thing I have said out loud about a river in a room actually says.”

Nobody in that lane stopped her. She read the second line out loud, in a road, in front of about seven people, and she did not change a word of it, and that is the difference between this and the seventh of the fifty-fourth week and the whole of what the difference is.

“Then that is why,” said a carter standing at the back. “Nine carts a day. That is not a river, that is a schedule. I have been going round by the high road for eleven years because a piece of paper says so, and I never once asked what the paper said, and I did not need to, because the paper said it and the paper is on a wall.”

“It is on a wall in Sallowby,” said the woman of about forty, “and you are at the bottom of a hill four miles off and nobody has ever brought you up here to be told.”

Nobody answered that either, and about seven people in that lane had found out in about two minutes that a piece of paper they had never read all the way through depends on where a cart goes, and about a hundred and forty people in that village did not find out and never will unless somebody walks it round.

---

The under-manager had it by the fourth hour of the fourth of that week and he had it from a carter at a gate, and he came down off the hill on purpose and did not wait to be asked, and what he said in the room with eleven seats in it was not about the cart.

“Say the number out loud,” he said to the clerk of the Seat. “The one you have had in your book for a month. Say it and let me say the other one after it.”

Hollis Nance said it and he said it the way he says a price.

“A hundred and seventy-eight pounds, thirteen shillings and fourpence a year, for as long as the roofs want doing. Forty-one thousand and forty pence of it is nineteen roofs at nine pounds, and one thousand seven hundred and twenty-eight is the whole wage at nine shillings a week for sixteen weeks, and a hundred and twelve pence is a penny a day for a second hand on the water-book.”

“And the other one,” said the under-manager. “Nine carts a day on a road that adds a morning and a half each way for about a hundred and forty days, and the Anner bearing the whole of the cost of it, and nobody having ever said what a morning and a half costs because nobody in this basin has a figure for it and the figure is in a pit's book and not in a room.”

“Yes,” said the under-manager. “And here is what happened on the second of this week and I am going to say it once and I am not going to be asked about it twice. A carter took a load over the shoulder because a customer is four days short of Orison and the shoulder is two days shorter for that one place, and he was right, and the road is on a map that is nine years old, and it is in a yard now where a cart-yardful of men have it in chalk with no money under it, and the whole of what that carter did was decide that one load was worth two days.”

He put his hand flat on the table, and Jory Vellan does that, and about four people in that room had never noticed and about four had.

“And that is the whole of it,” he said. “There is no villain in this and I want that said in this room before anybody else says a word about it. That carter did not think about a paper. That carter did not think about a dam. He looked at a road and two days and a load of ochre, and he was right about all three, and about eleven men in a cart yard in this market town have been putting a road in chalk on a cart's side since the fifth of last week and not one of them is a bad man and not one of them is a good man, they are men who count carts.”

“And what it does to the sheet,” said the woman of about forty, “is that the sheet says the Anner pay the whole of the above, and the sheet does not say the Anner shall pay it while nine carts go the long way round, and the sheet has never once said a word about a road, and there is not a single word of a road in the eleven hands on it.”

“There is not,” said the under-manager. “And the second line of the four pages is nothing but a road, and that paper is on the same wall, and about four hundred and ten people have never read either of them, and a carter has just found out in two days what is on that wall, from a woman who read it out to him in a lane at the closing of the water.”

---

And the thing that came out of it, and it came out of the under-manager on the second half of the fourth of that week, standing at a shut causeway in the wind, is the thing that nobody in this valley had put together before and it took about four minutes to say and about seven people were on the bank.

Eleven people built a piece of paper in eleven places over about a week and a mine paid for it, and a man of thirty-one took his own name off it because he was right to, and a man of thirty-four who cannot read it reads it out in a road at the closing of the water and about forty people find out what has been done that day. And a mine has a standing interest in a number that put water in nineteen houses, and that is written down and it is a fact and it is not the fault of anybody.

And there is a number on a dam, and there is a number on a road, and the road number is a number of carts and the dam number is a number of feet, and the two numbers are on two pieces of paper on the same wall in the same room, and neither of the two papers has ever mentioned the other, and until this week there was no reason in this basin to know that they were in the same room.

A sheet that says a mine shall pay a wage and a gate and a penny a day for as long as the roofs want doing, and an order that says a mine shall bear the cost of nine carts a day on a high road for about a hundred and forty days a year, are the same instrument. They are on the same wall. They have been on the same wall for a fortnight and a third of that fortnight has not been read by anybody it concerns, and a carter with a good reason has just taken one of the nine off the high road, and nobody in this basin can now say whether the mine is paying for eight carts or nine, and the man whose job it is to say is the man who wrote both of the numbers down in a book a hundred and thirty years old.

He did not say he was frightened. He said his name was Jory Vellan and he was fifty-two and he had been under-manager for nineteen years and that he was going to go down to the dam now, and that the clay was waiting, and that this was the sixth time in a month that a thing in this basin had turned out to be a road.

---

The fifth roof came off on the fourth of that week, which is the fourth of the fifth of that week, and it came off the sixth house from the bottom of the lane, and the timber for it had gone up two days behind, and a man of fifty-six did it in about four days, and the work was not enough, because the roof went on about four days later than the first one went on and there are about a hundred and forty days of this weather in front of it and eighteen roofs, and because a man of fifty-six with a ladder on his shoulder has found out this week that a lane of about two hundred yards is four days of work in this basin, and a joiner with a rule has found out that a frame in a bank is not going to be looked at again until somebody has money to pay his shilling an hour, and a girl of seventeen is a penny a day and a man of thirty-four reads a paper out loud that he cannot read, and none of those four facts is the same size and all four of them are in the same week.

# Chapter 217: Nineteen Houses

They counted it at the first light on the fourth day of the fifty-second week, and the people who counted it were the people who live in it, and the counting took until the noon light and was done door to door on foot because there is nowhere in Hollow Sallow a cart can go that the carts of the Anner could not have gone on the causeway nine days ago, and the water was in the lane and had not gone back.

Eleven. That is what the order said and that is what the marks said, and the eleven were right about the eleven.

Nineteen.

The eight were not houses that anybody had claimed were below anything. They were houses that had come up after the marks were cut, in the year the third house from the top was rebuilt and in two years after that, and one of them was a house that had been a barn in the year of the mark and had a door and a hearth and a family in it now, and the man who had turned the barn into a house had cut no mark on it because there was nobody alive in the lane at the time who had been told to, and he is forty-four and he did not know for four days that his house was in anything.

“Eleven was never a number of houses,” said Garrow Lee, from the top of a low wall with his rule in his hand. “Eleven was a number of marks. And I have been trying to get somebody to measure the marks since the sixth year after they were cut, and everybody in this lane has been perfectly honest with me for about nine years about the fact that a mark is a mark, and nobody has ever once asked the eight.”

Nobody in this valley is a villain, and it is worth saying plainly because in about four days a great many people were going to want one. The mine did what it was told. The clerk wrote four complete lines in a fair hand and read them out twice and said out loud before anybody else had worked it out that there was no hole in any of them, and nobody noticed. The bond said the Anner should hold the dam to a height of eleven feet above its own sill, and that Hollow Sallow should not be flooded by the Anner, and that the Anner was not answerable for any flooding not held by that height, and the Anner held the dam to eleven feet above its own sill, and the bond is a bond and the first two of those three sentences are the promise and the promise was kept, exactly, by the party it names, in the way it was written.

Both halves were kept. Nobody broke anything. The village the mine had promised to protect is flooded by the fulfilment of the mine's promise, and that is not a sentence anybody in this basin is going to be able to improve on this year.

---

About a hundred and forty people, out of about four hundred and ten, were in the nineteen. The rest of the village had a foot and a half of the Sallow in the lane at the first light and had it out by the noon light, and the girl of seventeen who had read the third line out loud five times had it out of her own house by the second hour because her house is the third from the top and it is the highest of the eleven, and being the third from the top had turned out to be the whole of what anybody knew about it.

Nobody drowned. Nobody is ruined. Both of those are true at the same time in the same lane on the same morning and there is no word in this basin that holds both of them, and the four people from the valley spent the morning finding that out, one house at a time, and it took until the light went.

The man who does roofs came over the top of the bank at the second light with a ladder on his shoulder and did not go near the water, and he looked at nineteen roofs from the bank for about as long as it takes to drink a cup of something, and then he came back up and said a figure and would not split it.

“Brann Slee,” he said, to the four of them, before he was asked anything. “Fifty-six. I do the roofs on this lane and on the eleven above it and about forty more, and I have done nine of the nineteen, and I have done four of them more than once. Nine pounds, and that is a roof wet through, taken off and put back and the timber under it, and I have never given anybody a price in parts in my life and I am not starting this year, because the last time I broke a roof into pieces for a man in a good coat he took the battens out of it and left me the thatch.”

“Nine pounds a roof,” said Tarin Keel.

“Nine pounds a roof,” said Brann Slee, “and there is no man in Hollow Sallow who can tell you what a roof is made of, and that is not because they are stupid, it is because nobody has ever had to price one, and if you ask a woman in this lane what her roof costs she will tell you it is not a thing you pay for, and she will be right about her own life and wrong about a roof, and both of those are going to be in the same conversation in about a week and there is nothing anybody can do about that in advance.”

---

Then the thing came up, in the flat white nothing that has put things in front of him in about a year and a half, in a lane with about thirty people standing in it at the second hour of the morning, and it did not say one word about the nineteen or the hundred and forty or the water.

**THE FOUR LINES NAME A DAM, A HEIGHT, A DAY AND A PARTY TO PAY, AND THEY NAME NOBODY ELSE, AND THE PART OF THE DOCUMENT THAT WAS NEVER DRAWN IS THE WATER.**
**WHAT IS NOT DECIDED: WHETHER A THING THAT WAS NOT NAMED MAY BE CARRIED BY A PARTY THAT WAS, AND BY WHOM, AND ON WHAT TERMS.**

He read it twice and it took nothing out of him and it answered nothing, and the matter it was about had been made ten days before by a clerk with a pen in a room four miles off, and the water it was about was standing in a lane.

Nobody in that lane saw anything, and his leg was what his leg has been, and nobody in this valley has been told a word about any of it, and the third line of the four pages is on a wall in Sallowby and is correct on its face.

---

The woman who keeps the sluice said it at about the second hour, and she said it to a man of thirty-one who had a book in his coat and had not taken it out.

She had been in water to the knee since the middle of the night and she had come up out of it to get a barrow, and she put the barrow down first, and then she said the sentence, and it is nine words long and it is the finding of this chapter and it is hers, and she is not a lawyer and she is not a lawyer because there is no lawyer in this basin and has not been one since before the first shaking.

“It named a dam,” said Ilma Treen. “It named a height. It named a day. It never named the water.”

Nobody argued with her about it. A man of thirty-one with a book in his coat did not write it down for about four minutes and then he did write it down, and he wrote it in his own hand, and it went into a penny exercise book on the basin side of a page that had a day at the top of it, and it is the first of the things said out loud in this basin in the last sixteen days that is not about a clock and not about a deed, and it is the shortest sentence any of them have produced, and it is about four hundred years of somebody's habit of writing orders down.

The clerk of the Seat was in the lane and heard it and did not write it down, and the joiner was in the lane and heard it and had a rule in his hand, and the chainman heard it and had nothing to write with.

“It is not the clerk's line,” said Hollis Nance, afterwards, on the bank, to nobody. “I wrote four lines that did not say that. I said out loud that there was no hole in them and I was right and there was no hole in them, and what is missing out of them is not a hole, and that is the thing I have been unable to say in a room for nine days.”

---

“That is the roofs,” said Tarin Keel, who has counted carts on roads for nineteen years. “That is one man, one roof, wet through, taken off and put back, and there are nineteen of them.”

“That is the roofs and nothing else,” he said. “There is nobody in this valley who can pay any part of it and there is no way of working out what the other eighteen things a flood does cost, because there are no other eighteen things yet. They have not happened. And a man of fifty-six has told me a roof is a roof and will not break it into pieces, and that is the correct answer and it is also the end of the arithmetic.”

Ilyan Vester did the second half of it out loud, because the habit of reading a figure back in front of the people who are going to have to live in it is the only instrument he has ever had, and it is not enough. Nine pounds is two hundred and sixteen pence, and two hundred and sixteen pence times nineteen is forty-one thousand and forty, and forty-one thousand and forty pence is a hundred and seventy-one pounds with nothing over.

A hundred and seventy-one pounds is forty-one thousand and forty pence, and the whole year of a woman who keeps the sluice at Hollow Sallow is nine shillings a week for sixteen weeks, which is a hundred and forty-four shillings, which is one thousand seven hundred and twenty-eight pence, which is seven pounds and four. Forty-one thousand and forty divided by one thousand seven hundred and twenty-eight is twenty-three and three quarters, and the answer is about twenty-four years.

She was standing about ten feet away in the road with a barrow and she had heard the number and she did not say anything about it, and nobody said anything to her about it, and she has been standing in the middle of that figure for eleven years and she is in it again this morning with two feet of the Sallow in her own house and a barrow she cannot get out of the lane with.

---

At the first light on the fifth of that week a man of thirty-one with a book in his coat went down a lane in Hollow Sallow to a house on the lower side where the door was still on its hinge, and the door was the one thing in that house that had not been hurt, because the hinge is above the line the water got to and everything else in that house is under it.

A woman of about forty came out behind him with a ledger under her arm that she had not been carrying the day before, and it is the Seat's water-book, and she had taken it out of the drawer in a room in Sallowby and carried it four miles down a hill in her own two hands because the four of them had gone to the upper end of the lane and she was not going to wait for them, and the book is a hundred years old and is kept in four hands and is the only record anybody in this valley has of what the water did, and she held it up out of the wet and went down the lane with it, and she did not open it in the lane and she did not open it in the house, and she put it on the sill of a house that had two feet of the Sallow in it and turned round and went back up the hill to the bank, where a clerk of a Seat of forty-four was standing in the road with a lamp in his hand, doing his work.

# Chapter 232: A Road That Does Not Go Through Orison

The market at Sallowby is once a week and it is the only day of the week on which about four hundred people, a pit, a water-seat, a scrivener and a yardful of carts are in the same street, and on the fifth day of the fifty-fourth week the nine carts were on the high road at the first light with their loads covered and the rest of the market got on with it, which is what a market day in this basin has been for nineteen years.

The high road is the long way round. It comes down off the ridge, and where the causeway goes under the river on the flat it goes up and over and round by the shoulder, and the round adds a morning and a half each way, and about nine carts a day for about a hundred and forty days a year make that addition because the sluice is standing and the causeway is shut, and the Anner bears the whole of that cost and would rather not and is bearing it, and it is the second line of a four-page order on a wall in the water-seat that says so in one sentence which nobody outside about thirty clerks has ever read.

That is the whole of the money in this basin, and it was said out loud in a cart yard on the fifth day of that week by a man of fifty-two in ochre dust, and then it was said a second time by a man who counts carts and who made the arithmetic say the other thing, and then it was said a third time by about nine men who are not the same men and who were not asked.

---

What happened in the cart yard was that a carter asked a question out loud.

He asked it about carting, which is what carters talk about, and it was not a political question and it was not about the chamber at Orison and it was not about a bench. He asked whether anybody had ever counted how many days of the year the shoulder was under two feet of the Sallow, and the answer was that nobody had ever counted it because the shoulder is not a road and nobody had ever wanted to make it one.

And that was the whole of the beginning, and about four men in that yard stopped what they were doing, and one of the four is a man who counts carts on roads for a living and who had a figure in his head before the sentence was finished.

“There is a road on that shoulder,” said Tarin Keel. “I am not saying it is a good road. I am saying it is on the map, and the map is older than the water and was drawn by a man who was surveying the pans and not the road, and it goes along the top of the shoulder for about four miles and comes down into the flats on the other side of the ridge, and it comes out at a market.”

“How far?”

“Four days short of Orison,” said Tarin Keel. “And I have never been to the end of it and neither has anybody in this yard, and I know it is four days short because a man came through here with salt before the water and said it was, and I have been carrying that figure ever the way I carry figures.”

Nobody in that yard said anything for about as long as it takes to turn a cup round on a board.

“There is no gate on it,” he said. “That is the whole of it. That is the entire argument and I have been carrying it round this yard since I was twenty and never said it out loud because there is no money behind it and I am not a man who says a thing out loud when there is no money behind it. Every trade in this basin has carried salt and ochre past the gate at the top of the pass for eleven years and not one of us has ever asked what is at the other end of it. I have asked. It is a room with a yard in it, and there are about thirty clerks in that building, and there is a bench of four above them, and the bench has been right three times since the first shaking about this basin and every one of the three was right in a way that cost us something.”

“And the market at the end of the shoulder road,” said the scrivener, “has got no court in it.”

---

Wenna Skell said that, and she was thirty-three and she copies deeds over a chandler's shop and has copied about six hundred and forty of them in eleven years, and about ninety of them are for the Anner line, and she was in that yard because a page of hers had to go up the hill with a copy in it and she took it herself.

“I have been up that road twice,” she said, “and I have never been past the second ford with a load, and I have never been to the end of it. But I will tell you what I know, and I know it because I copy the deeds of houses in this town and I copy them for the people who own them and not for the bench.”

She put the page down on a cart's side.

“Everybody in this valley thinks the water-seat is there for the water. It is the other way about, and it has been the other way about as long as that room has been open, and I do not think anybody has ever said it out loud. There is a room in that town with a press in it, and the press has a bond of forty pounds in it, and the bond is the Anner's, and a bond is forty pounds in a box, and that is the whole of what the bond is for — so that nobody has to go and look. And the box is in the press, and the press is in the room, and the room is why there is a clerk in it, and the clerk is why there are eleven seats, and eight of the eleven have never had anybody in them at all. The water has been brought to that room every year since the turn before and nobody in this basin has ever gone the other way.”

Nobody in that yard argued with her about it, and about four of them had never thought about it and about six thought about it and went on doing what they were doing.

“And that is why the road is the thing and not the water,” she said. “Nine carts a day and a morning and a half each way, and four pounds a ton at the mouth and eleven at the market, and seven pounds a ton between them, and that seven is the road. The road is the reason there is a dam. The dam is the reason the causeway is shut. The causeway is the reason there are nine carts on the high road instead of the same carts on the flat. And every one of those reasons is a thing a man can point at, and none of them is the water, and the Seat is called the water-seat because that is the easiest name to give a room that is really there for a box.”

---

The route was on a cart's side by the sixth hour, in chalk, in nine or ten hands, and it had four miles of shoulder on it and a bend at the second ford and a market at the end of it with no name in this basin except the market at the end of the drove road.

It cost nothing to draw. Nobody had been asked whether they wanted it and nobody had been asked whether they would pay for it and nobody in that yard thought for about four minutes about either of those two questions, and then it was twelve o'clock and the market was over and the cart yard emptied out the way it empties.

The thing that is the shape of the next book in this valley was named once, in a cart yard, on the fifth day of the fifty-fourth week, by a man who counts carts and who said it because he had counted the carts and not because he had read anything, and it was not followed, and nothing else in this week was about it.

Nobody in this basin has a figure for that road. The one man who has something that could be turned into a figure was asleep behind a cart shed on the fifth of that week because he had been on a dam until the middle of the night, and what was in his head stayed in his head, and the hands in that yard wrote a road on a cart's side and no number under it.

---

At the second light on the sixth of that week the nine carts were on the high road with their loads covered, and the high road adds a morning and a half each way, and a carter checked his horses, and a carter who counts nothing checked his horses twice because it was the fourth morning of the fifth week and he had been doing it eleven years and there is nothing else in a morning.

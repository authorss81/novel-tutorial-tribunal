# Chapter 243: Sixty Tons Is Two Counts

The number that has been sitting in this basin like a stone in a shoe since the fifth of the last week stopped being a stone on the second day of the fifty-sixth week, and nothing was done to it, and the man who did it had counted carts on roads for nineteen years and was standing in a cart yard with his coat off.

He had it in about four minutes and he said it in nine words, and the nine words are these, and he said them to a barrow and not to a room.

“We counted the pit. Nobody ever counted the valley.”

---

Nobody had put sixty tons into a barrow before. What they had put into it was a difference.

The chamber of plenty has one thousand and forty tons written down in its own book about the trade of this basin in the eighth year, which is four years back, and this basin has been saying about eleven hundred tons for nineteen years, and the difference between the two numbers is sixty tons, and at the seven pounds a ton that a ton is worth between the mouth of the pit and the market at Sallowby that is four hundred and twenty pounds a year.

That was what it had been for a week. It was on a board in chalk against the outside of a cart shed at Hollow Sallow, and about seven people had walked past it and two had stopped, and nobody in Hollow Sallow had asked what it was for.

What changed it was that a man put his coat off.

“There is no weigh-book,” said Tarin Keel. “Not at the pit and not at Sallowby and not in the seat's room and not in that shed you have chalked on. I have asked. Four of us went to four doors in half an hour. A hundredweight of salt at the pans is a pound and fourpence. A ton of red ochre at the mouth of that pit is four pounds and at Sallowby eleven. And there is not one book in this basin with a weight in it, and there never has been one, and that is not because anybody is dishonest. It is because nobody has ever had to write a weight down to be paid for it.”

“Six hundred tons of nothing,” he said, “is what sixty tons looks like if you count what goes past a gate. It is what it looks like if you count a cart twice on a wet day, and there was a wet day in the sixth year and I know it was a wet day because I was on the road on it.”

---

Then he did the part that is the finding, and he did it out loud and he printed the working, because four people in this valley have learned that he does that and about four more have learned it this week.

“Sixty tons is not sixty tons of anything,” he said. “It is sixty tons of two people counting. One thousand and forty tons is what a clerk in a city wrote down about this valley four years ago, from what somebody sent him, and a ton in that book is a ton on paper and a ton in this yard is whatever came off a cart that day. And neither of those two men was lying and one of them has never been in this valley and the other one is not sure he has been in it in nine years.”

“And that is the whole of the trouble with a number that is four years old,” he said. “You cannot put a figure four years old against a memory of nineteen years. Not one man in this valley can do it, and I have tried, and I have been doing the other thing for nineteen years and I know exactly what a memory of nineteen years is worth, which is nothing at all after about the fourth year and very good for about a week.”

Somebody in that yard asked him what sixty tons was if it was not sixty tons.

“It is a hundredweight count and not a day,” he said. “A ton is twenty hundredweights. So sixty tons is twelve hundred hundredweights, and that is a figure you can check on a gate in an afternoon, which sixty tons is not. And a hundredweight of salt at the pans is a pound and fourpence, which is a hundred and sixty-eight pence. Twelve hundred times a hundred and sixty-eight is two hundred and one thousand six hundred pence, which is eight hundred and forty pounds. And if twelve hundred hundredweights of anything had come up off a road into this valley in a year, somebody would have paid for it, and there is not one entry in this basin of a hundredweight of anything bought in, because there is no book.”

He put his boot in the yard and the yard did what it does.

“And I will say the small part too, since we are in here,” he said. “Four hundred and twenty pounds a year is five and seven-tenths in the hundred of the seven thousand two hundred and eighty pounds that the seven pounds a ton comes to on the chamber's own figure, and seven thousand seven hundred on ours. Five and seven-tenths in the hundred of a road nobody in this valley has a figure for. It is not worth arguing about on its own. It is worth arguing about because we have all been treating one thousand and forty tons as a fact about this valley and it is a fact about a piece of paper, and a piece of paper four years old is a fact about four years ago.”

---

The man of sixty-one came down off the flats on the afternoon of that day because a carter told him there was a man at Sallowby saying the number of saltings had been counted wrong, which it has not, and the man of sixty-one came to say so and stayed four hours.

His name is Fen Tregerran and he has kept the pans and the count of saltings for thirty years and a salting is a drying and not a season and a dry-year one runs about seven weeks and a wet-year one runs eleven, and there is therefore no length in a salting and no first drying of a year that holds twice. The pans are the only clock in this basin that has never been out. He is the one person within four miles of that yard whose knowledge costs nothing and is worth everything, and nobody had ever asked him for it until a week ago, when a man of thirty-four asked him to date a paper and he refused in about nine words.

He stood in a room with eleven seats in it and he listened to about four minutes of a number and then he asked the question that turned the whole thing over, and it was one sentence, and it was not about salt.

“How many?” he said.

Nobody in that room knew what he meant.

“Of what,” said Hollis Nance.

“Of whatever it is that you are all counting,” said Fen Tregerran. “I have been scraping salt thirty years and I have a count of every salting in this basin since I was thirty-one and I have never once been asked what comes up off a road. I have counted what comes off my own pans every day for thirty years and I can tell you to the salting what went into those pans and where the water came from. I have never counted one thing that came into this valley. Nobody has. In thirty years not one person in this basin has written down a single load coming in.”

He put his hand on the table, which nobody expected him to do, and the clerk of the Seat of forty-four said afterwards that it was the first time in nine years that anybody had put a hand on that table who was not paid by the seat or by the pit or by himself.

“So one of you has got the number of things going out of this valley in your hand,” he said, “and one of you has got the number of things coming into it in nobody's, and the difference between those two is your sixty tons, and it is not anybody's fault, and it is not going to be anybody's fault in a hundred years either, because there is no book.”

---

That was the whole of the second day of the fifty-sixth week and it is worth saying out loud what it did, because a number going down is not a thing anybody in this valley has had happen to one in about a month.

It is the first time in about a month that a number in this valley has got smaller rather than larger.

That number was not sixty tons of anything and it never was, and it is now a question with a shape, and the shape is that a clerk in a city wrote down what somebody in this valley told him four years ago, and the somebody is dead or is a man who has not been here in nine years, and there is no way anybody standing in this room can find out which.

Nobody thanked anybody for it. About four people in that room had said the word *scandal* in the last week out loud and about four of them had been quoted, and one of the four asked the man who counted carts to say the word again in a room so that he could hear how it sounded, and he would not, and the reason he gave is that he has not said it once and is not going to start in the last week of a year.

And four miles north of that room, on a shelf of flat ground above Coldbeck, a man of sixty-one came off the far side of the fourth pan at the closing of the light with his board in his hands and set it down against a stone, and cut one notch in the upper edge of it, which is what he has done at the closing of every day for thirty years, and which is a count of notches and is not a count of days and is not a count of anything that anybody in this valley is arguing about, and he did not stop to look at it.

# Chapter 213: The Last Day Of The Fifty-First Week

The water was against the ninth doorpost at the second light on the last day of the fifty-first week, and it was two feet in the lane, and the lane was still walkable if a person walked on the higher side of it, and about nine people were on the bank at the top of it at the second light on the last day of the fifty-first week, which is four more than had ever been on that bank at once.

The man of seventy-four came up the lane with a bucket of tar in one hand and a ball of string in the other, and he had come from Coldbeck, and he was born in the second house up the lane, and everybody in the lane knew that and nobody had said so to him.

He had been in the Seat's one room on the first of the week and had been given the whole of it, and he had not said a word during any part of it, and he had heard the number in that room and had gone home and had not slept much, and on the seventh day of the week he had taken a bucket of tar out of a shed where it had been since before the first shaking and had come up the lane to see whether the number was true.

“You are going to put the water on the doorpost,” said Garrow Lee, from the top of the bank.

“I am going to put the water on the doorpost,” said Ilya Kever, “and then I am going to do the same at the fifth, and then I am going to come back and stand on the bank, and if you have a rule with a number on it I should like to see you use it.”

He did it the way a man does a thing he has done before and has never had to explain. He put his hand down flat in the water at the foot of the ninth doorpost until he had the level of it, took the string, snapped it off against his teeth at the length of his forearm, and then, with the string stretched from the water up to the mark, he cut a line into the doorpost with the corner of the bucket and smeared it flat with his thumb so that the tar stood out of the wood about the width of a finger.

Then he went up the lane to the fifth house, and he did exactly the same thing there, and the two new lines were level with each other, because water is level, and everybody standing on that bank could see from where they were that they were.

“That is what I have come to say,” he said. “That is the only thing I have come to say, and I have not said it yet, because you have to put the second one in before you can say it, and a man of my age does not get a second chance at a doorpost.”

He walked back down to the ninth and stood in the lane and looked at the two old marks, and then he got the string out again and held the top of it against the old mark and let the bottom of it hang to the new one, and he did it twice, and then he went up to the fifth and did it there, and then he came back and stood in the middle of the lane with the string hanging in his hand.

“There,” he said. “Now there is a number in this lane and it is not eleven, and it is not any number that has been said out loud in this valley since the second of the week, and it is the only number in this valley that has been got by putting water on a thing twice.”

---

The old mark on the ninth was a foot below the new one. The old mark on the fifth was a foot above the new one.

That is a foot and a foot, and it makes the two old marks two feet apart with the water level between them, and it is a number that anybody in that lane can check by looking, and it was checked by about nine people in about as long as it takes to put a barrow down.

Garrow Lee came down off the bank on the second step and stood in the lane and looked at the two posts for a while without saying anything at all, and then he went back up and got the lath and his rule and came back down and put his rule against the two old marks and read them and wrote them on the lath in chalk, and he did it in front of everybody and he did it twice.

“A foot apart,” he said.

“They are two feet apart,” said Ewan Troke. “You can see where the tar is.”

“They are a foot apart.” Garrow Lee did not raise his voice and he did not look at anybody, and he said it the way a man says a figure he has given a great many times. “I have put my own shoulder against both of those marks, and I did it in the sixth year after they were cut, and I have done it every year since, and they are a foot apart, and my shoulder has not changed since I was twenty-six, and I am forty-one, and I have got nothing to be wrong about.”

“You have,” said Ilya Kever. “You have not had water on both of them in the same hour. That is all you have not had.”

Nobody argued with him about that and nobody argued with the joiner about it either, and it went into a penny exercise book on the bank in a lead hand, and it is the whole of the finding of this chapter, and it is a foot, and it is a foot in one measurement and two in another, and it was got by two careful men and neither of them is lying and neither of them is careless and they cannot both be right.

“Nine years back,” said the man of seventy-four, from the lane, “a chamber nine days off this valley said in writing that a height marked on a doorpost is not a measurement. They said it on a grocer’s paper. I have never read it and I have never heard of it and I have been putting tar on doorposts since before there was a turn of any kind, and I want somebody to tell me straight whether they said it about this.”

“They said it about this,” said Ilyan Vester.

“Then they are nine years late and they are right,” said Ilya Kever, “and I am seventy-four and I am not going to have a chamber I have never seen tell me that putting water on a doorpost is not a thing anybody does. It is a thing anybody does. It is the only thing I have ever done that anybody wanted twice.” He put the string in his coat. “And you are all going to want to know whether I cut the eleven in this lane and I am not going to tell you, because there is more than one man in this valley that a chainman has pointed at, and I have never once been asked, and I am not going to start on the last day of a week.”

---

The lane asked the question at the closing of the water and it asked it out loud, and it took about twenty minutes for eleven people to get to it and then it took four seconds to say.

The four pages had been read out in that lane twice in eight days and the third line had been read out five times by a girl of seventeen who had been sent to read it to a neighbour with one eye, and by the closing of the water on the seventh day of the week about a third of Hollow Sallow was standing in it, and the third line says *that eleven feet be the height, and that the eleven houses of Hollow Sallow be below it*, and a woman of thirty-three who copies deeds over a chandler's shop read that line out again on a bank with a tar bucket standing beside her, and then she put her hand flat on the doorpost in front of her and asked the question nobody in this village has ever asked about a document.

“High as from where,” she said.

Nobody answered her. The four of them had spent twelve days in a valley to be able to answer a question like that and there was nobody in the lane to give it to, because the answer is a sill, and a sill is four miles up a river at a dam, and the four people standing on that bank were none of them from four miles up a river, and the woman who keeps the sluice was at the gate and the joiner was on the other side of the lane and the girl of seventeen was holding a barrow and had read the line five times and could not have said what a sill is.

The man of seventy-four answered it anyway, and he answered it in the wrong way, and he answered it because he was the only person on that bank who had put anything on a doorpost in his life.

“The height is the height,” he said. “It is a number somebody wrote. It is on a wall in Sallowby and I have not been in Sallowby since the first of the week and I am not going now. If it is a foot off, then the wall in Sallowby is a foot off, and that is a thing for a room with a table in it and not for a lane.”

The lane did not accept that and did not argue with it either. It had asked a question and had got an answer and the answer was the wrong shape, and about four hundred and ten people went to bed in that village knowing that there were two marks on their own doorposts a foot and two feet apart, and about nine of them knew that the number on the wall in Sallowby is the same eleven that has been in a void bond in a box under a floor since before any of them were born.

---

The day went on the page at the third light, in his own hand, on the basin side, and he did not say it to a page, because he had said one out loud in a room two days before and had discovered that a number said out loud in front of five people costs nothing and is worth something, and there were about nine people on that bank and it was going to be in somebody's mouth by the closing of the water whatever he did.

So he said it out loud, on the bank, at the noon light, with the tar bucket there.

“One hundred and sixty-one days out of the water-book,” he said, “since the second shaking, and I have said it out loud three times in three days and I am going to keep saying it, because there is about nine people on this bank who have all been standing in the same water as each other since the second of the week and not one of them has agreed on what day it is. The water-book is the Anner’s. It is kept in four hands. It is a day out of a river and it is not a day of a grain year and there is nothing in this basin that can turn it into one. If the four pages are ever read again, that is the only day anybody in this valley can stand in and look at.”

Garrow Lee wrote it on the lath under the two old marks and the two new ones, and he wrote it in chalk, and he put a barrow against the lath so the wind could not have it, which is a thing a man does who has been watching another man do it to a board for two days.

---

At the closing of the water the man of seventy-four put the ball of string in his coat and picked up the bucket, and he went about eleven steps up the lane and then he stopped, and came back, and put the bucket down on the flat stone by the ninth doorpost, and knocked the tar out of it onto the stone until the stone had a skin of it, and then he picked the bucket up again and went up the lane with it empty.

He had been putting tar on the doorposts of that lane for about sixty years and the buckets were his own and there was one for each house, and the new line on the ninth and the new line on the fifth were black and shiny and would be a foot under and a foot over the old ones by the time the Sallow went down, and the water went on doing what it was doing at the closing of the water, and a woman of thirty-eight opened a gate of oak that had been in the same place since before the first shaking and did it in the ordinary way, and the four men went down to a dam four miles up the river and put clay on it in the same order.

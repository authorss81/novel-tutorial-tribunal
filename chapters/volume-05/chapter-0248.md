# Chapter 248: What The Four People Who Keep The Record Say About This Valley

Nothing came back on the last day of the fifty-sixth week, and nothing can, and the reason was said out loud in a room with eleven seats in it at about the second hour and nobody argued with it and nobody was disappointed by it in the way that people are disappointed by a thing they had already decided about.

A second paper went into the gap in the wall on the first day of that week. A man with a bag comes twice in the year and he was in on the sixth of the month before last and he will be back in about six weeks. It goes nine days in a cart to a city and it comes back as two paragraphs or it does not come back at all, and nine days is nine days in the wrong of the year, and the first of the fifty-sixth week was a fair way off the first of the fifty-seventh.

“The bench is seized of the matter,” said Hollis Nance, “and it said so in two paragraphs in the fifty-second week and it is going to consider this basin in its own time, and it is not going to hear anything else about this basin this year, and I have read that paragraph more times than I have read anything in my own hand-book, and it is not a threat and it is not a punishment. It is a bench doing its job properly from a long way off, and I have worked out this week that a bench doing its job properly is a thing with no face on it, and that is the reason nine people in four hundred and ten have spent a fortnight being afraid of a piece of paper.”

---

So the room did the only thing available to it, which is what a room with nothing to wait for does, and it is worth putting down exactly what it did, because four people in it had spent nine years keeping this valley's record and not one of them had ever read it out to another.

The four were the clerk of the Seat, who keeps a hundred-year-old book in four hands and an index of indexes on a shelf; a woman of thirty-three who copies deeds at a penny a page and has copied about six hundred and forty of them in eleven years and about ninety of them for the Anner; a man of fifty-two who keeps the chain-book of a pit in a hand that is not his own; and a joiner with a rule who has been trying since the sixth year after the marks were cut to get somebody to measure the marks.

That is four people. It is not a bench and it has no name and it is not anybody's body and it did not meet, and it is the shape of a bench of four without a single thing about a bench in it, and about four people in that room noticed that on the seventh of this week and nobody said so.

They read the record out. It took about an hour and it was not interesting and the woman of thirty-three has read six hundred and forty deeds and said that the hour was the least interesting hour of her working life and that it was also the first hour of her working life that she has spent on her own valley.

And then they got to the end of it, and the end of it is a hole, and it has always been a hole.

Here is the whole of what nine years of keeping a valley's record comes to, in the order it comes in. There is a list of drains. There is a water-book in four hands. There is a chain-book in a pit office with a run of trench measurements in it and then six marks and then a gap. There is a hand-book with a fourth column in the head of it and about four hundred and sixty entries in the three that are full. There are nine years of a paper going into a gap in a wall and nine answers. There is an index of indexes on a shelf with a heading of two words.

And there is no column anywhere, in any of it, in any hand, for what this valley trades.

“There,” said Hollis Nance. “Nine years. And not one of us has ever put a weight in a book, and not one of us ever asked anybody for one, and the only number that has ever existed about the trade of this valley was written down by a man in a city out of something somebody sent him four years ago, and we do not have the something, and it is in a column headed not sent, and I have known where it was for a month and I have not been asked about it and I was not going to be.”

---

And here is where a man of fifty-two said a thing a man of thirty-one had said two days before in the same room, and it is the same fact and it is not the same sentence, and the second one is better and it is in a worse voice.

A record can be perfectly kept and still be wrong about a place. The reason is that nobody who kept it was ever in it.

A man of thirty-one had said that on the fourth of that week, standing at a table with a finger on an empty column, and he had said it about a building four hundred miles off. Jory Vellan said it on the seventh of that week about the four people in front of him, and he said it standing up, and he said it in about four minutes, and the four minutes were the most useful four minutes that room has had since the water.

“I am going to say the whole of it and then I am going to go down to the dam,” he said. “Two of us have been in the bottom half of that lane. Two of us have not. The clerk has not been down that lane since the water came and the woman who copies has not been down it since the year of the fire at Coldbeck. And the two of you who keep the numbers are the two who have not been down it.”

“You are not going to be surprised about me,” he said. “I have been in that lane a hundred times and I went down it in the second hour of the flood and I counted nineteen roofs from the bank without going into one of them, and I have been up this hill since the fifth hour and there is clay on the dam that has to go on before the light goes, and I do not have a speech about myself and I am not going to make one.”

He put his hand flat on the table.

“But you are both right,” he said. “Both of you are the only people in this county who can say what was done to that lane in eleven years and neither of you has been in it since the year before the water came, and that is not a fault and it is not stupidity and it is not a rule anywhere. It is what happens when the person who keeps the record is the person who is good at the record. And there is no form for anybody to say I do not want to be that person. And if either of you had been in that lane in the year before the water you would have written a different figure about the tonnage, or you would have written down that you did not have one, and either of those would be worth more to this valley than anything I am going to say at the dam.”

Nobody in that room was satisfied by it and that is the finding, and not one of the four disagreed with him, and about four of the people in that room who were not one of the four understood for the first time that a record is not a thing that is kept, it is a thing that is somewhere, and the somewhere is a person.

---

And the one thing that got settled in public on the seventh of the fifty-sixth week was the number, and it cost the mine something and it cost the four their name, and here is the whole of it in about four minutes with the working printed, because a man who keeps a chain-book has been doing that since the fourth year.

“The settlement stands,” said Jory Vellan. “The Anner pays the whole of the hundred and seventy-eight pounds, thirteen shillings and fourpence a year, for as long as the roofs want doing, and be asked again in one year. Nothing about that has moved and nothing about it is going to move this week. And it is now set on the chamber's own figure of a thousand and forty tons, and not on ours, and I have signed that in my own hand and the clerk has written it on the outside of a sheet that is going nine days in a cart, and that is what the second paper bought and I have not thanked anybody for it.”

“What it costs the Anner is this,” he said. “A pitman is ten shillings a week for fifty weeks, and that is six thousand pence in a year, and a day of a pitman's week is a hundred and twenty pence over six days, which is twenty pence, and twenty pence is one shilling and eightpence. Four times a year, once a season, a man at the gate of the Anner with a book and a rule and a day, and the Anner pays him, is twenty pence on each of four days, which is eighty pence, which is six shillings and eightpence, and that is one and a third in the hundred of what a man at that pit earns in a year, and I have had men at my gate for less than that and got more out of them.”

“And here is what it costs the Anner, and it is not the six shillings and eightpence. The Anner is now bound by a number it has never seen the inside of. It is going to be found. Four times a year somebody at that gate is going to write down what goes out and what comes in, in a book the seat keeps, and whatever comes out of the first four of those days is the number this valley trades, and the Anner will be held to it before it knows what it is, and it will not be able to stand in a room in a year's time and say the chamber got this valley wrong. It can say that today. It said it two days ago in this room and it was right. It cannot say it after the first quarter is written down, and I have given that up on purpose, in a room, in front of them, and I would like it written that I gave it up and not that it was taken.”

“And it costs the four of you your name,” he said, “because you have all just read nine years of a record out loud in an hour and there is no weight in any of it, and that is not anybody's fault and it is going to be in a letter four hundred miles off inside a month with a bench's name at the head of it, and the four names on it are yours, and there is nobody you can hand that to.”

---

And then the clay, and the four men and the barrow, and the second light going off the top of the hill while about nine people were still talking in a room with eleven seats in it of which eight have never had anybody in them at all.

Jory Vellan put the barrow down himself at the top of the bank, which he does, and he did not say anything about a good name to anybody, and nobody in that room was satisfied, and about four of the people who had been frightened of a bench for a fortnight found out that the thing they had been afraid of was a thing with no face on it and that this valley had just spent its whole good name in public on a number, and that neither of those two is the same as being safe.

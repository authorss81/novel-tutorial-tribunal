# Chapter 223: What The Anner Can Pay

There are three bodies in this basin with money in them and about four hundred and ten people with none, and one of the three is a chamber of plenty nine days' ride away that has said in two paragraphs that it is seized of the matter and will consider it in its own time, and the other of the two is a water-seat with eleven seats above a clerk of forty-four and four hundred and eleven pounds a year in it that came out of a kitchen and cannot be produced out loud by anybody in Hollow Sallow, and the third is a pit.

The under-manager put the whole of it on the table in the Anner's office on the third day of that week in about six minutes, and he did it without being asked and without any of the four of them having got as far as the door, and he said afterward that he had been waiting since the sixth day of last week for somebody to come up the hill and ask him a question about money, and that nobody had, and that the reason nobody had was that the four people in that valley had spent six days walking nineteen houses and had not once walked up to a mine.

“Let it all be the Anner,” he said.

Nobody spoke for as long as it takes to drink a cup of something.

“I want to say why before any of you says no, and I want to say the numbers, and then I want you to do what you like with them. About eleven hundred tons of red ochre and salt a year out of this pit. Four pounds a ton at the pit's mouth. Eleven pounds a ton in the market at Sallowby. That is the whole of what anybody in this basin has ever been arguing about and neither party has ever said it out loud and it is not the argument. The seven pounds a ton between those two figures is seven thousand seven hundred pounds a year, and it is not a profit. It is the road. It is what four miles of cart road between a pit and a market is worth, and the road is a hundred and forty days a year and the road is why there is a dam.”

He turned the chain-book round on the desk and did not open it, which is a thing he does.

“Nineteen roofs. A man of fifty-six has told you nine pounds and he will not split it, and I have not asked him to, because the last time a man in a good coat broke a roof into battens and thatch the man took the battens out and left the thatch. Nineteen at nine pounds. Nine pounds is two thousand one hundred and sixty pence. Two thousand one hundred and sixty pence times nineteen is forty-one thousand and forty pence. Forty-one thousand and forty pence is a hundred and seventy-one pounds with nothing over. That is a hundred and seventy-one pounds, and I have written it in this book in my own hand this morning before any of you were up this hill, and the clerk of the water-seat can come down and check it and it will be right.”

---

They checked it and it was right, and then Tarin Keel did what he does, which is to make the arithmetic say the other thing.

“The frame and the gate,” he said. “Four pounds ten for the frame and one pound eight for the oak. One thousand and eighty pence and three hundred and thirty-six pence is one thousand four hundred and sixteen pence, and that is five pounds eighteen, and a man has had a lath with that figure on it in a frame for about two years and has not been able to sell it.”

“Put the gate in it,” said the under-manager.

“The arrears,” said Tarin Keel. “Sixteen weeks at four and six is eight hundred and sixty-four pence, which is three pounds twelve, and there are three years of that and three times eight hundred and sixty-four pence is two thousand five hundred and ninety-two pence, which is ten pounds sixteen, and it is a woman of thirty-eight's and she has never chased it and she is not going to chase it, and if it goes in this year it goes in this year.”

“It goes in this year,” said the under-manager, and then he said the thing he had come up the hill to say and he said it in one breath and then stood still while it was in the room.

“And I am not going to pretend I do not know what I have just done. Nineteen years and I have not spent a pound of the road on this valley, not on a roof and not on a gate and not on a wage, and the reason I have not is that the road is the road and a mine does not buy a village. And a mine that pays a wage once a week is a different kind of mine than the one I have been the under-manager of since I was thirty-three, and I would like that in the book in my own hand as well, and I am not saying it is a reason to refuse.”

“It is a reason to keep the height,” said Ilyan Vester.

“Yes,” said the under-manager. “It is a reason to keep the height, and that is the arithmetic and I have not pretended otherwise. I will hold eleven feet to the Anner's own sill until the water is out every year I am under-manager of this pit, and I will hold it in the wet years as well, and I will not be asked for a reason and I have not been asked for one in nineteen years.”

---

And that is the part of it that nobody in that office put into the arithmetic, and a man who counts carts on roads for a living put it into the arithmetic about ten minutes later and on a bank four miles off rather than in a room, and it is one line long.

“A body that has begun paying is a body that will be asked again,” said Tarin Keel, to about six people at the second light on the fourth of that week, and then he did the sum out loud, which is what he is for, and he printed the working and not only the answer because the four of them had learned that in three weeks.

“Nineteen roofs is forty-one thousand and forty pence. The whole wage, nine shillings a week for the sixteen weeks, and not half of it, is a hundred and forty-four shillings, which is one thousand seven hundred and twenty-eight pence. Sixteen weeks of seven days is a hundred and twelve days, and a penny a day for a second hand on the water-book is a hundred and twelve pence, and a hundred and twelve divided by twelve is nine shillings and fourpence. Forty-one thousand and forty, plus one thousand seven hundred and twenty-eight, plus one hundred and twelve, is forty-two thousand eight hundred and eighty pence. Forty-two thousand eight hundred and eighty is a hundred and seventy-eight pounds, thirteen shillings and fourpence, every year, for as long as the roofs want doing.”

He stopped there and let it sit and then he finished it.

“Seven thousand seven hundred pounds a year is one million eight hundred and forty-eight thousand pence. Forty-two thousand eight hundred and eighty out of one million eight hundred and forty-eight thousand is two and three-tenths in the hundred, and a man in a good coat would call that nothing. It is nothing. That is the finding, and it took three days and it is the whole of what I have: the thing the mine can pay is not the difficulty.”

---

The one-offs came to four thousand and eight pence, which is sixteen pounds and fourteen shillings, and the frame is four pounds ten of it and the oak is one pound eight and the three years that are owed to a woman is ten pounds sixteen of it, and the number was said out loud once and was not said out loud again that week.

And the paper cost five pence. Two pages at a penny is two pence and a sealed copy is threepence, and the woman who copies deeds over a chandler's shop in Sallowby copied this one for nothing and said so in about nine words when she was asked what she was charging, and the nine words were that a penny a page is what a page is worth and that she was not being paid for the work and had not been for two years and was not going to make an exception of a piece of paper in a bad month, and then she asked, in the ordinary way, for the eleven pence she was owed by a woman two streets down, and the answer was that it would be paid on this market, and it was not on this market, and it has not been on any market since and nobody in that shop ever wrote it down.

At the end of the third day of that week the under-manager put the chain-book open on the desk for the first time in about nine days and wrote in it, in his own hand, in the space he leaves for money, four lines and a total, and the four lines were a roof, a gate, a wage and a penny a day, and the total was a hundred and ninety-five pounds, seven shillings and fourpence for the first year including everything that is owed, and the chain-book is the Anner's own book and it is a hundred and thirty years old and is kept in a hand that is not his and has never been checked against a deed, and about four men in the working years can read it and one of them is the under-manager and none of them will ever read those four lines again.

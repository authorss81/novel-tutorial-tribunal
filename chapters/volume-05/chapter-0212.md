# Chapter 212: The Standing Order Nobody Has Read

The frame at Hollow Sallow is oak and it is about twice the height of a man and it has been in the same place since before the first shaking, and the gate that is in it is four years old, and behind that gate there is a second frame, newer, that came at the same time and does nothing at all.

A joiner of forty-one had it chalked on a lath and had left the lath leaning in the frame, and the chalk said four pounds ten for the frame and one pound eight for the oak, which is five pounds eighteen the whole of a gate, and he had been up here three times about it and had told them so, and he was not going up a fourth.

“It is not the frame that is the problem,” said Garrow Lee. “The frame has been here longer than anybody. The problem is that the thing that decides the water is a piece of oak standing up in the mud with a hole cut in it, and it does not cost five pounds eighteen, and it is the only object in this basin that anybody at the Seat of Sallowby has ever been asked to look at.”

He put his hand flat on the frame the way a man does when he has said more than he meant to.

“Then somebody has been asked to look at it,” said Ewan Troke.

“Somebody has been asked to look at it three times. Once by a man who had to go back to a room and explain, once by a man who was told it was not the Seat’s business, and once by a man who said it would be the Seat’s business when there was money, and there has not been money.”

---

Ewan Troke went down to Sallowby in the middle of the morning and came back up the hill with one page in the flat of his hand, holding it the way a man holds a thing that is not his and that he has been asked for, and he did not have it in a case and he did not have it under his coat, and he gave it to the bank and not to the man of thirty-one, because the man of thirty-one is not a party to anything and has said so in three rooms this week.

They read it against the gate. It is one page and it is three years old and it is the standing order of the water-seat concerning the gate, and it is the only order in this basin anybody has ever had to keep to, and it has been kept to every year without anybody knowing that it existed.

> STANDING ORDER OF THE WATER-SEAT OF SALLOWBY CONCERNING THE GATE AT HOLLOW SALLOW, MADE IN THE ELEVENTH SALTING OF THE FORTY-NINTH YEAR, HANDED TO THE KEEPER AND NOT COPIED, THREE YEARS AGO:
> THAT THE GATE BE OPEN FROM THE DAY THE PANS ARE TURNED AT COLDBECK, AND BE STOOD SHUT AGAIN WHEN THE WATER IS OUT OF THE LANE, AND THAT THE KEEPER SHALL DO IT IN THE ORDINARY WAY AND SHALL NOT BE ASKED FOR A REASON.
> THE DAY IN IT IS A DAY OF THE PAN-BOOK, AND IT IS NOT A DAY OF THE SHELF AND IT IS NOT A DAY OF THE TURN, AND THIS ORDER HAS NO OTHER DATE AND NONE HAS BEEN ASKED FOR.
> AT THE FOOT, IN THE SAME HAND, AND NO NAME ON IT ANYWHERE: THIS ORDER IS THREE YEARS OLD AND IT IS THE ONLY ONE.

Garrow Lee read it twice. It was not his to read and he was not asked to and he read it anyway, and he is the fourth person in three days to do a thing in this basin without being given a form for it.

“It is the eleventh salting of the forty-ninth year,” said Ewan Troke, “and the bond in the box under the floor is the eleventh salting of the fortieth year, and that is the same salting of a year nine along, and a pan-book year is a year and nine is a year, and that is the whole of the arithmetic anybody in this basin can do on it and nobody has ever done it.”

“The day the pans are turned,” said Garrow Lee. “Not a day. The day the pans are turned. A man in this basin wrote that down and thought he had been exact, and he has been exact, and it is the exactest sentence anybody has ever put in a document about this gate.”

“Because the pans do not turn on a day,” said Ilyan Vester.

“The pans do not turn on a day. A salting is a drying. In a dry year it is about seven weeks from the water going in to the pan giving up its salt, and in a wet year it is eleven, and a man of sixty-one at Coldbeck has been keeping that count for thirty years and has said out loud on a bank that there is no length in it and no way to put it against a number, and the four of us heard him and one of us wrote it down.” Garrow Lee took the lath out of the frame and stood it against the page. “So this order does not say a day. It says a weather. It says *the day the pans are turned*, and in a wet year that is about a month after the water wants the gate, and in a dry year it is about a fortnight before, and the only person in this basin who could tell you in advance which of those two years you are in is a man of sixty-one who nobody has asked.”

“Who made it,” said Sera Quill, from the bank.

“There is no name on it,” said Ewan Troke. “Not at the foot, not at the back, and not in the Seat’s index, and I asked the clerk before I came up and he said he knows the order is there and has never known who made it, and that a thing can be valid without anybody being able to say whose hand it is, and that he would like that written down in whatever you are writing as a thing the Seat says and not as a thing he thinks.”

---

The money of it came out on the bank inside ten minutes, because it is the only thing about a sluice that anybody in this basin has ever been able to say out loud, and it took four sentences.

Ilma Treen was not there. She had gone up to the third house from the top with a barrow and would be back at the closing of the water, and the four of them stood at the frame and did her figures for her out loud and then had to do them twice, because a number said about a person who is not in the room is a thing that goes into a book wrong.

Nine shillings a week for the sixteen weeks the Sallow runs, and nothing the rest of the year, and that is a hundred and forty-four shillings, and a pound is twenty shillings, and a hundred and forty-four shillings is seven pounds and four, and there is no other money in it and there never has been. The Anner gives four shillings and sixpence of the nine on the first day of the week in an envelope. The Seat owes the other four shillings and sixpence and has owed it for three years, and sixteen weeks at four and six is eight hundred and sixty-four pence, and that is three pounds and twelve, and three years of it is two thousand five hundred and ninety-two pence, and that is ten pounds and sixteen.

And the part that is not a debt at all: she works the water a hundred and forty days a year and she is paid for a hundred and twelve of them, because a hundred and twelve is sixteen weeks of seven days, and the twenty-eight days she is not paid for is four more weeks, and a wage is a week’s wage, and four weeks at four and six the week is two hundred and sixteen pence, and two hundred and sixteen pence is eighteen shillings exactly, with nothing over and nothing to explain away.

“All of that is a person doing four weeks of the most useful work in this valley for nothing,” said Tarin Keel, “and not one line of it is in a dispute.”

“It is not in a dispute because the Seat has not got it,” said Ilyan Vester.

“The Seat has four hundred and eleven pounds a year in it,” said Garrow Lee. “I have heard it said in a kitchen and so has everybody who has ever been in a kitchen in Sallowby in the last ten years, and it is not anybody’s figure, and there is not one person in this village who can produce it out loud, and I have tried three times to get somebody to say it and every one of them says it in a different voice and then stops.”

“So the whole of what is known about a woman who has held water in this hollow for eleven years,” said Sera Quill, “is a number from a kitchen and a figure in a book in a room, and the two of them have never been in the same sentence, and the one in the book is four and six a week and the one in the kitchen is four hundred and eleven a year, and four hundred and eleven divided by fifty-two weeks is about seven pounds and eighteen, and nobody in this basin has ever said that figure out loud and I have just said it and I would like it struck out.”

---

The water-book came out at the noon light, because the thing the sluice-keeper had been asked about by nobody could not be asked of her until the hour she worked at, and the hour she worked at was not until the light went.

Ewan Troke had it in the flat of his hand again and he read it out against the frame with the page of the order beside it, and the last thing in the book was in a hand that is not the water-book's hand and never has been, and the last time anybody had looked at that hand was on the fourth day of the week in a wet bank nine feet below where they were standing.

“Shut sluice. The pans are on and the water is in the hollow. Nobody come. — I. T.”

“They turn the pans on the ninth,” said Garrow Lee. “That is what he means. That is the day the pans go. And the water was in the hollow on the day the pans went, and the gate was shut, and she wrote it on the Seat’s own book in her own hand and nobody came.”

Nobody asked her what she was writing on it. That is the whole of it and it is worth putting plainly, because the four of them had come nine days to find out what a document in this basin is, and the answer came out of the back of a page in a mill hand while the man who wrote it was up a hill with a barrow of stone.

“It is three weeks and a day old,” said Ewan Troke. “I counted it on the way up because I have been counting the days out of that book since the second of the week and I have nothing else to count with. And the slow came up on the evening of the sixth day of last week, and that is a fortnight and a day after she wrote it, so she wrote *the water is in the hollow* a fortnight before it came up, and every year, and nobody has ever asked her how she knows.”

Ilma Treen came back down the lane at about the time the light goes and put the barrow down before she said anything, and the four of them were standing at a frame with a page of an order on it and a water-book open against the oak.

“The pans were on on the ninth,” she said, before anybody said a word. “And they were on late, because the sixth salting has run eleven weeks this year, and there is a man of sixty-one up at Coldbeck who has said out loud in front of four of you that a wet salting runs eleven weeks and a dry one seven, and I have believed him ever since and I have never been able to say so to anybody. So the gate was shut when the pans went and the water was already in the hollow, and I shut it because the causeway was under and I shut it because that is what the order says, and the order says the day the pans are turned, and I have known for eleven years that the day the pans are turned is a different day every year and nobody has ever said so out loud to me.”

“You wrote it in the book,” said Ilyan Vester.

“I write in the Seat’s book because the Seat cannot make me buy one. I have a mill on the Anner water and I have to be told what the water did. That is all it is, and it is on forty leaves of that book and more, and nobody has ever asked me what is on them.” She looked at the frame. “You have had that order off the Seat. Three years I have had it. It is the only paper I have ever been given and it is about eleven lines and I could not have told you one of them.”

“It is three lines.”

“It is three lines and I could not have told you one of them, and you have had it since the second light this morning, and I have been the gate for eleven years.”

---

The gate was opened at the closing of the water, as it is opened, in the ordinary way, by the person who is paid four shillings and sixpence a week for sixteen weeks of the year and is not paid for the rest of it.

Garrow Lee put the lath back in the frame on the way down, and the chalk was still on it, and the five pounds eighteen was still on it, and the second frame behind the second gate was still doing nothing, and the four pages on a wall in Sallowby said nothing whatever about a gate or a frame or an oak, and the bond in a box under a floor in a room said nothing whatever about a gate or a frame or an oak either, and both of those documents were correct, and there was about two feet of the Sallow in the lane and the ninth was three weeks and a day ago.

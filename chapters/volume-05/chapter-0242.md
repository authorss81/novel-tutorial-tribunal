# Chapter 242: The Price Of A Second Paper

The first day of the fifty-sixth week is the day a thing that has been true since the second shaking gets a figure put on it in a room with eleven seats in it, and the figure is five pounds and six shillings, and it has been that figure for nine years and nobody in this valley has ever had to look at it twice.

Hollis Nance said it out loud because a man of fifty-two asked him to, and he said it in the way he says a price, and then he said the working, because about four people in this valley have learned in a month that a man who keeps a book will show you how he got there.

“A pound for entry,” he said. “That is two hundred and forty pence. Four pounds for the day at the chamber, which is nine hundred and sixty pence. Nine days' board at eightpence a day, which is seventy-two pence. Two hundred and forty, and nine hundred and sixty, and seventy-two, is one thousand two hundred and seventy-two pence, which is five pounds and six.”

“It is the price of asking for one thing,” he said. “There is no price on the list for asking about thirty-two papers. There has never been a price on the list for asking about thirty-two papers because nobody in nine years has ever put thirty-two papers in at once, and I know that because I have counted them, and the counting took about four minutes and a piece of lead.”

---

There are two good reasons that five pounds and six has not been spent in this valley in about a month. The clerk gave the first of them in a room a fortnight ago and the second one is better than the first, and neither of them is the reason, and the reason was said at about the fourth hour of this morning by a man who had come down off the hill on purpose.

The first is that nobody has five pounds and six. A hundredweight of salt at the pans is a pound and fourpence. A pitman is ten shillings a week for fifty weeks, which is twenty-five pounds a year, and a week of that is a hundred and twenty pence, and one thousand two hundred and seventy-two pence is ten of those weeks and seventy-two pence over. A sluice-keeper is paid nine shillings a week for the sixteen weeks the Sallow runs, and a week of that is a hundred and eight pence, and one thousand two hundred and seventy-two pence is eleven of her weeks and eighty-four pence over, which is about three quarters of the sixteen weeks a year she is paid for.

The second is this, and it was said at about the fourth hour of the first day of this week, by a man of fifty-two who had come down off the hill on purpose and had not been asked.

Nobody argued with the price. It is the best arithmetic anybody in this basin has on a piece of paper and it is the reason this is a valley and not a room, and Jory Vellan did not argue with it either, and he said so before anybody thought he was going to.

“I am not going to tell you that five pounds and six is nothing,” he said. “I have said that once in this valley and I am not going to say it twice. Five pounds and six is a week of a pitman's pay and it is three days of the wages of the woman who keeps your sluice, and if I say it is nothing then I am a man saying a thing he knows to be false in a room, and I have enough of those to get on with.”

He put his hand flat on the table, which he does, and about four people in that room had never noticed and about four had.

“But you have all been sitting on the wrong figure since the sixth day of the fifty-fourth week,” he said. “The five pounds and six is the smallest thing in this room. It is the smallest thing in this room by more than thirty times over, and I will show you the thirty if anybody wants to see it. Nobody in this valley has not spent it because of the five pounds and six. Nobody in this valley has not spent it because of what a second paper does.”

---

He took a while over it and he was not showing off, and what he said was in three pieces and each piece was a thing he had thought about alone.

“A matter goes up that gap in the wall,” he said, “and it comes back as two paragraphs, and a thing with a date on it comes back on a date. One date is a thing you wait for. Two dates on one matter is a thing a bench can do something with. A bench that is seized of a matter, which this one is, can look at the second date and decide that the first one has been dealt with by the arrival of the second one, and say so, and be right, and close the file, and every one of the four of them will be doing their job properly when they do it. I have been in that building. Not in the room. In the building. And I know what a file looks like when a second paper goes on it.”

He stopped, and Tarin Keel said the number without being asked, because he counts things for a living and cannot help it.

“The settlement of this valley is a hundred and seventy-eight pounds, thirteen shillings and fourpence a year,” he said. “Forty-one thousand and forty pence of roofs, one thousand seven hundred and twenty-eight of the whole wage at nine shillings a week for sixteen weeks, and a hundred and twelve for a penny a day in a second hand. And five pounds and six is one thousand two hundred and seventy-two pence. Thirty-three of those go into the settlement and there are nine hundred and four pence over, because a thousand two hundred and seventy-two times thirty-three is forty-one thousand nine hundred and seventy-six, and the settlement is forty-two thousand eight hundred and eighty. It is not thirty-four. I have counted it twice and I have a bad hand in a cart yard and I would like somebody to count it a third time.”

“Thirty-three,” said Jory Vellan. “I have been wrong twice today and once in six years, which is about the average, and neither of those two is what I wanted to say.”

---

Here is what he wanted to say, and he said it to about seven people and it took about four minutes and it is the reason five pounds and six was not spent in this valley for a month and is the reason it was spent on the first day of this week.

“A number somebody has written down is worth something,” he said. “A number nine men say in a yard is worth nothing by the second day. And this valley has been settling a village for a month on eleven hundred tons, and eleven hundred tons is what a yard in this market town has been saying since before I could count, and the chamber of plenty has had one thousand and forty tons written down in its own book for four years, and it is four years old and it is a stranger's, and it is the only figure in this basin that anybody ever put on paper about what this valley trades.”

“Eleven hundred tons less a thousand and forty tons is sixty tons,” he said, “and sixty tons at seven pounds a ton is four hundred and twenty pounds a year, and four hundred and twenty pounds is two and a third times what that settlement costs, and I have not been able to put my hand on that in a month and I have had a man of fifty-six's four roofs to think about instead.”

He did not say what four hundred and twenty pounds a year of difference was for. Nobody in that room asked him and this record has to say that he was asked by nobody, because he offered it to nobody.

---

Then he paid it, and he said out loud what he was buying, and the whole of the room heard the shape of it.

“Right,” he said. “The Anner will send a second paper. Five pounds and six, out of the Anner's own cost of the high road, which is nine carts a day for about a hundred and forty days and which the Anner has been bearing the whole of since the hundred and fifty-fifth day. Not out of the seat's money and not out of the valley's. I want that written on the outside of the sheet in your hand, clerk, because a matter nobody is paying for comes back in nine days and I have watched that happen.”

“Write on it as well what I want,” he said. “Not an answer. I want the chamber to confirm its own figure of a thousand and forty tons, and I want the settlement to go on that figure and not on ours, and I want the word *asked* on it, because I am going to be asked about this in a year and there is nothing on a wall in Sallowby that says I asked.”

“Nobody has thanked me and nobody is going to,” said Jory Vellan, “and I am not asking for it. I have not bought a conscience. I have bought a date. I have put a second paper under a rule that a second paper lets a bench close the first, and I have done it on purpose, and if you want to know what I think that says about me you are welcome to say it in a room and I will not stop you.”

The man of thirty-one did not thank him and did not stop him, and he said nothing at all, and what he was doing for the whole of that four minutes was looking at a man who had just handed him a weapon and a reason not to use it in the same breath.

---

The sheet went into the gap in the wall at about the seventh hour, and the gap is about the size of a person and it is two-fifths full, and the clerk of forty-four wrote the amount and the payer and the thing wanted on the outside of it in his own hand, and then he wrote the date on it, which is the second time since he came that he has written a date on a thing that was not a deed, and the first was a bundle with a knife-cut end on the string that is on the same shelf and has not been opened.

“A man with a bag comes twice in the year,” he said, to nobody, and turned the pen upside down on a board and turned it over again. “He was in on the sixth day of the fifty-fourth week, which is the week before last, and he will be back in about six weeks and he is paid at Orison and not by us, and whatever goes in that gap on this evening goes nine days in a cart and comes back as two paragraphs or does not come back at all.”

And a woman of thirty-three in a room over a chandler's took a sheet of good paper out of a drawer and began to copy the fair sheet of the Anner's second paper at a penny a page, and it is four pages, and it is four pence, and she is not being paid for it out of the Anner's five pounds and six and she did not offer and was not asked.

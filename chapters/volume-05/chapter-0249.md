# Chapter 249: A Paper That Says In Its Own First Line That It Can Be Ended

This valley got a thing of its own on the first day of the fifty-seventh week, and the thing is worse than the ruling a man of thirty-one proposed in a cart shed in the fifty-first week and better than what the water did, and both of those are true and about four people in that room said so out loud and nobody said the other one.

It is on the wall of the Seat's one room, above a settlement that has no name at the foot of it.

---

Nobody drafted it in a room. That is the first thing about it and it is the thing that took three days to arrange and about nine people to arrange, and what happened was that eleven people said one sentence each in their own houses and a scrivener wrote down what was said, in her own hand, at a penny a page, and the clerk of the Seat copied it out fair and put it on the wall, and there is not a clause in it that anybody in a room improved.

The eleven are the hands on the settlement. They are the only people in four hundred and ten who can be instructed, and the reason is in a lane and not in a room and it is that eleven people can un-instruct themselves with one sentence and nothing whatever done to them, and that has been true for about a week and has now been used once.

---

The eleven feet is in that document and it is not on the settlement, and the reason is not a secret and is not anybody's fault.

It is in a third document, in three copies, in three places, and it is a difference of four feet written down in inches, and it is the worst answer anybody in this basin has arrived at in about a month and it is the right one, and the reason it is the right one is a ruling the chamber of plenty made nine years ago on the petition of a grocer at Coldbeck, which is that no order may name a height without naming the sill it is taken from.

> THE DIFFERENCE OF TWO LINES. ONE SHEET. ONE HAND. THREE COPIES, AND THE THREE COPIES ARE NOT IN ONE PLACE: THE LINE IN THE BOND OF THE ANNER IS ELEVEN FEET ABOVE THE SILL OF THE ANNER'S OWN DAM. THE LINE IN THIS DOCUMENT IS ELEVEN FEET ABOVE THE SILL OF THE BED BELOW THE VILLAGE OF HOLLOW SALLOW, WHICH IS THIRTEEN FEET BELOW THE SILL OF THE FRAME AT THE SLUICE, WHICH IS NINE FEET ABOVE THE SILL OF THE ANNER'S OWN DAM, AND IS THEREFORE FOUR FEET BELOW THAT SILL. AND THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN THE TWO LINES IS FOUR FEET, AND NO HOUSE IN THIS VALLEY STANDS ON THOSE FOUR FEET, AND NOBODY IN THIS VALLEY HAS AGREED TO THE DIFFERENCE AND NOBODY HAS REFUSED IT.

It cost five pence. A page of a deed copied at Sallowby is a penny and a sealed copy is threepence, and this is one sheet copied twice and sealed once, and a penny and a penny and threepence is fivepence, and the Seat paid the fivepence out of the press, and it is in a room with a key in a clerk's coat, and it is the cheapest thing this valley has ever bought and it is the most useful.

The three copies are in the press of the Seat of Sallowby, in a lane at the bottom of a hill in the hands of a man of thirty-four who cannot read it, and in a book room at the Anner Pit on a table under the chain-book, and the three people who can hold those three copies cannot read one another's.

And a joiner with a rule said the whole of what that means in about as long as it takes to get a boot off, standing by a wall, and he is a man who has spent nine years trying to get somebody to measure a line of chalk on a doorpost and has never once managed it.

“Three copies is worth more than one,” said Garrow Lee. “Say that again to yourselves. A bad answer in three copies can be argued with by three people. A good answer in one can be argued with by nobody at all, and I have spent nine years trying to get a good answer out of this valley about a line of chalk on a doorpost and I could not do it, and I can argue with a bad one this afternoon.”

He put his thumb on the sheet on the wall.

“Four feet is not the answer. Four feet is where we are standing. I have a rule and I have a mark and I have a bed that somebody cut by hand before I was born, and if a man in a city in nine years is to be told a height then he is to be told a sill as well, and he is, and the four feet is written down and the four feet is the whole of the difference between what was promised and what is written here. It is a bad answer. It is in three places. Take it.”

---

And here is the charter, and it is in one hand, on a wall, and it is not in the press, because the press is for deeds and for bonds and for the things that are owed, and this is none of those three.

> THIS CHARTER MAY BE ENDED. ANY PERSON OF THIS VALLEY MAY GO FROM IT AT ANY TIME AND SHALL LOSE NOTHING BY GOING BUT THIS CHARTER.
> IT IS GOODS AND DAYS AND NOBODY IS HELD BY IT BUT THE PEOPLE IN IT, AND THE PEOPLE IN IT ARE ELEVEN, AND THEY CAN END IT IN A ROAD AT THE CLOSING OF THE WATER BY SAYING ONE SENTENCE, AND NOTHING DONE TO THEM.
> THE LINE FOR THE ELEVEN HOUSES IS THE LINE IN THE THIRD DOCUMENT AND NOT THE LINE IN THE BOND, AND THE DIFFERENCE OF FOUR FEET IS WRITTEN DOWN IN THREE COPIES IN THREE PLACES.
> THE ANNER IS A MINE. THE FOUR PAGES ON THE WALL OF THIS ROOM ARE NOT TOUCHED BY THIS CHARTER AND STAY ON IT AS THEY ARE. THE WAGE OF THE KEEPER IS NINE SHILLINGS A WEEK FOR THE SIXTEEN WEEKS THE SLOW RUNS, AND THE THREE YEARS THAT WERE OWED HAVE BEEN PAID, AND IF THIS CHARTER IS ENDED THE WAGE IS ENDED WITH IT.
> IT SHALL BE REVIEWED AT THE CLOSING OF THE WATER ONE YEAR AFTER THE DAY WRITTEN AT THE HEAD OF THE PAGE IN THE WATER-BOOK FOR THAT DAY, BY ANY PERSON OF THIS VALLEY WHO BRINGS IT.

There is no name on it. That is a decision and it was argued about in a room for about four minutes and here is the whole of the argument, and it is the third time in about three weeks that this valley has produced the same shape of thing and it is the first time it has used it on itself.

A paper nobody is on cannot be asked anybody anything. A woman of thirty-three said that about a man of thirty-four in a road in the first week of the month and she was right, and a man of thirty-one said it about himself in a lane in front of the whole of that lane, and he had said it to explain why he had taken his own name off a piece of paper, and a clerk of forty-four said the other half of it in a room in the same fortnight, which is that a dash is a thing a person puts where a name used to be so that a later person thinks nothing was ever there.

Nobody put a name on this and nobody put a dash on this either, and the paper that is below it on the same wall still has a space at the foot of it with nothing in it and no dash in it, and the two papers are about a foot apart and about four hundred people in this valley can see both of them from a road.

“Then what is it for?” said a man of about forty with a crowbar, who had carried the second of the four lines four miles in a week in the mouth of this valley and had not been thanked for it then either.

“It is for the day,” said the woman of about forty who washes the pen. “It has no name on it because a paper with a name on it can only be asked things by the name on it, and this one cannot be asked anything, and the whole of what it is worth is that anybody in four hundred and ten can walk into that room on the day in that last line and put it in front of nine people, and it does not matter in the slightest which of them it is. It is the first thing this valley has ever had that anybody can be shown the end of. Everything else we have made in a month is a thing that has to be kept by somebody, and the keeping of it is the whole of what it is.”

---

And here is what it costs, and a woman of thirty-eight said it before anybody asked her for it, and she said it standing at her own gate in four miles of mud in a coat, and she is the only person in this valley whose wage is in it.

“It is the only paper anybody in this valley has ever handed me that I can see the end of,” she said, “and the end of it is my wage. Nineteen roofs at nine pounds a roof is a hundred and seventy-one pounds. Nine pounds is two thousand one hundred and sixty pence, and two thousand one hundred and sixty times nineteen is forty-one thousand and forty pence. The whole of the settlement is forty-two thousand eight hundred and eighty pence, and forty-two thousand eight hundred and eighty less forty-one thousand and forty is one thousand eight hundred and forty pence, which is seven pounds and thirteen shillings and fourpence. And that one thousand eight hundred and forty is the whole wage at nine shillings a week for sixteen weeks, which is one thousand seven hundred and twenty-eight pence, and a penny a day for a hundred and twelve days, which is a hundred and twelve pence. That is the whole of what this charter cannot hold, and it cannot hold it because a paper that can be ended cannot hold a wage, and a wage is the only one of these things that is still going when the roofs are done.”

She put her hand on the frame, which she does.

“So this paper is worth the roofs and it is not worth me,” she said. “And the roofs will be done in a year and then this paper is worth a gate and a penny a day. And I would rather have a paper I can see the end of than a paper I cannot, and I have wanted one of these since the day before the first shaking, and I am going to say in a room that I would rather have it, and I would like it written down that I said it before I was asked and not after.”

Nobody in that lane wrote it down. About four of them had already walked up the hill.

---

And the last thing that happened on the first of the fifty-seventh week was an arithmetic that nobody had asked for, and a man of thirty-one had no part in it and was in a cart shed behind two houses when it was done.

Forty-one thousand and forty pence is a hundred and seventy-eight pounds, thirteen shillings and fourpence a year less the wage and the penny, and forty-one thousand and forty pence is also what a flood in this valley cost in the fifty-second week, to the penny, because the flood put water in nineteen houses, and a house with its roof wet through is nine pounds to put back, and eighteen of them were still wanted a month ago.

So the first year of a paper about a dam was forty-one thousand and forty pence, and the water was forty-one thousand and forty pence, and the two are the same figure, and one of the two put water in nineteen houses, and there is no instrument in this valley that can tell a reader of that sheet which of the two a hundred and seventy-eight pounds is.

And nine people in this valley had spent a fortnight being afraid of a good name that belonged to four people four hundred miles off, and about four of the nine have not noticed that anything has happened this week, and about four of the nine are glad, and about one of the nine is not, and the whole of the reason any of them is glad is that a good name is a public thing and it has now been spent in public, in a room, by a man from four hundred miles off, on a page of a printed index, and it cannot be spent twice and it does not need to be.

A sheet went up on a wall in a market town, and a girl of seventeen was paid a penny in a jar at the closing of the water, and a man of thirty-four read the first line out loud in a road at the bottom of a lane as he does now, and there is nobody in this valley who can check him, and there is nobody in this valley he could not ask.

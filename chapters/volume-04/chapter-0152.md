# Chapter 152: The Fourth Term

The bench of Orison’s chamber of Plenty sits above a room that smells of wet wool, and Sera Quill was in that room at the second bell on the sixth day of the fortieth week with a pen in her hand and a sheet of paper that was not her own on the table in front of her, and she had been holding it for about nine minutes and had not written on it.

She was twenty-eight. She had been licensed as a recordwright of a public record on four terms, and the fourth of them was that **she takes no matter of her own while she holds this one**, and she had said out loud, in a room, that it cost her a shop, two rooms and a business, and the business had been carrying her family’s unfiled claim for six years.

The claim wanted a page that might not exist. The page was out of a bound volume in a building four miles from that chamber before she was born, and the cut was in a newer hand than the thread beside it, and her own duplicate was gone out of a case in her own rooms with no mark and no cut thread. Two events on two dates. She had wanted them to be one event for six years and it had cost nothing to hold and that was the only thing in her life she had wanted that had cost nothing.

Ilyan Vester came up those stairs at the second bell on the sixth day of the fortieth week with a bag on his shoulder and a bad leg and no coat buttoned, and he had walked four hundred miles in something under four weeks, and he stood in the doorway of a records room a mile and a half from where he had slept and did not say anything for about four seconds.

“You have not written on it,” he said.

“I have not written on it.”

“You have been holding it for nine minutes.”

“I have,” said Sera Quill, “and you have been counting, and I want you to know that I noticed and that I am not going to be pleasant about it, and then I am going to ask you for the sheet and I am going to ask you in a way that you will find difficult.”

“All right.”

“Not yet.” She put the pen down. “I have to release the matter first, and I am going to do that in front of you, because you are the last person on the record of it who is standing in this room, and because the fourth term of my licence was drafted by Sel Harrow on this court’s own motion at my own asking, and I said at the time that it would be used against me and it has been used against me four times, twice by benches, once by a clerk and once by Harrow himself, and I entered all four in my own book and I entered that he drafted it.”

“What are you releasing.”

“The ninth-bridge matter. All of it, as of this morning, entered in the public record, naming my reason, in my own hand, with the date.” She had the form in front of her and she had not looked at it. “My reason is that it is finished. Not that it went well. It went the way it went, and the appeal survived and it is not secure, and I have carried the terms of it to a review and to two other districts that asked, which is the second and third of my terms, and there is nothing left in it that I am the only person who can carry.”

She wrote for about four minutes.

“It is done,” she said. “And as of this morning there is no matter of mine in front of me, which means that in about four months I may file a claim of my own for a page that may not exist, and that I have been not able to file for six years, and that the reason I could not file it was the price of a licence that this court thought was fair.”

He did not say anything, and after a while she said, “You are going to ask me about the family claim.”

“No.”

“You are not?”

“I am not going to ask you about it,” said Ilyan Vester, “because you have not offered it and because if you have not offered it then the answer is no and I would be asking you to say so twice.”

“That,” said Sera Quill, “is the first sensible thing anybody in this building has said to me in about a year, and I am going to make it harder for you than that.”

“All right.”

“The claim is six years old and it is unfiled and it is mine. It is not in any bridge case and it is not in any city case and it is not in a matter about a wall or a gate or a bar of land or a plate. When it is filed, if it is filed, I will be a person in a matter, and there will be things in that matter I do not want my own position in the record of. And I have known you for about a year and a half and I know exactly what you do with a person in a record.”

“I do not put them in it.”

“You do not put them in it *by asking*.” She turned the pen over. “You arrange it. You go into a shed and you ask a woman of twenty-four in four seconds whether she would keep three pages, and you do not tell her what keeping them means, and you knew what it meant, and she said no and she was right and you wrote it down on a page and did not repair it, and it was the best thing anybody did on that coast and you will not be able to tell me for another year that I am wrong about any part of it, and I have read the three sheets because there are three of them in an office four hundred miles from here and a man with a brush in his cuff will send them to me eventually, and I read them and there is not one word of any of them you arranged.”

“That is not a compliment.”

“It is not meant as one.” She looked at him. “It is a thing about your method. You never lie to anybody. You arrange a door and you stand in it and you do not put a name on anything, and the arrangement is the whole of what you do and there is no part of it you could be put in front of a room for. Except that you are put in front of rooms anyway, because your name is in four hundred and sixty-three forms, and now it is in a third book in a district six miles up a towpath where you have been two days, and I have not seen that book and I am not going to ask to, and I want you to hear me say that I am not asking and that it is not because I do not want to know.”

He said, “What is it because of.”

“Because if I look at that book then I am a recordwright in a district that is not my client, examining a record on the ground of a thing I have been told about by a person who cannot read it, and I do not know what the standing of that is and neither do you, and I would rather be a person who does not know for eleven years than a recordwright who looked once.”

That was the whole of it and it took about six minutes and neither of them raised her voice once.

---

Then she asked for the sheet.

“A sheet,” she said, “in the back of that book, in a hand I asked you not to write in, with two questions on it. One of them is about consent.”

“Yes.”

“It has been in your coat for about a year and a half and I have not asked you for it, because I sent it and I knew what it was for, and because a man who is four hundred miles inland and cannot read and is walking into a district with no money has a use for a piece of paper and I am not sentimental.” She put her hand out, flat, on the table. “Give it back.”

He took it out of the back of the book. It was folded in four and it had been folded in four for a very long time and it had gone soft along the folds.

He gave it to her and he did not say *you can leave it*, and she took it and put it in her own book and shut the book, and neither of them said anything else about it and neither of them ever did.

The room was quiet for about ten seconds.

“I am going to say the other thing now and I am going to say it once and then we are going to talk about a basket,” said Sera Quill. “You have been on a coast for fifty-nine days and you have come back and you have not said one word about the thing that happened in a shed, and I have read the three sheets, and I am not going to ask you about it, and I want you to know that I am not asking and why.”

“Why.”

“Because if I ask you about it you will tell me, and you will tell me the version you could put your hand up to in a room, and you have got that written down on a page somewhere and I have read the page and I know what is on it and what is not on it, and the thing that is not on it is the only part I would want.” She picked up her pen. “So I am not asking, and you are not to tell me, and in about four years I am going to ask you again and you are going to have to do it properly, and I am telling you now so that you cannot say later that you were not warned.”

“All right,” said Ilyan Vester.

“Good. Now. There is a basket going up a towpath tomorrow and a woman called Sena Ollet who carries it, and she cannot read what she is carrying, and there are about nine people in that district who cannot read a thing that is going to be put in front of them in about twelve days, and four of those nine are the whole of a bench of four, and I am going to write you down what that is in my own book, and then I am going to buy us both something to eat, because you look like a man who has been living on the thought of it.”

“What is it in your own book.”

“I am a recordwright of a public record,” said Sera Quill, “and I have just written down in a public book that a district six miles from a city of about ninety thousand people has four out of four of its governing seats occupied by persons who cannot read a form. I have written *the bench of Ryefold* in a column of my own and I have written the date, and I have written *and none of the four has said so*, and that last clause is the truth and it is going to be the most useful sentence anybody has written about that district in about nine years, and none of the four is going to know that I have written it, and if any of them ever finds out they are going to be furious with me and they will be entitled to be.”

---

They ate in a place with a tray of something and no cloth, and she told him three things about the second mouth and he told her two things about the boat, and neither of them mentioned the claim again, and at the end she put a folded paper on the table between the trays and pushed it across with two fingers.

“That is not from me,” she said. “That is a Bench abstract, entered in the ordinary way, and it is the whole of a forbearance granted to the district of Ryefold in the thirty-ninth week of last year, and you cannot read it, and I have read it, and I am going to tell you the one thing in it that matters and then I am going to let you work out the rest, because if I tell you the rest you will do the rest in about four minutes and it will be wrong.”

“Which thing.”

“It has an end on it.” She took her hand off it. “Every one of them has an end on it. That is what the word means and the word is not on it and I have looked. It ended in the thirty-ninth week of this year, which was the week before you landed on this bank, and I have had that abstract for about a year and I did not know until about a minute ago that the district it belongs to is the one at the end of your towpath, and I have been in this city for eleven years and there is not one person in it who reads a forbearance abstract except me and the two clerks who print them.”

She stood up.

“Nobody in that district has been told. You have been there three days and you have a basket on the towpath tomorrow or the day after and a woman who carries it, and I am going to ask you one question and then I am going to leave you with it, and I am not going to answer it and I am not going to help you with it, because you are going to be much more use to six hundred people than you will be to me this afternoon.”

“What question.”

“When a thing that a district was given for a year ends, and nobody in the district is told, and the only people who know are a person in a city and a man in a record he cannot read, and a boy with a basket who is being paid ninepence a day to carry a number up a towpath” — she stopped, and looked at the door, and then at him — “then who has the duty to tell them, and what is it called, and does anybody in this world have a name for the person who does it?”

And she said, “That is the last thing I am going to say about it, and I am not going to say it again, and if you write it down as mine I will deny it and I will be able to.”

---

The bowl was the outside of a mug and the tea was cold and there was no more money between them than there had been on the boat, and at the second bell he went down four floors with a bad leg and a bag.

He walked the four miles out to the district instead of waiting for a cart, because a cart was threepence and a mile of it was his, and because the leg had been his own for about two hours and a half and he knew what it was worth and what it was worth was fourpence.

It went at about the second mile, on a bridge over a cut, all of it from the hip down, and he stood against a parapet for about four minutes while the water went underneath and did not have anything to do with him. There were about nine people on the bridge and a carter and a woman with a handcart of sacks, and not one of them looked at him, because a man leaning on a parapet in the middle of the afternoon in a country like this is a man waiting for somebody and everybody has somewhere to be before a cut.

When it came back he finished the four miles, and at about the ninth bell he was in the cart shed behind a granary with three shillings and tenpence, and he did the arithmetic on the back of his own hand in the dark, and then he wrote two things on the seventh page of a penny exercise book.

*There are four people in this district who govern it and not one of them can read a form, and one of them has told me so herself, and she did not know she was telling me anything.*

*The rules on this shelf are all the same shape as the rules on the coast and the words on them are all the same words, and a word on a form is a claim that a thing is temporary, and a claim like that does not take the rule. It takes the paper. And I have not yet found anybody in a hundred miles who has asked to read the paper.*

And then the two lines that were on the other six, in the same place, because they are the two lines he has put on seven pages and he does not have a third.

The eighth page stayed blank that night and the morning after, and there was nothing to put on it, and that was the second time in a month and a half that he had found a thing worth writing down and had not written it, and the reason was that the only two people in this world who would have been glad to read it were four hundred miles apart and a hundred miles up a river, and there is no longer any part of this in which one of those two distances is a reason.

# Chapter 185: The First Of The Forty-Eighth Week

The room is up nine steps off a lane in a city of about ninety thousand people and it is not a court and it is not a bench of four. There is a table at the top of it, a chair for the person sitting, a public notice on the door, and a row of chairs along one wall for people who have come to watch, and on the wall behind the chair there is a thing that has been in that room longer than anybody in it has been alive and is a word on a board, and the word is the one that had already been on the wall at the fourth sitting and at every sitting before it, and it is not on the door and it is not in the minute and it is the reason a district of six hundred people has not been heard in nineteen years.

The corridor outside is nine feet wide and eleven yards long and there is a bench in it that is not for sitting on.

A man of thirty-one was in that corridor at about the second bell on the first day of the forty-eighth week. He was not a party. He was not named on the notice. He was not on a list, and he had asked to be on no list, and he is carrying one thing, and the thing it is is in a room on the other side of nine steps, and he was not going to be in that room, and he had known that since the second day of the forty-second week, at a counter in a city four miles off, when a clerk of twenty-six read a sheet round to him and did not push it and did not read it and told him that a person of the district applies and that there is no line on the sheet for anything else.

Nobody sent for him. That is the whole of it and it is worth being exact, because there are four words for what happened to him in nine weeks and three of them are wrong.

---

Inside, at about the second bell, a man of forty-six stood up and made an application in four sentences, and he had written them himself on a sheet at a shilling and fourpence, and he had not read them, and nobody in that room except one woman knew that and she was not in the room.

What he said was this.

That the district applied for the year and got it, and spent it on carriage, and the carriage belonged to other people.

That the district is a tenant of a bank of the second mouth and holds a granary on a lease, and the lease is not renewed, and the renewal is at the discretion of the account, and the discretion has run out, and nobody has exercised it, and the district is still in the building.

That the account is nine thousand seven hundred pounds and its charge is four hundred and eighty-five pounds a year, due at the close of every grain year, and the district has never been told what the charge was and has never been told when it began, and cannot pay it out of a cut and never could.

And that the district asks that the account be required to show, in the room, on the day, its own book of entries for this district, being the loads, the rate, and the claim.

Aldis Fenner asked four questions, and about half of them were not questions, and the ones that were are not worth setting down because they were about the form and not about the thing. The one that was worth setting down he asked at about the third bell, and he asked it flatly, because it is the way he asks everything.

“You cannot read what you have signed.”

“No,” said Nevin Hake.

“Then I am going to read it out, and you are going to stand there while I do, and you are going to tell this room if I get a word of it wrong, and if you tell me I have got one wrong this room will put the right one in and not the one I said. Do you understand what I have just done to you.”

“Yes,” said Nevin Hake. “You have made me the reader of my own sheet in a room, and about nine of the people in here will now believe that a man who cannot read has checked four sentences, and that is not what has happened and I would like it written down that it is not what has happened.”

“It will be written down,” said Aldis Fenner, and his clerk wrote it, and it is in the minute, and the minute is one sheet and it is the only copy in the world and the district has it and does not know that it has it.

He read the four sentences out. He got every word of them right. He is about sixty-two and has sat in that chair twice a year for nineteen years and his job is to keep a list of what has been said.

---

Then the account's own book was produced, and it was produced on the day, and it is a party that produces on the day whatever a person of the district asks for, and what it produced was four sheets at fourpence a sheet which the district had already paid for once out of a reeve's apron and had to pay for again because a page cannot be read out of a book in a room in a city six miles off.

Amos Kellock read it. He was nineteen and he had been in the account's own office for two years and he was entitled to take depositions and was not entitled to anything else, and he had asked for nothing and had been given it, and he stood up with a page in his hand and the hand was not shaking, and about eleven people were in that room and none of them knew what was coming.

“Three thousand four hundred loads entered,” he said. “At the county’s entered rate of two shillings and eightpence a load. Carriage paid against the account, three thousand loads at three shillings. Short, one hundred loads. And a claim of four hundred and six loads at fifty-four pounds two shillings and eightpence, of which nine pounds was paid on the sixth day of the forty-third week and the remainder of forty-five pounds two shillings and eightpence waits to a year.”

He put the page down.

“That is the account’s own book and it is a true page and I have checked it twice this morning and it is the only page in nine hundred that anybody in this room has ever asked to hear read out loud, and I am going to say one more thing about it and then I am going to stop, and I am going to say it because the man who asked me for that page is a man who cannot read, and he is not a person of this district, and he is the only man I have met in two years who asked me what was in a book instead of asking me for a date.”

“Does it,” said Aldis Fenner.

“No,” said Amos Kellock. “It has three columns. Loads, rate, claim. And in nine hundred pages there is not one word about a sheet, and there is no form anywhere in this county for asking about one, and the register that does hold them is in the same building as that book and about eleven feet from it and has never been asked to see it, and I have never been asked to bring them together and there is no form for asking.” He looked up. “The second thing is the comparison and it is one comparison and it took me four days to get it into a sentence and I want it read out right.”

“Go on.”

“The register publishes a scale of carriage at three shillings a load. A sheet is valued at what it saves you, and what it saves you is a load, and so the register’s own number for a piece of paper is one load. In this district’s own book a load is two shillings and eightpence, and a sheet costs two shillings and eightpence. So the register’s own valuation of a piece of paper, translated into this district’s own book, comes to exactly the price of the piece of paper, and the load is not in it.” He put his hand flat on the table. “That is a fact about how a register is written. It is not a claim about a man and it is not a complaint about anybody in this room, and I have not come here to make one, and I would like this room to understand that it would be the same fact in a hundred and one counties.”

Nobody said anything.

“And on the register’s own scale,” he said, “a man with one cart moves about four hundred loads in a cut, which is sixty pounds, and he paid two shillings and eightpence for the sheet, and sixty pounds is four hundred and fifty times two shillings and eightpence. A man with no cart is not in that scale at all, and he pays the same two shillings and eightpence, and the reason is not that anybody was cheated of anything. The reason is that the unit is a load, and a load is a thing a cart does.” He took his hand off the table. “A piece of paper in this county is priced at what it is worth to a man who already has a cart.”

---

Ansel Cork stood up and said four things, and he had said them four times in a year and this was the fifth, and not one of them was a lie and not one of them was a mistake and he conceded nothing at all.

He said the account kept the book and produced it on the day and it was not privileged and the district had got a page of it at fourpence a sheet and had paid twice.

He said the register was not the account's and the account had no power over it and the price of a sheet was a clerk's fee and was the same in a hundred and one counties and had nothing to do with the scale of carriage and nothing to do with this district.

He said that nine thousand seven hundred pounds at four hundred and eighty-five pounds a year did not stop, and had not stopped through the cut, and the district had never been credited with the year it had been given and never would be, because the year had been spent and a spent year is not a thing that comes back.

And he said that the account's position had not altered and the account was not a party to any ill will and the account would prefer that district to apply again and be heard, and that the account had put nine thousand people into a winter meal last year and had the receipts, and that it was the fifth time it had said that in about a year and it would keep saying it.

Then Nevin Hake said one sentence, and it was not clever and it was the only sentence anybody said all morning that was about the district rather than about a book.

“I am not here to say the account has done anything to us,” he said. “I am here to say three figures and to say them slowly. Four hundred and eighty-five pounds a year, due at the close of this grain year, on an account of nine thousand seven hundred pounds. A hundred loads of hire not paid for, which is fifteen pounds. And a reeve’s wage of two pounds ten a year, eleven weeks out of it, which is ten shillings and sevenpence, and she took sixteen pence out of her own apron this week for a page out of that account's own book.” He put his hand flat on the table. “I cannot add those three figures together in my head. I have not been able to add anything together in my head since I was nineteen. I would like them in a minute, and the minute is one sheet, and I have not got another sheet, and neither has the district, and the notice of four sheets that put me in this room was five shillings and fourpence and it was never paid for.”

Nobody entered the three figures and nobody was asked to and Aldis Fenner said the sitting had forty minutes of it left and called the next matter.

---

The matter was not decided on the day it was heard, and the reason it was not decided is the plainest thing in the world and nobody in that county has ever done it before.

The person who carried it asked for a day.

That is all. He stood up at about the fifth bell, in a room where four hundred and eighty-five pounds a year had just been said out loud for the fifth time in a year, and he said that the district was asking for the page to be read and the page had been read, and that about two hundred and forty of the six hundred people of this district were on a list in a drawer in a room six miles up a towpath, and that the list was in a reeve's hand, and that there was a second sheet with about nine names on it that had not come, and that there was no column anywhere in this county for a person who is on a list and is not in the room.

And he said that the four hundred men in about nine hundred yards who would be affected by what that page showed were not in the room either, and had not been asked, and had been asked nothing at any time in two years, and that the one of them who had been asked anything had said no in four seconds and had been right, and that the district was asking for a day on which they could be in the room or be sent somebody, and that a day in this county is chosen by the person who carries the matter out of the sitting days, and that this is the second week after the cut, and every carter in about nine hundred yards is free to walk four miles off a road on it, and that there has not been a fortnight run like this one in this district in the whole time this district has been doing anything at all.

Aldis Fenner looked at him for about four seconds.

“Nobody in this county has ever chosen a day for a matter,” he said. “That is not a criticism. I have sat in this chair nineteen years and it has never once been asked for and I have never once been asked why, and the reason is that the year runs so that a cut and a sitting are the same fortnight, and that is not anybody’s design. It is a year.” He put his hand on the table. “It is granted. Not decided — granted, and listed, and the account will be heard on the day, and the party that carries it will be in the room on the day or it will be carried, and I am telling you that now while you can hear me.”

Nobody cheered. There were about eleven people in that room and about nine of them had come to watch and had watched.

---

And in the corridor, at about the sixth bell, on a bench that is not for sitting on, a man of thirty-one who could not read was not told any of it, because nobody in that building owed him anything and nobody was going to go and find him, and a woman of thirty-nine came down the nine steps at about the sixth bell and stood at the north end of a nine-foot corridor about nine feet from him and did not sit down and did not say good morning.

“I have got one thing to tell you and I am going to tell it to you and then I am going to walk home four miles, and I am not going to be able to tell you how I feel about it until I have done that,” said Osa Trin, “and you are not to ask me before I have.”

“What happened.”

“They read the page out. All four lines, in a room, in numbers, and the boy from the office read them and he read them right, and there is a sentence in the minute now that says a piece of paper in this county is priced at what it is worth to a man who already has a cart, and the minute is in a book in a building six miles up a road that goes north, and I have read it, and it is the first sentence anybody has entered in a book about this district in nineteen years that is about this district.”

He did not say anything.

“You said a thing to me in a doorway in this district about three weeks ago and I did not know what it was for then and I have not stopped since.” She put her thumb under a line that was not there. “You told me a thing about the people in this district that I had not thought of, and it is the thing I have just heard a boy of nineteen read out in a room four miles off, and I want you to understand that it is the same thought and that you had it three weeks ago in a doorway and there was no book in this county that would have printed it then, and there is one now.” She stopped. “That is not a thing you did and I want it said that way round. I have been in this corridor for three hours with a sheet in my hand and I did it, and you did it in a doorway, and neither of us did it in a room.”

Then she said the other thing, and she said it to the wall.

“They have given a day. It is not in this grain year. It is the first sitting of the next one, which is the other half of a year, and I do not know the date and I am not going to guess at a date in a corridor.” She took her thumb away. “And the hundred loads is in the book. It is on a line in the book in the building six miles up the road and it says short, one hundred loads, in the account’s own column, in a page the district paid sixteen pence for. That line has been in that book since the cut and nobody in this district has ever known it was there, and it is the first time anybody has read it out loud in a room, and it is in a minute now, which is one sheet, in a building six miles up the road that goes north, and nobody in this district can produce the minute either.”

He said, “That is not the same as it not having happened.”

“No,” said Osa Trin. “It is the same as it having happened twice.”

---

At about the ninth bell, in a cart shed behind a granary, with a leg that had not been his own since a morning four hundred miles and some weeks behind him, a man of thirty-one did the thing he has done at the end of every working day since the second day of the fortieth week, which is to take a penny exercise book out of his coat and tear a page out of it and sit down on an upturned pail with his back against a cart wheel.

The year did not go out of him.

That is the whole of it and it is worth being exact, because he had said the price out loud in front of eleven people in a room above a chandler's shop on the seventh day of the forty-sixth week and had said that he was not paying it on the first of the forty-eighth week, and the first of the forty-eighth week came, and he stood in a corridor for four hours outside a room where a carter of forty-six he had asked in nine words had said four numbers out loud and had been believed, and nothing was taken, and nothing was paid, and he is still carrying it, and it is going to be carried past a review, and the sentence he said about that in that room eight days ago was that if he stops carrying it the thing stops being visible, and it has not stopped being visible, and the matter has been given a day and the day is in the other half of the year, so it is going to go on being visible for a year, and he did not have to do anything about that this morning except turn up at the second bell and read nothing at all in a corridor, which is the same work he did in a door on the first day of the cut, a fortnight ago, and which nobody has thanked him for, and which is the only work he has been asked to do in eight weeks that is not carrying something.

He wrote on the fifteenth page. The two lines at the end of it are the two lines that are on the other fourteen.

*I asked a man of forty-six in nine words, on the seventh day of the forty-seventh week, in a room with a fire in it, whether he would like to be the one who stands in the room, and he had already said yes in a lane the day before without being asked, and both of those are true and only one of them was a question, and I have not been able to get past that and I have stopped trying to get past it.*

*The page was read out in a room four miles off, in numbers, by a boy of nineteen who was not asked to and asked nothing for it, and a piece of paper in this county is priced at what it is worth to a man who already has a cart, and that is in a minute now, and it is the first sentence anybody has entered about this district in nineteen years that is about this district, and I did not say it. A reeve and a carter and a bench of four said it, and a factor in a good coat said the price of his own trade in a yard in the forty-third week and was not thanked, and a man in a good coat has wanted a list of the three hundred and thirty-nine for four years and has not got one and is going home in about a week.*

*It is mine to write down and it is not mine to decide.*

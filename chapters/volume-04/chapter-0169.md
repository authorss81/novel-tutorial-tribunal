# Chapter 169: What A Reeve Is

There is no meeting on a weighbridge. A weighbridge is a beam and a post and a man with a chalk and it is the only instrument in a district of six hundred people that nobody has ever been able to argue with, and on the fifth day of the forty-fifth week, at about the fourth bell, about forty of the two hundred and forty people on that quay could hear every word of what happened, and the other two hundred could hear most of it.

Marda Fen did not call it. That is the first thing and it is the thing that made it a thing at all. A carter asked her a question about the weigh, in the ordinary way, at the ordinary hour, because a carter with a cart in a cut asks a reeve about a cart in a cut, and she answered it, and the man next to him asked her another one, and by the fourth question there were about nine people within about nine feet of her and by the tenth there were about forty and nobody had done anything to make that happen.

“Eleven thousand loads,” said the first carter. “Is that right. Is it eleven.”

“It is about eleven and I have never weighed all of it and neither has anybody, and I am going to say *about* eleven thousand every time I say it for the rest of my life, and if a clerk ever asks me for the figure I am going to tell him about eleven thousand and I am going to tell him that is what I say to the weighbridge.” She had the beam up. “Three thousand four hundred goes out of it in this cut. That is the figure the account uses and it is the figure I use and we have been four hundred pounds apart on it for nineteen years and this week it is the same figure, and I would like whoever is listening to hear that, because it is the only number in this district that has ever agreed with itself.”

“What's left in the account,” said a man she had not seen since the spring.

“Nothing,” said Marda Fen. “There was nine pounds in it on the sixth day of the forty-third week and I gave all nine pounds of it to a carter who was owed fifty-four pounds two shillings and eightpence, and that was the correct thing to do and I have not slept since, and the account now has nothing in it, and it is nine thousand seven hundred pounds, and the charge on it is four hundred and eighty-five pounds a year and it is due at the close of every grain year and it is not going to stop.”

“Four hundred and eighty-five a year,” said somebody at the back. “Since when.”

“Since the carts went on it. It was four hundred and sixty-two before that, and the year of protection was worth four hundred and sixty-two and the carts cost four hundred and sixty, and the difference is two pounds.”

---

That is the shape of it, and it went on for about half an hour, and the questions came from the quay and Marda Fen answered them in the working voice, and the working voice is not loud and is not slow and is the voice a reeve uses when a carter wants to know what a load weighs, which is the voice this district has heard nine thousand times, and the reason the quay went quiet about eleven times in that half hour is not respect and it is that nobody had ever heard the answers.

“How much hire is in.”

“Three thousand loads of it is paid for and it is at three shillings a load, and that is four hundred and fifty pounds, and there is about ten pounds of weigh and loading on top of that, and that ten pounds is the same in every year in my time and I have asked twice and been told it is the stones.”

“That's four hundred and sixty.”

“That is four hundred and sixty and it was paid last year, not this year, and I want to be exact about that, because that is the thing that has been done to us and I have had a year to get used to saying it. Four hundred and sixty pounds of hire went on the account in the fortieth week of last year. And what came into that district that year was four hundred and sixty-two pounds of not paying interest. And that is not a scandal. That is a bad year, then a good year, then about two hundred people in four rooms making about four hundred decisions in a week with somebody else's money in front of them, and the carts were the only thing it could be spent on, and I would spend it on carts, and I have done the sum a hundred times and it comes out the same way every time and it is two pounds, and two pounds is the whole of what I have got to say about it.”

“What's missing for the cut.”

And this is the one she had been waiting for, and everybody in about nine feet could tell that she had been waiting for it, and it is the whole of the chapter.

“Three thousand one hundred loads have to go on hired carts,” said Marda Fen. “The four of this district will do about three hundred between them, and I have said *about* on both numbers and I have been saying *about* on that number for nineteen years while a man with a sledge takes a pitch off a woman on the strength of it. So: three thousand one hundred loads on hire. Three thousand loads are paid for. A hundred loads are not.”

“A hundred loads.”

“A hundred loads at three shillings a load is fifteen pounds. That is the whole of what is missing in this district, in money, to move its own harvest, and it is fifteen pounds, and we have nothing, and we have had nothing since the sixth day of the forty-third week, and the cut begins in three days.”

Somebody said, “Fifteen pounds is nothing.”

“Fifteen pounds is nothing,” said Marda Fen. “It is four hundred days of that line on the board, and it is six times my wage for a year, and it is about a quarter of one cart’s season, and I have got about eleven weeks out of two pounds ten a year, which is ten shillings and sevenpence, and I have got fourpence of my own in my pocket which I spent on a copy of a minute two days ago, and the sum of what I have said to you in the last four minutes is a list, and the list does not add up to anything, and I have never in twenty-two years read a list out on that quay and I have just read one out, and I am not going to do it again for at least a month because I have four hundred carts to weigh.”

---

And then the questions stopped, and the quay did what a quay does, and about two hundred and forty people stood in the rain on a stone quay in a district of six hundred people and had just heard a public answer, in numbers, from a person, in the open, and it was the first one in the history of the thing, and there is no form anywhere in this county for what you are supposed to do with one.

Nobody thanked her. There is a rule in a district of six hundred people, four hundred years old, and it is not in a book, and it is that a reeve answers a question about the weigh and then everybody gets back to the weigh.

But about four people on that quay said things afterwards, out loud, to other people, in the ordinary way, and the four things they said were these, and they are the whole of what came of the half hour.

A carter said, “I have asked her that same question about the rent of a second cart every week for two years and she has told me twice and I have not once been told what the district owes in a year, and I am not blaming her and I want that said, because I have been saying in this lane for a year that there is a number somewhere and I did not know where and I have found out where it was.”

A man with a handcart said, “Nine thousand seven hundred. I have heard nine thousand. I did not know that was the number and I have been saying that we are into it for something and I have been wrong and I have been wrong out loud, in a lane, for a year.”

The boy who carried the notice up the towpath, who is thirteen, said, “She read the numbers out the same as she reads a list. She did it that way for the numbers. I have listened to her read a list about four times and I did not know she was going to do that with money.”

And a woman of about sixty-one who had come down to the quay for grain and had no business there said, to nobody, in the ordinary voice, in about nine words, and about four people heard her, and this is what she said:

“Nobody in this district has done anything wrong. Do not let anybody tell you different, because they will use it, and it will be the only thing in the world this district has got. Six hundred of us have each been told a true thing, in four rooms, by people who were not lying to us. And not one of us has been able to find out what it costs.”

She went up the quay and got her grain and went home, and nobody said one word to her, and she is not on a list in this district, and nobody in that room will ever find out her name.

---

And Marda Fen went back to the second door and unweighed four carts and then sat down on a sack at about the seventh bell, and Ilyan Vester came past with a basket that was not going anywhere, and she said one thing to him that she had not said to the quay, and it was about eleven seconds long.

“You asked my Osa a question on the fourth day of this week, in a doorway, in front of about eleven people,” she said, “and it has been the most useful thing anybody has done in this district in about nine years, and I want to tell you the price of it, because I have been telling people prices since you got here and you are the first one who has ever handed one back.”

“What is it.”

“That she has to read that list out on the first day of the cut, in that building, with the door open, in front of every person in it, and she has never once read a list out to a room that was allowed to answer her,” said Marda Fen, “and I did not think about that when you asked her and I have thought about it every day since and I have not worked out whether I am angry with you or whether I am going to have to stand in that doorway with her and hold her up, and I have not worked it out, and the cut is in three days, and I have not slept two nights, and nine pence a day is nine pence a day, and you will be paid at the end of the week whatever happens, and that is not a kindness, that is a wage line, and if it were a kindness I would not do it.”

“All right,” said Ilyan Vester.

“Good,” said Marda Fen, and went back to the beam.

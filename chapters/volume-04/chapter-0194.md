# Chapter 194: Two People With A Licence And Neither Of Them

The room was not the room up nine steps. It was two floors up and four flights down from a counter in a lane, in the same building, in a city of about ninety thousand people, four miles from Ryefold, and it smelled of wet wool, and a recordwright of a public record sat at a table in it with her own book open and a pen in her hand and a man of thirty-one standing at the other side of the table with a page torn out of a penny exercise book in his coat.

That is the whole of the third day of the forty-ninth week and it is worth being exact about who was in the room, because there were three people in it and two of them had licences and neither of the two could do the thing he had come for.

He had come four miles to get a thing written down by somebody who is allowed to write things down. That is the whole of the errand and it took him about a minute to say and about four hours to find out that it was not available to him in any form.

“I am not your advocate,” said Sera Quill.

She said it before he had got the page out of his coat, which is a thing she does, and she is twenty-eight and she was licensed as a recordwright of a public record on four terms and the fourth of them was that she takes no matter of her own while she holds this one, and she released the ninth-bridge matter in the fortieth week of this year in a public book in her own hand with the date on it, for the reason that it was finished, and the reason it was released was so that in about four months she might file a claim of her own that is six years old and is not filed.

“And I want to be exact about why, because I have been wrong about this before with a man who was.” She turned the pen over once. “A recordwright writes down what a person says in the person’s own words and is answerable for the words and not for the thing. An advocate is answerable for the thing. If I stood in a room and argued that a year should be repaid, then I would be an advocate, and there is no advocate in this room, and there is not going to be one this morning, and I have not been one in about four years and I am not going to start at ten o’clock on a day in the forty-ninth week.”

“All right.”

“You are not going to argue with me and I want that on the record as a thing you did not do.”

“I am not going to argue with you,” said Ilyan Vester, “and I am going to ask you one question instead, and it is a bad question and I have been carrying it for about a fortnight and I could not leave it.”

“Ask it.”

“You told me a thing in this room in the fortieth week of this year about what I do with people, and you said I do not put them in it, I arrange it, and I said no I do not, and you said the arrangement is the whole of what I do and there is no part of it you could be put in front of a room for, except that you are put in front of rooms anyway because your name is in four hundred and sixty-three forms.” He put his hand flat on the table and left it there. “It is in one more now. I have been in this building eleven times since the fortieth week and there is a form with my name at the foot of it in a file six miles up a road that goes north and I did not fill it in and I did not buy it, and I have not been in front of a room for anything, and I am in one this morning and I am not a party to it and I have not sent for anybody.”

Sera Quill did not answer that for about eleven seconds, which is the longest she has been silent in a room with him in it.

“Then you have got two things in a day,” she said. “One of them is a building with a man in a good coat in it who is not a party to anything either, and I am going to tell you the second one now, because you have had it since the fortieth week and you have not asked me and I would rather you did not have to.” She put the pen down flat. “A person cannot be produced. That is the rule and it is the rule everywhere and it has been the rule for longer than the building. A person can be asked, and a person can come, and a person can put his own name on a sheet and be a person in a matter in front of a room. Nobody can send for him. There is no form for sending. And you have been the cheapest thing in this county to put a name on for about a month and you know it, and I have watched you know it, and if you go into a room this morning as hands then you will be hands for a year and there is nothing anybody in that room will be able to do about it afterwards.”

“I am not going in as hands.”

“No,” she said. “You are going in as a person, and I am going to write your name down, and you have asked to be on no list for about a month and I am going to tell you the two things about that and then I am going to do it anyway.”

---

The counter was on the ground floor and there was a man behind it who had been in that post two years and had read the same eleven hundred forms about nine hundred times, and he was better at the counting than at the room, and he looked up, and it took him about two seconds to work out who was standing there.

Roald Tern put his pen down.

“You have asked me nothing this morning,” he said, “and I would like it noticed that I have been waiting about a quarter of an hour for you to and that nobody in this building has ever noticed that before, including me.”

“I have one question and it is about a sentence you entered.”

“It is about the sentence I entered on the first of the forty-fifth week.” Tern did not say it as a question. “Four words, in a room, to a man who came to a counter with nothing and a leg, and the minute has the sentence and the four words are *has decided it has nothing to say*, and the whole of what happened to a district of six hundred people in this county is in those four words and in the hand they are in, and they are in my hand and not in his, and I have never said that to anybody and I have thought about it every day for about a month.”

He squared the tray.

“You want to know what I thought.”

“Yes.”

“I thought he was right,” said Roald Tern. “That is the whole of it and it has taken me a month and I have not slept properly since, and I could not put it in a column, because there is no column in that office for a clerk’s opinion and there is not one in this one either, and that is the whole of what is wrong with me and it is not the fault of the four words. If you had brought me a form with a box that wanted what I thought I would have written it in about four seconds and been asleep by the ninth bell.”

He put the pen back down and did not pick it up again.

“Now I am going to say the other thing and then you can go upstairs. I am a clerk of a counter. I am not an advocate, I have never been an advocate, and there is no version of this morning in which I am on your side of anything. I can tell you what a form costs and I can tell you what a counter is for and I cannot stand in a room and be answerable for a year. If you want somebody to be answerable for a year, you want a person of your district, and there is one thing about that which I am going to say because you are about to go back four miles and find out the hard way.”

---

The hard way took about four seconds and it was in a room that smelled of wet wool and it was said by a woman of twenty-eight who had her own book open.

“What you need is not a draft,” said Sera Quill. “A draft is one sheet and it can be written by anybody with a licence and there are two of us in this building and neither of us may act for you and I have just told you why. What you need is a person of the district who is answerable for a room, and there is no advocate in a hundred miles of this and there is no form for appointing one and there has not been one since the building went up.”

“I know that.”

“You know it and you have come to me anyway, which is the third time.” She shut the book on her finger. “So here is the thing, and I have not said it to you in nine weeks because I have been waiting for you to do the arithmetic yourself. There is exactly one person in the district of Ryefold who can be answerable for a room. She is thirty-nine. She has been the person of that district since she was nineteen. She can fill in a form, she can read a letter from a stranger, she can read a sentence that is wrong, and there is no other person in that district who can do any of those three things, and you have known that since the second day of the fortieth week of this year.”

“She is a person in the matter.”

“She is a person in the matter,” said Sera Quill, “and that is the end of it, and you have been not asking her for nine weeks and I have watched you not ask her and I have not helped you, and the reason I have not helped you is that I do not think there is anything in this county that can be got out of a person who is already in the thing.”

“That is the same shape as a thing you said to me in this room in the fortieth week.”

“It is the same shape and it is not the same thing, and I want it said that way round before either of us writes it down anywhere.” She turned the pen over twice, which she does. “The fourth reason I gave you in this room in the fortieth week was about three sheets in an office four hundred miles from here and a man with a brush in his cuff who will send them to me eventually, and what I said was that you do not put people in a record by asking, you arrange it, and that the arrangement is the whole of what you do. That was about a thing I have read and you have not. This is about a woman of thirty-nine who can read a list of two hundred and forty names out loud in a building with one door in it. If anybody ever writes the two of them down as one thing then the record is wrong, and the record being right is the whole of what we have been doing in this room for a year.”

---

So that is what happened on the third day of the forty-ninth week, and it is the whole of it: two people in a city four miles off a district of six hundred people each did a thing they were allowed to do and neither of them did a thing they were not allowed to do, and a man was in a room as a person and not as hands, and the cost of that was named in the room and not afterwards.

He named it himself. He put the page on the table flat and put his own hand on it and said the three things out loud, in the ordinary way, and she wrote them in her own book in a column of her own with the date, and he could not read what she had written and did not ask her to read it back, and that was a thing he had not done with her before, and he did not do it for a reason, and he has never written that reason down and is not going to now.

“A name,” he said. “Ilyan Vester, of no district, thirty-one, and I am not a party and I am not an office and I have no remedy of my own and I have not chosen one.”

“A date,” he said. “The day a draft goes in, and it is not today and I am not going to guess at a day in a room, and the last time somebody in this building guessed at a day for me a district lost a year.”

“And a cost,” he said, “and I am going to say the whole of it and then I am not going to say it again in this building and I would like that written down as a thing I have said once. It is one year of my own memory, out of thirty-one, and it is a specific year and not a fog and not a wound, and it is the last of the years in which I was in a room with a person I had not yet met, and nobody in this room can check which year it is and that is not a trick of mine, and I am not going to be relieved of it and I am not going to be told afterwards what was in it, and if anybody in this room wants to tell me that the price was fair they can say it now and I would like to know before I pay it and I do not expect to hear it.”

Sera Quill wrote it down and did not say one word about any part of it, and about a minute afterwards she shut the book and said one sentence, and the sentence was not about the year.

“There is nothing of mine in anything I have written this morning,” she said, “and there is a claim of mine six years standing in this city that is unfiled and has been since before I had a licence, and you have not asked me about it once since the fortieth week and I have not raised it and I am not going to raise it. I am telling you that I noticed. That is all of it. I noticed, and I have said nothing, and you may not go up that stair and use noticing as though it were help.”

“All right,” said Ilyan Vester.

“Good. Now go and be a person for a day and leave the drafting to us, because that is what the two of us are for and neither of us can do the other half of it and the half we can do is a page and a page is the only instrument any of us have ever had.”

At about the fourth bell he went down four floors with a bad leg and a bag, and the counter was shut for the middle of the day and the man behind it was eating something standing up, and neither of them said good morning.

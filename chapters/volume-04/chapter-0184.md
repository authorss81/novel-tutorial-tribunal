# Chapter 184: One Of Them Becomes A Person

The room above the chandler's shop had a fire in it on the seventh day of the forty-seventh week and it was the day before a sitting four miles off, and four people were in it and a reeve came up at the fourth bell and did not go down again, which is her room on the days she sweeps the floor out.

He asked the question. It was the same nine words he had asked three times in a lane seven days before, and he said them standing up at the end of the table, in the open, with the fire behind him, and about nine people were in the room and two of them were not of the district and had come for the noise.

“Would you like to be the one who stands in the room.”

Nevin Hake said yes before the end of it, and he had said yes in a lane seven days ago without being asked, and both of those are true and only one of them was a question.

“I said it in the lane,” he said, “and you have now asked it, and I want both of those in the book in that order, because the second one does not make the first one better and it is the first one that is going to cost.” He put his hands flat on the table. “I cannot read. I said it in this room in the forty-first week and I have not sat in it eleven years to say it a second time. Tomorrow I sign a sheet I cannot read and stand in a room four miles off and answer for it to a man I have never met, and I know what that is worth because it has already been done in this room once and Perrin Gant did it and told us what it cost and he was right.”

Perrin Gant did not say anything for about nine seconds, which is not long, and then he said the thing, and he said it out loud and he did not soften it and he did not put a hand on anything.

“I am not going to pretend I have not heard that,” he said. “I am going to do a great deal worse than not hearing it. You are asking me, in this room, this morning, whether I object to a man doing to-morrow exactly what I did on the twenty-fourth day of the forty-third week. And the answer is that I do, and I have been sitting here since the second bell working out how to say it in a way that does not sound like a man who is afraid, and I could not find one, so I am going to say it straight and you can do what you like with it.”

Nobody in that room said anything.

“The thing that cost this district a year was not a signature,” said Perrin Gant. “It was a room with nobody in it who could read. That is the whole of it. I have said it nine ways in nine days and every one of the nine has been a version of that sentence and this is the first one I have got to say without it sounding like an excuse.” He turned his hand over. “I signed it because nobody else would. You are signing it because I will not. And the district loses a year on it, and the year is four hundred and eighty-five pounds of charge that does not stop, and the account is nine thousand seven hundred pounds, and none of that has got one single thing to do with whether Nevin Hake can read.”

He put his hand flat on the shut book, which is a thing he does.

“And I am not going to be allowed to have this both ways. If I say no to a man who cannot read signing a thing, then I have said no to me, and I signed one in this room and I have not slept properly about it since and I did not know on that morning what it would cost and I do know now. So I am going to say the sentence that has to be said and it is the sentence I have been avoiding since the second bell, and it is this. It is not the reading. It is that the four of us have spent nineteen years being four people, and a bench is not a person and there is no line on any sheet in this county for a bench, and the only way this room can be in that room tomorrow is if one of the four of us stops being four people for a day and becomes a person, with a name on a sheet, and answerable, alone, for six hundred people he cannot read a word of.”

He took his hand off the book.

“I have not been a person in a record in nineteen years and I did not know that until about four seconds ago. I have been a convenor. And a convenor is four people with a book between them, and the book is in this room, and the book is nineteen years old, and none of the four of us has ever put himself in anything.”

---

The sheet is one sheet and it is a shilling and fourpence, and it had been bought on the second day of the forty-seventh week out of a reeve's apron, and the notice of four sheets at a shilling and fourpence a sheet was five shillings and fourpence, and the district has now bought that notice twice, which is ten shillings and eightpence, and the second one of the two is the one that was never paid for and it went out on the fourth day of the forty-sixth week to four parties and not to one carter in about nine hundred yards, and there is nobody in this district who is going to be able to fix that and the man who wrote it in his own book said it was the worst thing on the list and it was his.

Here is the head of what went on the sheet, and it is the whole of the parts of it that anybody in this room will ever see, and the rest of it is a matter of forms and a room four miles off:

> THE MATTER. THE CLAIM OF THE DISTRICT OF RYEFOLD TO THE ACCOUNT OF THE SHELF GRANARY, LISTED FOR THE FIRST DAY OF THE FORTY-EIGHTH WEEK.
> THE PERSON APPLYING. A PERSON OF THIS DISTRICT, WHO IS IN THE DISTRICT, AND WHOSE NAME GOES ON THIS SHEET AND CANNOT BE GOT OUT OF IT AFTERWARDS BY ANYBODY.
> WHAT IS ASKED. THAT THE ACCOUNT BE REQUIRED TO SHOW, IN THE ROOM, ON THE DAY, ITS OWN BOOK OF ENTRIES FOR THIS DISTRICT, BEING THE LOADS, THE RATE, AND THE CLAIM.
>
> SIGNED. A PERSON OF THIS DISTRICT.

That is four blocks and a signature and it took about nine minutes to fill in, and three of the four people in that room could not read one word of it, and the one who could read it filled it in and did not correct a word of it and was not thanked and did not expect to be, and that is the arrangement of this room and it has been the arrangement of this room for nineteen years.

And here is the thing that makes it legal, and it is the smallest thing in this room and it has been in this room since the second day of the forty-sixth week:

“A person of the district applies,” said Osa Trin, from the door, where she had been standing for about nine minutes without any of them having noticed her, which is a thing that happens in this room. “And a person of the district is represented. And four sheets on notice to every party. That is the whole of the sheet and Gant had it read out to him in this room in the forty-third week and I read it out twice with my thumb in two places. Four people is four persons. One of us makes it. One of us stands in the room on the day. The other three are not the district and are not asked to be. They are witnesses, and we swore four of them on a page on the twenty-second day of the forty-third week and it went on a nail, and the man who keeps the register stood in a yard and told us what it was for.”

“Which is a stranger's name in it as a person and not as hands,” said Perrin Gant.

“Yes,” said Osa Trin. “And that has never once come up in nineteen years because nothing has ever come up in nineteen years.”

---

Wil Tamworth asked the question and he is thirty-three and he asks what a number means, and he asked it standing up, and it took him a second to get to it and he got there.

“Then what happens to the reading.”

Nobody answered him for about nine seconds.

“Nothing happens to the reading,” said Osa Trin. “The sheet is made and signed by Nevin. The sheet is read out to the room by a person of an office, which is what happened to the deposition at the fourth sitting and it took about four minutes and it is the ordinary thing. And the person who answers a question in that room is a person of the district and the question is asked by the chair and the answer goes into a minute, and there is no rule anywhere in this county that says the person answering has to have read what they are answering about.” She came away from the door. “And that is how a district of six hundred people that has one reader in it gets to be a person in a room. It gets to be a person in a room by having a carter stand up there and say things, and the things he says will be true, because he is not a man who says untrue things, and there is no form that makes anybody check.”

Nevin Hake said the rest of it and he said it to the table.

“I am going to say what I am afraid of, because I have watched this room do that twice and both times it was the right thing to do. I am afraid of standing in a room four miles off and being asked a question about a number I cannot read, and saying a number wrong, in a room, in front of a clerk, and it going into a book in a hand that is not mine.” He looked at the shut book. “And the answer to that is that there is a person in this district who can read every number there is, and she is not in that room tomorrow, and she is going to be in this lane at the first bell, and I am going to walk down it and ask her to read me what I am going to be asked about, and she is going to say no.”

Nobody in that room contradicted him, because she had already said no in a doorway three days before and it had cost her nothing to say and everything to say.

“Then what,” said Wil Tamworth.

“Then I will say the numbers out loud as I know them, which is a hundred loads short and fifteen pounds short and four hundred and eighty-five pounds a year on nine thousand seven hundred, and if I am wrong then I am wrong in a room and it is in a minute and a minute can be read by nine hundred doors over about a year.” He took his hands off the table. “I would rather be wrong out loud in a room four miles off than be right about a thing on a sheet I cannot read, and that is the difference between Gant in the forty-third week and me tomorrow and I do not know whether it is enough and I have not got anybody to tell me.”

---

It was not a ruling. Nothing was decided except a name, and a date, and a cost, and those three were said out loud at about the sixth bell in a room with a fire in it by a convenor of sixty-one who is not going to be the person, and who wrote all three into the bench's own book in his own hand with the date and shut it, which is a thing he does, and did not ask for it to be read back to him because it was not a question and there are not many of those in nineteen years.

“A name, a date, and a cost,” said Perrin Gant. “Nevin Hake. The first of the forty-eighth week, which is tomorrow. And one sheet at a shilling and fourpence out of a reeve's apron, and five shillings and fourpence of notice the district has never paid for, and a day of a carter of forty-six that nobody in this district is going to pay him for and that is a hundred loads of his own grain and I have it written on a bin-board and it is about.” He shut the book and put the key in his pocket. “And one thing I am adding because it is mine and it has been on the list since the fourth day of the forty-sixth week: a bench of four that swore a page on the twenty-second day of the forty-third week is going to be a bench of three with a stranger in it tomorrow, and a bench of three is not a bench, and there is nothing in this county that will make it one, and I have been a convenor for nineteen years and I have just found out the size of the thing I have been holding.”

At about the seventh bell Marda Fen went down the lane to the beam, and there were about four hundred carts in that yard and about seven thousand loads in that building and one pair of hands, and she read the board on the way past it and did not say anything about it and did not correct it, which is what she does, and then she shut the second door.

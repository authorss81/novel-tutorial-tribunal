# Chapter 168: Two Hundred And Forty Names

The order in which a person of the district may take grain at the district price against the account is a list, and the list is kept in the reeve's hand, and it is about two hundred and forty names long, and it has been kept in the same hand for nine years, and there are two people in this district who can read that hand.

There are two. The reeve, who wrote it. And Osa Trin, who has read that hand every week for nineteen years and has never once been asked by anybody in this district whether she can.

Nobody in this district has ever asked Marda Fen what the list is for, and that is not because she is difficult. It is because she has been asked about it, in effect, every week for nine years, by about two hundred and forty people who turn up at the second door and hold out a hand, and a woman holding out a hand at a door is not somebody who is about to be asked a question.

On the fourth day of the forty-fifth week there were about eleven people on that quay and the fourth door-bell of the week, and Ilyan Vester was at the second door with a basket on his arm that was empty and was not going anywhere, and he had been in that district thirty-eight days and had not once asked her anything, on purpose, because she had told him on the second day of the fortieth week that if he repeated any of what she had said to any of the four he would stop his wage and do it in chalk where they could read it.

And then he asked her, in the open, at the second door, in a working way, with his hand on the box the chalk was in, in about nine words.

“Osa. How many names, and what happens to one.”

---

Osa Trin was in the second door at that moment with her sleeve rolled and a list in front of her on the board, and she had been the reader of that district since she was nineteen, and in the four days since the thirty-fifth day there had been about four hundred things she had done for other people without being asked and there had not been one thing anybody had asked her.

She put the chalk down.

“Why that,” she said. “Of everything four people could have said to me this week.”

“Because you are the only one of us who can read it who is not the woman who wrote it,” said Ilyan Vester, “and because I have been in this district thirty-eight days and I have been paid ninepence a day and I have carried a basket up a road six miles each way and I have not done one single thing in this district that was about anybody in it, and that is on a page in a shed and it is a page about me, and the cut is in four days and I would like to spend an hour of it on this.”

She looked at him and did not hurry.

“There are two hundred and forty-one,” she said.

“There are two hundred and forty.”

“There are two hundred and forty on the account’s list and there have been two hundred and forty since before I started keeping it, and I have got a hundred and one *and*,” and she went to the drawer and took out a second sheet, which is thinner and greyer and folded in three, and which she has kept since she was nineteen years old. “And the hundred and one is nineteen years old and it is a list of the people on that account’s list who did not come and did not send word, and I have gone through it once a year, and I have struck off the ones that are dead, and I have carried the ones that are not.”

“What happens to a name on it.”

"Nothing happens to a name on it," said Osa Trin. "That is the answer and you are not going to like it and I have known it for nineteen years and I have never said it out loud. A name on that list that does not come is not stopped, because there is nothing here that stops anybody. It is not written off. It is not put in a second column. It is carried. It is carried by me, because I am the one who keeps the list, and when a person does not come I walk up that lane to their door and I find out why, and about nine times in ten it is a fever or a cart or a debt, and about nine times in ten the answer is that they will be in next week and about nine times in ten they are, and the tenth time they are not, and the tenth time I go again."

"And the hundred and one is."

“One hundred and one is the number of times in nineteen years I have gone twice,” said Osa Trin, “and I have not gone twice to anybody in about six years, and I know exactly which ones are the eleven where it was three times or four, and I can tell you the names of all eleven without the sheet, and I have never been asked for them, and there is no column in that list or in that one or in any book in this county for a person who went twice.”

She put the second sheet down on the board, flat, beside the first one, and did not let go of it.

“Now you are going to ask me the obvious thing,” she said, “because you have that look, and I have watched that look arrive on about four people in nineteen years and I have watched what it does to a district, and so I am going to answer it before you ask it, and I am going to answer it in front of about eleven people rather than in a corridor, because if I say it in a corridor it is a thing I said to one man and if I say it in a doorway it is a thing I said in front of a reeve.”

“Go on.”

“A person who keeps a thing for other people without being asked,” said Osa Trin, “is how a district ends up with a person who cannot stop.” She took her hand off the sheet. “I have been the reader of this district since I was nineteen. I have read for four men on a bench and a reeve and about two hundred and forty households, and I have read for nothing, and nobody has ever asked me, and I have done it anyway, every week, for nineteen years, and I have got extremely good at it, and there are two people in this district who can read that hand and one of them is me and the other one is the reeve and she wrote it, and there is no office of this district, and there has not been one for nine years, and the account is in a city six miles off and it has never sent anybody.”

She said the last three sentences in a completely level voice and then she said, “That is not a complaint. I want it on the record that it is not a complaint, because the last person who said that in this doorway was me, about four days ago, and I have thought about it since and I have decided that when I say a thing is not a complaint I usually am, and I have decided to stop saying it, and I have not stopped.”

---

Marda Fen said the arithmetic, because the arithmetic was hers and it had been hers for nine years and nobody in that doorway had ever asked her for it either.

“The two hundred and forty on the account’s list,” she said, “is not two hundred and forty households. It is two hundred and forty *names*, and a name is a name, and about a hundred and sixty of them are men and the rest are women and children, and a man on that list in the thirty-first week of last year died.”

Nobody said anything.

“His name is on the account’s list. It is the fourth line. It has been on that list since before I had the box and it will be on that list in nine years because that is how long a list is kept. And I cannot strike him off.” She put her hand flat on the board. “Striking a name off that list is the account’s act and not the reeve’s, and the account is six miles up a road in a building with a window, and there is no form for a district to send up saying *this one is dead*, because the form is made by the account and the account has never in nine years made one, and so his household has been drawing on the fourth line for nine months and I have let them, and I have carried it, and I have not told his wife and she has not told me and we have both been extremely careful about the fourth line.”

“Then his wife knows,” said Wil Tamworth.

“His wife knows that she is on the fourth line and that the fourth line is being carried,” said Marda Fen. “His wife does not know that her husband is not alive. Nobody in this district has told that woman and I am not going to be the one and I have thought about it every week for nine months and I have not found a way to do it that does not put her on the towpath in front of two hundred people, and that is the whole of my finding.”

“And the two hundred and forty-first,” said Ilyan Vester.

“Is a woman called Nesh Arrad,” said Osa Trin, “who came into this district in the twenty-second week of last year with four children and a stove and no paper at all, and the reeve put her on the list in her own hand, at the bottom, in a different ink, and there is no authority anywhere in this county for a reeve to do that, and it is not forgery, because nobody has forged anything, and the fourth line above hers is a dead man. So there is a dead man in the middle of that list and a woman at the bottom of it who is not in it at all as far as the account is concerned, and both of those are the reeve, and both of those are nine years of nobody asking anybody anything.”

---

And then the reeve gave the other number, because by that point there were about fourteen people at that door and there was no version of the morning in which she was going to be allowed to keep it.

“Nine years,” said Marda Fen. “Eleven years, if you count from before the box. Forty-one loads. That is what I have carried out of my own authority on that list and not one load of it came out of the account, and the account does not know and does not need to know, because it is forty-one loads in nine years and a district moves three thousand four hundred loads in a cut.”

“Three shillings a load,” said Osa Trin, because she could do it and because nobody else in that doorway could.

“Three shillings a load is what the register says a load is worth, and forty-one loads is six pounds three shillings,” said Marda Fen. “And a year of that protection was four hundred and sixty-two pounds, and I have never once in twenty-two years been told that figure by anybody who came to this door, and I have got it off a stranger’s sheet in a coat, and I would like the room to hold those two numbers side by side for as long as it can stand it and then never do it again, because a woman who does that is a woman who has stopped weighing grain.”

About fourteen people in a doorway did that for about as long as anybody can do a thing like that, and then a carter asked about his own load, and the morning went back to being a morning.

---

And then a man in a good coat came to the second door, which he had been in twice, and asked for a list, which is what he said in a yard on the third day of the forty-second week that he would do.

Ilger Rusk did not shout and did not have a bag on him for once. He stood at the step with his hat off, which he had not done in the room on the day before.

“I want a list of the three hundred and thirty-nine,” he said. “In this county. Names and places. I keep the register myself and I have no clerk on it and if I die it goes to a successor who has never been in a yard, and I have said that out loud to a man with one leg in a yard in this district and I am saying it again at a door because I would like one of those two to stick.”

“There is not one,” said Marda Fen.

“There is not one,” he agreed. “I did not think there was. I have wanted one for about four years.” He looked past her at the board on the wall, at about two hundred and forty names in a hand, and at the wage line under them in chalk, and he did not ask for either. He stood there a while, and Marda Fen stood in the doorway of the second door of a granary in a district of six hundred people and let him look, and neither of them said one word about what was on that wall, and that was the most careful thing that has happened in that doorway in nine years.

“Then I will be in this district until the cut,” said Ilger Rusk, “because a cut is when a man with one cart finds out what a sheet is worth, and I will be at that door most days, and if anybody in this district ever decides to be one of the three hundred and thirty-nine in reverse, I am the man to come to, and I will not put a single word in a book about it, and I will take the sheet back if you want the sheet back.”

Nobody in that doorway had any idea what he meant and two of them never found out.

---

And then Osa Trin asked him for something, and she asked him at about the eighth bell with about nine people still at the door, and she asked him in front of all of them, and the reason she asked him in front of all of them is that she had learned in four days what a thing said in a corridor is worth in this district.

“I am going to have to read that list out,” she said. “On the first day of the cut. In the weighhouse, with the door open, because a reeve is not allowed to read a list with the door shut and I have looked that up and it is in the book and it is two lines. About two hundred and forty people will be in that building and every one of them will hear their own name said out loud and about nine of them will not be there, and there will be a dead man in the middle of it, and there will be a woman at the bottom of it that the account does not know about, and I am going to do it because I have been doing it for nineteen years and there is nobody else.”

“I know,” said Ilyan Vester. “What is it you want.”

“Come and stand in the door,” said Osa Trin.

She said it and then she heard herself say it and she waited, which is not a thing a person does twice, and four seconds went by.

“On the first day of the cut, at the first bell, in that door,” she said. “And you will not say anything. And you will not have read anything, and you will not be able to read anything, and I want the one person in that building who has read nothing at all, so that when I read a list out to two hundred and forty people, nobody in that room can persuade themselves that the reading means something. Because it will mean something. It will mean that I am the one holding it. And I have been the one holding it for nineteen years and I have never once had anybody standing in a door who could see me holding it.”

The leg went on that step at about the eighth bell and went all the way, from the hip down, and stayed for about eleven minutes, and about nine people in a doorway watched a man of thirty-one put his weight against a door-frame and stay there, and not one of them said one word about it then or afterwards, which is the fifth time that has happened in this district and the first time it has happened at the second door.

And Ilyan Vester said yes before he had worked out what it was.

He wants that said plainly, because it is the whole of the mistake he has made in this district and it is a different mistake from every other one of them. He did not know whether it would help. He said yes, on a step, with a leg that had gone, in front of nine people, to a woman of thirty-nine who had been the reader of a district of six hundred people since she was nineteen years old and had never once been asked what any of it was.

He has agreed to stand in a doorway on a day in four days' time, in the one building in that district where a record is going to be read out loud to everybody in it, and to be there for no reason that can be written down, and the only reason he is any use at all is that he is the one person in that room who cannot be convinced by a page.

And he did not think about that until the leg came back, about a minute after the yard went quiet, and then he thought about it for the whole of the six miles, and what he thought about it was not the door.

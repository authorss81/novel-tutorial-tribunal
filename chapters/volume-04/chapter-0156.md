# Chapter 156: A Decision Is A Thing A Person Makes

The lease notice came up the lane on a boy’s back on the first day of the forty-second week, and it was one sheet, and it went on a nail in a room above a chandler’s shop, and there were four people in that room and three of them could not read it.

Nobody had asked for it. Nobody had sent for it. Perrin Gant said, standing up, that it had been coming since the middle of the cut before last, and that his father had told him the same thing about the lease before that, and that nobody in that room had ever seen a piece of paper about it in his life and he had gone back through the bench’s own book in his own head in about ninety seconds and could not find one.

Osa Trin took it off the nail and read it standing up, at the fourth bell, with her thumb under the line, and she read it once and then read it again with her thumb in a different place, which is what a person does who has been asked to find a thing and cannot find it.

“All right,” she said. “It is up. It is up on the first of the forty-second, and the renewal is at the discretion of the account, and the price is a fortieth of everything in the granary, and it is payable in grain and not in money, and it is payable before the cut. And there is a line at the bottom that says a district may not sell the lease, and there is no line anywhere on this sheet that says what a district may do instead.”

“How long is there before the cut.”

“Four weeks,” said Nevin Hake, and then, “Three and a bit, by the time there is any of it. And I have been doing that sum every day for a year.”

Nobody thanked Osa Trin and nobody asked her to take it off the nail, and the sheet stayed on the nail, which is a thing about this district and not about her.

---

He went down the towpath at the second bell on the second day of the forty-second week with a question, and it was the only question he had, and it was this: whether a forbearance that has run out can be had again.

The answer was on a sheet of paper four miles inside a city of about ninety thousand people, and it took him four miles there and four miles back and about three hours standing at a counter, and he did not get it by reading it.

The chamber of Plenty’s counter at the Orison court is a counter like any other counter except that behind it there is a man who has read the same eleven hundred forms for two years and has not read one of them twice. Roald Tern is twenty-six and he has been in the post two years and he came into the chamber out of a counting-house and is better at the counting than at the room.

“A forbearance may be applied for again,” he said, before Ilyan Vester had got to the end of the question. “Once a year. At the fourth sitting. On notice to every party. The application has to be made by a person of the district and the district has to be represented at the sitting, and the notice goes out eleven days before, and the district pays for the notice at one and four a sheet, and a district of six hundred people is four sheets, and that is five shillings and fourpence to say that it is still poor.”

“And if the district does not come to the sitting?”

“Then the district does not get the forbearance,” said Roald Tern, “and it does not get a hearing about not getting it, because there is nothing to hear. That is the rule and I did not write it and I could not tell you who did. A party that does not appear is carried as uncooperative.”

“Uncooperative with what.”

“With the matter.” He looked up from the counter. “That is the word on the sheet and it is the word on the wall behind me and I have never known a party that thought it meant anything else. It means the party did not come. It does not mean the party was angry. It does not mean the party was wrong. It means the party did not come, and the court cannot go and find out why, because a court that goes and finds out why has stopped being a court and started being a man with a cart, and everything I have read says we do not do that.”

He put the pen down and looked at the man in front of him properly, for the first time.

“You are not a person of this district.”

“No.”

“Then you cannot make the application, and you cannot be a bearer of labour in it, and if you are named in it you are named in it as a person and not as somebody’s hands, and there is a form for that and it is one sheet and a shilling and fourpence, and that form is the only place in this building where a man of no district can stand without being an inconvenience to somebody.” He paused. “I say that as a clerk and not as a kindness. It is the one thing in here I have never had to explain to anybody and you are the first person who has not needed it explained.”

“Why have you never had to explain it.”

“Because in two years nobody has asked me a question that came from nowhere,” said Roald Tern. “They come in with a form and a fee and they want a date. You came in with nothing and you have been standing here a quarter of an hour and you have not asked me for a date once, and I have found that interesting, and I am going to be honest with you about the fact that I have found it interesting, because in about two months somebody is going to look at what I have entered today and I would rather it said a true thing than a kind one.”

---

He asked the second question, and he asked it because it was the question he had come with, and he asked it in the ordinary way, and it is the worst sentence he has said since a city four hundred miles behind him, and he said it about himself, and it is going to cost a district of six hundred people the fourth sitting, and the fourth sitting is in about eleven days, and there is no second one this year.

“And if a district cannot afford to send anybody?” said Ilyan Vester. “Then it is a district that cannot afford to send anybody, and you write that down, and the record says so.”

“I can write that down.”

“Does the record help them?”

“No,” said Roald Tern.

“Then the record says a district that could not afford to come is a district that would not come, and the two are not the same thing, and the second one is what the court will use, and it is what a room will use, and in about a month somebody is going to stand up in front of four people and put your record in front of them and say *the court asked and the court wrote it down* — and the court did not ask, and the court wrote down a thing that was not the question.”

The clerk said, “That is fair, and I cannot do anything about it, and I have been in this post two years and I have entered that sentence in a book about nine hundred times.”

“A district that sends nobody has decided it has nothing to say,” said Ilyan Vester, “and a decision is a thing a person makes. I will not stand in a room and call that anything else.”

Roald Tern wrote something down, and then stopped writing, and looked at it, and then wrote something else underneath it, and then nodded.

“That is a good line,” he said, “and I am going to enter it, because it is the ordinary rule and it is not yours and it is not mine, and I would rather it were entered in a clerk’s hand than a stranger’s. Thank you. That is exactly what this counter says and it is exactly what the sheet behind me says, and you have saved me about four minutes of explaining it to somebody who came in angry.”

He gave the slip back, and the slip had the date of the fourth sitting on it and the price of the notice and nothing else in it that Ilyan Vester could read, and he put it in his coat beside an abstract that had ended a thing eleven days ago, and he walked four miles back to a district where four people could not read either of the two pieces of paper in his coat.

He was at the top of the lane at about the eighth bell, and the leg went on the last of the four miles, and it was his own again by the time he got to the top of it.

And it is worth putting down, while it is on the page and while nobody has heard it, what he actually did in that room and in what order, because he did it in the order he always does it in and he has never once been able to see the order from inside it.

The first thing he did was ask the question he came with, and he got the answer, and the answer was a date and a form and five shillings and fourpence and a category.

The second thing he did was find the flaw in the category, out loud, to a clerk, in about a minute, standing up, and he was right.

The third thing he did was make the category comfortable, and that is what the sentence was for, and he did it to spare a man of twenty-six the unpleasantness of explaining a rule he had to enforce, and it worked, and the clerk was grateful, and the clerk entered it in a clerk’s hand.

The order is the whole of what he is. It has been the whole of what he is since a city four hundred miles behind him with a bad coffee machine in it. It is the reason a woman of twenty-four refused him in a shed and was right. It is the reason a man of sixty-one at a bench on a coast said *you keep being careful in the wrong direction* and was right. And it is going to be read back to him in a room in about three weeks, out of a deposition in his own words, in a hand that is not his, by a person he has not met, in a matter about six hundred people who cannot read anything that has happened to them.

He wrote none of that down. The ninth page of a penny exercise book that night is four lines long and it is about a basket.

*Fifteenth day. Nine pence on a board in chalk against a name, which is the same as the ninth bridge and is the whole of what I have got, and the woman who pays it read the board before she wrote on it, and I have worked for four people in two years and she is the first who has.*

*Asked a clerk a question and got a date and a form and a word. The word is that a party that does not come is carried as uncooperative, and the word is right, and my agreement with it is now in a clerk’s book in a clerk’s hand with my face on the counter in front of it.*

*I do not know why I said the thing I said. I have known all afternoon. There was a man of twenty-six who had to say a rule to a stranger, and I made it easier for him, and I have made a great many things easier for a great many people, and it is the only thing I have ever been any good at, and I have never once in my life done it for nothing, and today it cost a district of six hundred people nothing at all, because there was no district in the room.*

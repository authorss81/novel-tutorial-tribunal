# Chapter 196: The Harvest Is In

The harvest was in on the fifth day of the forty-ninth week and it had been in since the seventh day of the forty-sixth, and the reason it is worth a morning is that about seven thousand loads of it was standing in a granary on the second mouth in a district of six hundred people, and the district is going to eat this winter, and nobody in the district is going to be ruined by anybody, and the two halves of that are the whole of the last nine weeks and the whole of the last year.

Marda Fen put the figures on the bin-board at the second door at about the second bell, standing up, in chalk, in her own hand, the way she has done it every working day for twenty-two years, and about nine people were in the lane and none of them were there for the figures.

“About eleven thousand at the start of a year and about seven thousand at the end of one,” she said. “About three thousand four hundred go out in the cut and about three thousand one hundred of those go on carts that belong to somebody else and about three hundred go on four carts that belong to this district. That has been said out loud twice in a room and once in a yard and I am saying it a third time at a door because it is the fourth of the forty-ninth week and the door is where I say things.”

“Three weeks,” said Nevin Hake, from the beam.

“Three weeks is two and a bit and I am not going to argue with a carter about a number of days,” said Marda Fen. “I have not counted and you have, and I am not going to argue with a carter about a number of days, and I am going to go back to what I came out here to say, which is that the whole of what that cut cost this district was about a hundred loads, and a hundred loads is fifteen pounds on the register’s own scale, and a hundred and forty loads went over this beam on the sixth day of the forty-seventh week into the account’s own book at the county’s rate of two shillings and eightpence a load, which is eighteen pounds thirteen shillings and fourpence, and there is no claim on it and nothing was asked for.”

She rubbed nothing out, because there was nothing to rub out.

“Now the other half. Nine thousand people went into a winter meal in this county last year. Four hundred carts, hired at a rate a man set himself, and a man in a good coat has said that in a yard in this district in the forty-eighth week and every part of it was true, and it is going to be done again this year, because the account is nine thousand seven hundred pounds and it has put nine thousand people into a winter meal once and it has the receipts, and it is the fifth time it has said so in about a year and it will keep saying it.”

Nobody in that lane said anything.

“So this district eats,” said Marda Fen. “That is the finding and I have had it since the forty-eighth week and I have not said it out loud because it does not sound like anything and because saying it in a lane in front of carts sounds like a complaint about somebody. This district eats this winter, and about six hundred people in it are not going to be hungry, and not one of them is going to be ruined, and about four hundred and eighty-five pounds a year is going to fall due at the close of this grain year on an account of nine thousand seven hundred pounds, and the district is at nothing, and it has been at nothing since the sixth day of the forty-third week, and it is going to be at nothing at the close of the year as well.”

She put the chalk in her apron.

“Nobody is going to be ruined. That is the whole of the good news and I have been a reeve for twenty-two years and I have never had a year go by that I could say that about, and I am not going to be thanked for it and I am not going to be told it is a good thing by anybody, because the person who arranged it did not arrange it for us.”

---

The rest of the day was the rest of the day, and it is worth putting down in order because there is no other order in a district where a cut ended on the seventh day of the forty-sixth week and a quarter of a year is in front of it.

At about the third bell four hundred carts went out of about nine hundred yards in about nine hours doing no work at all, in threes and fours, to a quay where there was nothing for them, and came back, and a basket went up a towpath to a counter six miles off, a towpath that is six miles in each direction, and the basket weighs about eleven pounds when it is not full and about forty pounds when it is, and the woman who carries it is paid three shillings a week in a cut and nothing the rest of the year and has carried it for nine years and has never read one thing in it.

At about the fourth bell a man of thirty-four with one cart and four people came off the weigh with a load of his own and asked the reeve whether she would weigh by the second bell in the morning, and she said she would, and he said he would be back at the first, and that is what happens at the end of an afternoon in a yard off a lane in this district about nine times a week.

At about the fifth bell a reeve of fifty-four did not do a sum and that was the thing that happened, and about nine people were in the lane and four of them were waiting for a sum because she does one most days.

She stood at the bin-board with a piece of chalk and did not put anything on it, and then she said the number out loud into a lane, once, and put the chalk back in her apron.

“A hundred and seventy-five loads,” said Marda Fen. “That is the lease. A fortieth of what is in this building now, and it was a fortieth of about eleven thousand in the forty-third week, which was two hundred and seventy-five, and a hundred loads is fifteen pounds, and I worked that out in this doorway in the forty-seventh week in front of a bench of four and a man with a basket and I said then that I was not going to be pleased about it. I have said that number four times since the fortieth week of this year and it has never once been on this wall, and the wall is where I put everything else, and there are eleven thousand loads on it and seven thousand loads on it and a figure of nothing on it that has not moved since the sixth day of the forty-third week, and the one figure I have never put on it is the one that decides whether this district has a building.”

Nobody wrote the number of a lease on the board, and she did not ask twice, and she went back to the beam.

At about the sixth bell a boy of nineteen was six miles up a road that goes north in a counting-house lane, at a counter with a window in it, and he was writing numbers into a book about a district and he was two years in the post and entitled to take depositions for eleven months and not entitled to anything else, and there were three lines in that book that had not been in it before the forty-third week of this year, and one of them was a hundred and forty loads at the county’s rate and one of them was a hundred loads, and the second of those had a number at the foot of it and no name on it at all, and he had not been asked to put that number there and had not put it there and it was there because the office enters what comes in.

At about the seventh bell the second door of the granary was shut, after the board was read, which is what she does.

---

And the man with the basket did nothing at all on the fifth day of the forty-ninth week, except carry a basket from a door to a cart shed and back, which is what nine pence a day is, and except do the one thing he has done every working day since the second day of the fortieth week of this year and had never once been asked to do, which is to sit down on an upturned pail with his back against a cart wheel at the end of it and write down what each of about forty people in a district of six hundred had said in their own words, and to keep the three of them apart.

The three of them were the district’s own account of itself, which was in the bench’s own book in a room above a chandler’s shop and was two hundred and forty names and a page and a blank; the account’s own account of itself, which was a book kept by district with three columns in it and about nine hundred pages and not one word about a sheet; and the register, which was a shelf and a man and a habit in a city and had never been asked to see either of the other two and had never seen either of the other two.

Forty people. A reeve and a carter and a convenor and a reader and a factor in a good coat and a boy of nineteen and a man with a basket and about thirty others, none of whom he had asked to be in this district, all of whom had done the ordinary work of an ordinary year in the ordinary way, and about a hundred loads of their own grain that had gone out of a bin for nine pounds because a wheelwright of fifty-seven happened to be standing in a doorway.

He had been in this district since the second day of the fortieth week and the finding of the whole of it was a sentence and the sentence was not his, and he had spent about a fortnight of it being unable to help, and that was the correct way round, and there is no part of this in which anybody did anything wrong.

At about the ninth bell he shut the exercise book and did not tear a page out of it, because there was nothing in that day that was not already in one of the three, and a page is the only instrument he has and he has never once used it for a thing that was not a thing.

And in a building with about seven thousand loads in it and one pair of hands and a beam at the near end of a yard, and a board on a wall with about eleven thousand and about seven thousand on it in chalk in a woman’s hand, and a lease at a fortieth of a number that had got smaller by a hundred loads, and a sheet with a blank in the third line about the size of a hand going back four miles in a woman’s coat, and four hundred carts in a yard behind a granary with nothing to do for the third week running, the harvest was in, and the district was going to eat, and nobody in it was going to be ruined, and the person who had arranged that had not arranged it for them and said so himself in a yard in the forty-eighth week and had not been asked to arrange anything else.

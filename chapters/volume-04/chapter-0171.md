# Chapter 171: The Door

The weighhouse at Ryefold is a building with one door and a beam in it, and the door is nine feet high and about four feet wide, and there is no second door, and that is the whole of the building’s public life. It is on the quay at the near end, below the granary, and it is where the grain comes off a cart and goes into a bin and where a person holds out a hand and is written on a list.

On the first day of the forty-sixth week, at the first bell, the door was open, and about two hundred and forty people came into it in about eleven minutes, and one of them was a woman of thirty-nine who had been reading that list for nineteen years and who read it out loud to all of them that morning, and the reeve of the granary stood six feet behind her with her hands in her sleeves, and one of them was a man of thirty-one who stood in the doorway and read nothing at all, which is what he had agreed to do, on a step, four days before, with a leg that had gone on him in front of about nine people.

He had not worked out what it was. That was the honest state of it and he has not improved on it since. She had asked him for a person in a door and he had said yes before he had found out what a person in a door is for, and then he had spent three days finding out, and what he had found out was a thing he could not put in a sentence, and the finding was that a man who cannot read, standing in a door where a list is being read out loud, is a man about four hundred people can look at.

---

Osa Trin had the list on the board inside the door and she had the chalk in her hand and she read it standing up, and she read it in the voice she has used to read things in that room and above a chandler’s shop since she was nineteen, and the voice does not change for two hundred and forty names because the voice is not the thing that is doing the work. The voice is a thing she has got. The list is the work.

The list is in the reeve’s hand and it has been in the reeve’s hand for nine years, and the reeve wrote it, and there is one person in that building who can read it, and she is the one reading it, and the reeve is standing six feet behind her with her hands in the sleeves of her coat and is not looking at the page.

“Door,” said Marda Fen, at the front, to nobody in particular. “It is open. I want the door open. It is two lines in the book and it says a reeve does not read a list with the door shut and I have looked it up twice and the second time was this morning.”

“It is open,” said Osa Trin.

Nobody had asked her to open it and nobody thanked her for opening it, and about eleven people came in late because the door was open and two hundred and twenty-nine came in because a reeve was reading.

She read the list. It is two hundred and forty names and it takes about nine minutes, because the names are read at the speed of a name and not at the speed of a sentence, and there is a place in the middle where she has to turn the board over, and there is a place near the end where a woman in the second row has to go out and come back in.

Here is the head of it, which is the only part of it anybody outside that building will ever see, and it is in a reeve’s hand and not in anybody’s print:

> THE ORDER. A PERSON OF THE DISTRICT MAY TAKE GRAIN AT THE DISTRICT PRICE AGAINST THE ACCOUNT.
> 1. MARDEN SELL. THE HOUSE AT THE TOP OF THE LANE.
> 2. ALICE PRUE. THE SAME HOUSE.
> 3. WENN HALLARD. THE SECOND DOOR, NEXT THE CHANDLER’S STAIR.
> 4. THE HOUSE AT THE WEIGH, BEHIND THE SECOND DOOR. ON THIS LIST BEFORE THE REEVE HAD THE BOX, AND THE HEAD OF IT NAMED, AND NOTHING UNDERNEATH IT SINCE THE THIRTY-FIRST WEEK OF LAST YEAR.
> — and two hundred and forty lines, and the fourth of them is a household of a man who died in the thirty-first week of last year, and the name at the foot of the fourth line is his wife’s, and she is in the building.

---

The leg went about two minutes after she started, and it went from the hip down, all of it, and he put his right shoulder into the jamb of the door and stayed there.

About forty people saw it. He has been in this district forty-two days and about nine people have watched something happen to him and not one of them has ever mentioned it, and this was the first time it had happened in a doorway with two hundred and forty people in the room, and what it looked like from the floor of that building was a man who had come to a place on a bad leg and had gone worse in it and had not gone out.

Nine of the two hundred and forty worked out why he was in the door.

He knows that it was nine because he counted them afterwards, in his own way, which is to say that he knows which nine faces went from him to her and back again, and he has been doing that since a coast four hundred miles behind him and it is the only arithmetic he has never got wrong.

One of the nine was Osa Trin.

She did not say so. She had asked him not to, and the asking had been nine words and had not been a request, and what she had actually said in that room four days ago was that if he told anybody in this district why he was standing in that door then the standing in the door would stop being worth anything inside about a minute. And he had said that he was not going to tell anybody, and she had said *good*, and then the leg had gone on the step and had stayed about eleven minutes and nine people had watched it, and the reason he had said yes to the whole thing on a step in the first place was that he did not know whether it would help.

He has now got four days of that answer and it is: partly.

It helped with about nine people, and it did nothing at all for two hundred and thirty-one, and the nine are not the two hundred and forty, and the two hundred and forty are the ones the list is for.

---

Nobody in that building asked anybody anything.

That is the whole of what happened on the first day of the cut at about the first bell in a building with one door, and it is worth being exact about it, because it is the thing this district has been standing next to for about six weeks and it has now walked into a building with one door in it: a public reading of a list is not a hearing. A hearing is when a person is asked a question and has to answer it. In about eleven minutes in that building a woman read two hundred and forty names out loud and not one person in it said a word, and about two hundred and twenty of them heard their own household named by a person they cannot read, and about nine of them were not there, and one line in the middle of the list is a man who has been dead since the thirty-first week of last year.

A man who cannot read can stand in a door of a building for nine minutes and make the reading into something that is not a formality, and he cannot make it into a hearing, and there is no arrangement of a man in a door that does that, and the reason there is not is that the thing the room needed was a person who could ask a question, and the only person in that room who could ask a question and be answered was the woman holding the list, and she was working.

Nobody thanked him. He had been promised nothing for it and had not asked for anything, and about eleven people came past him in the doorway on their way out and two of them said good morning, and one of those two was a carter who wanted to know whether the reeve would weigh by the second bell.

---

The fourth line is the one he has thought about since, and he has thought about it in the ordinary way, which is to say that he has thought about it at the second door, at the weighbridge, in a lane, and in a cart shed behind a granary, and it has not once become a different thing.

The fourth line is a household at the weigh, behind the second door. The man whose household it was died in the thirty-first week of last year. His name is at the head of the line. His wife’s name is at the foot of it, under a bar, in the same hand and in a slightly different ink, and it has been there since before that woman had the box, and the line has been carried every week since the thirty-first week of last year, and nobody has struck him off, because striking a name off that list is the account’s act and not the reeve’s, and there is no form for a district to send up saying that a person is dead, because the form is made by the account and the account has not made one in nine years.

She was in the building. She was about fifty-eight and she had come at about the second bell and she had stood at the back with her hood down, and when the fourth line was read out she did not move, and when the fifth line was read out she did not move, and when the reading was finished she came forward to the second door with her hand out and took her grain and went home, and she does not know.

Nobody in that building knows. That is the finding and it is nine years old and it belongs to a woman of fifty-four who has not slept properly about it since the thirty-first week of last year and who is not going to be the one to tell her, and who said so, out loud, in a doorway, on the fourth day of the forty-fifth week, in front of about fourteen people, and gave the reason, and the reason is that it puts the woman on the towpath in front of two hundred people.

And the man in the door could not do it either, and that is the thing worth putting down, because he has spent six weeks being the man in the room who is nearest to a thing.

He could not do it because he cannot read the list and cannot therefore certify what is on it, and because he is not a person of this district, and because a person of this district is the only instrument in this county that can make a claim on a list, and because the reeve would have had to ask him to do it, and she will not, and because he was in a doorway holding himself up with his shoulder against a jamb and he had been asked to read nothing and he was doing exactly that.

---

At about the second bell Osa Trin came out of the door with the chalk still in her hand and stood on the step and looked at the quay, which was full, and did not look at him for about nine seconds, and then she said the thing she had to say and she said it in the working voice, because there were people on the step.

“Forty-two minutes,” she said. “It was forty-two minutes and not nine. I have been reading that list for nine years and I have never once read it out loud and I have never once known how long it took, and it is forty-two minutes, and I have got about nine names on a second sheet that I did not read out, and those nine are the ones who did not come today, and there has been more than nine on it every week for about thirteen years and there has not been more than nine on it in about six.” She put the chalk in her pocket. “There were about two hundred and forty people in there and about a hundred of them have not been in this building since the spring before last, and they came today because the door was open, and in four weeks’ time not one of them will remember whether the door was open.”

“Nine minutes,” he said.

“That is the reading. That is nine minutes of it. The forty-two is the waiting.” She put the chalk in her pocket. “There were about two hundred and forty people in there and about a hundred of them have not been in this building since the spring before last, and they came today because the door was open, and in four weeks’ time not one of them will remember whether the door was open.”

“Then what was the door for.”

She looked at him then, and it was the first time she had looked at him in about four minutes, and what she said was not an answer to the question and she knew it was not an answer to the question and she said it anyway, in a doorway, at about the second bell, on the first day of a cut.

“It was for the nine,” she said. “You will never know that. That is what it was for and it worked on nine people out of two hundred and forty, and I have been the one holding it for nineteen years, and I have never once had anybody standing in a door who could see me holding it, and you have been standing in that door for forty-two minutes and about nine people in this district now know that I am not doing it on my own, and that is nine, and it is not two hundred and forty, and I am not going to be able to make it two hundred and forty, and I have not got a second door and there is only one door in that building.”

She went back in.

---

The cut started at about the second bell and it started with a hundred and forty loads over the beam and about two hundred and sixty waiting in the granary, and the district is a hundred loads short of paid hire for the whole of it, which is fifteen pounds, and there is nothing in the account, and both of those figures were said out loud on this quay three days ago by the woman standing at the front of that building, in front of about two hundred and forty people, and nobody has done one single thing with either of them since, and she said at the time that she was not going to say them again for at least a month.

At about the fourth bell, when the light had come up properly and the rain had stopped being interesting, the beam was working and a woman of fifty-four was calling numbers off it in the voice that nine thousand people in that district have heard her use, and a man of thirty-one was carrying an empty basket he was not going anywhere with, because a basket is the only thing in that lane that puts his name in a place where anybody can find it, and the wage line is read before it is written and is written at the seventh bell, and there is a thing in that district that is the same number today as it was yesterday and it will be the same number tomorrow.

It is two hundred and forty.

It was two hundred and forty yesterday and it has been two hundred and forty since before a woman of thirty-nine started keeping a second sheet in a drawer, and it is two hundred and forty names and about a hundred and sixty of them are men and the rest are women and children, and it is a number that exists in one hand, in one building, and in one drawer, and the only person in that district who can read it is the woman who was reading it out loud at the first bell with her sleeve rolled up, and there is no second copy of it, and there is no column anywhere in this county for a person who is on it and does not come, and there is no form for a district to send up saying that a person is dead, and there is a drawer at the back of the second door with a sheet in it that is thinner and greyer and folded in three and nineteen years old, and the two hundred and forty-first name on it is a woman called Nesh Arrad who came into this district in the twenty-second week of last year with four children and a stove and no paper at all, and she is in this district, and she is not in it.

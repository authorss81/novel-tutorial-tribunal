# Chapter 176: The Woman At The Second Fire Is Not On This Shelf

The letter came up the towpath in a basket on the sixth day of the forty-sixth week, in the middle of a cut, in a basket that goes six miles to a counter in a city and six miles back with whatever the counter has sent, and it was not sent to anybody and it was not addressed to anybody, and it came out of a basket in a lane at about the second bell and was put on the bench’s own table by a woman of thirty-one who has carried that basket for nine years and who is paid three shillings a week in a cut and nothing the rest of the year.

“I have not read it,” said Sena Ollet. “I have never read one and I could not read this one and I have not asked what is in it, and I am telling you that I have not asked because you are going to ask me whether I have and I would rather have said it first.”

“Thank you.”

“Do not thank me, that is not what it is.” She turned the slate on its three-legged frame about a quarter of a turn, which is what she does with her hands when she is not going to be interrupted. “There is a woman in that basket’s history, somewhere in the eleven years, who read a number off a slip at the counter and put it in a ledger, and she was not asked to do that either, and I have never found out her name and I have looked, and I have told two people about her in nine years and one of them wrote it down. I am telling you because you are the only person in this district who writes things down about people who are not him, and because a letter is a thing somebody wrote and it is not a thing a basket should know about, and I have said my piece and I am going to go and be paid three shillings a week.”

---

The room above the chandler’s shop had four people in it at the fourth bell and the letter was on the table and three of them could not read it.

That is the shape of everything in that room and it is now the shape of the district, and the letter is the first object that has arrived in that room which could not be used at all by anybody except one person, and the one person was not asked to open it, and the room waited.

“From a counting-house in the city, four miles off,” said Osa Trin, turning it about the way she turns everything. “And it is not to the bench. It is to *the district of Ryefold*, and it has a woman’s name at the bottom of it, and she has written *four sheets sworn at your own bench*, and she has read our page.”

Nobody in that room said anything for about nine seconds.

“Read it out,” said Perrin Gant.

And she read it out, standing up, with her thumb under the line, twice, the way she reads everything, and this is the whole of it:

> THE DISTRICT OF RYEFOLD. FOUR SHEETS, SWORN AT YOUR OWN BENCH ON THE TWENTY-SECOND DAY OF THE FORTY-THIRD WEEK, THE FOURTH OF THEM IN A HAND THAT IS NOT THE BENCH’S.
>
> I HAVE READ IT. I AM NOT WRITING TO ASK FOR IT TO BE TAKEN OUT, AND I AM NOT WRITING ABOUT ANYTHING IN IT THAT A CLERK AT THIS COUNTER COULD DO ANYTHING WITH, AND I HAVE SAID THAT FIRST SO THAT YOU WILL KNOW WHAT THIS IS.
>
> WHAT I AM WRITING ABOUT IS THE FOURTH SHEET. IT SAYS THAT THE BURDEN OF THE CLAIM IS NAMED IN FOUR HANDS AND FOUR PLACES AND THAT NONE OF THE FOUR HAS AGREED WITH ANY OF THE OTHERS. THAT IS TRUE. IT IS THE ONLY TRUE THING ON THE FOURTH SHEET, AND IT IS THE ONLY LINE IN FOUR YEARS THAT I HAVE BEEN ASKED TO ENTER IN THE BOOK AT THIS COUNTER AGAINST A DISTRICT’S OWN PAGE.
>
> A CLERK IN THIS OFFICE WILL HAVE TO ENTER, IN ABOUT NINE MONTHS FROM NOW, WHICH OF THE FOUR CARRIES IT.
>
> I AM ASKING THE BENCH WHICH OF THE FOUR CARRIES IT. IF THE BENCH CANNOT SAY, I WILL ENTER THAT THE BENCH CANNOT SAY. THAT IS A THING THAT CAN BE ENTERED, AND I WOULD RATHER ENTER IT THAN LEAVE IT BLANK, AND THAT IS ALL THIS IS.
>
> N. LUND. THIRTY-FOUR. A CLERK, NOT A PERSON OF THIS DISTRICT AND NOT AN OFFICER OF THE ACCOUNT.

---

And the finding is short and it is the shape of the whole of this turned over one more time, and it arrived by post in a basket, and nobody in that room had to do anything except be a bench of four that had sworn a page on a day it appointed because a stranger told them a page was for being legible and nobody asked him a single question about it.

Perrin Gant said the thing about the bin, and he said it himself, and nobody else in that room would have remembered it.

“I said that,” he said. “I said on the day I swore it that it would go to a counter in a city and the man at the counter would read it in about a minute and put it in a bin, and that it would be a thing in a bin for four years, and I said I knew that, and I said I had done it anyway, and I said the reason was not courage.” He put his hand flat on the shut book. “It did not go in a bin. It took three weeks. A man read it in about a minute and he did not put it in a bin, he wrote back, and he is not a person of this district and he is not an officer of the account and he is a clerk of thirty-four who says the only true thing about our page is the line where we told the truth about ourselves not agreeing, and he has used that line to ask us a question.”

Nobody said anything.

“Three weeks,” said Gant. “From the day it went up the lane to a basket. Twenty-five days. And I have been sitting in this room for twenty-five days telling anybody who asked that a page is a thing that goes into a bin, and I was wrong about the bin, and I would like it noticed that being wrong about the bin is the best thing that has happened to me in nineteen years and that I cannot do one single thing with it.”

Then Wil Tamworth, who is thirty-three and asks what a number means, asked the number.

“Four hands,” he said. “And we swore it. Gant, we swore it. Do we know which four?”

“We are going to find out,” said Perrin Gant. “That is the whole of the answer and I am sixty-one and it is the first time in nineteen years that I have found out something about our own paper in a letter from a stranger, and the finding is a finding and I am calling it one, and it is this: the truest sentence on the fourth sheet is the one about the four hands not agreeing, and that sentence is the only reason a clerk four miles off has written to us, and it is going to be the only reason a clerk in this office enters something in about nine months. We wrote down the truth about ourselves and it was read, and the reading of it has cost us the only thing a district of six hundred people cannot afford, which is not being asked a question it cannot answer.”

“And we cannot answer it,” said Nevin Hake. “That is the part. Three of the four of us cannot read what we swore. So the four of us are being asked a question about a piece of paper and three of us have never seen the piece of paper, and the fourth has, and I am not going to say the fourth’s name because the fourth is in the room and the fourth can hear me and if I say it I am doing to her the thing this district has been doing to her for nineteen years, which is putting her in the middle of it and calling it an honour.”

Nobody said the name.

And Osa Trin said the thing that has to be said about a letter of that kind, and she said it flatly, and it took her about nine seconds, and she did not put it down afterwards, and the chapter is where it is because there is nowhere else for it to be.

“He cannot take it back,” she said. “That is what I want said about it and I want it said by a person and not by a form. The man who wrote that letter has a book with our page’s name in it and in about nine months a clerk in his office will have to put one line against it, and whatever the bench says to him in the meantime, or does not say, he will put one line. And we can all of us, to a man, get up in a room and say we did not know we had sworn that, and it will not touch the book, because a bench of four cannot un-swear a thing, and because we swore it, and because we would not want to.”

She put the letter down on the table, flat, beside the shut book.

“He has not asked us to do anything,” she said. “That is what is in it and it took him four paragraphs to say it and every paragraph of it is a man being careful with something he has no right to be careful with. He has not asked us for the page. He has not asked for it to be taken off the nail. He has asked us a question that we cannot answer, in about nine months, and there is no form for not answering it, and there is no hurry.”

“Then there is no hurry,” said Perrin Gant. “Put it in the book. Not on the nail — the nail has got three things on it and it is a nail and I said so in this room in the forty-third week before anybody had said a word about it, and I am not having a letter from a city four miles off going on a nail in a room where four people have lived for nineteen years and one of them can read.”

He wrote it in with the date, in his own hand, on the page after the thirty-four names, and he could not read what he had written and he has never once been able to read what he has written, and there is exactly one person in that room who can read his hand and she read it back and did not correct one word of it and was not thanked and did not expect to be.

Nobody in that room has to answer that letter today. The first of the forty-eighth week is in nine days.

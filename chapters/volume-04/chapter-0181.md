# Chapter 181: A Discretion Is Not A Refusal

A district of six hundred people was holding a granary and a bank of the second mouth on something that had stopped running, and the sheet that said so was four lines long and had been on a nail in a room above a chandler’s shop for thirty-seven days, and nobody had taken it down and nobody had put anything under it.

The tenancy ended on the first day of the forty-second week. The renewal is at the discretion of the account. There is no line on the sheet saying how long a discretion runs, and the fourth line points at a building six miles up the road that goes north, and nobody in that district had been inside that building for anything except a deposition.

Here is the whole of it, and it is the whole of it because it is four lines and it is printed and the district has had it since the first day of the forty-second week:

> THE LEASE OF THE GRANARY AND THE BANK OF THE SECOND MOUTH IN THE DISTRICT OF RYEFOLD IS ENDED AS DATED. THE RENEWAL IS AT THE DISCRETION OF THE ACCOUNT.
> THE PRICE OF A RENEWAL IS ONE FORTIETH OF EVERYTHING IN THE GRANARY, PAYABLE IN GRAIN AND NOT IN MONEY, AND BEFORE THE CUT.
> A DISTRICT MAY NOT SELL THIS LEASE.
> THE TERMS ON WHICH THE DISCRETION IS EXERCISED ARE HELD AT THE OFFICE OF THIS ACCOUNT AND MAY BE HAD ON APPLICATION.

That is the sheet. Perrin Gant had had it off the nail and held it up to the window four times in the forty-third week and it is four lines and there is no fifth, and the fourth of the four is a door, and the door is six miles up a road that goes north, and there is no form anywhere in this county for asking a person to open a door of that kind.

---

They went down the lane at about the second bell on the third day of the forty-seventh week because there was nothing else to do with a morning, and they went the three of them who could leave a quay: a convenor of sixty-one, a carter of forty-six, and a man of thirty-one with a basket he was not going anywhere with.

The granary door was open and the reeve was inside it with a broom in her hand and about seven thousand loads behind her.

Marda Fen put the broom against the wall when she saw the three of them, which she does not do for anybody, and she said, “Say it in here and not in the lane, because in here I can answer it and in the lane I would be guessing in front of the carts.”

So they said it in there. Hake said it, because Hake is the one of the three who says a thing out loud, and he said it flatly and without any of his own opinion in it, which is a thing he can do and does about twice a year.

“We have a granary and a bank of the second mouth and about seven thousand loads in it and six hundred people, and the thing that lets us have them has stopped running, and nobody has told us it has and nobody has told us it has not.”

“All right,” said Marda Fen. “Two things, and I am going to say them both and you can do what you like with them.”

She put her hand on the back of a bin.

“The first one is that a discretion is not a refusal. I have had a discretion over one thing or another in this building for twenty-two years. I have never once been given one in writing. I have never once known how long it ran. I have never once been refused anything, and I have never once been given anything either, and I have carried forty-one loads out of my own authority in nine years and got nothing for it and been asked about it by nobody.” She moved the hand to the next bin. “So if you have come to tell me the lease is gone, you have not come to tell me the lease is gone, and I am saying that as a woman who has been in this room for twenty-two years and not as anybody’s clerk.”

Nobody said anything.

“The second one is worse and it is the one I want in the book.” She turned round. “A thing that has run out has not been taken off you. I have thought about that for two days and I have got it exactly where I want it. Nobody has come. That is what a thing being taken off looks like — somebody comes, and there is a letter, and there is a man at the door with a form, and it is done. Nobody has come down this lane. There is one door to this building and I shut it at the seventh bell and I read the board before I shut it, and that is the whole of the protection this granary has got, and it is mine, and it has been mine since before the three of you were on this quay.”

She picked the broom up again.

“So the granary is not lost. The granary is held. And held is not the same as had, and I have been holding it since the seventh day of the forty-sixth week and nobody has noticed the difference, and I am telling you the difference now because you are three people standing in my doorway and one of you cannot read and you are all going to go and do something with it.”

---

The price of it was the thing they could all do something with, and it took about nine minutes and it was done in the ordinary way with a chalk and the back of a bin-board, and Marda Fen did the sum and said every figure out loud as she went, which is what she does.

“A fortieth of what is in here now. About seven thousand. That is a hundred and seventy-five loads.”

“Two hundred and seventy-five in the forty-third week,” said Nevin Hake.

“That was a fortieth of eleven thousand and it is on the sheet on the nail upstairs and nobody has taken it off the nail, and that is the whole of the arithmetic and it is nine minutes of it.” She rubbed the chalk out with her thumb. “So the lease has got about a hundred loads cheaper since the forty-third week, and about a hundred loads is fifteen pounds, and I am not going to stand here and be pleased about that.”

“Why not,” said Perrin Gant.

“Because I have been in this building for twenty-two years and the price has never once been a thing that got cheaper by the district using the thing it was paying for.” She put the chalk in her apron. “If the lease is a fortieth of what is in the granary, and the granary empties when we sell, and the carts we sell it with are not ours, then the price of the lease is worked out on a number that somebody else’s carts have already made smaller, and every year that goes on the price goes down and nothing else does, and I have not got a word for that and I have had two days.”

He put his hand flat on the bin.

“Write it as about,” he said. “Both numbers are about. Eleven thousand was about and seven thousand is about and a hundred and seventy-five is about and a hundred is about, and a yard is a bad place in which to do arithmetic and a bin-board is worse. If anybody ever wants the exact figure it is in a book six miles up the road that goes north, and this lane has never had it in it and it is not going to get it this week.”

“Then the bench will have the about,” said Nevin Hake.

“The bench will have the about and I will keep the exact one out of it, and if that is the wrong way round then I am the wrong way round and I have been the wrong way round for nine years.”

---

Osa Trin came down the lane at about the fourth bell with a slate under her arm, and she had not been sent for and nobody asked her for anything, and she stood in the doorway of the second door with the reeve’s board behind her and a hundred and forty loads in the bin and about forty carts standing in the yard because the cut was over and there is nothing for carts to do until the next one.

She said the thing that made the morning into something, and she said it in about four sentences, standing up, with her sleeve pushed back, and she did not look at the man with the basket once while she said it.

“The application for the first of the forty-eighth week has to be made by a person of this district, and it has to be signed by that person, and the person has to stand in a room four miles off on the day. That is five days from this morning.” She put the slate against the door frame. “I can do all three of those things and I am not going to, and I would like the reason said out loud in this doorway this morning so that nobody in this lane has to work it out on their own, and then I am going back up to the room and I am not coming down here again today.”

Nobody said anything.

“I have been the person who reads things in this district since I was nineteen. I am the person of this district. If I sign the application then this district has one woman in it who is in every matter that this district ever has, and there is no bench, and there is not a bench’s worth of difference between a bench of four and one woman with a slate, and every one of the four of them will tell you that in a doorway inside a week.” She took the slate back off the frame. “And the second thing is worse. If I sign it and I am stood up in that room and asked a question about six hundred people, then the answer to that question is a thing I have read out loud off a board in a building with one door, and about two hundred and forty of us are on that board, and about nine names on a second sheet did not come, and there is no column anywhere in this county for a person who is on a board and is not. So the person who stands in that room is a person whose only instrument is a list of names and the only person who can read the list is the one who would have to be in the room with them.”

She picked her sleeve back down.

“I am not a door. I have been one for nineteen years and I would like it written down somewhere that I know it and I have not been able to stop.”

---

And then the man of forty-six said it, in the lane, outside the second door, to about nine people and the carts, and nobody had asked him, and the man with the basket had not been allowed to ask him, and that is the whole of how it happened and it is the first time in this district that the practice has been done to the man who invented it.

“I will make it,” said Nevin Hake.

He said it flatly and he went on, and he gave the price before he gave the fact, which is a thing he has learned off a man of thirty-four and has never once said so.

“I am not going to be asked anything about this and if anybody asks me anything about it I am going to say I said it first. Here is the price. I cannot read.”

He put his hand flat on the door frame, which is a reeve's door frame and not his, and there is nobody in this district he would not put a hand on.

“I said in this room in the forty-first week that I could not read a form and had never been able to and I have not sat in this bench eleven years to say it a second time. Gant did it in the forty-third week. He signed an application in the room upstairs that he could not read, and he said out loud in that room that he would answer for the whole of it in a room four miles off to a man he had never met, on her word, and on the thirty-fifth day of that month a boy of nineteen read a piece of paper out in a room four miles off and this district lost a year on one word, and the word was not what did it. The signatory did it. Gant has said that out loud himself and he was right and nobody has improved on it since.”

Nobody in the lane said anything, and about nine people were standing in it, and the carts were standing in the yard behind them doing nothing.

“So I am going to do the exact thing that man did, and I know what it cost, and I am going to do it in five days, and I am not going to be one of the reasons it does not get done.” He took his hand off the frame. “And I will tell you the reason and then I am going to stop talking and go and move a hundred loads of my own, because that is a day and I have not got it. The reason is that a district of six hundred people can only be a person in a room one way, and that way is the reader, and the reader has just told us in this doorway what that costs and what it is, and if the only people who can sign anything in this district are the people who can read anything in this district, then this district is one woman and a slate, and in a year there will be a second one of me and in nine years there will be a bench of four and every one of them will be the reader.”

He looked at Gant.

“You asked me eight days ago, in that room, whether the bench would be a party, and I told you it was one of us, and I have been standing in this lane for about a minute working out that I meant it and did not know that I meant it.” He put his hands in his coat. “I am not asking for a day. A day is a day. I am telling this lane what a day is so that nobody in it can be surprised in five days, and then the four of us upstairs are going to have to find out whether one of us can be a person for a day without the district losing another year, and that is tomorrow, and it is not this morning, and I am going to move a hundred loads.”

He went down the lane to the yard.

---

The reeve read the board before she shut the second door, which is what she does, and the figure on it was the same figure as yesterday’s and it will be the same figure tomorrow and it is the figure that has been on that wall in chalk since the fourth day of the forty-first week, and there is a man in this district who has not worked out for about six weeks why a man who cannot read has a line on a wage board and there is nobody in that lane who is ever going to tell him.

> VESTER. NINE PENCE. THE THIRD OF THE FORTY-SEVENTH.

And behind her, in a building with about seven thousand loads in it and one pair of hands and four hundred carts in the yard, a reeve of fifty-four took a broom and went along the front of the bins and counted what was in them, because she counts it every day and has counted it every day for twenty-two years, and it is the same figure it was yesterday, and it will be the same figure tomorrow, and it will be a different figure on the first day of the next cut, and there is nobody in this district who has any idea what that figure is worth and there is a man six miles up the road that goes north who does.

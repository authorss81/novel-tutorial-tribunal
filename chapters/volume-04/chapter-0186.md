# Chapter 186: What A Room Does With A True Thing

The second day of the forty-eighth week was the first day in about seven weeks that a yard in this district had four hundred carts standing in it with nothing to do, and about four hundred men standing about in the yards and at the gates and on the wall above the weigh, waiting for a cut that is not coming until next year, and there is no other week in a grain year that looks like that week.

That is the whole of what the week is for, and it is the reason what happened in the room above the chandler's shop on the second day of the forty-eighth week was going to happen, and about nine people were in the room, and the fire was out, because a fire in that room is lit when somebody in it is cold and nobody in that room was cold.

Nobody had called a meeting. The room was full anyway, and that is a different thing, and everybody in the lane knows the difference.

“Go on,” said Perrin Gant. “You read it out yesterday and I have not understood a word of it and I have not slept and I want it read again.”

“There is nothing of mine to read,” said Osa Trin. “It is in a minute, in a room four miles off, in a clerk's hand, and you did not get a copy because a copy of a minute is fourpence a sheet and this district is at nothing, and I have not got sixteen pence either, so there is no copy and there is you and there is me and there is what he said.”

He squared it against the edge of the table without opening it, because the number he wanted was in his own head and the book was not.

“Then read it out of your own head.”

“I have had it in my head since the sixth bell yesterday and I have been walking home and back and I have not got it in the right order and I am not going to do it out of my head in a room, because the four sentences that matter are four sentences about a number and a number out of a head in a room is a thing this district has been doing for nineteen years and it is how a book says *no advice was given* about four hundred decisions.” She turned round to the room. “So somebody is going to have to say it out loud in this room from their own head, and I am not going to, and I do not know who is going to.”

Four people looked at each other in a room with no fire in it.

Nevin Hake said it. It took him about four minutes and he had it in the right order and he got every figure right, and he was in that room eight hours after standing in one four miles off and being believed, and he had not written any of it down because he could not read what he had written and there was nobody in that building to read it to him, and he had got it right anyway, and there are four hundred and sixty-three forms in a district eleven miles off a bridge that put a stranger’s name in a line he could not check, and this is not one of them, and he knows that.

Then nobody said anything for about nine seconds, and the thing that happened next happened in a doorway and it was said by a man who had said four hundred times in his life that he wanted a piece of paper and had never been given one.

---

Ilger Rusk had been in this district for eleven days past the end of the cut.

He said why at the door before anybody asked, because he is a man who has said the price of his own trade out loud in a yard and has not stopped, and what he said was that a factor's business does not stop when a cut does, that he has carts in three counties and about two hundred of them, and that he is waiting out the last of it, and that the room is where the thing he wants would be if it existed, and that it does not exist, and that he had not gone home.

“You read it out,” he said, to Hake. “And every word of it is true, and I have been trying to get that list for four years, and I am going to say something now that I have never said to a bench of four, and then I am going to go back to the quay and stand in it.”

Nobody stopped him.

“The nine hundred went out and three hundred and seventy came back with a name on them. Five hundred and thirty came back with nothing on them or did not come back at all. Of those, about three hundred and seventy came back blank and about one hundred and sixty did not come back at all.” He had the figures in his mouth and they were his, and he had had them off a yard, and he was not going to be corrected and he was not going to be a villain about it. “Three hundred and thirty-nine of the three hundred and seventy are a man with one cart. Thirty-one are a man with more. That is my number, it is the Chancellor's number, he keeps the register himself and has no clerk on it, and there is no other count of it in the world. About nine thousand in issue on the Shelf. Every one of the three hundred and seventy has got a cart.”

“Marget Oad and Wilfs Cane and Bain Rayne and about thirty others have given up their second carts,” said Perrin Gant, from the book, without opening it. “Thirty-four of them, in nine counties. Seventeen thousand loads. I wrote the names in on the sixth day of the forty-fifth week and I have read them out of my own book twice since and I did not correct one of them because there was nothing in it to correct.”

“Right,” said Ilger Rusk. “That is the largest quantity of released carriage anybody in this county has produced in two years and this district needed a hundred loads of it, and it is a hundred and seventy times what it needed, and it was in the wrong county, and it was two days after the cut, and a loaded cart cannot go on a towpath in the afternoon. I have said all of that in this room once and I am not going to say it again.” He put his hands behind his back. “So here is what a room does with a true thing.”

He looked at the four of them and then at the door.

“It does nothing with it. That is the finding and it has taken me four years and about nine hundred letters to get to it and I am not going to pretend I am pleased. You can read a number out in a room. You cannot hand a number to anybody. And the only thing a number can be turned into is a list of people, and a list of people is nine hundred doors, and there are about nine hundred yards in this district and about four hundred carters in them, and a door is a man with a cart on the other side of it who has been out since the first bell and is going to be out again at the ninth and has four people and about a quarter of a pound of flour in the house.”

He stopped and nobody filled it in.

“Nine doors a day, in a lane this size, is a hundred days. And a hundred days of the only price this district writes down for a day is three pounds fifteen. And a hundred days cannot be done by a man with a cart, because the hundred days are inside a cut and a man with one cart in this district moves about four hundred loads in a cut, which is sixty pounds, and that is his year.” He looked at the room. “So the price of a list of the three hundred and thirty-nine is one man's year, or one man's cart, and three pounds fifteen, and this district is at nothing and has been since the sixth day of the forty-third week, and there is no one hundredth person. I have wanted that list for four years. I am not going to get it, and I am not going to get it this week when the four hundred men in the yards have got nothing to do, which is the best week anybody in this district has ever had for it and is the only week in nine years.”

“Why,” said Wil Tamworth, who is thirty-three and asks what a number means, and who had been given things to do that mattered for nine days and had this one.

“Because a list of people is a number with the people still in it,” said Ilger Rusk, “and I have been asking for the number for four years and there are about nine of them in this county who have it, and I have had thirty-four names off a trestle in a good coat’s handwriting in this room eleven days ago and I cannot get one of the rest off anybody, and thirty-four is not a list. You cannot get a name out of a number by asking the number. You have to go and stand in front of the man, and there are nine hundred of them, and about nine a day, and a hundred days, and I am one, and I have carts in three counties and no list, and I am not going to be a hundredth person.”

And then Marda Fen said the thing that made it the finding of the week, and she said it from the doorway with her sleeve rolled up, and she had come up to say one thing about the beam and had stayed, and she is fifty-four and has been in that granary for twenty-two years.

“You have all been talking about a class,” she said. “About four hundred men who have got a cart, and about three hundred and thirty-nine, and about nine thousand on the Shelf, and about a queue that has not happened. You have been talking about it as though it were a thing in a book that a man in a good coat wrote down, and it is not, and I have been in this lane for about nine minutes and I am the only person in this room who has to make a bad week happen with a piece of chalk and a pair of hands.”

She put her hand flat on the door frame.

“There are about eleven thousand loads in that building at the start of a year and about seven thousand at the end of one. About three thousand four hundred of them go out in the cut and about three thousand one hundred of those go on carts that belong to somebody else, and about three hundred go on four carts that belong to this district. That is the class. That is what a class is in this district: it is the sum of what went on other people's carts, and there is no column for it anywhere in this county, and I have been the one who wrote down about eleven thousand and about seven thousand every day for twenty-two years, and I have never once had anybody ask me what a load was worth.”

“Three shillings,” said Ilger Rusk.

“That is the register's figure and it is not mine,” said Marda Fen. “Mine is what the beam says and the beam says loads. And the difference between your three shillings and the account's two and eight is fourpence, and I have known the two numbers since the sixth day of the forty-third week and I have never once known what the fourpence was, and I have thought about it every day for about two weeks and I have got one answer and it is the wrong one and I am going to say it anyway because I am tired of not saying it.”

Nobody in that room stopped her.

“One shilling and fourpence is a sheet,” said Marda Fen. “Three shillings is what your register calls a load and two and eight is what that book up the road calls a load, and the difference between your two loads is fourpence. So a sheet is four times the difference, and I do not know why it is four times and not once, and that is the exact place I have been stuck for two weeks, and I have been stuck at it on my own in a building where nobody asks me anything.” She took her hand off the frame. “And I have thought for about two weeks that the reason this district can be short a hundred loads and cannot say it out loud in a room is that a sheet and a load have got the same number in them somewhere and nobody has ever read the two books at the same time. And I am probably wrong, because a boy of nineteen has read them at the same time and come up with something else, and he is nineteen and I am fifty-four and he read them right.”

“Then why did you say it,” said Wil Tamworth.

“Because it is a true thing I have been thinking about on my own for two weeks in a building where nobody asks me anything, and there is a rule in this district about people saying true things in rooms and the rule is that you say them and then you find out whether they are any good.” She picked her sleeve back down. “They are not any good. It is four in the afternoon and there are about four hundred men in those yards and about nine hundred doors and a hundred days, and I have said a true thing and it has not moved one of them, and that is what a true thing is worth in a district with a class in it instead of a person in it. It gets said. That is all it gets. It gets said in a room, and then everybody goes back to the beam.”

---

Nobody argued with her and one man in a good coat agreed with her and did not say so, and wrote four figures on the back of a form and put the form in his bag, which is what he did in this room eleven days ago and is a thing he is going to be doing for a while.

And the man with the basket, who had carried one thing for fourteen days and had not been able to open a second one, and had spent half an hour in a lane in the middle of that fortnight trying to make room and had made none, said the only thing he said all afternoon, and he said it standing up at the end of the table with his back to the window.

“I want to say what I have not got room for, because it is in the room now and it is about me and not about the class, and I would rather say it than have it found out in a year.”

A room with about nine people in it did what that room does, which is to go quiet.

“There is a thing about the three hundred and thirty-nine that I have wanted to open since the fifth day of the forty-sixth week, in a lane, in half an hour, and did not open it then and cannot open it now, and the reason is not that it is hard and the reason is not that nobody would help me with it. The reason is that I am carrying a thing, and while I am carrying it there is room for one thing and not for two, and there is nobody on the other side of that to be unkind to. It is a pair of hands.” He put his hands on the table and took them off again. “So the class in this room is the one class in this county that has an instrument pointed at it, and the instrument is a boy of nineteen, and the boy is in a building six miles up a road and there is one of him and he is nineteen and he is not sorry. And I cannot ask him for a second thing while this one is open, and I am not going to ask him, and that is the cost and it is not anybody's fault and it is not going to be paid today.”

Nobody in that room said one word about that for about four seconds, and then Perrin Gant shut the book and put the key in his pocket, which is a thing he does, and the room emptied the way that room empties, which is four people at a time and no farewells.

And at about the seventh bell a reeve of fifty-four shut the second door of a granary after reading a board, and there were about four hundred carts in the yard behind her with nothing to do for another year, and about seven thousand loads in the building behind that, and a beam, and a bin-board, and a figure on it that is the same figure it was yesterday, and it is going to be the same figure tomorrow, and there is a man in this district who has been paid nine pence a day for about seven weeks and has never once asked anybody what it is for and has a leg that has not been his own since a morning four hundred miles and some weeks behind him, and he is carrying one thing, and the thing he cannot do while he is carrying it is be useful to a second person, and the granary shut and the beam is there in the morning.

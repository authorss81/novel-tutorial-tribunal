# Chapter 163: What the Page Is For

There are two doors on the quay at Ryefold and the second one is the one people come to, and the second one has a wage board on the wall outside it, and on the sixth day of the forty-third week, just after the fourth bell, a boy of thirteen put a number on that board that is not on it now and never was again.

That is not how it was meant. About thirty people on a quay that morning saw a boy write something on a board, and by the fifth bell it was a thing that a district had done, and by the sixth it was a thing that two men in a room four miles off could have found out about in a week.

It went like this.

---

The claim came back.

Ormond Ketch is fifty-one and he came eleven miles and brought the same boy and he came on the sixth day of the forty-third week at the third bell with a sheet in a leather pocket, and he put the sheet on the second door's step rather than in the hand of the reeve, which was a thing he had thought about on the way and which nobody noticed.

“I have been in this district once,” he said. “On the sixth day of the forty-second week, and I waited on that quay from the third bell to the sixth and thirty-one loads went on my cart in the daylight with about two hundred and forty people standing there, and nobody stole anything and nobody was a thief and I went home and I have thought about it for ten days.”

“You were first,” said Marda Fen. “You were first and I told you so and I was right.”

“You were first,” said Ormond Ketch. “And I have not moved, and I am not going to move, and I have not come to take it out of anybody. I have come because a man on that quay asked me a question I did not have an answer to, and I have been thinking about his question for ten days and I have got one now, and I am not saying he was right, because I have not seen him again and I do not know his name and he did not give it.”

“And the question?”

“He asked whether my claim was against the account or against the district’s grain,” said Ormond Ketch. “And I have got an answer and it is on the sheet in my pocket and it has been on the sheet since the thirty-second week of last year and I have never once read that part of it.”

---

The sheet is four hundred and six loads at the county rate, and the county rate on that sheet is two shillings and eightpence a load, and it is a true sheet and the figure is four hundred and six loads because that is what the man had carried for the account in a hard season and the sheet says so and the county rate is the county rate.

Four hundred and six loads at two shillings and eightpence a load is fifty-four pounds two shillings and eightpence, and the rate is the county's own rate off the register and not anybody's figure, and it is the only number in that building that anybody is going to have to use twice before the week is out.

Dace Ferrow is fifty-seven and he has a wheelwright's shop two streets off that quay and he has been building carts in this county for forty-one years and he is the fourth cart on that list of four that the district owns, and he was in the second door at the third bell that morning because he has a wheel out of one of them and because he has waited since the twenty-second week of last year to be paid for a job and the reeve is eleven weeks out of wages and had told him so in person in a yard.

He listened to about four minutes of it and then said it, standing up, with a spoke in his hand, and about nine people heard him.

“A claim does not say what it wants,” said Dace Ferrow. “A claim says what it is owed. And what it is owed is owed out of the account, and the account is a thing, and there is money in the thing this month, and there is no line on that sheet that says grain. I have been in this trade forty-one years and I have stood in about nine hundred granaries and I have never once heard a man say *grain* out loud when he was owed money, and the ones who did say it were saying it because they were standing in a granary and not in a room, and they said it in about the tone of voice you would use for weather.”

“They said it because the money is not there,” said Marda Fen.

“They said it because the money is not there,” said Dace Ferrow, “which is a different sentence and it has taken this county about four hundred years to get to the same place, and I am not complaining about the four hundred years, I am telling you what they sound like, and they sound like weather.”

---

Here is the whole of what happened in the next eleven minutes and there is nothing in it that any of the four hundred carters in about nine hundred yards would have needed to be told.

Marda Fen wrote it out. She wrote it on the back of the claim sheet because she had nothing else to write it on, and she wrote it in her own hand, and it took her about four minutes, and she read it out to the room before she signed it and she asked Dace Ferrow twice whether she had it right and he said yes both times and he said the second time that she had it right the first time too and she told him not to be kind in her doorway.

Then she went into the office that is not an office — a cupboard with a desk in it, nine years and no more of a room than that — and she took nine pounds out of a box in the form of a purse that a clerk had sent her, and she counted it out on the second door's step in front of about thirty people, and she gave it to a carter of fifty-one.

“Nine pounds,” she said. “That is all of it. And the rest of you is forty-five pounds two shillings and eightpence, and it is to a year, and you may have the paper, and I have put a line on the back of your own sheet saying that nine pounds has been had and forty-five pounds two shillings and eightpence waits, and I have signed it and the date is today, and there is nobody in this district I can send it with and it is going in your own pocket.”

“The cart,” said Ormond Ketch.

“Goes back empty,” said Marda Fen. “There is not one load on it and there is not one load going on it, and I have made a note of that in the same hand, because in about a year somebody is going to say that I sent forty loads out on the sixth day of the forty-second week and I want the paper to say what I did on this one and they are different days and they are both me.”

He took the nine pounds and put it in the leather pocket with the sheet, and stood there.

“I am going to be honest with you,” he said, “because I have been sitting on a cart for ten days and I have earned the right to one straight thing. You have given me a third of it and you have given it to me in a form, and I would rather have had forty loads and no form, and there is a man on that quay who told me a thing for nothing and I have thought about his thing for ten days and I want you to know that he is not the reason I am here and he is not the reason I am not. I would have come on the sixth of the fortysixth week anyway.”

“Then I have not helped you and it has cost me nine pounds,” said Marda Fen, “and I would like the room to notice that I said that out loud before anybody else could, and then I would like the room to go and look at a wall.”

---

There were three hundred and ninety people on that quay by the second bell, and about a hundred and forty of them in the second door, and the whole of what that building knew afterwards was this.

The account's floor is not sold in slices by the person it belongs to. It is sold in slices by the person it is owed to, and the person it is owed to has never once, in four hundred years, had occasion to do it in money, because the money is not there, and everybody in about nine hundred yards has assumed grain so long that the assumption is now the practice, and there is no form anywhere in this county that says which, and the man who is owed is handed the form and does not read the part that would have told him he had a choice.

And the district's grain did not go out, and the ration list of two hundred and forty names was still whole that afternoon and would still be whole on the first day of the cut, and that is the only sentence anybody in that building could have said, and none of them said it: the man who is owed has still got forty-five pounds two shillings and eightpence outstanding, and he is not a thief, and he will be back in a year, and he is right.

And the district is at nothing.

---

And then Marda Fen said the thing, and it is worth setting down because everything else that day was arithmetic.

“In a corridor outside this door,” she said, “on the sixth day of the forty-second week, I said that four hundred carters have said that to each other in about nine hundred yards for four hundred years, and that it is not a finding. I said it to nobody, and it was not a finding, and I said it because a man had just found the one thing in this building I had not thought of in twenty-two years, and I wanted it noted that it was not a finding.”

Nobody said anything.

“It is now a thing that has been done,” said Marda Fen. “Once. To one claim. On the back of that man’s own sheet. And I have signed it and dated it, and there is no second copy of it, and I have told you the reason I am telling you, and the reason is that if the four hundred carters are going to be right about this then somebody is going to have to do it twice, and the second one will be in a bad week, and in a bad week I will be the one holding the pen, and I have never in twenty-two years written a line on this account that the account did not already have.”

“That is the first sensible thing anybody has said about a granary in my time,” said Dace Ferrow, “and I would like it noticed that I am a wheelwright and I do not have a vote and I am saying it anyway.”

---

And then the number went on the board, and this is the part of it that is in every record of this district and that nobody in this district understands to this day.

The board on the wall outside the second door is a wage board. It is chalk, and it is read before it is written, and it takes a name and a day and a sum, and it is the only instrument in this district that anybody can be a person in.

Somebody said the number out loud in the second door at about the fourth bell — four hundred and six, said by Marda Fen, in the tone of a woman who has been weighed grain since she was thirty-two, and she was putting the claim sheet away while she said it and she did not know she had said it out loud.

And the boy who carried the notice up the towpath, Alick Odell, thirteen, was in the doorway with the lamp-oil measure on his errand, and he has a piece of chalk, because a boy in a doorway in a granary always has a piece of chalk, because there is a board on that wall and it is the only thing in that lane worth writing on.

And he wrote it. Four hundred and six. Under Vester's line, under Brack's line, at the bottom of a wage board, in a hand of a boy of thirteen who cannot read.

He did not know whose name to put against it. He looked round. Marda Fen was in the office. The bench was not there. There were a hundred and forty people in that doorway and not one of them had been asked.

So he wrote, in the column where a name goes, the only word he could have put there and that anybody in that building can read:

> SIX CARTES. FOUR HUNDRED AND SIX.

Six carts was a thing he knew and could not spell, and he had had it in his mouth every working day of his two years on that towpath without ever once having had to write it down, and there were a hundred and forty people within reach of the wall and not one of them knew what was on it, and he did not ask anybody, and he has never been asked.

And the board was chalked and it was not read that day, because a wage board is read at the end of a week and it was not the end of a week, and it was wiped on the third day of the forty-fourth week by a girl of sixteen who was told to do it, and neither of those two children has ever been told what they did and neither of them will ever be told by anybody who was in that doorway.

Perrin Gant heard about it four days later from a carter, wrote it in the bench's own book in his own hand with the date, and did not put it on the nail, because he could not read it and had not had it read to him, and a nail in that room is for paper.

And the reason that matters is this: for four days there was a number on the wall of a granary in a district of six hundred people that said four hundred and six loads of that district's own grain had stayed in the building because a wheelwright had said a sentence in a doorway, and there is no copy of that number anywhere in the world, and the district cannot produce it, and if anybody ever asks Ryefold what it did on the sixth day of the forty-third week, the answer is a claim and a signature and a date, and the number is gone.

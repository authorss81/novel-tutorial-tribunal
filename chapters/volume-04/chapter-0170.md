# Chapter 170: A Deposition And A Year's Worth Of Cartage

The room above the chandler's shop had about eleven people in it at the fourth bell on the sixth day of the forty-fifth week, and Ilger Rusk came in with a sheet of paper and put it on the bench's own table face up, and there were thirty-four names on it in his own hand, and he had written it out himself, and he said so, and nobody in that room believed him for about nine seconds.

“They are in your hand,” said Wil Tamworth, who was thirty-three and who asked what a number meant.

“They are in my hand because I wrote them in my hand at four different tables in nine counties in nine days, on the ends of my own day's work, on the back of forms, on a slate, twice in a pencil that went down on a wet table,” said Ilger Rusk. “I did not write them out clean. If you want a clean copy there is a woman in a house off the Nethergill road who does copying and I have not asked her because she is fourpence a sheet and I have not got fourpence for four sheets, and that is the truth about this document and I have put it first before anybody in this room decides how much it is worth.”

“Why thirty-four.”

“Because thirty-four of them told me.” He put his hand flat on the sheet. “In nine days. Men and women with one cart who bought a sheet off me, or off a man I trained, or off my factor's widow in Nethergill, and who came to a table and said a thing to me, and I want the room to hear the shape of the thing, because the shape is the whole of it and it is not a confession and it is not an accusation and it is not a scheme.”

“Go on.”

“All thirty-four said the same three things in the same order,” said Ilger Rusk. “They said: I am not going to be here in a bad week. They said: I have got one cart and my one cart is going. And they said: I have not got a second cart and I have not been able to work out why I do not have a second cart, and I have not been able to work out why that is the thing I am sad about. Not one of them said the word *scam*. Not one of them said they had been cheated. Not one of them was angry at me and I have been angry at people for fourteen years and I know what angry sounds like at a table.”

---

He did the sums, and he did them in the room, out loud, on the back of a form, and he did not do them quickly and he did not do them for effect, and this is the whole of them and every figure in it can be checked by anybody in the room with a chalk.

“Three hundred and thirty-nine of the three hundred and seventy in this county are in the hands of a person with one cart,” he said. “That is the Chancellor's own number and he said it in a yard and I have not been able to improve on it. Three shillings a load is the register's own published scale and that is what a load costs to move, so that is what a sheet is valued at, and that is the only reason a sheet is worth anything at all. A man with one cart in a cut moves about four hundred loads. Four hundred loads at three shillings is sixty pounds. A man with a second cart moves about nine hundred. Nine hundred loads at three shillings is one hundred and thirty-five pounds. So a second cart is worth seventy-five pounds of carriage, and a sheet that lets a man with one cart be in front of a queue that has not happened is worth sixty pounds at the moment the queue happens, and it cost two shillings and eightpence, and the reason it cost two shillings and eightpence is that the man selling it is not selling you the thing.”

“And the thirty-four,” said Perrin Gant.

“The thirty-four have each unbooked a second cart for this cut.” He wrote it on the back of the form. “Each of them had a second cart in the air. That is five hundred loads apiece of capacity that is not coming. Thirty-four times five hundred is seventeen thousand loads.”

The room did a thing that is worth recording, because it is the only arithmetic anybody in that district has done in four years that produced a number bigger than the district.

“Seventeen thousand loads,” said Nevin Hake.

“Seventeen thousand loads,” said Ilger Rusk. “And this district has to move three thousand four hundred in this cut and has paid for three thousand of them and is a hundred loads short of having paid for the rest. A hundred loads is fifteen pounds and a quarter of one cart's season. So: thirty-four men have let go of seventeen thousand loads of carriage in nine counties, freely, in writing, with their names on it, and the district this morning is short a hundred, and the cut is in two days.”

Nobody said anything for a while.

“Now the part where I tell you why that is not a tragedy about anybody in this room,” he said, “and I have had nine days to get to the bottom of it and I have got to the bottom of it and the bottom of it is *because a load in one county is not a load in another county.* There is no instrument in this world that moves one. Those carts are eleven miles off and on the other side of a road that is not a towpath, and a loaded cart cannot go on a towpath in the afternoon, and I have known that since I was twenty-two and none of the thirty-four knew it when they told me, and two of them asked me afterwards how to send them here and I had to say it to their faces. Seventeen thousand loads. Wrong place. Correct capacity. The best possible number in the world, on the wrong side of a county, four days too late.”

He put the chalk down.

“That is why it is not a scheme and not a confession. I did not do that to them. Thirty-four men each did one sensible small thing on their own account, in private, about a thing that has not happened, and I sold all thirty-four of them the same thing and I told all thirty-four of them the truth. And the sum of thirty-four is nothing, and the sum of four hundred is a catastrophe, and the difference between those two numbers is not in anybody's arithmetic. It is in the day.”

---

And Perrin Gant asked the only question anybody asked in that room for about nine minutes, and he asked it in about eleven words, and he is sixty-one and he has been convener nineteen years and he has never once in nineteen years put a thing on a table that he did not know was true.

“Then what is the instrument,” he said, “for a sum of four hundred private things.”

Nobody answered him.

“We have this bench's book,” he said. “It is a good book. It is a better book than this district has ever had, and it has got a page in it that a man from a city told us is for being legible, and there is a copy of that page going up a towpath in a basket, and about nine months from now a clerk in a city is going to read it and write to a clerk in another county. So: a book. And I can put thirty-four names in it. I can put four hundred names in it. I can put the whole county in it inside a week, and I have four nails and a room and a woman who reads.”

He put his hand on the shut book.

“And I have been asking the room a question for about ten minutes and the answer is that a book is the only instrument any of us has for a sum of four hundred private things, and a book cannot go to Nethergill on one day and be a person in a lane on the next and ask a man whether he meant it. A book cannot stand in a door. A book cannot be argued with, and a book is not offended, and a book is not a person, and if I write four hundred names in this book then at the end of this year the only account of this district in the whole of Veyra is four hundred names in a room, and there is a number in a register that has got a name on it and it is not ours, and I said that in this room nine days ago and I have now said it twice.”

“Write them anyway,” said Nevin Hake.

“I am going to write them,” said Perrin Gant. “I have not got a better idea and I have had nine days and I am sixty-one and I am not going to be a convenor who has a better idea.”

He wrote them out. It took him about fifty minutes and he wrote them standing up, and Osa Trin read them back twice and did not correct one word, and when he asked her whether it was right she said, “It says what you have got,” and he said, “Then it is right, because it is all I have got,” and he shut the book.

And nobody in that room said the word *deposition*, and nobody in that room had any idea that a book and a deposition are the same sort of instrument, and that is a thing nobody in that room will ever find out, and it is the whole of what is on the last page of this week's work.

The block above is the first five lines and the last line, and the last line is in Ilger Rusk's hand and it is the only line on the sheet that is not a name, and he wrote it at four in the morning in a house off the Nethergill road because he could not sleep, and he has said so.

---

At about the seventh bell the room emptied the way that room empties, which is four people at a time and no farewells, and Perrin Gant shut the bench's book, and put the key in his pocket, and did not go down the lane first because he had been convener for nineteen years and had not once gone down a lane first.

And the boy of thirteen came up the lane with the lamp-oil measure and a piece of chalk and did not go into the room, because the room was shutting, and he stood at the bottom of the stair with the chalk in his hand for about eleven seconds and then went back down to the quay, because he has worked that lane for two years and he knows what a shut room looks like from the bottom of a stair.

And Marda Fen shut the second door of the granary at about the seventh bell, and read the board before she shut it, which is what she does.

> VESTER. NINE PENCE. THE SIXTH OF THE FORTY-FIFTH.

It was a day like every day. It was ninepence and it went on the board in chalk with a name against it, and the name was read before it was written, and the reeve read it and did not say anything about it, and it was the fortieth day of a man who had come off a boat on the second day of the fortieth week with four shillings and a leg and a bag, and who now had eleven pages in a penny exercise book, and a receipt for a sheet of paper he had sworn to and had never been able to read, and a name on a wage board in a district he had never been in, and the right of refusal, which is a thing he has never been able to offer anybody, and a rule about a category, which is a thing he said to a clerk about himself in the abstract in a city four miles off, and which has cost a district of six hundred people a year.

He wrote on the twelfth page, in a cart shed, at about the ninth bell, and it is four lines and the last two are the two lines.

*Forty days on this shelf. Thirty-four men gave up seventeen thousand loads of carriage in nine counties and it is a hundred loads too many for this district and it is two days too late, and the difference between those two numbers is not anybody's arithmetic.*

*A page is the only instrument I have ever been able to get anybody to use, and a page cannot go to Nethergill on one day and stand in a lane on the next, and a page is not a person, and I said in a yard four days ago that a document cannot stand in a door, and a document cannot, and I have been a document for four days.*

*It is mine to write down and it is not mine to decide.*

And the reason on that page is the reason he could stand behind in a room, and it is not the reason, and the two lines at the end are the two lines that are on the other eleven.

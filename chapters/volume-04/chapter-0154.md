# Chapter 154: The Bench of Four

The bench of Ryefold sits in two rooms above a chandler’s shop at the top of the lane that goes up off the quay, and it has sat there since before anybody alive, and on the third day of the forty-first week it had four members in it and all four of them were in.

It is four miles from the Orison court, and eleven from the ninth bridge, and seven from Nethergill, and about six from a low street of counting-houses with a building in it that has no name on the door. It is not on the way to anything. That is the whole of its geography and it is the reason nothing has ever happened to it.

“Right,” said Perrin Gant. “You have been in this district a week and you have carried a basket up a towpath two days and you have told me so before I asked about it, and she says you asked to be paid and you did not want to be paid out of the district’s money, and I have been convenor of this bench nineteen years and in that time I have had two people say a thing like that to me and one of them was trying to be clever.”

“I am not a person of this district.”

“No,” said Perrin Gant. “You are not. That is the first useful thing anybody has said in this room in about four years and I would like the room to notice that I have said *useful* and not *right*, because there is a difference and I am sixty-one and I have never been able to keep them apart in my own mouth and I have got four years.”

He was sixty-one and he had kept the bench’s own book for nineteen years and it was in a cupboard and it was the only book in the room that anybody in the room could not read, and he had never once said so out loud, and it was the reason he had said *useful*.

The other three were there. Osa Trin, thirty-nine, the bench’s second, who did the notices and could write a form and who had four nails and no glass and who was the only one of the four who could read a room. Nevin Hake, forty-six, the third, who had been a carter before he was a bench and who had the hands for it and who was out of his depth in grain and said so at half past six seconds after he came in. Wil Tamworth, thirty-three, the fourth, who was two years younger than Perrin Gant had been when he took the seat and who had asked in his first year for the bench to be given something to do that mattered.

“You are not a person of this district and you are not a party to anything on this shelf and you have no standing and you have no licence, and I know all of that,” said Perrin Gant, “because I asked the clerk of the court a question by post about a man with a bad leg who walked into a matter in this city about nine years ago and got a letter back that was four sentences long and unhelpful, and I have kept that letter, and I am telling you that I know, so that you cannot spend the next hour telling me.”

“That is fair,” said Ilyan Vester.

“Then here is the offer and I am going to make it once and in the plainest words I have and you are at liberty to refuse it and I am not going to be disappointed and I am not going to ask you again in different words.”

He put both hands flat on the bench’s own book, which was shut.

“Whatever is going to happen about the lease, this bench is going to have to swear something in this room, in public, on a day this bench appoints, in our own hand, on our own page. It is not a new thing. It is the oldest thing there is and it is four men and a book. And the only thing I know about you is a basket and a leg and nine days, and the only reason I am offering it to you is that a man who is a person of no district is a man no district can answer for, and a week ago the only two people in this county who were talking about that had both noticed you, and one of them is a clerk at a counter who has read a legal instrument aloud to a room for eleven years and the other one is a woman who carries numbers and has told me, twice, in the last year, that she is not going to be asked what she is carrying. So you will do. That is the whole of the reason. There is not another one.”

“I am not the advocate for this bench and I will not be a party to it and my name must not be at the bottom of it.”

“I have not asked for your name.”

“You have not asked for it and you are going to,” said Ilyan Vester, “and I am telling you now, before you agree to anything, that my name is in four hundred and sixty-three forms in a district eleven miles off a bridge and a sledge has already been used on it once, and it is going to be in a book here, because the only way I can be paid in this world is to be a person on a wage line in chalk, and if I am paid then I am written down, and if I am written down then your page has a stranger in it that anybody in this county can look up. I would rather you had that from me at a bench in a room than have it from a clerk.”

Nobody in the room said anything for about four seconds.

“Then it is on the page,” said Perrin Gant, “and it is on the page because you are carrying a basket for a wage and not because anybody made you, and I am going to write both of those facts down, and I am sixty-one and I have not written a stranger into this book since the year before last and I did not enjoy that either.”

“Yes,” said Ilyan Vester. “And I will say the rest of it and then I will stop talking, and I am saying it because nobody else in this room is going to and it is mine to say. I have been produced five times. Not by a bench. By whatever was nearest to me, five times, in about two years, and every one of the five was a thing that was simply the next thing along, and one of them cost a woman a page of her own work and one of them cost a man a day and one of them cost a district three shillings and ninepence and a day and a half. I am telling you that before you agree to anything so that you know what you are agreeing to, and I am not asking you for anything in return for telling you, and there is nothing in this for me. I have three shillings and sixpence and a shed and a leg, and I have been in this district a week because a man on a boat told me where the hunger was, and I did not ask you, and nobody asked me, and that is the worst thing about it and it is the only thing about it that is mine.”

He stopped.

Wil Tamworth, thirty-three, said, “You are going to be in the book whether you say that or not, and you have said it, and now the only question in this room is whether you can read the thing that is going to be put in front of us in a fortnight.”

“No,” said Ilyan Vester.

Perrin Gant put his hand flat on the book and did not open it.

“Then the second question,” he said, “is whether you can be in this room with that.”

“I can be in this room with that. What I cannot do is tell you what the thing says. There is a building four miles from the court with no name on the door and a man with a bad hand in it, and in that building there is everything any district in this county has printed in the last four years, and any person may read any of it for fourpence a sheet, and no person has ever done so, and I have been in it and I have tried, and I got about one word in four.”

“You cannot read.”

“I cannot read,” said Ilyan Vester, “and I am going to say the number out loud, which I have never done. Two people in the world have been told it. Four more on a coast four hundred miles behind me worked it out and have never said a word. And a clerk in a city about a day and a half from this room worked it out in about four seconds yesterday and told me to go and be four miles away from her for an hour. That is six people before this minute.

“And I have said it out loud to you at a bench in a room about four minutes after you told me that you knew I had no standing, and I would like it noticed that I did it in that order.”

Osa Trin, thirty-nine, said, “Noticed,” and made a mark on a nail head with the flat of her thumb, which is a thing that three people in a district do instead of writing a minute, and then said, “Gant, if he cannot read it and we cannot read it then the thing gets put in front of us and we do not know what it is, and I am not going to sign my name to not knowing what it is in front of four people I have known for nineteen years.”

“Then we do not sign it,” said Perrin Gant.

“We cannot not sign it. If we do not sign it the lease goes and the granary goes and about four hundred people in this district are tenants of a bank they do not own by the end of the season, and the bench has no second room to go and sit in.”

Nobody said anything for about a minute.

Nevin Hake said, “I cannot read it either and I am not going to pretend to be the only one about that, and I have been in this bench eleven years and I have never said so in this room, and I am saying it now because that man has just said something that cost him and I am not going to be the second person in this room who is cheaper than him.”

So there were three.

Three of the four could not read a form, and one of them could, and the one who could was thirty-nine and had four nails and no glass and had spent her whole working life being the person in a district who read things out loud for nothing, which is a thing in a hundred districts on this shelf and is not a thing anybody has ever written a column about.

And Ilyan Vester stood in the room with three people who had just told him the truest thing anybody had told him in a year, and the truest thing was not about the lease and was not about the basket and was not about the form.

It was that **Nevin Hake had been in that bench eleven years and had never said so, and he said it because a stranger had been honest in the room, and the price of his saying it was that he would never be able to sit in that room and not know it, and he had decided in about four seconds that knowing it was cheaper than not knowing.**

He had been in a room on a coast where a man of seventy-one put a hammer in his hand and said *there is a hammer in my hand and there is no rule on this coast that says who holds it*, and the hammer had been for about a month, and this was the same thing and it was not the same thing, and both of those were true at the same time and he did not have a page for either.

---

He carried the basket on the fourth and the fifth days of the forty-first week and got paid ninepence a day, in chalk, on a board on the wall of the second door of the granary, with his name against it, and the name was the whole of what he had come four hundred miles for and it cost him ninepence.

Marda Fen did the paying. She is fifty-four and she has been the granary’s reeve for twenty-two years and she is right about grain in a way that nobody in that building is going to argue with, and she signed the district’s application for the forbearance, and she read the board before she wrote on it, which nobody else in that building does.

“Vester,” she said, with the chalk. “That is the whole of it?”

“That is the whole of it.”

“You have told a bench of four you cannot read and you have told me nothing about what you were before that.”

“Yes.”

“Then I am going to write the day, and the basket, and ninepence, and I am not going to write that you carried it for six miles each way, because the board is a wage line and a wage line is not a place for a man to be generous about his own distances, and if it were, you would be owed about four times what I am paying you and I have not got it and neither have you.” She wrote the line and put the chalk down. “And I am going to tell you the one thing about this district that I have told two people in four years, and I am telling you because you are going to be in the room on the day it is read and I would like there to be one person in it who is not one of the four.

“We were given a year. A year of not paying interest, and it was the best year this district has had in my time, and we spent it. We spent it getting the harvest in, because a year of not paying interest is four hundred and sixty-two pounds, and the carts to move a harvest in a hard season are four hundred and sixty pounds, and there is no other money in this district in the last week of a cut. And the carts were not ours. They were hired. And the hiring went on the account.

“So we are further into this account than we have ever been and it is not anybody’s fault and there is nobody in this district to be angry at, and that year is over, and I found out about a fortnight ago, and I found it out because a man off a boat came to my door and asked me for a basket, and the reason I found out is that I went looking for a clerk to ask whether the year was still running and there is no clerk in this district and there has not been one for nine years, and there is one woman who goes up a towpath once a month and comes back with a slip, and I asked her, and she did not know and said so, and I sent her to you.”

“I did not know either,” said Ilyan Vester.

“No,” said Marda Fen. “You did not, and you were the first person I asked and I have not asked anybody since, and I am going to keep it that way for about a week, and then I am going to have to ask somebody, and I have four people in a room above a chandler’s shop and I know exactly what I am going to have to say to them, and I have not said it yet and I am not going to say it to you either, and if you repeat any of this to any of those four I will stop paying your wage and I will do it in chalk where they can read it.”

“All right,” said Ilyan Vester.

“Good.” She picked the chalk up again and put it in the box. “Ninepence. Tomorrow. Do not ask me for a day off, I have not got the days, and if anybody in this building offers you one, take it and do not tell me.”

At the fourth bell the leg went on the quay, in front of about forty people, all the way from the hip down, and it was his own about three hours later on a towpath with a basket in his hands, and he told nobody then or afterwards, and about nine people in this district have now watched something happen to him and not one of them has ever mentioned it.

# Chapter 158: A Road Is Priced in a Quarter of an Hour

On the sixth day of the forty-second week a cart came into Ryefold at about the third bell and stopped at the weighbridge, and it had forty loads of Ryefold grain on it, and it was not stolen, and there was no prize on it, and nobody in that district had done anything wrong.

The claim was dated in the thirty-second week of the year before. It was an ordinary claim on the account — a carter of a county town had carried grain for the account in a hard season and had not been paid and had entered the claim in the ordinary way at a counter, and a claim is a thing a person may enter and a claim is dated and the date is what orders it. The order was first. The claim was first. And on the morning of the sixth day of the forty-second week the account could be satisfied in the district’s granary, because the account holds grain, and a man who is owed grain is owed grain, and the reeve is required to pay claims in their order.

Marda Fen told him the order was right. She told him the order was right in about four sentences, standing at the weighbridge, with about forty people behind her, and she was not defending anything and she was not yielding anything and every word of it was true.

“The claim is first,” she said. “I am not going to stand here and tell a man he is not first. He is first. He came in the thirty-second week of last year and I have had the sheet in that drawer since the thirty-fourth and I have not been able to pay it because I have not had the grain, and the grain came in and this is the day it came in and it is his.”

The carter was about fifty and he had come eleven miles and he had a boy with him and he said he would wait until the sixth bell.

Which left about four hours, and about six hundred people, and a reeve with about nine days of the district’s own ration in a granary and a rule that a person of the district may take grain at the district price against the account, in an order, and the order was a list, and the list had his name at the top of it under the other claim, and there were about two hundred and forty names under it in a hand that nobody on that quay could read.

And the cart was loaded. That was the part that put a crowd on a quay. Thirty-one loads were already on it. Thirty-one loads of a district of six hundred people’s grain, standing on a cart, on a quay, at the third bell, in daylight, in front of two hundred and forty people who had been told for a year that their grain was coming.

And there was a man on the quay whom nobody in the district had asked for.

---

Tarin Keel is forty-six and is a route-arbiter, which is a job with about nine hundred people in it on this shelf, and which means that if a road is being worked badly he can stand in it and say so, and a carter who has been made to stop by an arbiter can enter that against the person who stopped him, and an arbiter who stops a cart without a reason in it is a person who has entered something into a court in a city about eleven miles off. It is a small power and it is real and it is the only power Ilyan Vester has ever stood next to that belongs to somebody else.

He had come down the towpath two days before because a cart had been standing in the road at the second mouth’s turn and had been standing there for a day and a half, and a route-arbiter does not have a second thing to do, and he had stayed because the district had asked him nothing and had a bench of four who could not read a notice and a cart standing in a road and about nine hundred yards of river quay with nowhere to put a cart that was not being used.

He was standing about nine feet off the weighbridge when Ilyan Vester came down, and he did not turn round, and he said:

“You have got four days and a leg and about a week of shillings. I do not know how I know that and I have decided not to ask.”

“I am not going to do anything here that is yours to do.”

“Good,” said Tarin Keel. “Because I am going to do something, and I have been standing in this quay for a day and a half deciding whether to, and I have decided, and I have decided it in the ordinary way which is that I have been asked by nobody and I am here, and the two of those are the same and it took me four years to be able to say that out loud.”

---

He put himself between the cart and the crowd and he did not raise his voice, and about two hundred and forty people on a quay went quiet in about four seconds, which is not a thing a magistrate can do and is not a thing a man with a bench of four behind him can do.

“That cart is going to be weighed and it is going out,” he said, “and I am going to say why in about nine sentences, and I want the man it belongs to to hear it and I want the man on the bench to hear it, and I do not want the man on the bench to agree with me and I am telling him now that he will not.”

He turned round.

“Gant. Your granary reeve says that claim is first and she is right. I have read the sheet — I can read a sheet, that is the one thing I have that nobody here has — and it is dated the thirty-second week of last year and it is a true sheet and there is no prize on it and it is not a fraud and the man is owed. Now. That is the whole of it and I am not going to argue with it. I am going to argue with the next thing, and the next thing is the nine days of ration in that building and the two hundred and forty names under his name on a list that one woman can read and three of you cannot, and I am going to say to you, Gant, that in about three weeks there is going to be a day in this district when the ration runs out four days early, and on that day this quay is going to be this quay with about twice the people on it, and it will not be a crowd, it will be a thing that happens, and it will happen because thirty-one loads went out on a cart this morning in daylight with two hundred and forty people watching.”

He said, “That is why I am standing here. Not because the man is a thief. Because I have seen a district do a thing in daylight that it cannot undo by nightfall, and I have seen it twice, and I was in command both times, and both times I was told afterwards that I had no standing to do anything and I had not asked for any.”

Perrin Gant said, “You have standing. You have had it since you walked onto this quay. I am sixty-one and I have been convener nineteen years and I could not have stopped that cart and you could, and if you do not do it I will have to write to a bench in a city and say that I could not stop it, and that letter will be in a drawer in about four months.”

“I am aware of that,” said Tarin Keel. “I am aware of it and I have thought about it for a day and a half and I am still going to do something, and Mr Vester is going to tell me I am wrong, and I want to say that I do not care, and I want to say it before he does so that it is not an ambush.”

---

“A man may be owed,” said Ilyan Vester, “and if a road in this country can be stopped by anybody with a strip of cloth, then nothing is owed to anybody after that, and this shelf has spent four hundred years building a coast where one person does not answer for another person about a crossing, and I have watched a woman of forty-eight refuse a community in a lane in front of nine people and be right, and she was right because a party is a person and not a silence, and it took me eleven days on that coast to understand that and it took me four years before that to understand why it is the whole of what a person is.”

He put his hand flat on the flank of the cart.

“So I am against stopping him. And I am against it in a room, and I am against it with a bench behind me, and I would be against it in a year when I am not tired. And you are not wrong that the thirty-one loads went out in daylight, and the reason they went out in daylight is that a cart does not go at night on a towpath. If it had gone at night nobody would be standing here and you would have had the same thirty-one loads and no crowd and no trouble and a district that cannot remember why it is short. That is not a reason to do it in the dark. That is a reason to do it in the daylight with everybody watching, which is what happened.”

“That,” said Tarin Keel, “is the best argument anybody has made me for letting a thing happen that I have decided is going to kill somebody in about three weeks, and I would like you to notice that you have made it by telling me that a crowd is a price. You are telling me a crowd is the cost of honesty.”

“I am telling you the crowd is the cost of your road, and it is, and I have not told you it is worth paying, and I have not told you it is not worth paying, and it is not mine to tell you. It is six hundred people’s. And four of them are in a room and three of them cannot read.”

“Then ask them.”

“That is exactly what I have been doing for four days and it is going nowhere, and I have been asking in the wrong order, and I have known I was asking in the wrong order since the eighth of the last year in a shed on a coast when a man of sixty-one told me so at his own fire, and I have not worked out what the right order is yet, and I have not got the right order at four o’clock on the sixth of the forty-second week with two hundred and forty people on a quay, and neither have you.”

“I have,” said Tarin Keel. “I have the right order and it is the only thing I am actually good at, and I am not going to apologise for it, and I am not going to argue with you in front of a crowd about which of us is better at asking, and here it is, and you may write it down or not.

“The cart is on a public road. It is loaded and it is not going anywhere for three hours. It can go, and it must go, and it will go — and a road is priced, and I price roads, and that is my trade and it is the only trade in this world I am actually good at, and it takes a quarter of an hour, and I have never in my life priced a road in a room and I am not going to start now.”

He got a slate. He did not ask for it and a man on a quay about fifty feet off had two, and brought one, and nobody in Tarin Keel’s life will ever know his name.

“Forty loads,” he said, standing up. “One cart. One day on a quay, and a second day on the road to the weigh at the county town, and it is a long second day because the towpath is a river bank and you cannot take a loaded cart on it in the afternoon. That is a load path and it is a real figure and I can give it to anybody who asks. The figure is a day and a half. And here is the thing that nobody in this county has ever thought about in the last forty years, and I am going to say it out loud in a yard, and you can all go home and think about how much money you have made.

“A road is priced. Anything that stands on a road is priced, and you do not have to be the owner of it, and the price is set by the man who knows the load path and not by the man who owns the cart. That is a quarter of an hour of arithmetic. It has been a quarter of an hour of arithmetic for as long as there have been roads. And in the last forty years in this county a man has written down one price for every cart on every road, because the price is what the road costs the county and not what the cart costs the person, and that is right and I have never once argued with it. And that is why a carter of this county cannot price his own day, and why every one of them borrows, and why the man at the top of this list has been waiting since the thirty-second week of last year for a cart he can afford, and why three hundred and seventy of you have been told for two years that a prize is a thing you buy at two shillings and eightpence.”

He wrote on the slate and turned it round.

“Fourpence a load,” said Tarin Keel. “The second load path. I will take the second load path and I will take it today and I will take it for a month and I will enter it against nothing, and I will do it in a shed up the lane with about nine of you in the room, and the money will be eight shillings and eightpence, and it will be paid in coin at the end of the day to the person who did it by name in chalk, and I do not want it and I am not going to refuse it because a man who refuses a wage teaches a district that wages are refusable.”

“That is eight shillings and eightpence,” said Perrin Gant, from about thirty feet off.

“It is eight shillings and eightpence, and I have had that road priced by a man in a county town for thirty-one years and he would tell you it is a fair price, and I am not going to argue with him either, and I would like somebody in this yard to write down that there are three prices for a cart in this county and all three of them are fair.”

And about forty people on a quay in a district of six hundred who had been standing there since the third bell looked at each other, and about eleven of them started walking up the lane, and Tarin Keel stood in the middle of a quay with a slate in his hand and did not say anything else, and he was not thanked by anybody, and he did not look like a man who expected to be.

---

Ilyan Vester went up the lane at the fourth bell with the leg not his own, and he did not go to the shed, because there was nothing in the shed he could do that a man of twenty-six in a counting-house could not do at a counter, and he went to the second door of the granary and asked Marda Fen one question, and it is the only question he has asked in this county in three weeks that anybody acted on, and it is in a room and not in a yard and it is not a speech.

“Ma’am. His claim is against the account. Is his claim against the district’s *grain*?”

“It is against the account.”

“Then the account holds money as well.”

“It holds about nine pounds and it owes me a wage at the end of the week and it has owed me that for eleven weeks,” said Marda Fen. “That is the whole of the money in this district and it is nine pounds and it is mine.”

“Then there is a thing on the quay this morning that nobody has ever done, and it does not need a court and it does not need me and it needs a pen and about two minutes. His claim can be satisfied out of the money instead of out of the grain, because the account is a thing and the grain is a thing and he has never once specified which, and every account in this county has been settled out of the grain because the money is not there.”

“There is no money.”

“There is nine pounds,” said Ilyan Vester, “and if his claim is above nine pounds then he takes nine pounds today and the rest in a year, and the thirty-one loads stay in the granary.”

Marda Fen stood behind a box with a chalk in her hand for about eleven seconds and did not write anything on anything, and then she said, “Get out of my granary.”

He got out of her granary.

And in the corridor outside she said, to nobody, in the ordinary voice, about the price of nothing at all: “It has been about four hundred years. It is not a finding. I would like it noted that it is not a finding, because a man from a city has just said a thing out loud that four hundred carters have said to each other in about nine hundred yards.”

At the ninth bell he was in a cart shed behind a granary with a leg that had not been his own since a day four hundred miles and some weeks behind him, and about nine people in this district have now watched something happen to him and not one of them has ever mentioned it, and he wrote on the tenth page of a penny exercise book.

*Nineteenth day. There is a man in this world who can price a road in a quarter of an hour and a man in this world who can price a year in four, and the second one has had a four-year head start, and neither of them is a liar, and only one of them has ever been asked the question and he was not in the room.*

*I did not do anything today that mattered. I said two things, and one of them was the best thing I have said in a year and it was to a man about my own worst habit and it did not help anybody, and the other one was a sentence about a claim and a grain and a money, and a woman of fifty-four has said out loud in a corridor that four hundred carters have said it to each other in nine hundred yards for four hundred years, and she called it not a finding, and she is right, and I am going to have to spend a long time working out how to lose it.*

*It is mine to write down and it is not mine to decide.*

*A shed is a penny a night and a wage is ninepence a day. A year of the two of them is three thousand six hundred and fifty pence, which is fifteen pounds four shillings, and I have fifty-seven pence, and the ratio is about sixty to one, and I have put it on this page because it is the only arithmetic in this district that I am any good at and it is the one nobody here does.*

*Fifty-seven pence is not even two prizes. A prize is two sheets at a shilling and fourpence, and it buys a man with one cart a place at the front of a queue that has not happened yet, and I could not buy one, and it would not be any use to me if I could, because I am not a person of this district and there is no district in this world in which my name at the top of a list would mean one thing to anybody.*

*And here is the thing I have not said out loud to a single person, and I am putting it on a page because the page is the only place left in this world where I am allowed to be this honest: I did not come here because of the grain. I came here because a man on a boat told me where the hunger was, and a woman in a room told me there was a paper with a date on it, and both of those were about a person, and I have been in this district nineteen days and every single thing I have learned has been about a price, and I do not yet know whether that is because this district is like this, or because it is the only thing I am able to see.*

# Chapter 164: The Man Who Reads For Pleasure

Six miles up the road that goes north out of Ryefold there is a yard behind the granary account's own office, and the office is a counting-house over the yard with a counter on the ground floor and a window on the first, and the account is not the chamber of Plenty and the two offices do not speak.

They do not speak because they are not supposed to, and being two offices is the reason, and the reason is about four hundred years old and is in nobody's book, and Ilyan Vester did not learn that in a room. He learned it in a passage, on the seventh day of the forty-third week, at about the second bell, from a man of nineteen who said it in the ordinary way while he was counting a tray.

“They do not speak,” said Amos Kellock, “and I have worked out in two years that it is not rudeness. The chamber is a court counter and it reads things. This is an account and it holds things. A thing that reads and a thing that holds are not enemies, they are just two different rooms, and the trouble is that everybody in the country thinks one room is the voice of the other one, and it is not, and when the two rooms have to say the same thing they have to say it in two hands and a form, and the form is a shilling and fourpence, and I have had that form in this tray for two years and nobody has ever paid for it.”

He is nineteen. He has been in this office two years, out of a counting-house on the second mouth, and he is the only person in this building that Ilyan Vester met in two days whom he would describe as reading for pleasure.

Not the accounts. The accounts are nine sheets a day and nobody in this building enjoys them.

“The returns,” said Amos Kellock. “About nine hundred district clerks. When the circulars go out somebody here opens them, and it is me, because I am the junior, and there is a sack and it comes in twice a year and it weighs about forty pounds and I have read about six thousand of them and I have never been asked what is in one.”

“What is in them.”

“Mostly nothing,” he said. “That is not a complaint either, I want to be exact, because the nothing is interesting. There are about nine hundred clerks in this county and about nine of them are people who would write you a letter if you asked them one, and about forty are people who read everything and answer nothing, and the rest are people doing a job in a room on an ordinary day. And the ones who answer are the ones who answer because they have to, and the ones who do not answer are the ones who did it properly, and both of those are correct, and nobody has ever asked me which is which because it is not a question anybody has ever had.”

He was counting a tray and he stopped counting it.

“You have come about the form,” he said. “The reeve wrote a name on the back of a claim sheet and the name went into a bag on the sixth and it came up here on the seventh and I have had it since the seventh and I have not done anything with it because I did not know what it was for, and then this morning there was a sheet on the counter with a man’s address on it and the address is a cart shed behind a granary, and I have worked out in about four seconds that the two of those are the same business.”

---

The form is one sheet and a shilling and fourpence, and it is the same form they keep at the chamber of Plenty, and there is a rule about it that Kellock read out loud from a card on the wall because he can read a card on a wall faster than anybody in that building and because nobody had ever asked him to.

“A fund that satisfies a claim out of a fund has to enter who advised it,” he said. “That is all it says. Not who decided it and not who signed it, because the reeve decides and the reeve signs and that is ordinary. Who advised. And in about nine years of that tray there have been four forms of that kind and three of them have said *the clerk of the district* and one has said *no advice was given*, and one sheet at a shilling and fourpence is a real price for a box with six lines in it, and I have thought about that price a great deal in two years and I have never once been able to make it smaller.”

“Who takes the deposition.”

“I do,” said Amos Kellock. “I am entitled and I have been entitled for eleven months, and the entitlement is a certificate in a drawer and the certificate is a form, and the form is the thing I just read you off the wall, and there is no more of it than that. It is not a great deal of authority. It is a pen.”

---

He did it at the counter with the tray pushed to one side and it took about twenty minutes, and it is worth setting out nearly all of it because it is dull and because the dullness is the point of the chapter, and because everything that happens to a district of six hundred people in Volume 04 is going to happen in about twenty minutes to a boy with a pen.

He asked the questions in the order they are on the sheet, and he wrote what was said, and he read it back before he signed it, and the leg went on the stair behind the counter at about the half hour and stayed for about nine minutes, and nobody in that building said one word about it then or afterwards, which is the fourth time that has happened in this district and the first time it has happened in a building with a tray in it.

At the end he read the whole thing back, and Ilyan Vester listened to about a third of it, because he had been listening to about a third of documents for four years and has never once pretended otherwise in front of a person who counts trays.

“That is not what I said in the second box,” he said.

“That is not the second box,” said Amos Kellock. “The second box is the date of the conversation, and we had our conversation on the sixth day of the forty-third week, and you said a thing to me at about the fourth bell on that day, and about forty people heard it and one of them was a boy with a piece of chalk, and I have written the whole of it in the second box, and you told me it was a sentence a man had said in a corridor four hundred times in four hundred years, and I have written that too, and I have not written whose corridor, because you did not say whose corridor, and because it is not a question the form asks.”

“That is correct,” said Ilyan Vester.

“I know it is correct,” said Amos Kellock. “I wrote it. It is nearly always correct, and I want to be honest with you about that, because in two years I have written perhaps nine hundred boxes and they are correct and I have never once had anybody read one back at me who was not paid to, and you are not paid to and you cannot read, and if I have got something wrong in here then the person it will be used on cannot tell me, and that is not a fault in you, it is the arrangement, and I have thought about it for a year and I have not found the other way round.”

He put the sheet flat on the counter and squared it with two fingers.

“There. And the last box.”

>
> THE PERSON WHO ADVISED IT. THE PERSON NAMED ABOVE.

The last box was in Amos Kellock's hand and it was the third line of it that was his own, because the first two had been written out for him and the third one he asked for and had copied off the reeve's form, and it was a name, and it was a name he could read.

He read it out. Then he said, “That is the last line and it is the only line on that sheet that is any use to anybody, and I want to say why before you pay me, because you are about to pay me and I would rather have said it after.”

“Say it.”

“Your name in that box is the difference between a reeve doing a thing on her own authority in a bad year, with nothing behind it, on her word, in about eleven minutes, alone, with nobody able to check her,” said Amos Kellock, “and an account having done it, once, on a record, with a stranger’s word at the bottom of it. That is not a small thing. I have read about nine thousand returns in this tray and in about four hundred of them there is a decision that somebody in that district made on her own and there is a line under it that says *no advice was given*, and I have thought about those four hundred about as much as I have thought about anything, and I have never been able to find the thing wrong with them, and I have never been able to find the thing right with them either, and I have got about eleven years to think about it and I have not.”

Ilyan Vester took out four shillings, which was all he had, and put a shilling and fourpence on the counter, and the rest of it he did not have.

Kellock took the shilling and fourpence and did not look at the four shillings and did not comment on them, and put them in a box under the tray, and gave him a receipt for the one, which was on a printed slip with a number on it and which Ilyan Vester folded and put in his coat beside a page he had sworn to hold and could not read and had no intention of reading.

Then Amos Kellock said, “Thank you,” and it was not about the shilling and fourpence.

“For what,” said Ilyan Vester.

“For saying out loud, about nine minutes ago, that I was not to write what you told me to write.” Kellock put the tray back. “I want to tell you that I have had that instruction before, twice, from two men who pay me more than you do and who own the building, and both times I took it, and both times I put in what they said, because it is a box with six lines in it and what is the box for. And you said it in about two seconds and you did not soften it and you did not explain it, and I have thought about your two seconds for about four minutes while you were on that stair, and I would like you to know that I have taken the instruction and I am going to keep it, and that I would have taken it anyway, and that you did not have to stand on that stair to get it.”

He said it at the counter, in the ordinary way, to a man he had known for about half an hour, and it was the kindest thing anybody said to Ilyan Vester in a district of six hundred people, and eight days later he sat in a room four miles from that counter and read out a sentence that a man had said in the abstract about himself, and it was his own hand, and the room did with it what rooms do.

He did not know that yet. He put the receipt in his coat and went down the stair, and the leg held for the four miles and then did not, and he stood at a milestone with his weight against a willow for about nine minutes at about the sixth bell, and there was nobody on that road.

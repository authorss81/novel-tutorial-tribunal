# Chapter 153: A Building With No Name On The Door

There is a street of counting-houses at the low end of Orison, four miles from the court, and one building in it has no name on the door, and about eleven people a week walk past it and none of them knows what is inside it.

Ilyan Vester had been in the city for about a day and he knew exactly what was inside the building, and he got there on the seventh day of the fortieth week at about the third bell, on foot, because it is four miles and a cart was threepence and he had three shillings and tenpence.

Dessin Nahl was at the counter of the Orison chamber and had been at that counter for eleven years, and she had walked eleven miles over six days once and paid two of the six out of her own, and she had a way of looking at a person that came from reading the same four hundred forms for eleven years.

She listened to about a sentence and a half of it.

“A printed instrument,” she said. “Any of them, or one in particular.”

“Any of them. A form a district printed. I want to see one.”

“There is one place.” She wrote something on a slip and did not give it to him. “Low end of the counting-house street, four miles. There is no name on the door and that is not an accident, and it is not locked, and there is no clerk, and if you go in and say you want to read a form you are going to have to ask a man with a key, and he is there two days out of seven and he sweeps.”

“I have been told he sweeps.”

“Everybody who has ever been in has been told he sweeps,” said Dessin Nahl. “That is what I want to say to you before you go, so that you can decide whether to be disappointed in him or not. He sweeps and he is the man with the key and he has been nine years, and in nine years the only thing anybody has ever asked him is where the shelves are, and he is not the one who decided to make it that way, and he is not going to pretend to you that he has been waiting for you.”

She put the pen down.

“Anything else?”

“Yes,” said Ilyan Vester. “When I was in the Orison chamber, and again some months afterwards, you read me out a paragraph of a form with a word at the top of it.”

“I did.”

“I understood about a third of that paragraph and none of the rest, and you did not know that, and you read it twice, and the second time you slowed down.”

Dessin Nahl looked at him for about four seconds.

“Eleven years,” she said, “of reading that paragraph four times a day to men who cannot get a word of it out on the first pass, and I slowed down the second time because I thought you had a cold. And you cannot read. And you have been standing in this room for a year and a half letting me read things to you.”

“Yes.”

“All right.” She did not sit down, and she did not put her pen down, and she did not say anything at all for about three seconds, which in that room is a considerable thing. “Then you are the wrong man for this and I am not going to say so in here, and I am going to say one thing else instead, and it is the only courtesy anybody in this building has ever shown you and you did not earn it: you have never once pretended to follow a word you did not follow, and I have wanted somebody to be in this room who did that for eleven years and it has never happened and now it has, and it is no use to me at all.”

“All right,” said Ilyan Vester.

“Go and be four miles away from me for an hour.”

---

The building was three storeys of grey stone with a shuttered upper floor and a door that had been painted eleven times and never lettered, and the door was not locked, and the passage smelled of cold stone and lamp oil and about four hundred years of paper in a room that gets four degrees of warmth in the year.

The man with the key was forty-one and his right hand was six years gone at the wrist and he could not open it after three hours without doing it one finger at a time, and he was on his knees in the passage with a broom when the door opened and he did not get up.

“If you have come for the third shelf it is not there,” he said. “The third shelf has been the third shelf since before I came. People come for the third shelf because somebody told them it was the old one and it is not the old one, the old one went in a cart in the winter before last and nobody wrote down where, and that is the whole of what I know about it and I have said that eleven times.”

“I have come to read a form.”

The broom stopped.

“What sort of form.”

“Any sort. I want to see what a district prints when it is told to print something.”

“All right,” said Corrin Vask, and got up, one finger at a time, and looked at Ilyan Vester for slightly longer than was comfortable. “You are the first person in nine years who has come in here and asked me that. They come in and they ask me for a document and they know the document and they give me the year, and I go and get it, and we are both satisfied, and that is the whole of what this building is.”

“Is it.”

“It is a shelf.” He said it without any weight on it at all. “I have got nineteen shelves in a cold room at the back and four years of everything anybody in this city has printed, running by plate, in order, and a person may come to the counter on a day and read any of it and copy any of it at fourpence a sheet, and that has been the standing instruction since before I came and it is a good instruction and it is the best instruction in this city. I could not tell you why nobody has ever done it.”

“Four years,” said Ilyan Vester. “You mean the series only goes back four years.”

“The incoming index stopped four years ago. That is a different thing from the series. The series holds everything printed in this city in the last four years, and before that it is a standing authority and an index with nothing in it.” He got the lamp. “Come through.”

The cold room at the back had nineteen shelves and a smell like the inside of a bell, and there was a plate on each shelf and a year on each plate, and a man who could read a plate could find any printed thing any district in this county had produced in four years in about the length of time it takes to say it.

“That is the whole of it,” said Corrin Vask. “That is a shelf, and a shelf is not a door, and an office with no person in it is not a door, it is a shelf, and I am the man in it and I have been here nine years and I have been asked about four times whether a thing was on the shelves and I have never once been asked what a thing was for. You may read anything you want and it costs you fourpence a sheet and it goes in the day book in the column with the sweeping of the floors, because that is the column it goes in.”

He put the lamp on a stand at the end of the aisle.

“If you want a volume out of the case it is a shilling, out of the drawer, in no minute and on nobody’s order, and if you want me to open it and hold it while you look, that is the sweeping of my floors for those days, and it goes in the book as not done, and there is one man for the floors and he is the man with the lamp.”

---

He read for three hours by a lamp he could not read.

That is the sentence, and it is the whole of the third of Volume 04’s first movement, and it needs about four seconds to say and a week to sit still under.

He could not read. He had said so out loud on a coast twice in five weeks and it had been remarked on by nobody, and about two hours ago a clerk at a counter in this city had worked it out in about four seconds and told him to go and be four miles away from her for an hour, and he had gone and done exactly that, and he had not noticed that he had done exactly that until about a mile ago. Corva Silt and no clerk were the only two people in the world who knew, and this was the third, and she was not going to be told and he was not going to tell her, and what he did instead was put his finger on a line about a third of the way down and read it out loud, word by word, guessing, and got about one word in four right, and the two words he got wrong were a word that is also a word in his own language and a figure.

That is what a man does at a plate.

Corrin Vask did not help him, because Corrin Vask cannot help anybody, and said so: *I can tell you a thing was printed in a year I have not been in. If you want to know what it says you want a person who reads, and there are about nine of them in this city and none of them is in this street.*

At about the sixth bell the door at the end of the passage opened, and a man of thirty-four came in with a slip in his hand and a hat he was not wearing, and he had come six miles on foot and it had taken him most of the day, and he had a cart outside with a load of sacks on it and about two hours of light left.

“Evening,” he said. “I want to read a form.”

“Which one.”

“I don’t know. That’s the thing.” He put the slip on the counter, and it was about the length of a hand and there was a number on it and a date and two stamps and nothing else that a person could see. “I paid two shillings and eightpence for this. I want to know what it says.”

Corrin Vask put the lamp on it and looked at it for about four seconds, and then he did not pick it up, which Ilyan Vester noticed and understood about six seconds later was the most careful thing he had seen anybody do in a year.

“Two shillings and eightpence,” Corrin Vask said. “That is a prize. That is two sheets at a shilling and fourpence, and the price is the same in every county in this Shelf and it is the price of the sheets, and I am not the office that entered it and I cannot tell you who was.”

“It will be in the register, and the register is in the counting-house by the court, and the register shut at the first bell.”

“When does it open.”

“At the first bell, and I am not the office that keeps it and I do not know whether it will be open by the time you get there, and I have been sorry about that for four years and there is nobody in this city I can send you to who will open it for you tonight.” He moved the lamp so that the slip was lit properly. “But I will tell you the one thing I can tell you, and it is not a reading of it and it is not advice: whatever that paper does, it was printed under a plate in this building, and the plate is on the shelf with your year on it, and the district that printed it printed it in this city and I have it in here, and it is fourpence a sheet and you may read it tonight and take it away with you and nobody will stop you and nobody will know you did.”

The man of thirty-four stood at that counter for about six seconds.

Then he said, “Where.”

And Corrin Vask took the lamp off the counter and went down the aisle with it, and a man of thirty-four with two shillings and eightpence and a cart outside followed him, and Ilyan Vester, who had come four miles to look at a plate and could not read one, stood in the cold room at the end of nineteen shelves and let them go.

It is the first time anybody on that coast was made safer by anything he did, and he did it by not being useful.

---

He was out on the street at about the eighth bell with a leg that had not been his own since a day four hundred miles and some weeks behind him, and he had three shillings and sixpence, and he had found out one thing and it was this: that in a city of about ninety thousand people there had been a room for four years in which any person could read, free of charge to look at and fourpence a sheet to copy, every single thing any district in the county had printed — and not one of them had ever walked in, and the reason was not that the door was shut.

The reason was that nobody had ever known. Four years of it, and a man with a bad hand sweeping the floor of a passage, and a plate on a shelf, and a standing instruction from an authority with a stamp and no person behind it that said any person may read any of it at a counter on a day.

And the man with the bad hand had not been waiting for anybody. He had said so, in a passage, on his knees, in about eleven words, and he was right, and the fact that he was right was the only thing in the building and it was not a fault of his and it was not going to be fixed by anybody coming in twice.

Ilyan Vester stood in a street in a city of ninety thousand people and worked out, in about four seconds, the same thing he had worked out on a bar road in about ninety seconds eight months earlier and in a lane in about four minutes four years before that: **a thing on a road is a person, and a person is produced by whatever is nearest, and the nearest thing to a form is a man who cannot read it, and it is four hundred years old and it is in nobody’s book.**

He had been produced five times now and he had said four of them out loud before anybody else did, and this one was the first that had not cost anybody a thing, and he stood in the street and could not work out whether that made it the same.

It was the same.

He went and found a cart shed and slept, and in the morning he was on a towpath with a basket, and it was the eighth day since he had put his bag down on a stone quay at the head of the second mouth, and there were four people in a shed behind a granary who could not read the thing that was going to be put in front of them, and he was the only person on that towpath who did not know what a basket was for, and he had been on the Shelf eight days, and he said it out loud on a towpath at the fourth bell, to nobody, the way he had said a tide count out loud to nobody on about a dozen mornings of a coast four hundred miles behind him.

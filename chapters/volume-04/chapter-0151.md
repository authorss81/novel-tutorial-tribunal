# Chapter 151: Three Days East

He went off that coast on the second day of the fortieth week, at the first bell, on a boat that was going east along four hundred miles of water and then up a river, and he paid four shillings for the third of it and had a bag with a coat in it and a penny exercise book.

It took three days. The man who had the boat was about fifty-four and called Fenn Ordram and he did not ask a single question in three days, which Ilyan Vester noticed and was grateful for in a way he could not have explained to anybody, and on the second evening, when the sea had gone grey behind them and the boat was under way with the current, the man sat down on the gear and said the only four sentences that mattered.

“The second mouth is the cheapest grain in the county and in about six weeks it will be the only grain anybody has,” said Fenn Ordram. “There is a district called Ryefold six miles down the towpath off it with about six hundred of them in, and they hold a granary on a lease, and the lease is up in a fortnight. That is the whole of what I know about it and I know it because I have carried a reeve’s clerk up that towpath four times a year for two years.”

“Why do you tell a stranger that.”

“Because you are going to ask me in about an hour.” The man put a hand-lead in the water and let it run. “I have been up this water thirty-one years and I know what a man looks like when he is going somewhere and does not have a reason, and you have got the look of a man going somewhere to be useful. So either you are going to Ryefold, and then you will want to know it is coming, or you are not, and then it costs me nothing to have said it.”

“I have not been to this country before in my life.”

“No,” said Fenn Ordram. “You have not.”

---

The wharf at the head of the second mouth was four hundred yards of stone and a crane and about nine people, and it was in the fortieth week of the grain year and the second day of it, and the river was a mile and a half wide and brown and going down, and everything in the world smelled of wet stone and grain dust and the specific sourness of a sack that has been wet once.

He had four shillings. He worked it out on the deck of the boat, sitting on his own bag, because a man who has four shillings and no district and no advocate is a different sort of problem from a man who has nothing.

A day was ninepence. A night was a floor or fourpence in a shed behind something. A week was therefore about five and threepence, and four shillings was five weeks and threepence, and he was not going to be on a river for five weeks.

He had also worked out, somewhere between the fourth and the second mouth, that he had no standing anywhere on this bank of the world, and that his name was printed four hundred and sixty-three times in a form eleven miles off a bridge in a district whose clerk had read a paragraph of it four times at night on a lamp, and that the word at the top of that form said the whole thing was temporary, and that a man with a sledge had used it to take a pitch off a woman with four people. That had been done before he had ever heard of a coast and it had not improved since and it was not going to.

He came off the boat with a bag and a bad leg and a blackglass token in an inside pocket that had never been warm, and there were three people on the wharf and a woman with a ledger-book on a stool and a man in a coat with a stamp on a chain.

The man in the coat got to him first, not because he was looking for him, but because he was looking at the bag.

“Paper,” said the man in the coat. “You want the carrier’s docket, or you want a ticket for the barges, or you have come off that boat with nothing and you are the other thing.”

“The other thing.”

“That is what I thought.” The man did not move out of the way and did not raise his voice, and there was nothing hostile in his face at all, and Ilyan Vester found that much harder to bear than hostility would have been. “You want to know what a man with no paper is. I will tell you, because I have told about four this week and there is no reason to make you stand in the rain for it.

“A man with no paper is a man nobody can prove anything about. That cuts both ways and it cuts them the same distance, and about ninety of us in this building like it and about nine of us do not, and the nine of us are the ones who keep the books. You will find the ninety first. They will feed you.”

He looked at the bag, and at the leg, and at the place on the coat where a man of thirty-one has worn a thing in the same pocket for long enough to make a shape in it.

“You are not from the coast,” the man said. “Coast people carry a book and a bit of glass and they do not sit down. You are carrying a book and a bit of glass and you have sat down twice since the boat came alongside. Go up the towpath. There is a district called Ryefold and it is six miles and there is a cart shed behind their granary and a man in it called Doss Pellam who will take your penny and will not ask you anything, and that is the whole of what I know about Ryefold and I have been off that boat four hundred times.”

And then he went and looked at a barge, and that was the end of it, and it had taken about ninety seconds.

---

The towpath was a raised bank of packed stone and mud between the river and a cut bank about four feet high, and it went east, and the second mouth was on his right for the whole of the first three hours and then the cut bank came up to his shoulder and the water went away behind a line of willows.

He walked it in about two hours and a half, which is a fair estimate for a man with a bag and a leg and nothing to hurry for, and he stopped twice, and the second time the leg went in on the bank about a mile out, all of it, from the hip down, and he stood on a milestone with his weight against a willow until it came out the way it came out, and there was nobody on the towpath to see it and he told nobody.

The rendering came up while he was standing there, on a river bank in a country he had never seen, at the same height and the same distance it comes up at: the boards, the table the size of a table a hand could go on, the lamp with the wick down, the window with weather on the outside of it that was not this weather, and four people at the table and one of them about forty-four with her back to him.

It did not move. It cost him what it costs. He waited it out with his hand on the willow and his eyes shut and it went away, and he has never been able to say afterwards what any of it was for.

At about the fourth bell he came round a bend and the cut bank came down and the river opened out and there were about eleven hundred yards of stone quay, and a building at the end of it with a door in it and a beam over the door about nine feet long, and about sixty carts standing in a line along the quay in the rain, and the smell of a granary that is working.

Ryefold.

He stood on the towpath in the rain and looked at it for longer than he meant to, and what he was looking at, and what he wrote down that night in a penny exercise book in six lines, is this: **there is no name over that door and there are sixty carts on that quay and about six hundred people in that district, and I have found no reason to be in any of it, and I am going to find one inside a day and a half because I have four shillings.**

That is the whole of how he chose it. It is worth putting down plainly, because he had spent fifty-nine days on a coast learning that the things nearest to him are not the things that need him, and he had gone four hundred miles inland and picked a place because a boatman told him where the hunger was, and he did not tell anybody he had done it, and nobody asked him, and the not-telling was in his coat and would come out about four months later in a shed two miles along a coast, in a woman of twenty-four’s mouth, in a different argument entirely.

---

There was a woman at a door at the near end of the quay with a slate on a three-legged frame and a string round her neck, and she was about thirty-one, and she was not doing anything with it.

“Granary’s shut,” she said, before he had asked her anything. “Weighbridge shuts at the fourth and the doors shut at the fifth and if you have come for grain you have come on a bad day, and if you have come about the lease you want the second door and not this one.”

“I have come about nothing. I have come off a boat and I am looking for a place to stand for a day.”

She looked at him the way a person looks at a number they have been given twice.

“That is a bad answer to give me and it is a worse one to give Marda,” she said. “She is in there and she will want to know what you are before she lets you stand anywhere, and she has been getting that wrong in the direction of careful for twenty-two years so you will get the careful version of it and not the other one.”

“I would rather have the careful one.”

“Nobody ever wants the careful one. Sit under the shed if you have to.” She turned the slate about a quarter of a turn, which he understood about four seconds later to be a thing she did with her hands when she was not going to be interrupted. “I go up the towpath on Mondays. Six miles to a counter and six miles back with a basket, and I am on the road about eleven hours and I am paid three shillings a week in the cut and nothing the rest of the year, and I could put you on the end of it tomorrow if you wanted the work and had somewhere to sleep, and I am not asking you if you want it, I am telling you it is there, because you look like a man who has four shillings and no district, and there is not a third thing to be.”

“Why are you telling me.”

“Because in about four days that lease is up and there are going to be about nine people in this district who cannot read a thing that is going to be put in front of them, and every one of them has told me so in the last year, and I have said the same thing to all nine and I have never once said it to a stranger, and this is the first morning in about two years that a stranger has come down that bank with a bag and no business.”

She picked the slate up and turned it back.

“I am Sena Ollet. I carry numbers. I do not read them and there is nobody in this district who reads one, and that is the entire job and I have been doing it since I was twenty-two, and if you want to be useful you can start by understanding that that sentence is not a complaint.”

---

He slept in the cart shed behind the granary for a penny and a man called Doss Pellam took the penny and did not ask him anything, and he was up before the light with his leg not his own and a bag and four shillings less one penny, and the sixth of the six torn-out pages in the book said what the man at the wharf had said and the date on it was the second day of the fortieth week, and the page after that was blank, and it stayed blank for two days.

The second morning he stood at the end of the quay and watched a weighbridge work, which is the one thing in this world he can follow from a hundred yards without a mark on it: a cart up, a beam, a man with a chalk, a number, a corner of his eye. He worked out that he could follow about a third of what was said on that quay and none of what was written on any of it, and that in about a fortnight the thing that is going to be put in front of four people who cannot read it was going to be put there, and that he had four shillings and no reason to be in any of it.

And then he did the other sum, the one he had been not doing for two days, which is that a man with no district cannot be paid by a district, and that a man with no paper cannot be a bearer of labour, and that the ninth bridge’s own tenth term is that the wage goes to the person at the moment of the work by name in chalk, and an empty line is a record. **So there was one door in this district and it was a wage, and the only way through it was to be paid, and being paid was the only thing in this world that would make him a person in a record instead of a thing in one, and it would also put his name in a fifth book in a district he had never been in, and he was going to do it on purpose, and that was the decision and he made it standing at a quay with about a third of it in chalk in his head.**

He went and found the woman with the slate and told her so, and she said, “Right,” and turned the slate about a quarter of a turn, and said, “Then go and be useful at the second door and not at mine.”

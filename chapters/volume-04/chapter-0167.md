# Chapter 167: The Word On The Form

Wil Tamworth is thirty-three and he has a brother-in-law at a weighhouse eleven miles off who is named Hesk Amray and who is forty-one and who has a sheet of paper in a drawer at that weighhouse, and has had it for about two years, and has said, every time anybody in this district has been in a room about anything at all, that a person can put that sheet in front of a man with a cart and make him do the right thing.

Nobody in this district has ever asked him what the sheet is, and on the third day of the forty-fifth week Wil Tamworth brought it up the lane in his own coat and put it on the bench's own table and said, “Now ask me,” and the room asked him.

“A word at the top of it,” said Hesk Amray by way of a brother-in-law, and then Wil Tamworth said the word himself, because he had seen the sheet, and he said it the way a man says a thing he has had in his mouth for two years.

> EXCEPTION.

“There is a word at the top of that sheet and the word is *exception*,” said Wil Tamworth, “and the word means that a rule is temporary. And I want the room to hear what I have worked out about it in two years, because I have worked out about four things about it and three of them are rubbish. The first is that you can put it in front of a man with a cart and he has to do the right thing. The second is that the right thing is whatever the man with the cart thinks the right thing is, and if the sheet said *the moon is made of green cheese* then that would be the right thing. The third is that the sheet does not take anything. Not the rule, not the account, not the man. It takes the paper.”

Nobody said anything.

“That is all it takes,” he said. “And that is more than nothing, and it is more than a person with one cart has ever had, because a person with one cart has never in his life had a piece of paper that a person in a room could not simply put in a drawer. And that is what it is for. That is the whole of what it is for. It is not a way of winning. It is a way of not losing in a drawer.”

---

Perrin Gant said the thing a convenor says, which is that the room had better find out how many of them exist before anybody in this district said another word about it, and that he had a figure in his head and did not know where it came from, and that he was going to be sick about saying it.

“A figure,” said Osa Trin.

“Nine hundred and forty,” said Perrin Gant. “I do not know where I have got that. It has been in my head for about four days and I have not said it to anybody, and I am sixty-one and I have got a number in my head that I cannot produce, and if this district is going to be a district that writes things down then that is the first thing we have to do about and I would like somebody to tell me I am not mad.”

“He is not mad,” said Marda Fen, from the doorway, and she had not come in and nobody had heard her. “Nine hundred and forty is about right. The last run of that form was four thousand and about three thousand have been taken up and the rest are in a box somewhere and the box is not in this district and I have never wanted it and I did not know until this minute that I wanted it.”

Nine hundred and forty, and about three thousand and sixty taken up, out of the four thousand the last run of that form came to, and Perrin Gant did the sum out loud twice on the back of his own hand to make sure that the woman at the doorway had got it right, and she let him, and neither of them said what the sum was for.

“You have wanted it since you said that word,” said Perrin Gant.

“I have wanted it since I said that word and I have been a reeve for twenty-two years,” said Marda Fen, “and I want it for the cut and not for the sitting, and I am telling the room which one it is so that nobody can put it in a book afterwards as though it were the other.”

---

And then a man in a good coat came into that room at about the fifth bell and put a bag of forms on the bench's own table and said, “I have been in this county about six weeks and I have been in this district three days and I have heard the word *exception* said eleven times in three different rooms and I have come to say that I sell the other one, and that the two of them are nothing to do with each other, and that anybody who tells you otherwise is selling you something.”

Ilger Rusk is thirty-eight and he is a licensed factor and he has a good coat and about two hundred forms in a bag and he came in on the third day of the forty-fifth week because he had said in a yard on the third day of the forty-second week that he would be back, and he had come back, and he had not said he would knock and he knocked.

The two of them are not the same paper. The prize is two sheets at one shilling and fourpence a sheet, it is the same in a hundred and one counties, and it buys a place in a line of people, and about three hundred and thirty-nine of the three hundred and seventy in this county are held by a person with one cart. The exception form is a thing a district prints for itself, and it costs a district about threepence a sheet to print a hundred, and it does not buy anybody anything at all, and nobody in this room has ever sold one and nobody ever will.

“That is the honest answer to what Wil Tamworth has been carrying for two years,” said Ilger Rusk, “and it is worse than he thinks, and here it is. A form with the word *exception* on it does not take a rule. He is right about that and it took me fourteen years and a good coat to work out. It takes the paper. And what it takes the paper *from* is the only question in the world, and a hundred forms printed by this district cost about two pounds five and this district has nothing, and so the hundred forms do not get printed, and the four hundred carters in about nine hundred yards do not get them either, and the form is the thing that does the work and there is no form.”

He opened the bag and took out a sheaf and put it on the table, and nobody touched it.

“Now the other thing,” he said, “and I am going to say it because I have said the price of my whole life in a yard in this district already and I have not slept since. There is a building four miles from that court with no name on the door. I have been in it. There are nineteen shelves in a cold room at the back and a plate on each shelf and a year on each plate, and the standing instruction of the authority that owns it is that any person may come to the counter on a day and read any of it and copy any of it at fourpence a sheet, and that has been the standing instruction for longer than I have been selling anything.”

The room did not go quiet, because it had about nine people in it and about half of them were looking at the man in the coat.

“How do you know that,” said Wil Tamworth.

Ilger Rusk looked at the man who had asked.

“Because I have been going in and out of that street for fourteen years and I have never once used the counter, and it has never once occurred to me that I could, and neither has it occurred to about four hundred men in this county, and I have wanted a thing out of that building twice in my life and both times I paid a man to go in for me, and the first of those men charged me four shillings and eightpence and the second of them charged me two shillings and eightpence and neither of them knew what they were looking at, and I have thought about that for about four years and it is the single most stupid thing that has ever happened to me and I have had four other stupid things happen to me and I can rank them.”

---

Nobody in that room had ever heard of the building.

That is the whole of the discovery and it is worth saying how it went, because the reason nobody in that room had ever heard of it is not that a door was shut, and it is not that anybody had decided they should not know, and it is the same reason as everything else in this district and in about nine hundred other yards in this county: a thing is only ever found by a person who has a reason to want it and no other reason at all.

Osa Trin said, “Then why has nobody in this district ever gone.”

“Because nobody in this district has ever known,” said Ilger Rusk, “and that is not a failure of a door. I have been fourteen years in a street with a door in it. There is a man in that building with a key and a bad hand and he is there two days out of seven and he sweeps, and in nine years the only thing anybody has ever asked him is where the shelves are, and I have been told that by four different people who all said it as though they had checked.”

And then Nevin Hake said, “How does the factor know it has nineteen shelves and a plate on each.”

And Ilger Rusk said, “I have read the standing instruction. It was printed in the thirty-eighth week of last year and it went to about four hundred addresses in this county, and I have it at home, and nobody has ever asked me for it.”

The room looked at the man on the bench.

And the man who was not on the bench said, “I was in it.”

---

It is worth setting down what happened next exactly, because it is the whole of it, and because a man of thirty-one has spent two years in this world being the person who is nearest to a thing, and this is the first time he has been the reason a thing took a month to arrive.

“I was in it on the seventh day of the fortieth week,” said Ilyan Vester. “I went there at about the third bell to look at a plate, and I was still standing in the cold room at the sixth bell when a man of thirty-four came in with a slip in his hand. There is a man called Corrin Vask and he is forty-one and his right hand is six years gone at the wrist and he cannot open a cold room after three hours without doing it one finger at a time, and he is nine years in that building and ninepence a day, and he cannot read what is in the shelves, and he said so to me first, before I asked him anything, and he said that a shelf is not a door.”

“How long have you known,” said Wil Tamworth.

“Thirty-one days.”

Nobody said anything for about eleven seconds.

“You knew,” said Tamworth, “that a building four miles off was open to anybody, and it was full of every printed thing anybody in this county had produced in four years, and that you had stood in it and been told so by the man who sweeps it, and you have let a month go by in a district of six hundred people where a bench has been trying to find out what a category is.”

“Yes.”

“Why.”

And Ilyan Vester said the true thing, and it took him about four seconds, and the reason he has never got over it is not that it was the wrong reason.

“I did not know whether it would help,” he said. “And I have never in my life gone into a room with a thing before I knew whether it would help. I did it on a coast eleven times and it cost a woman a page of her own work and a man a day and a district three shillings and ninepence, and every one of the eleven was a sensible thing to do on the day. And this time I had four shillings and a leg and a wage of ninepence a day and about six weeks of arithmetic in my head, and I was waiting to be useful, and being useful and being early are not the same thing and I have known that for about two years and I have been wrong about it four or five times in a way that cost other people money. And I did not say it in this room because I did not know what it was for.”

“That is the honest answer,” said Nevin Hake, who had said the same words about himself in a room on the third day of the forty-first week, and who said them again in a yard in about four weeks, and who was not thanked for it then and was not thanked for it now.

---

And then Ilger Rusk said the thing that closed the door, and he said it in the trade's mouth, and he was right, and it is the reason there is not going to be a way out of this in a room this year.

“Read the shelves,” he said. “Go and read every sheet in that cold room, fourpence a sheet, all four years, all nine counties. You will find the word *exception* on about two hundred prints and you will find the word *uncooperative* on about eleven and you will be able to say out loud in a room, with a page in your hand, that a rule about a district which sends nobody is temporary and is marked so. And it will be true, and it will be in your hand, and it will be the most useful thing anybody in this district has ever held.”

He shut the bag.

“And it will not take one line out of one book about one party,” he said. “That is what nobody has understood, including me for about eleven years, and it is the whole of what happened to you on the thirty-fifth day. *Exception* takes a clause. It has always taken a clause. What is in that book is not a clause. It is a line about a party, and a line about a party is not printed, because a party is a person and a person is not printed, that is the entire arrangement of this country. And so there is nothing in any building in four counties that can take a line about a person out of a book, and I have looked, and I have been in eleven of them this year, and I am telling you because I am the mechanism and I would like it noticed that I am not the wall.”

---

And then somebody said the name out loud, and it was Perrin Gant, and he said it because a bench that has a fact says it in a room.

“Corrin Vask,” he said. “That is the man. That is the man who could get a hundred of that form into a hundred houses in this district if anybody asked him for them, and I have just said a name out loud in a room and none of us can do one single thing about it. It is six miles. It is a man with a key who is there two days out of seven and sweeps the floors and nobody knows which two days. It is a cart at about ninepence a mile, and this district has not got a cart and has not got ninepence, and the cut is five days off and it moves three thousand four hundred loads. And it is a man who cannot read what is in his own building, and if we sent him a hundred forms he would be able to hand them out at a counter to any person who walked in, and I am telling you that I cannot get a message to him in a day and neither can any of the four of us, and I have been trying for about four minutes, and that is not a thing I am good at and I have known that for nineteen years.”

Nobody in that room had a way of reaching him.

That is the shape of the afternoon, and it is worth being plain about it because it is not dramatic: a bench of four, a granary reeve, a factor with a good coat, a carter with one cart and a sheet, a woman of thirty-one who carries a basket, a man of thirty-one who cannot read, and a boy of thirteen who cannot read either, and there is a man with a key six miles off who sweeps a passage, and between the nine people and the one man there is a piece of paper about fourpence a sheet and a door with no name on it, and nobody in that room can pay fourpence, and nobody in that room can read a plate, and the reason none of them can do anything is not that the arrangement is cruel.

The arrangement is four hundred years old and it is in nobody's book, and it works, and it has worked this morning and will work again tomorrow, and the one thing it has never once needed is a person to want the right thing early.

# Chapter 182: A Hundred Of A Thing

A yard nine feet deep between the granary wall and a cart-shed wall, on the fourth day of the forty-seventh week, with a trestle in it and nobody on the stool, because the man who owns the yard was six miles up a road that goes north in a counting-house lane with a boy of nineteen, and had said he would be back by the second bell and had not said what he was going for.

There were about nine people in the yard. Four of them had come from the chandler’s shop and had come because four people had come. Two of them were nobody’s errand and had come for the noise. The rest were carters off the quay, standing the way four hundred men stand at a gate, and about four hundred carts in that district had nothing to do this week for the first time in seven.

What was on the trestle was a sheet of paper with a number at the top of it and nothing else on it that could be done to anybody, and it was not finished.

---

At about the third bell, in a counting-house lane in a city six miles off, a boy of nineteen put a tray down and said the thing nobody had asked him for, which was that nobody can have them.

“Nobody can have them,” said Amos Kellock. “And I want to be exact about why, because you have asked me the question properly and I am not going to answer it in a way that lets you go off and do it anyway.” He was nineteen and had been in the account’s own office for two years and was entitled to take depositions and was not entitled to anything else, and he said so before anybody asked him anything, which is the ordinary thing and is a thing that nobody has ever been allowed to improve on in this county. “The sheets go into a bag and the bag goes into a county and the man who sells them is a factor and he is not an officer of anything, and there is nine hundred and forty of them in a box somewhere that is not in this district, and the man who has the key is six miles off and sweeps a passage and cannot read what is in his own nineteen shelves and is there two days out of seven, and nobody in your district can get a message to him in a day. That is not a refusal. A refusal is a thing with a person in it. That is just where they are.”

“Then what is on this trestle,” said Ilyan Vester.

“The printer’s proof. Which is one sheet, and it is a sheet and not two, and it cost three pence to pull, and a hundred of them will cost three hundred pence.” Kellock turned the sheet round the way he turns everything, which is a quarter of a circle, so that the top of it is nearer the man than the bottom of it. “And before you have said the sum out loud, because I have watched a bench of four in this county do a sum in a doorway and get it wrong in public, it is three hundred pence. That is one pound and five shillings.”

“There was a figure said in a yard in this district in the forty-third week,” said Iven Brack, “that a hundred of these cost about two pounds five.”

“Then the figure said in the yard was wrong by three quarters of a pound,” said Amos Kellock, “and it is in a book in this city and I have not got the book open, and a yard is a bad place in which to say a price and it is a worse place in which to say a sum, and I have not come six miles to argue with a figure in a yard. Three hundred pence is three hundred pence. If you would both rather have said two pounds five then you have said it and it is done and it has cost you nothing.”

The sum was done three times after that, once in the lane outside that counter and twice in a yard nine feet deep between the granary wall and a cart-shed wall, and the third time was by a man of thirty-four who had come back from six miles with dust on him and stood at the trestle and did not touch the proof.

“Three hundred pence,” said Iven Brack. “A hundred sheets at threepence. And a hundred and one counties, and the same threepence in all of them, because it is the price of the sheets and not the price of the thing, and a man said about two pounds five in this yard once and it was wrong and he is not to be asked about it again because it is three in the morning of a life he has not had yet.” He put two fingers on the corner of the proof. “And a pound and five shillings is money. Where is it coming from.”

Nobody answered him, and about four people looked at the lane, and what the lane had on it that morning was a granary at nothing since the sixth day of the forty-third week and a reeve eleven weeks out of two pounds ten a year, which is ten shillings and sevenpence, and a second notice of four sheets at five shillings and fourpence that went out on the fourth day of the forty-sixth week and was never paid for.

“Then it is me,” said Iven Brack.

He said it the way he says a price, which is before the fact and in the open, and nobody in that yard believed him for about nine seconds.

“Listen to the whole of it before anybody in this yard starts being pleased with me. I have been one of the three hundred and thirty-nine for two years. I have a cart and I have four people and about a quarter of a pound of flour and about eighteen pounds saved out of a second cart since the spring before last, and eighteen pounds is a hundred and twenty loads on the register’s own scale and a hundred and twenty loads is the difference between one cart and two.” He turned the proof over with the back of one finger. “This sheet has a number at the top of it and nothing on it that can be done to me, and I have read it about four times this morning, which is three more times than I have read anything in my life, and it does not say my name anywhere, and that is the first piece of paper in four years that has not got my name on it.”

He put his hand flat on the trestle.

“I am not buying a hundred of these for this district because I am generous and I want that said first, before the rest of it, because after it has been said the other way I will not be able to make anybody hear it. I am buying a hundred of them because I have been one of three hundred and thirty-nine men in this county for two years and in two years not one single thing I did has ever been written down anywhere in a form anybody could produce, and the sheet I did buy is in a book in a city four miles off in a hand of my own, and the use of a forbearance on a load is worth three shillings on the register’s own scale and I moved sixty-one loads under it, and that is nine pounds and threepence, and I have never seen nine pounds and threepence in any room in this county, including the one it came out of.”

He looked round the yard.

“So I have got a number. For the first time in two years I have got a number of my own that is not a name, and a name is a person and a number is not, and I have not got the smallest idea yet whether that is a good thing for me and I have not got time to find out. I am paying a pound and five shillings for a hundred of them and I have got about two hundred and forty pounds of work in this county to hand them out against, and I am not going to be thanked for it and I am not going to be argued out of it, and if any man in this yard wants to put a better price on the same hundred sheets he can have the trestle.”

Nobody put a better price on the same hundred sheets.

---

Then they went back up the road that goes north, all of them, at about the fifth bell, because there was one question left and it belonged to the boy at the counter and he was not going to be asked it in a yard, and it took about nine minutes to ask and about nine minutes to answer, and nobody in either building has worked out yet what it is and that is the whole of what happened in this district in the last four days.

The question was asked at the counter in the ordinary way, standing up, by a man who cannot read, to a boy of nineteen, and it was not a question anybody had asked before.

“If the sheet said a number instead of a name.”

“The number of what,” said Amos Kellock.

“Of the thing. Of the sheet. The one that is numbered — one, two, three, up to a hundred. And there is no name on it at all, and there is nowhere on it for a name, and nobody has to write anything on it, ever, by anybody.”

Kellock did not answer for long enough that Ilyan Vester had worked out he was not going to, and then he did, and he answered it in the flat voice he uses for a card on a wall, because that is the voice he has for a thing he has read in a book.

“Yes,” he said. “There is a column for it. It is in the register and not in this building, and I have never seen it filled, and I have looked. The register is kept in this city by one man and he keeps it himself and there is no clerk on it, and I cannot show it to you and I am not going to pretend I have seen it filled.” He squared the tray with two fingers. “But I can tell you what a number does in a book, because I write numbers into a book about nine hundred times a day, and here it is and it is the plainest thing anybody has said to me this year.”

He put his hand flat on the counter.

“A name has to be spoken by somebody who has met the man. That is what a name is. It is not on the sheet — it is in people’s mouths, and every one of those mouths has to have met him, or has to have met somebody who has met him, and the second one is not good enough and everybody in this office knows it. A number does not. A number can be written into a book in a hand that has never seen the man, in a building he has never been in, in a year when he is not thinking about it, and a clerk who has never met him and never will can pull it out and read it out in a room and every person in that room can hear it and check it against the sheet.” He took his hand off the counter. “That is the whole of it. That is what a number is for. We use them for everything and we do not think about it once.”

“And nobody has to be in the room,” said Ilyan Vester.

“Nobody has to be in the room. That is not a flaw in it and I am not going to stand here and tell you it is a flaw in it, because in two years I have wanted about four hundred times to be able to put a thing in front of a room without a person in it and it has never once been possible and it has never once been possible because a person is what a record is made of.” He picked the tray up. “It cuts both ways and I am going to say the other half of it now, because you are about to go off down a road with a hundred of them and I would rather you had both halves in your head than one.”

He put the tray down again.

“If a number can be produced by a clerk who has never met the man, then a number can be produced against the man, in a room, years afterwards, by a clerk who has never met him, and there is nobody in that room who can stand up and say that is not him, because you cannot say that about a number. You can say it about a name — you can get a man in a room and ask him whether that is his name and he will say it is or it is not. There is nobody to ask about a number.” He looked up. “I am nineteen and I have been in this building two years and I have thought about that for about a year and I have not found the way round it and I am not going to pretend I have. It is the cheapest thing in the world to put on a person and it is the most useful thing in the world to put on a document, and it is the same piece of paper and it is the same hand, and whoever is holding the pen decides which of the two it turns out to be.”

The counter was quiet for about four seconds.

“Write that down,” said Ilyan Vester.

“No,” said Amos Kellock. “If I write it down it is a thing an officer of this account has said about a register, in a year, with a date on it, and in about nine years somebody will use it. You write it down. You have got a book.”

He had a form on the counter by then, and it was a sheet, and the sheet cost one shilling and fourpence, and it was not one of the hundred and the hundred were not one of it, and both of those facts were true and both of them were printed on the same morning in a city six miles off.

---

The printing was done on the fourth day of the forty-seventh week and the sheets came off a stone in a shed off the second mouth and were dry by the fifth bell, and they were numbered from one to a hundred in the printer's own hand, and the printer was a man of about sixty who had printed four thousand of another thing in the thirty-first week of last year and had nine hundred and forty of them left and could not tell anybody where they were and had never been asked twice.

The face of one of them is this, and there is no word at the top of it, and there is a number, and there is nowhere on it for a name:

> RYEFOLD. NO. ___ OF ONE HUNDRED.
> ENTERED AGAINST THE ACCOUNT AT SIX MILES UP THE ROAD THAT GOES NORTH. ONE SHILLING AND FOURPENCE A SHEET.
> NOTHING IS TO BE WRITTEN ON THIS SHEET BY THE PERSON WHO TAKES IT.

The last line was the printer's and not the district's, and the printer said it was his, and he said it in a shed, and he said it because he had printed the other four thousand and had watched what happened to them, and nobody had asked him and nobody has thanked him and there is a hundred and one counties in which that sheet could be printed and there is not one person in any of them who knows his name.

And at the seventh bell the reeve shut the second door and read the board before she shut it, and the figure on the board was the same figure it was yesterday, and there is a man in this district who has been paid nine pence a day for about nine weeks and has not worked out what it is for and has not asked.

# Chapter 165: Set Aside

The corridor outside the chamber at Orison is nine feet wide and eleven yards long and has a bench in it that is not for sitting on, and Ilyan Vester stood at the north end of it at about the second bell on the first day of the forty-fifth week with a leg that had not been his own since a morning four hundred miles and some weeks behind him.

Nobody sent for him. That is the whole of it and it is worth being exact, because there are four words for what happened to him that month and three of them are wrong.

He was not a party. He was not named on the notice. He was not on a list, and he had asked to be on no list, and there is no form anywhere in this county for a man to put himself in a room where a thing about his own district is being decided, and the absence of that form is not a mistake and is not a failure and has a name that nobody has needed. He was not produced. Nobody sent a boy. He had walked four miles on a leg because a bench of four had asked him in a room to be in a room, and he was there, and the reason he was there is that he did not trust himself to hear about it later.

Osa Trin came past him at about the second bell with the application in her hand and did not stop and did not look at him and did not say good morning, and he understood all three of those, and about two hours later, in the same corridor, he understood two more.

---

The room is up nine steps and it is not a court and it is not a bench of four. It is a room with a table at the top of it, a public notice on the door, a chair for the person sitting, and a row of chairs along one wall for people who have come to watch, and about eleven people were in it including the man in the chair.

The notice on the door was the four sheets, on one nail, and the four sheets had cost a district of six hundred people five shillings and fourpence, and the thing they had bought was the right to be in the room, and everybody in the room was in it because of that sheet, including the people from other counties, whose sheets cost their districts the same.

The chair was a person of about sixty-two called Aldis Fenner and he had sat in that chair at the fourth sitting twice a year for nineteen years and his job was to keep a list of what had been said and his clerk wrote it, and about half of what Fenner did was arithmetic in his own head about how much time was left before the sixth bell.

Osa Trin made the application. It is four sentences and she read them out standing up, in the voice she has used to read things in that room over a chandler's shop since she was nineteen, and the four sentences are these, and they are true, and every one of them is in the page the bench swore on the twenty-second day and they do not say a word she did not write herself.

That the district applied for the year and received it, and spent it on carriage, and the carriage belonged to other people.

That the district is a tenant of a bank of the second mouth and holds a granary on a lease, and the lease is not renewed, and the renewal is a fortieth of everything in the granary.

That the account is nine thousand seven hundred pounds and has been for about a year, and that the district has never been told the annual charge on it, and that the annual charge is four hundred and eighty-five pounds, and that the district cannot pay it out of a cut and never could.

And that the district asks for one more year.

Nobody in that room said anything unkind about the district. That is the part that is hard to hold. A man of fifty-one called Ansel Cork stood up for the account and said four things and every one of them was true, and the third one was that the district chose its carts and the account did not, and the fourth one was that a district that cannot pay four hundred and eighty-five pounds a year is not a district that will be able to pay four hundred and eighty-five pounds a year next year, and that this is not an argument against the district, it is a description of the district, and he was sorry about the description and not about the debt.

Osa Trin said one thing back and it was the whole of her contribution. She said, “The district has not come to a sitting in four hundred years and it has come to this one, and it has paid five shillings and fourpence for the privilege, and I would like the room to know which of those two facts is the one that has happened before.”

Then her part was over. That is procedure and it is not anybody's invention: a party's representative is heard on the application, and then she is asked whether she wishes to remain while the parties answer on a matter of form, and she said no, because she has been in eleven rooms in nineteen years and she knows what the question is, and she went out into the corridor and sat down on the bench that is not for sitting on.

---

And then the account's office entered a thing, and it was nineteen minutes old, and it had been read four times this morning by a man of nineteen, and it was true.

The chair asked what the account relied on. Ansel Cork said the word on the wall, and the word on the wall is what a party that does not appear is carried as, and then he said, in the ordinary way, that the account also had a deposition, and that the account's office was entitled to enter it, and that it was in the room and anybody who wished might read it.

Aldis Fenner did not read it. He asked for it to be read out, in the ordinary way, because there were people at the back who had come to watch and a document read aloud is a document.

Amos Kellock stood up. He had a file under his arm. He was nineteen and he had been in the account's office for two years and he had entered about four hundred boxes in nineteen shelves' worth of returns and he had never once been asked to read anything out to a room, and he read it out.

> FORM OF DEPOSITION. ONE SHEET. ONE SHILLING AND FOURPENCE. ENTERED BY A PERSON OF AN OFFICE AND SWORN BY THE PERSON NAMED.
>
> THE MATTER. THE FORBEARANCE OF THE DISTRICT OF RYEFOLD, LISTED FOR THIS SITTING.
>
> THE PERSON NAMED. A STRANGER, AND NOT A PERSON OF THE DISTRICT.
>
> THE DAY OF THE CONVERSATION. THE SECOND DAY OF THE FORTY-SECOND WEEK.
>
> WHERE. AT THE COUNTER OF THE CHAMBER OF PLENTY IN THIS CITY, AT ABOUT THE SECOND BELL.
>
> WHAT WAS SAID, AS THE PERSON NAMED HAS STATED IT. THAT HE ASKED WHAT A PARTY WHICH DOES NOT APPEAR AT A SITTING WOULD BE CARRIED AS, AND WAS INFORMED THAT A PARTY WHICH DOES NOT APPEAR IS CARRIED AS UNCOOPERATIVE; AND THAT HE ASKED WHETHER A DISTRICT WHICH CANNOT AFFORD THE JOURNEY IS ENTERED THE SAME AS A DISTRICT WHICH HAS CHOSEN NOT TO COME, AND WAS INFORMED THAT THE RECORD DOES NOT DISTINGUISH THE TWO; AND THAT HE SAID: A DISTRICT THAT SENDS NOBODY HAS DECIDED IT HAS NOTHING TO SAY, AND A DECISION IS A THING A PERSON MAKES. I WILL NOT STAND IN A ROOM AND CALL THAT ANYTHING ELSE.
>
> AND THAT THE PERSON NAMED STATED THAT THE SENTENCE WAS HIS OWN AND NOT THE WORD OF ANY OFFICE, AND THAT HE SAID IT IN THE ABSTRACT AND NOT OF ANY DISTRICT NAMED.
>
> ENTERED BY A. KELLOCK, AN OFFICER OF THIS ACCOUNT, THIS DAY, FROM THE CHAMBER'S OWN WEEKLY POSTING OF ITS COUNTER MEMORANDA, WHICH IS DELIVERED TO THIS OFFICE EVERY WEEK AND IS READ BY NOBODY IN EITHER OFFICE.

Nobody moved for about eleven seconds. Roald Tern, twenty-six, was standing at the back of that room against the wall because it was his own counter's matter and he had come out from behind it for the first time in two years, and he had put both hands in his coat, and he did not say one word then or afterwards, and there is nobody alive who will ever ask him what he thought.

Aldis Fenner asked four questions, and about half of them were not questions, and the ones that were, were these.

“Is the person named in that deposition a person who has said that this district will not come to a sitting.”

“No,” said Amos Kellock.

“Has he said a district that does not come has refused.”

“He has not used the word *refused*,” said Amos Kellock. “He has used the word *decided*, and he has said out loud that a decision is a thing a person makes, and the last three lines of the third box are his own hand and not mine, and the last two lines of the fourth box are his own hand and not mine, and I put the first half of the sentence in the third box and he put the second half, and I would like this room to know that neither of us has any power over what this room does with the whole of it.”

“Did the account direct it.”

“No. The account did not direct it. I entered it in nineteen minutes this morning because I am entitled and because a party may enter a deposition, and I have thought about it for about a day and I have not slept and I have come here on purpose.”

“Was it true when you entered it.”

“It was true when I entered it,” said Amos Kellock. “It has not stopped being true since. It is his sentence and I have read it four times this morning and it is exactly what he said, and I would like the room to know that I have checked it four times, because I have not got the time for anybody to say afterwards that I wrote a thing that was not there.”

“It is not disputed,” said Aldis Fenner.

He said it in the ordinary voice, in the way a man says a thing that has never once been in dispute in his life, and he was right, and about eleven people in that room understood at the same moment that they had just heard the only sentence that had been said in that room which was about all of them, and that it had not helped anybody at all.

“None of it is disputed,” he said. “Not one word of it. Not the conversation, not the day, not the counter, not the words, not the fact that the man said them about himself and not about a district. None of it is disputed and none of it is going to be decided by anybody in this room, and the question before this room is not what that man said at a counter. The question before this room is what a district is, when it sends nobody.”

---

And the minute says this, and the minute is one sheet, and the minute is the only copy in the world, and the district has it and does not know that it has it, because nobody in that room was under any obligation to give it to anybody and nobody thought of it.

> THE CLAIM OF THE DISTRICT OF RYEFOLD FOR A FORBEARANCE IS SET ASIDE, THE DISTRICT HAVING BEEN CARRIED IN THIS MATTER AS A PARTY UNCOOPERATIVE, UPON THE DEPOSITION ENTERED IN THIS MATTER THIS DAY BY THE ACCOUNT’S OWN OFFICE, AND UPON THE SENTENCE OF THE PERSON NAMED IN IT, WHICH IS THAT A DISTRICT WHICH SENDS NOBODY HAS DECIDED IT HAS NOTHING TO SAY.
>
> THE ACCOUNT IS DISCHARGED FROM THE MATTER WITHOUT A HEARING ON ITS MERITS.
>
> THE COST OF THE NOTICE OF FOUR SHEETS REMAINS WITH THE DISTRICT.

It was not refused. That is the word the room used, four times, and it is the correct word, and a claim that is set aside is not a claim that has been heard and found wanting. It is a claim that has not been had. There is no hearing on the merits. There is nothing to appeal, because an appeal is a thing you take to a person higher than the one who did it, and this was not done by a person. It was done by a category, and the category had been in the room the whole time, on a wall, since before any of the people in it were born.

And the year did not stop.

Four hundred and eighty-five pounds did not stop, and the district is at nothing, having spent its last nine pounds on the sixth day of the forty-third week to a carter who is owed forty-five pounds two shillings and eightpence besides, and the district's own grain has to do the ration now out of the bin it is about to sell, and a man on a quay on the sixth day of the forty-second week said out loud that this would happen and that the ration would run out four days early, and he was right, and he gave the right reason, and there is no part of this in which anybody in that room was thinking about a quay.

Ansel Cork said, at the end, in the way of a man closing a folder, that the account's position had not altered, and that the account was not a party to any ill will, and that the account would prefer that district to apply again and be heard, and that the account had put nine thousand people into a winter meal last year and had the receipts, and that it was the fourth time it had said that in about a year and it would keep saying it.

And Aldis Fenner said that the fourth sitting had eleven minutes left in it and that the next matter was called.

---

The door of the room opened at about the sixth bell and the people in it came out into the corridor, and there were about nine of them, and among them was a man of thirty-one with a bad leg who had been in that building since the second bell and was not a party to any part of it, and against the wall was a woman of thirty-nine from a district six miles up a towpath who had been in the corridor for about three hours with a piece of paper in her hand and her thumb under a line.

She did not ask him to wait. She did not ask him anything.

What he said, after about nine minutes, standing up, at the north end of a nine-foot corridor, to a person he had known for a month, was: “You read it four sentences and you wrote all four and you were right about all four.”

“Yes,” said Osa Trin. “They had a deposition.”

And then neither of them said one word for about forty seconds, and the corridor was nine feet wide, and then she said, “It is the fourth sitting. There is no second one this year,” in the tone of a woman who has read a form and is reporting the date on it, and went down nine steps and out into a street in a city of about ninety thousand people and started walking home, which is four miles, and she got there at about the ninth bell.

And the sentence, the one that did it, is in a deposition in a file in a yard six miles up a road north of a district of six hundred people, and in a minute in a room up nine steps, and it is in a counter's memorandum in a chamber four miles from here that gets posted once a week to a building where it is read by nobody, and here is the whole of it, word for word, as it was read out in that room at about the fourth bell on the first day of the forty-fifth week by a man of nineteen who had checked it four times and did not know what it was for:

*A district that sends nobody has decided it has nothing to say, and a decision is a thing a person makes. I will not stand in a room and call that anything else.*

And here is the same sentence as the minute has it, which is the sentence that set aside a district of six hundred people's year, and which is true, and which is the only reason anybody in that room needed:

*A district which sends nobody has decided it has nothing to say.*

Four words are missing and the four words are the four words he said last, and the four words he said last were the ones that made it a man's sentence instead of a rule's.

# Chapter 192: What A Year Is In This County

The forty-ninth week of this grain year is the third week running in which about four hundred carts in this district have had nothing to do, and the week before it was the first such week in seven years and the week before that was the second, and there is no fourth run of three like it in a grain year and nobody in this district is making any use of it.

Marda Fen had a broom in her hand and about seven thousand loads behind her and a beam at the near end of a yard and a bin-board on the wall of the second door with a figure on it that had said the same thing since the sixth day of the forty-third week, which is nothing, and she counts what is in the bins every day and has counted it every day for twenty-two years and nobody in this district has ever asked her what a load is worth.

He asked her the question at the second door at about the fourth bell, which is late in a working morning and the hour she answers questions in, and she did not stop working while she answered it, and the answer took about four minutes, and nobody had asked her a question of that shape before in twenty-two years.

“What is a year worth,” said Ilyan Vester.

She put the broom against the wall.

“That is a bad question to ask a reeve before the second bell,” she said. “It is not a bad question. It is a question about two things and you have not told me which one you mean, and both of them have got a figure on them and neither of them is mine.”

“Both.”

“All right.” She took a piece of chalk out of her apron and did not use it. “I have been a reeve in this building for twenty-two years. A year in this county is not a period of time. A year is a unit of account, and it has been priced four times since the thirty-ninth week of last year, and I have been the only person in this district who has known all four prices, and not one of the four has ever been read out to me in a room.”

“Four times.”

“Four. Come and stand at the beam and I will say them where the lamp is.” She went out into the yard and he came after her and there were nine people in that yard, because there always are, and there was a cart on the beam and a carter waiting and she said one word to him and he waited, and that is what happens at a weighbridge about nine times a week.

She said them off her fingers, in order, the way she says every figure, and she said the dates out loud before the figures, and the dates were the dates.

“First. The thirty-ninth week of last year. The district applied and got it and the price of it was four hundred and sixty-two pounds, and the whole of that was a sheet nobody in this district has ever read, and I have never seen it either, and I have been in this building twenty-two years and I did not know until a man in a room four miles off told me in a lane that we had been given a year at all.”

“Second,” she said. “The forty-sixth week of this year. That is what the year was spent on. Three thousand loads of hired carriage at three shillings a load, which is four hundred and fifty pounds, and about ten pounds of weigh and loading, and the bill is four hundred and sixty pounds. The carts were not ours. Everybody in this yard knows whose carts they were and about four hundred of those men are standing in it.”

He said, “The difference is two pounds.”

“The difference is two pounds,” said Marda Fen, “and I have known that since the forty-third week and nobody in this district has ever said it to me and I am not going to be the first one to say it in a yard.”

“Third. The same forty-sixth week, or near it. The hire went on the account, because the account is the only credit a tenant district has. The account was nine thousand two hundred and forty pounds and the charge was four hundred and sixty-two pounds a year. It is now nine thousand seven hundred pounds and the charge is four hundred and eighty-five pounds a year, and the twenty-three pounds a year is the carts, and the year that was suspended is not on the credit side anywhere, because the year was spent, and a spent year is not a thing that comes back.”

“Fourth. The thirty-fifth day, in a room four miles off. A claim was set aside. It was not refused and it was not granted and the word the chair used was set aside, and the reason was a line on a party that has been on the account’s own book for nineteen years, and the price of that is four hundred and eighty-five pounds a year at the close of every grain year, and it does not stop for anything, and not one of those four prices was ever read out to anybody in this district, and I am the reeve and I have just read four of them out to a man with a basket.”

Nobody in that yard said anything, and the carter on the beam put his hand on the cart and waited.

---

The man with the basket asked the next question and it was the one he had walked four miles to ask and it took him about nine seconds to get to it and he got there.

“Then what does a year cost a person.”

Marda Fen looked at him for about four seconds and then she said the thing, and it is the thing she has been getting to for nine years and she got it in about eleven words.

“A year costs the same as a year,” she said. “There is no other price of it in this county. There is four hundred and eighty-five pounds a year on nine thousand seven hundred pounds, and there is two pounds, and there is a year of a man’s wages, which for me is two pounds ten and eleven weeks of it unpaid, which is ten shillings and sevenpence, and there is a year of a carter’s, which nobody in this district has ever written down, and there is a year of a wheelwright’s, which is a day and a boy of sixteen who cannot turn a wheel without him, and every one of those is a year and the only one of them that has ever been put in a column is mine.”

She put the chalk back in her apron.

“So if you have come to this door to find out what a year costs, you have found out that it costs whatever the thing is that somebody is paying, and the only person in this district who can tell you what a year of you is worth is a man who has been given a price and has not asked anybody whether it was fair, and that is you, and I have known you for ten weeks and I have not asked you a single question about what you are carrying and I am not going to today.”

“That is fair,” said Ilyan Vester.

“It is not fair or not,” said Marda Fen. “It is the first day of a week and I have a beam. Get off it.”

---

They weighed four carts between the fourth bell and the sixth and the numbers went into a book that has three columns in it and none of them is a year, and nine people stood about in the yard and a hundred yards off four hundred carts were standing in other yards doing nothing, and the whole of the price of a year in the district of Ryefold on the first day of the forty-ninth week was said out loud once, by the one person who had known all four of the numbers, to a man who cannot read, in about four minutes, and it cost the district nothing and it bought the district nothing and it is the first time it has been said in twenty-two years.

And then she said the other half of it, to the beam, and it is the half that is the reason she is not going to do it again.

“Do not ask me that in a room,” she said. “I have answered a quay once. I came down to a quay in the forty-fifth week of this year with four hundred and eighty-five pounds in my mouth and a notice at five shillings and fourpence and a hundred loads short, and about two hundred and forty people stood there and heard every figure and not one of them knew what to do with any of it, and I said I would not do it again for a month and it is now the forty-ninth week and I have had two sittings and a day with a clerk in a lane and I am going to say a different thing and then I am not going to say it again for a year.”

She looked along the yard at the four hundred carts.

“A cut is seven days and a cut is in the forty-sixth week, and every cart in this county with a sheet in a book has about five weeks to think about what happens if there is a bad one, and there has not been a bad week, and the queue is the first bad week, and everybody in this yard knows both halves of that and not one of us has said it out loud in a yard and I have been in this building twenty-two years and I am not going to be the one.” She picked the broom up. “I will say the four prices. I will say them four times a year for as long as I am standing at this beam. And I will not do it in a room, and I will not do it in a cut, and if a man in a good coat comes down this yard again I am going to give him the four prices and then I am going to go back to the beam and he can do what he likes with them, because that is what a reeve is. A reeve is not a room. A reeve is a beam.”

---

At about the seventh bell she read the board before she shut the door, which is what she does, and the figure on it was the same figure it was yesterday and it will be the same figure tomorrow and it is the figure that has been on that wall in chalk since the fourth day of the forty-first week, and there is a man in this district who has not worked out why a man who cannot read has a line on a wage board and there is nobody in that lane who is ever going to tell him.

And the granary shut behind her with about seven thousand loads in it and one pair of hands and four hundred carts in the yard, and a lease at a fortieth of what was in that building in the forty-third week, which nobody had renewed and nobody had refused and nobody had been told was there, which is about a hundred and seventy-five loads, and a fortnight in which about four hundred men in about nine hundred yards could walk four miles off a road and had not yet been asked by anybody to walk any of them.

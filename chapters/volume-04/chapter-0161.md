# Chapter 161: The Notice

The room above the chandler's shop has a nail in the north wall with a lease notice on it, and the notice has been on that nail for ten days, and nobody has taken it down, and on the fourth day of the forty-third week there was a second thing on the same nail.

Perrin Gant said so out loud before anybody said anything else, standing up, in the voice he used for the bench, which is not much louder than the voice he uses for a man on a towpath.

“That is one nail,” he said. “I am saying it now, at the top, because in about four years somebody is going to come in here and find two nails, and one of them is going to be in a drawer, and I am not going to be here.”

Osa Trin had brought the second thing up from the chamber of Plenty at the fourth bell and she put it on the nail and read it standing up with her thumb under the line, and she read it once and then read it again with her thumb somewhere else, which is what a person does who has been asked to find a thing and cannot find it.

Nobody thanked her for bringing it and nobody asked her to take the old one down.

---

The thing she brought was four sheets, and they were the notice, and the notice is the whole of what a district gets in exchange for five shillings and fourpence, and here it is, because the district has paid for it and the district is going to be held to most of what is on it.

> TO EVERY PARTY TO THE MATTER OF THE FORBEARANCE OF THE DISTRICT OF RYEFOLD. THE FOURTH SITTING WILL HEAR AN APPLICATION MADE BY A PERSON OF THE DISTRICT, AND THE DISTRICT MUST BE REPRESENTED AT THE SITTING BY A PERSON OF THE DISTRICT. A DISTRICT WHICH IS NOT REPRESENTED AT THE SITTING IS CARRIED AS UNCOOPERATIVE. FOUR SHEETS. ONE SHILLING AND FOURPENCE A SHEET.

“Eleven days,” said Osa Trin. “They gave me the date on a slip and I have written it on the back of this in my own hand and I am reading it out so that it is in this room in somebody’s mouth. The fourth sitting is on the thirty-fifth day. That is eleven days from the day this went on the nail.”

“Two hundred and forty miles?” said Wil Tamworth.

“Four,” said Nevin Hake. “It is four miles to the court and I have walked it.”

“You have not walked it since you had that cart put on the road in the third week of the last cut,” said Perrin Gant, “and you did it in an afternoon and it did you no good, and I am not going to let a man of forty-six do it twice to be interesting. Four miles. It is four miles and there is about a bus that goes up past the second mouth on the days the market is on and I do not know which days the market is on and neither do you.”

“There is no bus to the court,” said Osa Trin. “There is a bus to the second mouth. The court is up a lane off it and the lane is four hundred yards and there is nowhere in the lane to stand out of the rain.”

The room did the thing that this room does, which is to go quiet, and which Nevin Hake has said in this bench’s hearing is the most useful instrument any of them own.

---

What the notice said, once it was read, was four things, and three of them were ordinary and the fourth one was a door that could only be opened by one person at a time, and the door was this.

To be heard, the district had to send somebody. To send somebody, somebody had to be paid a day, because a day is a day and the towpath is six miles each way. The person who went would be gone from the granary and the quay and the bench for one day, and the wages on the granary board are paid at the end of the week, and the account holds about nine pounds.

“Five shillings and fourpence for the notice,” said Nevin Hake. “Nine pounds in the account. Marda is eleven weeks out on two pounds ten a year.”

“Then it is five shillings and fourpence against nine pounds,” said Perrin Gant, “and against a wage that is nobody’s here, and against a day. I want that on the bench’s book and not in a speech. Five shillings and fourpence, one day, and one person.”

“Two things,” said Wil Tamworth. “A day is a day. And the notice is four sheets at a shilling and fourpence a sheet because it is a *sheet*, and there is nothing in those four sheets that this district could not have written itself in one, and I have said that in this room before and nobody has written it down, so I am writing it down now in the sense that I am saying it and being wrong if I am.”

“You are not wrong,” said Osa Trin, “and it does not matter, and that is the whole of what has happened in this room in about four years.”

---

And then it came out that nobody could read the form, and everybody in the room had known that since the third day of the forty-first week, and nobody had said so, because there had been nothing on the table that a person could be needed for.

The application is one sheet. It has to be made by a person of the district. It has to be signed by that person. And the district has to be represented at the sitting, and representation is a person of the district who may be asked questions and may answer for six hundred people.

“I can read it,” said Osa Trin.

“Yes,” said Perrin Gant.

“I have not said I would sign it.”

“I did not ask you.” He put his hand flat on the bench’s own book, which was shut, which it always was, and which was the only book in that room that nobody in that room could read and which he had never once in nineteen years said so. “I am going to say the whole of it in one go and then nobody is to interrupt me for four minutes, and at the end of it there are going to be two people in this room who have to do two halves of one thing, and I am the man who has to do one of the halves, and I am telling you which one before I say why.”

---

Here is the difficulty and it is not a mystery and Perrin Gant said so twice, once each way.

The person who goes is the person who can read. Osa Trin is the only one of the four of them who can read. There is no other person in this district who can read a form. There has not been another one since the woman who kept the book before her went eleven years ago, and she could read a form and she read forms in this room for nothing for thirty years, and there is nobody left who can say what that was like, and that is the whole of what a district of six hundred people is made of in one sentence.

The person who signs is the person the bench trusts. That is Perrin Gant, and it is not a compliment, it is a fact about four hundred years: the convenor signs, because the convenor's name is the one anybody in another county will look up.

And a person cannot be both, and not because of the rules. Because the bench would send its convenor to a sitting four miles off in a market week to stand in a room and be questioned about something he cannot read, and that is not a thing anybody does to a man of sixty-one who has kept their book for nineteen years; and the bench cannot send Osa Trin, because Osa Trin is not a person it can send, because she is not a person to it, because in nineteen years that bench has never once asked her what she thinks, and asking a person to stand in a room and answer for six hundred people is the largest thing a bench can ask, and this one has never asked her anything at all.

“I will not go and be told,” said Osa Trin. “That is what I am refusing and I would like it written down that I said the words *be told*, because Gant is going to say afterwards that I refused on grounds of health.”

“Nobody is writing anything down,” said Gant.

“*You* are not,” said Osa Trin. “You are sixty-one.”

---

Nobody shouted and nobody accused anybody of anything, and that is how a room in this district has behaved for four hundred years, and it is not a decoration.

Nevin Hake said that he could drive a cart to the court and that the cart would be two days out of the granary and that a man in a district of six hundred who spends two days of a cut in a lane four miles off is a man who has to be told what to do by the other three, and that this bench had better ask itself whether it wants that, and that he was the one in the room who would have to answer it.

Wil Tamworth said that the bench had asked for something to do that mattered in his first year and had been given notices for nine years, and that a notice about a notice was not it, and that if the only thing this district could put in a room in a city was one woman and one sheet then the district had better decide today that it was a district that sends one person and not a district, and say so out loud, because a thing said out loud is a thing a person chose.

Gant said: “I did not agree to that.”

“You agreed to it when you read it out,” said Tamworth, “and I am the fourth of the four and I have been outvoted in this room eleven times and I have got a very good memory for the wording of the minority.”

And Perrin Gant, who was sixty-one and who had been convenor for nineteen years and who had never once in nineteen years put a thing on a table that he did not know was true, put his hand flat on a shut book and said:

“Osa Trin. You will make the application and you will read it out and you will sign it, and I will not question one word of it, and I am going to be answerable for the whole of it in a room four miles off to a man I have never met, on your word, and I have never done that in nineteen years and I am going to do it, and I would like the room to understand that I have thought about it for about two days and I have not slept much and I am not being brave, I am being sixty-one.”

“I have not agreed to any of it,” said Osa Trin, “and I am not going to agree to any of it in about a day, and you have put me on a notice for a day in eleven days and there is no more time than that, and I will tell you tonight.”

“You will not,” said Perrin Gant, “because there is nobody else.”

---

The lease came up at about the half hour after the sixth bell and it came up because the bench had nothing else to do with the last half hour, and it came up as a thing that had already been decided by nobody.

The tenancy ended on the seventh day of the forty-second week. Nobody had said so. The notice on the nail said the renewal was at the discretion of the account, and a discretion is not a date and it is not a refusal, and the district has been holding a granary and a bank of the second mouth for four days on a thing nobody has exercised.

“How long is the discretion for,” said Tamworth.

“Nobody knows,” said Osa Trin. “There is no line on that sheet. I have read it four times and I have got it off the nail and held it up to the window and it says the renewal is at the discretion of the account and it does not say how long a discretion runs, and there is no letter under it, and this bench has never had a letter under anything in my time.”

“So there is no week of grace,” said Nevin Hake.

“There is no week of grace and there is no week without it either,” said Osa Trin. “The account has not said no. The account has not said yes. It has got a discretion and it is going to keep it in its pocket until the cut is over, because after the cut it knows exactly what is in that granary, and before the cut it does not, and I will not say I would do the same and I have thought about it for about a minute and I would do the same.”

The price of the renewal is a fortieth of everything in the granary. The granary holds about eleven thousand loads. A fortieth of eleven thousand is two hundred and seventy-five loads, payable in grain and not in money, before the cut. And there is no line anywhere on that sheet saying what a district may do instead, and two of the four of them tried to write one in the autumn before last and gave up, and what Wil Tamworth wrote that is still folded up in the bench's own book, in a hand nobody can read except hers, and what it says is *that a district which cannot pay a fortieth in grain may pay it in labour*, and there is no such thing in this county as paying a rent in labour, and he knows that, and it is in the book.

Nobody read that out loud. It was not on the table.

---

And then the boy came.

Alick Odell is thirteen and he is the son of the woman who keeps the key to the chandler's stair and he is the fastest thing on that towpath and he has carried two hundred and fifty notices up it in the last two years. He came in at about the seventh bell with the money in a jar and the notice on his back, because a notice has to be served on four other parties and the only way to serve a party at a granary four miles up a road in the last week of a market month is to walk it.

“Four sheets,” he said. “And a shilling and fourpence a sheet, and the man at the counter said the price is the same in every county, and he said it twice.”

“Who gave you the money,” said Perrin Gant.

“The reeve. And she read the board before she wrote on it, and she said to say that the five shillings and fourpence is against the wage line and not against the granary, and she said to say that if anybody in this room wants to know what is left in the account they can come and stand in my doorway and I will read it to them, and I do not know why she said it and I have been thinking about it on the road.”

The room looked at each other.

“Say it again,” said Osa Trin.

“She said: come and stand in my doorway and I will read it to you.”

“That is not a thing she would say,” said Nevin Hake.

“It is the first thing she has said to me in about two years that I have written down,” said the boy, “and I have it here.” He took a folded paper out of his coat and gave it to him, and it was about a quarter of the size of a notice and it had a number on it and a name on it and it was the one thing Ilyan Vester had ever seen written about himself in a district he had been in for three weeks.

> VESTER. NINE PENCE. THE FOURTH OF THE FORTY-THIRD.

He looked at it for a while and could not read it, and did not say that he could not read it, and put it in his coat.

And Perrin Gant said the name out loud, because a bench that has decided something says it in a room.

“Osa Trin,” he said. “Thirty-fifth day. One person. That is what the notice says and that is what I have said and there is no more time than the notice allows and no more than about eleven days.”

And then Nevin Hake said, because it was the only thing in the room that had not been said and he had been sitting on it since the fourth bell:

“Who writes down what she says when she gets back.”

The room did not go quiet at that. The room did something else, and what it did was look at the fourth of the four of them who could read, and she was standing up by a nail with a thumb under a line, and she had not been asked.

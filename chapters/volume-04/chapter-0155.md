# Chapter 155: The End On It

The towpath is six miles each way and there are about two hours of light in the middle of it and a basket that weighs about forty pounds when it is full and about eleven when it is not.

On the twelfth day of the forty-first week he had it full, going up, and the leg was his own for about four hours of the six, and he noticed it, and he told nobody, and he did not make anything of it.

He had worked that out a long time ago and it was not a discovery and it was not a cure: the leg goes when he is carrying a thing that somebody else is going to have to read, and it comes back when the carrying stops. That was a notice down a road once, four years ago, and a paper cleared it for about four hours, and the finding that went in beside it was that the paper was the weight and the woman was not, and that the woman was still in his leg afterwards and she is still in his leg now.

This time there was no woman in it at all. There was a basket of numbers that nobody on either end of this towpath could read, and about nine of them were a bench of four, and they were going into a room in Orison in somebody’s hands, and the leg had gone four hundred miles ahead of him and had cleared and gone and cleared again on the strength of it.

He thought about that for about a mile and it did not resolve into anything, and then the sun came out and he put his weight back where it belonged and finished the walk.

The abstract was four sheets and it had cost her nothing and him nothing and it was in his coat.

He could not read it. He had not read it. It had been in his coat for two days and he had looked at it four times and he had got one word in four, the way he got one word in four off a plate in a cold room, and the word he had got was *year*, and he had got it three times out of the four, and the fourth time he had got it wrong and had spent about twenty minutes wishing he had not looked.

He took it to the bench on the twelfth, at about the fourth bell, and he did not put it on the table, and it took him about a minute and a half to work out why, and he worked it out by himself and nobody helped him, and it is worth saying how: the thing on the table would have been read by whoever picked it up, and the only person in that room who could read it was thirty-nine years old and had spent her working life reading things out loud for nothing, and the room had spent the morning deciding that this was a thing that had been done to her too often.

He put it on the table.

“There is a thing on that table,” he said, “and four of you cannot read it, and one of you can, and I am not going to ask her to read it out in a room. I am going to ask her to take it away for a night and read it and come back tomorrow, and I am going to ask her to say whether she is willing to before I ask her for anything else.”

Osa Trin did not touch it.

“I have been the reader in this district since I was nineteen,” she said, “and I have never once been asked, and I have read things in this room out loud to three people who did not want to hear them about nine hundred times, and I have never been asked, and I want you to understand that I have noticed that about nine hundred times as well.”

“Then I am asking.”

“Yes,” said Osa Trin. “I am willing to take it away for a night. I am not willing to read it out. I am not willing to have it in this room with the three of them listening to me do the thing I have done nine hundred times for nothing, and I would like it noticed that the difference between those two things is about four hours of my life and not about the truth of it.”

“Four hours is too much,” said Perrin Gant, from the chair, and he said it in the voice of a man who has been convener for nineteen years and has never once been thanked for anything and does not expect to be. “Take it. Read it tonight. Bring it back in the morning. Do not read it out, and do not make a copy for anybody, and do not tell us what is in it before you have had the night, and I will sit here tomorrow morning and take whatever you have to say however bad it is and I will not ask you to be careful about it.”

“It will not be careful about it,” she said.

“No,” said Perrin Gant. “It will not be careful about it. That is why I have asked you for the night and not for the half hour.”

---

He did not sleep, and it was not the leg, and at about the second bell in the morning he walked the six miles down the towpath and up the quay with nothing in his hands and his own leg, which was now the ordinary condition of him and had been for about a year and a half, and he was at the door of the second granary at the fourth bell.

Marda Fen was in and she had been up since the second and she had not sent for anybody.

“Sit down,” she said. “I am going to say it to you first and I am going to say it in about ninety seconds and then I am going to go and tell those four, and I want you to hear it in this room first because in about six days this will be a thing that four people know and a stranger does not, and I would rather it went the other way for one morning.”

“Go on.”

“The year ended fourteen days ago.” She said it flat, with her hand on the box the chalk was in. “The thirty-ninth week. It is on the abstract, it is in the fourth sheet, in the third line, and it is a date and not a condition and there is nothing after it.

> FOURTH SHEET, THIRD LINE, AND HE CANNOT READ IT AND HE HAS HAD IT IN HIS COAT FOR TWO DAYS: THE FORBEARANCE OF THE DISTRICT OF RYEFOLD, GRANTED IN THE THIRTY-NINTH WEEK OF LAST YEAR FOR THE LENGTH OF ONE GRAIN YEAR, IS DISCHARGED AT THE CLOSE OF THE THIRTY-NINTH WEEK OF THIS YEAR, AND FROM THAT HOUR THE ACCOUNT RUNS IN THE ORDINARY WAY.

“The account is live. The account is four hundred and eighty-five pounds a year and it has been four hundred and eighty-five pounds a year since the carts went on it, and it is four hundred and sixty-two pounds a year before that, and we have never once been told either figure by anybody who came to this door, and I have signed a piece of paper in a room in Orison that says the account, and I have read every line of it, and I did not know it was four hundred and eighty-five pounds a year. I knew what the hire cost. Nobody told me what the year cost. It is not the same and I did not know it was not the same.”

“That is on the abstract as well?”

“No,” said Marda Fen. “That is not on the abstract. The abstract has the end on it and not the rate. And the rate is in another book, in a building with a window, in a street with four of those in it, and there is a man in that building who sweeps and has a key and no name on the door, and anybody may read anything in there for fourpence a sheet, and I have known that for about eleven years and I have never been, and the reason I have never been is that I have a granary and a cut in a month and a reeve’s wage of two pounds ten a year, and the hour and the fourpence and the two miles are not a thing you spend in a cut week.”

“Do you want to go?”

“I do not want to go,” said Marda Fen, “and I cannot afford to not want to, and if I go I will be two hours down the road and the weighbridge will be standing still for half of it, and I have sixty carts standing on that quay at four in the morning and the district has got eleven days of forbearance left in its accounts and no money at all. So I am not going. And I have just said that out loud to a stranger, which is a thing I have not done, and I would like you to notice that I did it and not to make anything of it.”

“All right.”

“Here is the other thing and it is worse and it is why I sent for you before I sent for them.” She took her hand off the box. “That abstract is a Bench record and it is a formal one and it is the only copy in this district. Osa has read it and Osa cannot keep it, because Osa is one person and she is a reeve’s clerk in a way I am a reeve and she is not, and there is no office of this district and has not been for nine years. And if I take it into that room this afternoon, then four people who cannot read it will know that the person in the room who can read it is holding the only copy of the only document that says when we ran out.”

“So take a copy,” said Ilyan Vester.

“I have said that and I have said what it costs and I am not going to say it twice, and neither are you.” She looked at him. “Four sheets, fourpence a sheet, is sixteen pence. That is the copy. And then it is in a district, and a district keeps a paper for about eleven years before somebody in it wants a thing out of a drawer in a bad year and cannot find it, and eleven years from now it will be in a box in a counting-house with a plate on it and four years of print in front of it and a man with a key, and I will be dead, and it will be four pence a sheet to a person who does not know what it is.”

“Then take two copies.”

“I cannot take two copies out of a building with no name on the door on a day,” said Marda Fen, “because a man with a key carries them out one at a time in his coat and he has a bad hand and he is not a clerk and he does not know what it is, and if I ask him for four sheets and then four more he will remember me, and I do not want to be remembered by anybody in that street until I know what I am doing.”

She put the chalk in the box and shut it.

“So it is one copy,” she said, “and it is going into that room at the fourth bell this afternoon, and I have not told those four a single thing, and you are not to tell them a single thing either, and in about an hour I am going to walk up that lane myself and put it on their table, and I am going to tell them it has ended, and I am going to tell them I have known for eleven days and I did not tell them, and I am going to tell them that the reason I did not tell them is that I could not work out what to tell them, and that is the truth and it is the worst thing anybody has said in that room in nineteen years and I am going to say it standing up.”

She went, and the door was open, and the second bell had not gone.

---

He wrote on the eighth page of a penny exercise book, in a cart shed behind a granary, at about the sixth bell on the thirteenth day of the forty-first week.

*A district was given a year and spent the year on carts that belonged to other people, and the year ended fourteen days ago, and the reason nobody knew is that the paper that says when a thing ends is in a building six miles from this shed and getting to it costs a reeve two hours of a cut week and a clerk four pence and a mile, and she is right about all of it, and the reason she did not go is not that she could not read and it is that she could not afford to stop.*

*And the only copy is going up that lane in the hand of a woman of fifty-four and the room it is going into has three people in it who cannot read it, and the fourth is the one who is holding it, and I have watched that happen twice in two years in two counties and I did not understand it the first time and I am not sure I understand it now, and the reason it happens is that a thing which can only be carried by a person is a thing the district cannot be without, and this district cannot be without Osa Trin, and therefore this district cannot be without her, and that is not a complaint and it is the mechanism and I have not got a name for the mechanism yet.*

*Thirteen days on this shelf. It is mine to write down and it is not mine to decide.*

*And I have not put one word of the fourth sheet in any of this because I cannot read it, and I am not going to have it read to me, and the next four days are going to be the four days in which that turns out to have been the correct thing to do and the thing that costs the most, and I have written that down in advance on purpose, in a shed, on the thirteenth day, so that whatever happens I cannot pretend afterwards that I did not know what I was doing.*

The last of those four lines is the first thing he has written in this world in a year and a half that is not about somebody else.

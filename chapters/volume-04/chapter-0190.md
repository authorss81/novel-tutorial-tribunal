# Chapter 190: A Number On A Sheet

The fifth day of the forty-eighth week ran the way the fifth day of a week runs in a district that has just come out of a cut, which is to say that nothing happened until about the fourth bell and then four things happened in about an hour and all of them were ordinary.

The beam was working, because there are about four hundred carts in this district and about seven thousand loads in that building and the carts are not going to wait for a year, and a reeve of fifty-four was calling loads off it in a voice that nine thousand people in this county have heard her use.

A bench of four had been in the room above the chandler's shop since the fourth bell and had come out of it at the sixth, four at a time and no farewells, and a convenor of sixty-one had gone down the lane first, which is the first time in nineteen years and was noticed by about four people.

And at about the second bell a woman of fifty-four had done a thing at the second door that took about nine minutes, on her own, with nobody in the lane, and it is the last thing that happened in this district in six weeks and it is not a remedy.

---

She had done it because a hundred and forty loads had gone over that beam on the sixth day of the forty-seventh week and had gone into the account's own book six miles up the road that goes north at the county's entered rate of two shillings and eightpence a load, and she had said the figure out loud as it went on, the way she says every figure, and the figure was in a book and would be there in nine months and in nine years, and nobody in this district would ever be told it was there.

And on the first day of the forty-eighth week a page of that book had been read out in a room four miles off, in numbers, and one of the four lines on it said short, one hundred loads.

And a convenor had said, ten days ago, in a second door, in the ordinary way, standing up with a beam working behind him, that there is nowhere in the world that records that this district was ever a hundred loads short, and that if any clerk in this county asked this district what it did on the first day of the forty-sixth week then the answer was a page, and a page was not a person, and the page was in a room.

And she had been standing at that beam every day since, on her own, with a piece of chalk, and had not been asked, and had not asked anybody, because a reeve of twenty-two years in a building that has not had a clerk in it since before she came does not ask anybody about a number.

So she did it on the fifth day of the forty-eighth week at about the second bell, and here is the whole of it, and it is a sheet and not one of the hundred, and it is in her hand, and it is a number and not a name, and it is legal, and it took about nine minutes.

> RYEFOLD, SECOND MOUTH. LOADS ENTERED 3,400, OF WHICH 100 WERE NOT PAID FOR. ENTERED BY A REEVE OF THIS DISTRICT. ONE SHEET. FOURPENCE TO COPY.
>
> NO CLAIM IS ENTERED ON THIS SHEET. NOTHING IS ASKED FOR.

She wrote it on a sheet of the district's own, at the second door, on the back of a bin-board, standing up, and she read it back to herself once, which is a thing she does, and she did not read it to anybody because there was nobody in the lane to read it to, and then she put it in an envelope. On the envelope she wrote a number, and it was not a name, and it was the number that was at the top of one of the hundred sheets, and she had copied it off one in a yard off the lane on the fourth day of the forty-seventh week while about nine people were standing in that yard and not one of them knew she was doing it, because a basket of paper with no number on the outside of it is a basket a man cannot find again, and she has been putting numbers on the outside of things for nine years and there has never been anywhere for them to go.

Nobody asked her what the number was for. Nobody in that lane knew a hundredth sheet from any other sheet and nobody has ever asked a reeve what a figure is for.

---

The account's own office is six miles up the road that goes north and the account's counter is in a counting-house lane in the same city, and a boy of nineteen took the envelope at about the fifth bell on the fifth day of the forty-eighth week and turned it over once, which is what he does with everything, and read the two lines on the front of it, and read the four lines inside it, and did not ask a single question about any of it, and that is the thing he is going to be asked about in about a year by a person who is not him.

“You have entered a number,” he said.

“I have entered a number,” said Marda Fen.

“Nobody enters a number on a sheet of their own. They enter a claim, and a claim is a person, and a person is answerable for it.” He put the envelope down flat. “There is no column for that. I have been in this building two years and I have never once seen a sheet come in off a person in a district without a claim against the account, and I have read about nine thousand returns and there is not one of them with a number on it that is not a figure out of a return.”

“Then what do you do with it,” said Marda Fen.

“I enter it and it is entered, and it goes in the book by district and it stays in the book, and it is not a claim and it does not cost the district anything and it does not cost the account anything and it is not a remedy and it is not a victory and it is a piece of paper.” He squared it. “And in about nine months it will be in a book in a building that your district cannot reach, and there is no column anywhere in this county for a form that was sent and nothing came back of it, and I have wanted that column for two years and I am not going to get it this week.”

She looked at him for about four seconds.

“I have been thinking about a list all morning,” said Marda Fen, “and I have got one and it is no use and I am going to say it out loud anyway because you have been decent to me twice and I have not been able to say anything to anybody in this district for about nine years.”

He waited.

“It is the people in this county who have read a thing out to somebody and not asked that person one question about themselves afterwards. There is a boy of thirteen who carries notices and cannot read them. There is a woman of thirty-one who carries a basket six miles up a towpath and says so before you ask her. There is a boy of nineteen in this building who has been here two years. There is a woman of thirty-nine in a room four miles off who reads things for six hundred people and has never once been asked what any of it is for, and I have wanted to ask her for nineteen years. And now a reeve.” She put two fingers on the counter and left them there. “And I have been in a granary for twenty-two years and I have never once been on a list of anything that anybody in a good coat has read out of a book.”

“Nobody in this county can say that is a list. It is five people and a number of nine pence. And I have wanted a column in a book for about nine years and I have just this morning found out that I have one, and it has my own number on it and not my name, and I did that on purpose at about the second bell this morning on the back of a bin-board, and I want you to write that down in your own book, because I am not going to be here to say it again and there is nobody in this district who can say it for me.”

Amos Kellock wrote it down. He wrote it in the book about the district and not in the book about a party, and he wrote it in his own hand, and he did not read it back to her, and in two years he has not entered a line at the request of a person of a district and this is the first, and he is not going to tell anybody in this building that he has done it until the day he has to.

“Right,” he said. “And I am going to say the other half, because you have been in a lane with a sheet and you know what the whole of this is and I do not want you to have only one half of it in a granary for the next nine months.”

He put his hand flat on the counter.

“That is the cheapest thing in the world to put on a person. A number. Not a name — a name is a person and a person can be asked whether it is his. A number cannot be asked anything. In nine years, or in ninety, a clerk in a building nobody in this district has stood in can pull that sheet out and read that number into a room, and there is not one person in that room who can stand up and say it is not that granary, and it is not a person, and it does not know what it is for, and it cannot say so. And you have done it this morning on purpose, and you are right, and I have had two days with the same fact and I have not been able to make it smaller.”

“It is the same thing,” said Marda Fen. “A number is the cheapest thing in the world to put on a person and it is the most useful thing in the world to put on a document, and it is the same piece of paper and the same hand, and whoever is holding the pen decides which of the two it turns out to be. A boy of nineteen said that to a man with a basket in a lane six miles down this road two days ago and I have not stopped hearing it and I did not have the words for it and you did and I have just used them on a counter in a building I have never been inside.”

“Yes,” said Amos Kellock, “I did say that.”

“You did. And I have been a reeve for twenty-two years and I have entered about eleven thousand and about seven thousand on a bin-board every day since before you were in this building, in my own hand, in chalk, and not one of them ever went anywhere, and this morning one of them did, and the difference is that this one has a piece of paper under it and fourpence of copying against it, and that is the whole of the difference between a number in a building and a number in a book, and it took me nine years and a reeve of fifty-four and fourpence to work out.”

She picked her fingers up off the counter.

“Tell the boy of thirteen,” she said. “He wrote a number on a wage board in the forty-third week and a girl of sixteen wiped it four days later and neither of them was told anything and nobody has ever told them anything, and I am not going to tell them and it is not mine. But you are a clerk in an office with about nine thousand returns behind you and there is a form that goes up that towpath in a basket, and I have wanted a column for it for nine years, and I am not asking you for the column. I am telling you there is one and it is a basket and there is a woman of thirty-one on it who has never read a thing she has carried and has never asked and says so before anybody asks her.”

Amos Kellock wrote that down too.

---

At about the seventh bell a basket went up the towpath. It is six miles each way and there are about two hours of light in the middle of it and a basket that weighs about forty pounds when it is full and about eleven when it is not, and the woman who carries it is paid three shillings a week in a cut and nothing the rest of the year and has carried it for nine years and has never read a thing in it and has never asked.

Sena Ollet had ninety-one numbered sheets in it and an envelope with a number on the outside of it, and she weighed it on a steelyard at the bottom of the towpath before she set off, because that is what she does, and it was about forty pounds and it was heavy for the top of that path and she said so out loud to nobody.

“You are going to be six hours on that path for about nine pence a day if anybody is paying you,” said Ilyan Vester, who was standing at the bottom of it with a basket of his own that he was not going anywhere with, because a basket is the only thing in that lane that puts his name in a place where anybody can find it.

“Nobody is paying you for that basket and you have been carrying it for about seven weeks,” said Sena Ollet. “And I have just watched a reeve put a number in a book that has not got her name on it, and she is fifty-four and she has been doing that granary since before you came off that boat, and she did it on purpose this morning and she knew what it was for and she had to pay fourpence for the privilege.” She shouldered the basket. “So you do not tell me what a basket is for.”

She went up the towpath. It is six miles and it took her most of the day and she got there at about the ninth bell and there was nobody at the counter, because the boy who opens the post is not there every day and nobody has asked him to be, and she put the basket down on the step of a building with a window in it and came away from it, and it is a basket that goes six miles to a counter and six miles back with whatever the counter has sent, and whatever she leaves there will be in a book in about nine months, and there are two nines in that arrangement now and neither of them is a punishment, and one of them belongs to a building that nobody in this district can reach in a day.

And at about the ninth bell, in a cart shed behind a granary, the leg was his own.

It had been his own since about the fourth bell in the yard at the weigh and it had not been announced and nobody had noticed and it was his own for about four hours, and about nine people in this district have watched something happen to him in the last seven weeks and not one of them has ever mentioned it, and there is no part of this in which anybody found out anything.

He wrote on the sixteenth page of a penny exercise book, sitting down on an upturned pail with his back against a cart wheel, and the two lines at the end of it are the two lines that are on the other fifteen.

*Sixty days on this shelf. Ninety-one numbered sheets went out in a basket two days ago and a number went into the account’s own book this morning in a reeve’s hand, and both of those are the same act, and the act is whoever is holding the pen. It took a woman of fifty-four nine years and fourpence of copying to do the second one and a man of thirty-four a pound and five shillings to do the first one, and neither of them did it for me and I was not asked, and a woman of thirty-one carried the first one six miles up a towpath on three shillings a week and has never read one and says so before anybody asks her.*

*A harm that is one person long is a day, a shilling and fourpence and a second hand. A harm that is four hundred people long is a page, and a page cannot undo it, and there is not one other instrument in the world, and I have not found one in sixty days, and a number is still a page and I have had sixty days in which to want it to be something else. And a woman with four children and a stove and no paper has been on a list in a book six miles up that road since the second day of the forty-seventh week in a second hand, and nobody in this district is going to know what the account did with it for about nine months, and that was the first one and it is not shut. And a matter is listed for the first sitting of the next half of the year and I am still carrying it and the year has not gone out of me and it is not going to in this grain year, and that is not because I did anything.*

*It is mine to write down and it is not mine to decide.*

And there was a book shut on a name in a room at the top of a lane, and a minute in a book in a building six miles up a road that goes north, and ninety-one sheets of a pound and five shillings in a sacking bag in a yard with a bit of string on it, and a basket coming back down a towpath in the dark with nothing in it, and a beam, and a figure on a bin-board that is the same figure it was yesterday and will be the same figure tomorrow.

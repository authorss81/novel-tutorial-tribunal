# Chapter 514: The Fifth Day Of The Ninety-Fifth Week, And A Saturday On Which A Leaf Of Forty-Three Names Is Read Out In A Chancel Eight Feet By Eleven In About Eleven Minutes In Front Of About Nine People, And A Woman Of Sixty-One Then Reads The Whole Of It Out Loud In The Square Because A Name Is Not Put Back By Nine People, And Four People Who Are Descended From Four Of The Forty-Three Strike The Names They Are Kin To Out Of The List In Public With Their Own Hands

Fifth day of the ninety-fifth week. His two hundred and sixty-fourth morning. One hundred and fourteen days after the settlement. The fever thirty-three weeks and no days. Sixty-eight days since the division. Fourteen days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town two days from where he is standing.

It is a market on a Thursday. The square on that Saturday had about a hundred and ninety people in it and about nine of them went into the room. That is the whole of the arithmetic of the day: about a hundred and ninety out of about two thousand four hundred, less the nine, is about a hundred and eighty-one who heard nothing at all and will be told about it by somebody.

It is the first time in a hundred and forty-one years that anybody in this country has arranged to read that page.

The square filled between about the eighth hour and the tenth, the way a square fills. There was no market on and the bars of Thursday’s stalls were still lying in the mud, and the sweep had been done on the Wednesday night, and what was in that square was not a market. It was about a hundred and ninety people who had heard the word *struck* in a porch and in a market square and in a lane behind a pond, in about eleven days, out of mouths they could not count.

Nobody in that square had been asked to come. That is the whole of what the third term of Wednesday said, and it was tested on Saturday morning and it held.

---

They went in at about the tenth hour, and the three terms were read out at the door standing in about a minute by a woman of thirty-four, and about nine people in that square heard all three of them and the rest of that square heard a woman reading out of a doorway.

There were nine in the room. Marda Rill, who keeps the book. Abel Tarn, sixty-two, a weaver, the last of a name on the leaf. Harl Wenlock, forty-four, who holds the holding numbered five, which is the next one along. Edith Crow, sixty-eight, of the upper row. Barnaby Rill, eighty-one, on a chair by the fire. Odalie Fenn, sixty-one, of nine towns. Toby Henshaw, fourteen, who writes the week in chalk. Wynd Kettle, about seventy-two, of four acres at the bottom of the lane. And a woman of about forty from the wool bench, who was the reason two pieces of paper had been in that square on the Thursday.

The inner door was open and the window was down, because a book has to be read with the window down or people fall asleep.

The man of thirty-one was on the step, and he had a sheet of paper nowhere in sight, and about nine people in that square knew he had offered to read it out and had been refused in about two seconds.

He had offered it at about the ninth hour, at the door, in front of about nine people.

“*I will read it,*” Ilyan Vester said. “*I have heard all of it said about in nine rooms and two counties and I have never once had it in my own mouth. Give me the leaf and I will read every name on it and stop at the stroke and not read the words in the margin, and you will be in the room and nine people will have heard it from a person who cannot be sent for.*”

“*No,*” said Marda Rill.

It was about two seconds. She did not look up, and she had the leaf in her hand before he had finished the sentence.

“*A copyist of thirty-four in a market town two days off stopped me in a room with the door open on the fifty-first day after the division and put her finger on a sheet and told me a thing I have been carrying for about a hundred and twenty days. A thing read out in a room by a person who cannot be sent for afterwards is a thing with nobody behind it. On Saturday there are about nine people in that room and every one of them can be sent for to this building to say what they said this morning. You are the one person in this county who could say it and be sent for nowhere.*”

He understood it in the room, which was the difference, and the difference did him no good whatever, and he said so out loud, and about four of the nine people heard the place where it stopped and about four did not.

Then she went in and shut nothing, because there was nothing to shut that day, and the door was open and the window was down.

---

It took about eleven minutes.

That is the same eleven minutes the Wednesday leaf takes, and it took that long because there are forty-three names and beside every one of them a holding, and a figure in the fold, and a depth in the ground behind the meeting-house, and a number has to be read as a number and not as a sound.

She read the head of the page first, so that the four words would not come out of a room on their own. She read the four words once, in the voice she uses for minutes, and there was no day on it and no name on it and no seal and no number, and she read them and stopped.

Then she read the forty-three.

Nobody spoke for eleven minutes. The ones who wanted to speak did not. The ones who had come to hear an order read out found that there was no order in it. About four people in that room understood at about the fourth minute that the thing which had happened to their town a hundred and forty-one years back was a household being asked a question and giving an answer. About nine understood it at the eleventh.

About a hundred and eighty-one people in that square heard about four words over eleven minutes and would have described it afterwards as the man reading out the names of everybody who was ever struck off anything, which is what a hundred and eighty-one people do with a thing they were not in a room for.

At the fourth name she came to a name that is also the name of the weaver’s mother. It is in the hand of about two hundred and something years ago. The holding against it is a half-acre at the bottom of Kiln Lane, the figure in the fold is four, and the depth in the ground behind the meeting-house is seven feet.

“*That one is mine,*” said Abel Tarn, at about the fourth minute, in the voice of a man saying a thing he has said to a weaver’s guild.

“*It is yours and I am going on,*” said Marda Rill.

And she went on, and at about the ninth minute she came to the last of the forty-three, which is the last of a family, and there is nothing against it that anybody can read, and she read it and did not stop on it.

Then she read the stroke. It is a line from about an inch below the top rule to about an inch above the bottom one, and it goes right through the names without stopping at any of them. She said that it is a single stroke, that it is heavier than any other ink in four hundred and eleven years of that book, and that there is no way of telling from the ink in what order it was made.

Then the room had nothing in it.

That is the whole of the finding and it took about eleven minutes and it did not move anything. The largest thing that has ever happened in the town of Bramblefold is four words and no order, and about nine people in a room eight feet by eleven came out of that room on a Saturday morning having learned exactly that, and about nine of them had known most of it on the Friday.

Nobody said a word. The boy of fourteen wrote nothing. The claim at the back of the book was not read out and was not touched, and the two objections at the foot of it were not read out, and the leaf was not struck back, and not one thing was decided.

Then a woman of sixty-one asked for the sheet.

---

Marda Rill had written the forty-three names on the back of the sheet the three terms were on, in the ordinary hand. She is the only person in that country who can read the hand on the front of that book. And the only thing that can be carried out of a room eight feet by eleven to about a hundred and ninety people is a piece of paper.

She gave it to her, and the recordwright said what she was giving it for, in front of about nine people, in about four sentences.

“*Take it out and read it out and do not read a name I have not written on that sheet, and never read the four words, because I have read them once today and that is the whole of what they are. And when you have read the forty-three, do not tell that square what they ought to think about it. They have no form that lets anybody ask them. Read the list and then stand there.*”

“*I have been doing this for four years,*” said Odalie Fenn.

“*In nine towns where nobody knew what a descent was. There are about a hundred and ninety people in that square who know exactly what a descent is, and about four of them are on it.*”

Then the woman of sixty-one read the whole of what was on the page out loud in the square, standing on the step of that meeting-house with her back to the door, and it took her about nine minutes, and about a hundred and ninety people heard a list of forty-three names.

She read the holdings too: the figures in the fold and the depths in the ground behind the meeting-house. She had been told to read what was on the page and not a shape of it. She read them slowly. A woman of about forty in the crowd wrote them on the back of her hand, and a man of about thirty-five wrote them on the back of his hand and got them wrong and did not know.

Nobody in that square had heard a name of their own.

Then four people went up the step.

They did not go up together and they were not asked and nobody told them to. They went up one after another over about nine minutes, and the square did not make a way for them and then made one, and about four people in it worked out what was happening before the first of them had reached the top.

Nobody in that square is on that list. That list is a hundred and forty-one years old and every one of the forty-three names on it belongs to somebody who is dead, and a person standing in a square in this county can be a great many things and being one of those forty-three is not one of them.

What the four of them were is that they are descended. One of them is descended and has four rooms and a yard, and one is descended and has a holding with his father’s name on it, and one is descended and has never been asked, and one is descended and is about nineteen years old.

The first was a woman of about seventy. She said her grandmother is on the list at the thirty-first name, and that her grandmother came to this town from about ninety miles off about a hundred and fifty years back, and that she keeps a house on the lower row and has never had anything in writing about it, and that she will not be said in a square to come out of that woman.

The second was a man of about thirty-five who had written the figures on the back of his hand. He said that the name at the seventeenth is his grandmother and that he wrote the figures down wrong on Saturday and got them wrong again on Thursday, and that he would rather be the one holding the chalk.

The third was a woman of about twenty-eight and the fourth was a boy of about nineteen. Neither of them gave a reason that about nine people in that square could repeat afterwards. Both of them said the same four words, which were that a name on a list in a square is a thing a hundred and ninety people have got.

There was a slate on the step. It is about the size of two handsbreadth with a rim, and there is a stub of chalk in the leather at the back, and it is the slate the recordwright has written her terms on for nineteen years.

The woman of about seventy took the chalk in her own hand and struck her grandmother’s name out of the list. The man of thirty-five took the chalk after her and struck out the seventeenth. The other two did it each for the name they came from.

And every one of those four strokes was made by a person who is kin to the name it went through, and about four people in that square worked that out before the fourth of them had reached the top, and about nine worked it out in the lane afterwards.

Nobody in that square stopped them. About nine people in it said afterwards that the four had done it for the page, and about four said the four had done it against the page, and neither of those was right. The sheet went back to a woman of thirty-four at about the twelfth hour with forty names on it and four gaps in it.

“*A name taken out in a square is a name a person has been said to have,*” Marda Rill said, to about nine people in the porch, out loud, and then she said the other half of it to nobody.

“*I am not going to have it that they took themselves off. They were asked nothing. Somebody read a list out and they came up a step and did a thing with a piece of chalk, and in about eleven years there will be a person in this town who was told that they were on that list and struck off, and the striking will be the part that is left.*”

The fever was thirty-three weeks and no days old. A leaf four hundred and eleven years old had been read out in a room eight feet by eleven in about eleven minutes in front of about nine people. The whole of what came out of that room was a sheet of paper with forty-three names on it, and four of them gone out of it by the hand of a person each of them is descended from, in a square, in public, with a piece of chalk.

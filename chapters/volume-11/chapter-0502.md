# Chapter 502: The Second Day Of A Two-Day Road, And A Monday On Which A Cart Carries A Man Of Thirty-One Twenty-One Miles And Stops Four Miles Short Of Where He Is Going, And A Boy Of About Nine Tells Him At A Toll Bar That Took A Penny At Each Of Four Gates Ninety Years Back That He Is Not The First Stranger Of This Year, And Nobody In That Town Can Say Which Four The Four Gates Were

Seventh day of the ninety-third week, and the last day of it. His two hundred and fifty-second morning. One hundred and two days after the settlement. The fever thirty-one weeks and two days. Fifty-six days since the division. Two days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town two days from where he is standing.

The cart left Coldcombe at the seventh hour with eleven sacks of lime on it and one passenger. The carter was a man of about sixty who did not ask the passenger anything and put him on the tail-board because the shafts were full, and the tail-board of a lime cart is nine feet above a chalk road, and the going over the downs at nine feet is a thing the body finds out about.

The woman of thirty-four walked. She said she would come on to Bramblefold by the middle of the afternoon, and that there was four miles of it from the Gates, and that she knew every gate of it. She was gone before the cart got out of the yard. The carter looked after her and then looked at the passenger.

“*That is the recordwright,*” he said. “*There is nobody in that country who can tell you anything, and anybody who can tell you anything is somewhere else.*”

The chalk of the downs came up white and hard, and the hedges on the top were planted rather than grown, and were about a hundred and forty years old, which is a thing a person learns by looking at them and does not learn by being told.

The fever is thirty-one weeks and two days old and it is a number in a book four hundred miles and more behind him, and about a hundred and forty people are dead of it. The number has not moved in fifty-one days, and he is not the person who counts it and has never been able to find out who is.

The road went over the shoulder of the down at about the ninth hour and came down on the other side into a valley, where a stream ran in a ditch cut by hand about two hundred years back. There were elms along it in a double row, and about half of them were dead standing, and a man who plants elms for a living will tell you the same about both kinds.

At about the eleventh hour the carter pulled up where the track came off the road onto a bar. The bar was two oak posts and a rail and nothing else, and there were four of them in a row along four hundred yards of chalk with a gate in each.

“*That is the Four Gates,*” he said. “*Nine years I come here and it is the only stop on this load.*”

“*You pay at it?*”

“*I pay nothing at it. I put the horse up and I wait, because there is a boy on this track and he watches the track, and he comes out and looks at the cart, and it is in me now to wait.*”

There was a boy of about nine on the track about thirty yards off, in a shirt and no shoes, standing where a boy stands who has been standing in the same place for some time.

“*Morning,*” the carter said.

“*Morning.*”

“*That is a stranger off the load.*”

The boy looked at Ilyan Vester from the shoes up and then down again, and said that he was the second one. The second one this year. There had been a man in a wet week come up that lane with a case and gone down it the same day, and he had asked the same thing.

“*How much the gates used to be,*” the boy said.

The carter got down off the tail-board and stood on the chalk, which took a man of thirty-one with a bad leg about nine seconds.

“*Nine years I come here,*” the carter said. “*When I was your age I asked him the same thing. Ask him now, since you are here and I am not.*”

“*How much did they take?*”

A penny at each of them, the boy said. Four for a cart and four for a horse and a penny for a person on foot, and it used to be the whole of the year at the far one. They took it off ninety years back and put it in the charter instead of on the ground.

“*Which four?*”

“*No.*”

“*You don’t know?*”

“*There is four gates and one name,*” the boy said, and put his thumb at his own temple. “*They took the four names off the ground and put them in the charter, and my father says the charter is in the meeting-house in a chest, and he says everybody has read it and nobody knows, and he says his father said the same and his grandfather said the same.*”

“*That is three generations of a man not knowing which four gates.*”

“*Four if you count me, and I have not been asked.*”

Then the boy said that he would be going down to the town for the book, and when the man asked why he said it was because they all went down for the book and there had not been one of them there before, and his father said people only came for the book and never for the fair, and the fair was Thursday.

He said that with the flatness of a thing a house has said in a kitchen for a long time. Then he went off down the track at a run and did not turn round. The carter watched him go and picked up the reins.

---

The four miles down from the Gates to Bramblefold is a lane and then a lane. The second one has a wall on it about eleven feet high on the left-hand side, with a coping of chalk set on edge. The coping is a foot wide and about nine inches proud, and it is the top of the common wall, and it has been re-copied so many times that the chalk on the top of it is newer than the chalk in it.

He walked it at the speed of a man who is going somewhere. On the left of the lane, above the wall, there was a hundred and forty acres of down going up to the sky, and he could see about nine hundred apple trees standing in a square, and the ground under them was bitten brown all the way to the wall, and the bite was sheep and not frost.

At about the half hour after the second hour the lane came round a bend, and there was a town below him, on a slope, in a bowl, with about two thousand four hundred people in it by the size of the roofs. The whole of the middle of it was a market square with the stalls up and empty, because the market is on a Thursday and this is a Monday.

The smell came up the lane, and it was not the smell of a town. It was chalk burnt, and a little sharp underneath it. A man in the lane going down asked the passenger what the smell was before the passenger had worked out that the passenger was a stranger in his own county.

“*Kiln,*” he said, and went on.

It was burning its ninth day out of ten. The woman of thirty-four had said so at Coldcombe without being asked and had been right, and there is a kind of question a person can be answered on in a market town by asking it of a stranger, and it is worth asking, and the answer to it on this occasion was a whole country that burnt its own ground for a living.

He came into Bramblefold down past the smithy and the saddler’s shop and along a street about eleven houses long with a pump at the top of it. Nobody in that street looked at him, and one woman carrying two pails stopped and put one of them down.

“*You will be at the smith’s,*” she said, and picked the pail up again, and that was the whole of the welcome Bramblefold had for him, and it was correct.

He had one pound and fourpence. He said that to himself in a lane and made it add up twice, because a man who has been in a town of four thousand people for forty-eight days without once paying for a bed is not a man who should be loose with arithmetic.

A penny a night at the smith’s, which is a room over a shop with a bed in it and a fire in the grate. Bread is a penny, and a man’s day-work on the making-up of a wall is eightpence. At three pence a day he has eighty-one days and a penny over, and eighty-one days is eleven weeks and four days.

He worked that out on the edge of the pond. There is no mill at Bramblefold; there is a pond, and the wheel came off in his grandfather’s time, and the pond is where the town’s water is. The water is not the business of this town. The book is.

Then he did the thing that got him shut out of a roll in another town, which is that a man who has been a stranger everywhere will ask a stranger a question at a gate.

There was a woman of about sixty-one at the corner by the meeting-house door with a basket on her arm and a folded sheet under the lid of it. She was not of the town, and he asked her, in the ordinary voice of a man asking a stranger the time, whether anybody was in the meeting-house.

“*What for?*” she said.

“*I have come two days to ask about a book and I would rather know than stand about.*”

“*There is nobody in it. There is nobody in it on a Monday and there is nobody in it on a Tuesday, and there is one person in it on a Wednesday, and she is the one who keeps it. You will not get in to-day whatever you are here for.*”

“*I am not here to get in. I am here to know whether anybody is.*”

“*That is the same door and it is a worse one.*” She shifted the basket. “*Questions in this town are asked in a room, and not at a gate. I have been in this town eleven days and I have watched you walk in the lane past about nine people without saying good morning to one of them. You are going to want to be told what this town thinks, and I am going to tell you now, so that you can spend the night thinking about it instead of spending it on me.*”

“*All right.*”

“*What this town thinks is that there is a page in a book that has been struck through for a hundred and forty-one years and that somebody ought to be able to say why. There is nobody here to say it. I have written to nine towns, and one of them wrote back with a copy of a letter about a lane, and I came here because of a lane.*”

“*Four hundred miles?*”

She looked at him properly for the first time.

“*Where did you get that?*”

“*From a woman of thirty-four who is walking four miles behind me with a basket on her hip and a copy of four pages in her coat. The copy has a thumbprint on the third page.*”

“*Then you can come in or you can not come in,*” she said, “*and neither of them is a fact anybody in that meeting-house is going to enter.*”

---

He took the room over the shop and put five things on a board that had nothing on it. The copy of the roll, the copy of the instrument, the copy of the six lines, the copy of the request with the open witness line on it, and a page folded in four that had come back four hundred miles and that he did not take out of his coat.

The fever was thirty-one weeks and two days old. A town of about two thousand four hundred people on a chalk slope, with a market on a Thursday, a lime kiln burning nine days in ten, a wall about eleven feet high round a hundred and forty acres of sheep, about nine hundred apple trees inside the wall, a meeting-house with a chancel in it, and a book in the chancel that four people in the country can read and one of them is thirty-four and got in from Coldcombe four miles behind him.

The toll bar at the top of the lane had four gates in it and one name, and the four had gone ninety years ago into a charter in a chest, and a boy of about nine said that four generations of his house had not known which four, and that he had not been asked.

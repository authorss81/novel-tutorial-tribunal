# Chapter 516: The Seventh And Last Day Of The Ninety-Fifth Week, And The Morrow Is The First Of The Ninety-Sixth, And A Monday Which Is The Last Day The Ground In That County Will Carry A Barrow, And There Are Four Days Of The Work Left And Four Days To Do Them In, And The Four Days Go To The Top Of A Section And Leave The Bottom Of It With A Gap About Nine Feet Long And A Boundary Stone In A Shelf Inside It

Seventh and last day of the ninety-fifth week, and the morrow is the first day of the ninety-sixth. His two hundred and sixty-sixth morning. One hundred and sixteen days after the settlement. The fever thirty-three weeks and two days. Seventy days since the division. Sixteen days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town two days from where he is standing.

The ground is the only weather there is in this book. It is a day-count and not a week-count, and nobody in that lane converts it, and the whole of the reason it is in here is that it does not convert.

The making-up of a section of the common wall is sixteen days of work. There are twenty days in which the ground will take a barrow, and those twenty days were counted out from the fourth day of the ninety-third week, which is a Friday, and the crew has been on it since, and today is the seventeenth of the twenty.

After the twenty days the chalk takes the foot and goes soft. A barrow is not a barrow then, it is four men lifting, and there is no point in a man of fifty pretending otherwise for nine hours.

Thirteen days of work in seventeen days of window is a crew losing about four days to weather, which is about one in five on a chalk bank in a cold week, and a man of about fifty said that out loud at about the eighth hour and then said that anybody who thought a crew did better than that on chalk was a man who had not carried chalk.

Thirteen days of the sixteen are done. Three of the sixteen are left, and three of the twenty are left, and they are the same three days, and this is the first of them.

This is the last of those three on which the ground will carry a barrow. After today it will not, and the two days of work that are left after today are going to be done by hand and by barrow on the hard ground, which is about four times the work, and nobody on that wall has told the recordwright that and the recordwright has not asked.

The wall stops on the Wednesday night, because about nine of the men on it keep a beast and a Thursday in this town is a market, and a man who keeps a beast does not stand on a wall on a Thursday for a wall that will still be standing when he gets back. That was said out loud at about the eighth hour and nobody argued with it, and it is the only part of the three days anybody in that lane agreed to without wanting something.

The wall is about eleven feet high and about four miles of it in all, counting the returns. There is a coping of chalk set on edge a foot wide and about nine inches proud. Four people of this town have recopied it since the wall was built, and that is what keeps the water out of the courses.

Ilyan Vester went up the lane with them at about the seventh hour, at the back, with the string in his hand, because that is where a man goes who cannot carry. About nine men and one woman went up the lane at about the seventh hour. The woman was thirty-four, and she carried with a shovel, and nobody asked her to, and she is the only person in Bramblefold who has done a day’s work on that wall inside the nineteen years she has been keeping the book.

The coping is the part of a wall that everybody argues about and the part that decides it. It is a foot wide, set on edge, about nine inches proud of the face. Where a coping is a foot and a half the water goes over; where it is a foot the water goes in. Four people of this town have recopied the coping of that wall in four hundred years, and two of them were women, and one of the women did about nine hundred feet of it in one year for nothing.

---

The choice was made at about the eighth hour. It took about four minutes and it was not between the men.

There were about four hundred yards of that section left and the four hundred yards were the bottom of it. The bottom of it is where the fold is, because the fold is sheep, and sheep are put on the low ground at the bottom of a down and not on the shoulder of it.

The top of that same section is the sightline. From the top of the wall you can see the whole of a hundred and forty acres and about nine hundred apple trees standing in a square, and you can see the whole of the top lane. The point of a common wall is that a man can see along it. About four men in this town have said that in about four years, and all four of them have been wrong about the bottom.

So the three days went to the top, and the bottom of that section got nothing at all.

The reason was given out loud at about the eighth hour by the man who sets the top. He is about fifty. He has been finding stakes on that wall for about thirty-one years and finds them by the eye, and he has never once in his life been wrong about one.

“*The sightline or the sheep,*” he said. “*That is the choice and there is no third of it, and I have been the man who chose the sightline three times and I am not going to be asked about it. The sheep have a hedge. The sightline has got nothing at all, and a man on this wall in a fog is a man on a wall in a fog.*”

Nobody argued with him about the sheep. About nine people in that lane understood that it was a real choice. About four understood that it was a choice between nine people standing in the rain in the wrong place and nobody standing in the rain at all.

A man of about thirty came up the top lane at about the ninth hour with a dog and asked whether he could get through. He could. He went back down the same way he had come and said nothing about it, and nobody stopped him, and about four people in that lane watched him go through and about nine did not.

And then the man of forty-four said the thing that made the gap a document, and he said it at about the fourth hour with his own hand flat on the coping.

He is Harl Wenlock, forty-four. He holds the holding numbered five, which is the next one along, and he has held it twenty years, and his grandfather bought it off a family that was leaving for four pounds, and he has the bill of sale.

He had a cold joint out. It is about four feet below the new work, where an old man laid a stone on a wet bed in the year of the long frost. The stone had been in a lump ever since.

The new work came down onto it on the Saturday of the ninety-fourth week, and four feet of wall went out from under the course above and lay on the lane in one piece about nine feet long. The nine feet of it is still lying where it fell, and nobody has laid it back, because laying it back means a footing course first, and the footing course is the thing that came down.

“*A wall with a gap in it is a wall that somebody can come through,*” he said. “*That is not a figure of speech. A man can go through nine feet of it on his hands and knees in about four seconds, and so can a dog, and there is a lane on the other side of it and a fold on the other side of the lane.*”

“*It is a lane people walk,*” said the man of fifty.

“*It is a lane people walk, and there is no man in this county that I know of who is going to shut a nine-foot gap in a common wall in a wet week in which the ground will not take a barrow. I am one of them, and I have said so before anybody asked me.*”

Nobody in that lane made a move, and it is worth recording that nobody in that lane made a move, because it is the shape of the whole of this town in about thirty words spoken on a wall by a man who is not in the wrong and who has been right for nine years.

“*So there is a gap in it,*” Harl Wenlock said. “*And from today the gap is a document.*”

Nobody had said that. About four people in that lane had been looking at nine feet of nothing for two hours and had not arrived at it.

“*It is in the second column of a book four hundred and eleven years old, in the same hand as the rest of it. There is no column in that book for a gap. So somebody a hundred years from now will read that there was a wall, and that a section of it was made up in a fortnight, and they will read it in about four seconds. They will not know there was nine feet of nothing at the bottom end of it. That is what a document is.*”

“*Then put it in the book,*” said the man of thirty-one.

“*You cannot put it in the book, and you have known that since the second day you were in this town.*” Harl Wenlock took his hand off the coping. “*And I cannot put it in the book for a better reason than that, and the reason is that I am one of the sixty households. I have been in that book since I was seventeen. And I have never once in nine years been the only person who knew where a thing was wrong.*”

---

And the man of thirty-one made his mistake at about the fifth hour of a Monday afternoon, and it was the mistake of a man who has spent a hundred and sixteen days making a thing legible so that it can be held.

He wanted the wall finished. That was the whole of the wanting. It was a small wanting, and it was the only one he has had in this country that nobody had to talk him out of.

He went down and stood in the gap with his back to the lane and his hands out at his sides, in the way a man stands in a doorway.

“*Then I will stand in it,*” he said. “*Until somebody comes. There are three days of weather left, and I have a bed and I have a penny, and I can stand in a nine-foot gap in a wall on a Monday afternoon and be two feet shorter than the coping on either side of me. About two thousand four hundred people in this town could see me doing it from a street.*”

It took about four seconds for about nine men to tell him, and they told him all at once, and none of them was the man of forty-four.

“*A man standing in a gap is not a wall.*”

“*It is not.*”

“*It is not, and you are right that it is not, and you are wrong that it is nothing.*” That was the man of fifty, with the string still in his hand. “*A man standing in a gap is a thing about a man. The gap is behind you, and so is the cold coming through it, and so is the nine feet. You have not made a wall. You have made a man in front of a wall. And the wall will be in that book a hundred years from now and you will not.*”

Nobody on that wall said anything else about it. About four people in that lane understood the difference between a man in a gap and nothing at all. About nine understood that he had been told no and was still standing there, which is a different thing and not the thing anybody meant.

He came out of the gap at about the sixth hour, because his leg had had enough of it, and stood on the top lane for about a minute looking at a hundred and forty acres of down and about nine hundred apple trees with nothing on the trees in the lower corner.

The lower corner is a quarter of an acre in the middle of about eleven acres, and it is a frost pocket, and it is a fortnight later than the rest of it and worth about twice as much if it comes at all, and about forty half-size trees stand in it on about thirty feet of rows. A man of sixty-two works that pocket for nothing. The frost took four weeks of fruit off it on the Monday of the week before last. There is no column in that book, or in any instrument in this county, for a week of weather taking off a man who is owed nothing for what he does to it.

Then he walked down with the string and did the only thing he was good for, which was hold a line while a man of fifty read a course, and about nine men on a wall did not ask him a single question about it and did not start for him, and the not-asking was the thing that had undone him on the first Saturday and it did it again on the last one.

The fever was thirty-three weeks and two days old. About nine feet of the bottom of a four-mile wall was left standing open on a Monday, because the three days went to the top. And at the end of the nine feet, in the last course that is still standing, there was a shelf in the stonework about four feet off the ground and about nine inches deep, with a boundary stone in it that had nothing cut on it at all, and the man of forty-four put it there with his own hand and said that one was worse.

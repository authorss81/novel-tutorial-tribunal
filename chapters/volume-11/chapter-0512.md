# Chapter 512: The Third Day Of The Ninety-Fifth Week, And A Thursday Which Is The Market, On Which About Two Hundred People In A Square Are Shown Two Pieces Of Paper And Cannot Tell Which Of Them Was Written By A Person, And A Weaver Of Sixty-Two Says Out Loud That A Bill Of Sale Is Between Two People And A Page In A Book Is Between A Person And A Thing That Was Dead Before He Was Born, And A Man Of Forty-Four Says The Thing He Has Been Holding For Nine Years

Third day of the ninety-fifth week. His two hundred and sixty-second morning. One hundred and twelve days after the settlement. The fever thirty-two weeks and five days. Sixty-six days since the division. Twelve days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town two days from where he is standing.

The boy of about eleven came up the lane at about the seventh hour with a key in his hand and gave it back, and the man of thirty-one had been in that room over the smith’s since the Monday night of the ninety-third week and the room was his till this morning.

The boy said that the man at the smith’s had not asked for the money and was not going to, and that the room was the man’s own, and that the man of thirty-one was not to be asked anything about where he slept, and the man of thirty-one had not asked.

He said it out loud on the edge of the pond, because it is a thing a person does on a Thursday when a market has not begun.

He had one pound and fourpence. That is two hundred and forty-four pence, and a bed is a penny and bread is a penny and a man’s day-work on the making-up of a wall is eightpence, and at three pence a day that is eighty-one days and a penny over. Eighty-one days is eleven weeks and four days. He had a room until the seventh hour of that Thursday and after that he had the number and nothing else, and a porch in this town is not a step and a roof, it is a stone bench about six feet long, and it is the only thing in that square that is free.

Then Ilyan Vester went up the lane to the market with the rest of the town, on a leg that had got no better overnight and would not get any better that day.

---

It filled between about the eighth hour and the tenth, the way it does. It is about two hundred people and it is not two thousand, and there is no cattle and no cloth and no iron. There are about nine stalls and a great many eggs and a wool bench, and a man who comes down off the downs once a quarter with about forty ewes, and the man who sweeps the middle sweeps it again at noon for nothing, out of the custom.

About nine men of the wall were in that square with their coats over their shoulders and went back up the lane at about the twelfth hour, because the ground goes on the twenty-fourth day and there are four days of the work left and the work is on a day as well as a Saturday now.

The thing that happened in that square on that Thursday was that two pieces of paper got into it.

Nobody arranged it. A woman of about forty at the wool bench had heard in the porch on the Tuesday that there was a claim at the back of the book. She had heard that the man who holds the next one along has a bill of sale. She said in a kitchen on the Wednesday night that if there was going to be a claim, the two things ought to be in the same place at the same time, and there was no form that said she could not.

So the two things were in the same place at the same time. The bill of sale came out of a coat. The claim was a leaf at the back of a book that was in a locked room nine hundred yards off, and somebody had read the claim out of a doorway on the Tuesday and a man of about thirty had heard it.

That is the whole of how two pieces of paper got into a market square. One of them was carried there in a hand and the other one was remembered by about nine people and got itself into the air, which is the only way a thing in a chancel ever gets into a square.

Then about nine people in that square could not tell which of the two had been written by a person.

A woman of about fifty at a stall said it first, and said it to nobody in particular, and it was the sentence the whole of that Thursday turned on.

“*One of these has got a man’s hand on it and one of them has not, and I cannot tell which is which, and I have lived in this town fifty-one years.*”

Abel Tarn put his hand on the bill of sale, which was face up on the end of the wool bench because a man of forty-four had put it there and had not taken it back.

He is sixty-two and he weaves, and his looms are in two rooms over a joiner’s shop at the bottom of the town, and he is the last of a name on a leaf of that book, and he has a claim entered against the holding numbered six, which is a half-acre at the bottom of Kiln Lane, and of the fold, and of the ground behind the meeting-house, and nothing is carried.

“*That one is between two people,*” he said. “*My grandfather is not in it. Harl Wenlock’s grandfather is in it. Two men stood in two rooms and one of them said take four pounds and the other one said aye, and the four pounds were counted out, and both of them are dead, and that paper is the only thing left in this argument that anybody in this square has ever met.*”

He put his finger on the air over the other one.

“*And that one is between a person and a thing that was dead before he was born.*”

About four people in that square did not follow the second half and about nine did.

“*Say it again, because I have not got it,*” said the woman of about fifty.

“*A bill of sale is between two people. A page in a book is between a person and a dead man. Forty-three of them, and every one of them with a holding against it and a figure in the fold and a depth in the ground behind the meeting-house. One of them had a son, and the son had a daughter, and the daughter had a boy. There is nobody in any of those three rooms to have a say in it, and the paper is still in the book, in a hand, and the hand is two hundred and something years old.*”

“*Then it is not a claim on Harl Wenlock,*” said a man at about the ninth stall.

“*I never said it was,*” said Abel Tarn. “*A claim on Harl Wenlock is a claim on the next one along and it is his and his grandfather’s and he has the paper. I have asked for a half-acre. A half-acre is not a man. A half-acre does not have a bill of sale and it does not have a grandfather, and it is not going to argue with anybody, and it is the only thing I have ever asked for in the open air.*”

---

And then the man of forty-four said the thing he had been holding for nine years.

He had it ready and he had had it ready since the Tuesday. He had not said it in the porch on the Tuesday and he had not said it in the room. He said it in a market square on a Thursday at about the eleventh hour, in front of about two hundred people, which is the one thing a person in that square is worst at and best at.

“*Listen to the word,*” Harl Wenlock said. “*You have all had it since Tuesday and not one of you knows what it is. Column. A hundred and forty acres and four miles of wall and nine hundred trees and forty-three names, and every one of them is in a column.*”

Nobody in that square stopped him, which is worth recording, because about two hundred people in a market in a country district are the worst thing in the world at stopping a man for a word and the best thing in the world at hearing one.

“*I have been pointing at a stone in a wall for nine years and I have been right for nine years, and I told a weaver on Saturday that a pointing-out is not a man on a wall, and that was true and I have not taken it back. And here is the other half of it and it has been in my mouth since I was seventeen.*”

He put his hand flat on the bill of sale on the wool bench.

“*You cannot argue with that.*” He took his hand off it and did not touch anything else. “*You cannot argue with it because it is between two men who are both dead and the money went one way and it went once. There is nobody in that paper to say you are wrong. You can say I was cheated and I will hand you the paper and you can read it.*”

“*And the book?*” said the woman of about fifty.

“*You cannot argue with that either. You can only ask it.*”

“*Ask it what?*”

“*Ask it what it is for. There are four columns and there has been four for four hundred and eleven years and there is no fifth, and a fifth is a thing you put things in, and a thing with no fifth in it cannot be argued with because it has nowhere to put your answer. It can be asked. It cannot be argued with. And nobody in this county has ever asked it anything.*”

“*Not once in four hundred years?*”

“*Not once that anybody can put a name to,*” he said. “*A page cannot be argued with, only asked, and this town has been reading it as a thing to be argued with for four hundred years, and it is not one, and that is why every man in this square feels they are losing.*”

About nine people in that square heard all of that sentence. About two hundred heard the word *column*, and the word went out of the square in four directions, and by the following Thursday about two hundred people in this town knew what a column was and about nine of them knew why it mattered.

---

And that is where the man of thirty-one made the mistake, and it was the mistake he has made in about nine rooms in a hundred and fourteen days, which is to make a thing legible so that it can be held.

He wanted the man of forty-four softened. He had wanted him softened since the Tuesday, when the man stood up in a porch and said that a claim will cost him the next one along the day somebody works out what a claim is worth, and had been thinking about it in a bed over a shop for two nights.

So he said it in the market, in the middle of about two hundred people, and he said it well, and it was for the man of forty-four.

“*Nobody in this square is against you. I want that said. A claim is not a thing anybody has made up to take a holding off you. The woman from the barn came to this town about a page and not about you. The recordwright has entered a claim and not carried a penny of it, and the man who is the last of the name on that leaf has never once said your name in a market.*”

He had got as far as *and the man who is the last of the name on that leaf has never once said your name* when the weaver of sixty-two turned round, and he did it in about four seconds, and he was not loud and nobody in that square had to be asked twice what was being said.

“*I did not say he was,*” Abel Tarn said.

The whole of it was gone in about four seconds. A man of about fifty at the ninth stall had not understood any of it and understood all of it.

“*That is the whole of it,*” the weaver said. “*I did not say he was against me and I have not said it once in nine years and I am not going to start on a Thursday. I have a half-acre and I asked for it in a market in front of two hundred people and I asked for nothing else, and if you want to be good to somebody in this square you can be good to me.*”

About four people heard the whole of that. About two hundred heard the word *column* and carried it out of the square.

The fever was thirty-two weeks and five days old. A man of thirty-one had one pound and fourpence in him, a stone bench in front of him, and about two hundred people in a square. The word going round that square was the name of a thing in a book that nobody in that county has ever asked anything.

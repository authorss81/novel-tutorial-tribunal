# Chapter 511: The Second Day Of The Ninety-Fifth Week, And A Wednesday On Which The Terms Of A Reading Are Written On A Sheet And Read Out Standing In A Porch In Front Of About Nine People, And A Man Of Thirty-One Is Not In The Room And Works Out In About Four Minutes That A Rule Which Protects Nine People From Being Asked A Question Also Protects Them From Being Asked To Stand Up

Second day of the ninety-fifth week. His two hundred and sixty-first morning. One hundred and eleven days after the settlement. The fever thirty-two weeks and four days. Sixty-five days since the division. Eleven days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town two days from where he is standing.

The Wednesday happened at the ninth hour and took about eleven minutes, the way it has taken about eleven minutes for about nine years.

The man who stood at the end of the bench for it had come in a wet coat again in a dry week. It is not about the weather and has not been for about nine years, and everybody in that porch worked that out in about nine seconds on the first Wednesday and has not said anything about it since.

It is not a public day and it is not written anywhere. It is the one Wednesday in the week that the recordwright of this town reads one leaf out of that book, and she does it because a man of about seventy-two walks two miles for it and because his father did it, and neither of those reasons is a form.

When he had gone, about four people in that porch went away and about five stayed, which is not a number anybody keeps.

Barnaby Rill was not in that porch and had not been sent for, and the recordwright said so out loud before she read anything, in case anybody in the square thought that was an oversight.

“*He is eighty-one and he cannot read the third hand, and there is nothing on that leaf for him that he cannot already say better than I can. He is not needed for the terms of a reading. He is in the room on Saturday because it is the only warm room in that building, and because nobody has asked him whether he wants to be in it, and if anybody in this porch wants to make a thing of that it can be made now and not on Saturday.*”

Nobody in that porch made a thing of it, and about four of them noticed that she had offered, which she does not do.

She came out of the chancel with a sheet. It is not the paper of the book and it is not a leaf of it. She had written three lines on it and she did not read them off her own hand, which she does not usually do, and Ilyan Vester noticed that and did not know what it meant for about two hours.

Then she read them out, standing, in the voice she uses for minutes, and about nine people in that porch heard all three of them. About nine in the square heard a woman reading something out of a doorway on a Wednesday morning and not one word of it.

*The page is to be read and not the four words. The four words are to be read and not the men. Anything said in the square afterwards is said by the person who says it and not by the book.*

There are three of them and they are the whole of the arrangement, and there is nothing else written anywhere about what is going to happen in that room on Saturday.

A chancel in that building is about eight feet by eleven and it has one window and one door with a lock on it, and the lock is the only lock in the building, and it is there because the vestry cupboard is in that room and the book is in the cupboard. On Saturday the door will be open and the window will be down, because a book has to be read with the window down or people fall asleep, and about nine people will be in the room and about a hundred and ninety will be in the square outside and will not hear a word of it.

That is the arrangement, and it is the same arrangement as every Saturday for nineteen years, and on Saturday for the first time in a hundred and forty-one years it will be the arrangement for something else.

The woman of sixty-one was on the step before the last of the three had been said.

She is Odalie Fenn and she has been in this town seventeen days and she has written to nine towns in four years asking for pages, and she sleeps in a barn at Kiln Row, and she is not of this county.

“*You have taken the largest thing in that book and put it in the second line,*” she said.

“*I have put it in the second line,*” Marda Rill said.

“*Then tell me what you have done to it besides writing it down.*”

“*The first line says the page is to be read. Not the four words. The four words are a heading in a margin at the head of a page and they are not the page. The page is forty-three names, and beside every one of them a holding, and a figure in the fold, and a depth in the ground behind this building. That is what is on it and that is what has not been read since the year it was struck.*”

“*And the four words are nothing.*”

“*The four words are read out. In the room, in front of the nine, once.*”

That was the point at which about four people in that porch stopped being on the same side of anything.

“*Once,*” the woman of sixty-one said.

“*Once. And I will not read them a second time to anybody who was not in that room, because a thing said twice in a week is a thing people are saying to each other and not a thing that was read out of a book by a person who can be sent for.*”

“*It is the only account this county has of the largest thing that has ever happened in it.*”

“*It is four words and no order,*” Marda Rill said. “*That is the whole of it. I have read it nine times this week. On Saturday about nine people are going to hear four words and no order, and then they are going to go out into a square and find that four words and no order is the easiest sentence in this town to say out loud.*”

“*That is why I want it said in the square and not in the room.*”

“*Then tell me again, and tell me the second half of it as well.*”

“*A name is not put back by nine people. I said it in a market on the Thursday of the week the man of sixty-two stood up, and about two hundred people in that square heard the word struck. About nine of them heard the rest of it. On Saturday about a hundred and ninety people will be standing in that square and they will be told it by somebody who was in the room, and about half of them will be told it by a person who was in the room and did not hear it all.*”

“*That is your method and it is not a method this town has,*” the recordwright said. “*I have a square with a hundred and ninety people in it and a room with nine and no form anywhere that says the nine may be asked what they said in the room.*”

“*Then make the form.*”

“*I have not got one and I have had nineteen years and I am not going to write one on a Wednesday about a Saturday.*”

It is a market on a Thursday. On a Wednesday that square has the bars of the day before’s stalls lying in the mud and the sweep in the middle not done. The sweep is done on the Wednesday night by two men who are paid a penny each out of the twenty-eight pounds a year that a hundred and forty acres of sheep bring in at four shillings the acre.

A hundred and ninety people do not come to a square on a Wednesday. They come on Saturday because somebody has told them there is a thing to hear. There are about three hundred people in a town of about two thousand four hundred who have found out that something is going to happen in a room. About a hundred and ninety of them will be standing in that square on Saturday morning and the other hundred and ten will not.

And then the woman of sixty-one took the third line off her, and the way she took it was the way a person takes a thing off a table they have been reaching for.

“*That one is mine,*” she said.

“*It is not yours on this sheet.*”

“*I have said that sentence, or a version of it, thirty-one times in four years, in nine towns, to about nine people at a time. I said it in a market square once and there were about two hundred in it and one of them took it down on the back of a hand. You may have it for Saturday. I will tell you who wrote it if anybody in this porch asks me, and I will not if nobody asks.*”

“*Then it is yours, and I am putting it on my sheet because a thing somebody else wrote down is still a thing that has to be read in a room. And you are not in that room on Saturday any more than he is.*”

The man of thirty-one had been in that porch since the seventh hour and he had not been in the room, and the Wednesday had been read at the ninth hour with the inner door open and the window down, and he had heard all of that leaf and about four words of it.

“*I want to be in the room on Saturday,*” Ilyan Vester said.

The woman of sixty-one looked at him. The recordwright did not.

“*No,*” said Marda Rill.

“*I have not asked a question about the book. I have asked for a chair.*”

“*That is the same question and you know it is, because you asked it on the Tuesday of the ninety-fourth week and I gave you the whole of the reason then, and you have spent a week on the step of a building being right about it.*”

“*I want the whole of it again.*”

“*A room where a book is read is a room where people say things they are going to be held to. You cannot be held to anything. You are on nobody’s book in two counties and you can be sent for from neither, and on Saturday there will be about nine people in that room, and every one of them can be sent for.*”

He got about four minutes out of that, standing at the end of a bench in a porch with a mark worn into the stone about two-thirds along it, with a leg he was standing on at an angle and a Wednesday behind him.

A porch in this country is not a step and a roof the way it is in another county. It is a thing that is not in the room and is not out of it, and the bench in it is about six feet long, and it has been sat on by about four hundred years of people waiting to hear whether a thing they were waiting about had happened.

The mark in the stone is about two-thirds along it. A man of about seventy-two stands there on Wednesdays. A man of eighty-one sat at the far end of it in the week before last. A man of thirty-one had been on it every day of the week before this for eight days, and nobody had ever said any of that out loud to anybody.

He did the arithmetic, and it was not money, and it took him about four minutes.

A rule that keeps a man who cannot be sent for out of a room is a rule about being asked a question. He had understood that half in about four seconds on the Tuesday of the week before. The other half came to him in a porch on a Wednesday afternoon with nobody helping him to it.

It does the other thing as well. The nine people in the room are not asked to stand up in the square afterwards, and that is the other half of what the room is for. Nine people can be read to in a room and can go home and say what they think. None of them has to get up in front of about a hundred and ninety people and say it a second time. If one of them is asked to, the answer is no, and the answer is easy, because the rule is a good one and every person in that room knows it is a good one.

And about a hundred and ninety people in a square are going to be asked this week, by somebody, whether they are on that page or descended from somebody on it. About nine of the answers will come out of a room. The rest will come out of the answers those nine gave in the room, and a hundred and ninety people will be told a thing about a page by people who have never seen the page.

“*That rule of yours protects nine people from being asked a question,*” he said, “*and it protects them from being asked to stand up. The square is going to ask them to stand up, and every one of them will be able to say no. There is not one of them in this country who has ever been asked to say no to anything in his life.*”

He had said it out loud, on a Wednesday afternoon, in a porch, against the thing he wanted, and about four people in that porch understood that he had said it against himself.

The rest of the porch went on waiting for Saturday.

The fever was thirty-two weeks and four days old. A sheet that is not the paper of the book was in the hand of a woman of thirty-four, and it carried three lines. The second of them said that four words were to be read out of a margin on a Saturday, and that there would be no men in the room to read. About nine people in a porch had heard all three, and about nine in a square had heard a woman reading out of a doorway and not one word of it.

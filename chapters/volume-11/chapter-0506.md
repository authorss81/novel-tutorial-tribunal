# Chapter 506: The Fourth Day Of The Ninety-Fourth Week, And A Friday On Which A Boy Of Fourteen Writes The Week On A Sheet In Chalk At The Back Of A Smithy And A Recordwright Of Thirty-Four Does The Whole Of The Working In A Room With About Eleven People In It And Says Out Loud That A Name Is Therefore Worth Between Four Pounds Sixteen Shillings And Six Pounds This Year, And That Is The Reason Nobody In That Room Will Agree About Anything

Fourth day of the ninety-fourth week. His two hundred and fifty-sixth morning. One hundred and six days after the settlement. The fever thirty-one weeks and six days. Sixty days since the division. Six days since a page of that book was read out in a room with the door shut.

The week of a country town is made on the Friday night. The descent, the births, the deaths, the fold coming in and going out, and whose ground it is under the wall. It is written on one sheet, in chalk, on a board, and it goes up on the end wall of the smithy, where about nine people read it going in and out and about two hundred read it on the market day, and nobody has ever written down a rule that says it has to be there.

Toby Henshaw was fourteen and he wrote it in chalk on a board, because a man of sixty-one had done it in chalk for eleven years, and before that a woman had done it in chalk, and before that it was a slate. He can count and he cannot yet add, and he is paid a penny for the week, and the penny comes out of a hundred and forty pounds.

He had got as far as the second column on the Friday morning and had got the deaths wrong.

There is no column for death, and so a death on the week is written where it can be got into. What Toby Henshaw had written, at the head of the second column, in the middle of the holdings, was the name of a man of about eighty-four who had died on the Wednesday at Kiln Row, with his holding number against it.

A man of about eighty-four who has died has still got four acres and a number and a beech stake in a field, and the stake is still there.

“*That is right,*” the man of thirty-one said. “*That is exactly right.*”

“*It is wrong,*” said Toby Henshaw, without looking up. “*That is last week. He is on last week’s sheet as a death, so if I put him in the holdings again then he has died twice.*”

“*He has died twice because there is nowhere to put him.*”

“*Then he ought to have gone in the ground column.*” The boy put the chalk down. “*He had seven feet behind the meeting-house. He put a name on it about two years back. He has not been in it and he is not going to be, and his is not the ground, it is going to his brother’s boy, and the boy is fourteen and he is here.*”

And there was a moment in a smithy at about the ninth hour on a Friday morning in which two people in a room of about eleven understood exactly what had been said, and about nine of the eleven did not.

The boy of fourteen was the one who had said it, and he was not thanked, and he did not expect to be.

---

The working was done in the chancel at about the second hour of the afternoon, on Thursday’s board, wiped over on the back, because the front of the board was this week’s sheet and the back of the board was last week’s sheet, and neither of them is sacred.

She said, before she put the chalk on it, that she was going to do the whole of it in pence as well as pounds, and that anybody might stop her at any line, and that if she got a line wrong she would take it off and do it again, and that this was going to cost about a quarter of an hour and she would rather spend the quarter of an hour than be asked on Saturday whether she had multiplied a thing.

*THE FOLD. A hundred and forty acres, let at four shillings the acre, is five hundred and sixty shillings, which is twenty-eight pounds a year and no pence. Twenty-eight pounds is six thousand seven hundred and twenty pence. There are about sixty households in this town that keep a beast on the fold, and six thousand seven hundred and twenty pence divided among sixty households is a hundred and twelve pence, which is nine shillings and fourpence a household for the year.*

*WHAT HAPPENS IF THE LEAF IS PUT BACK. The forty-three names on that leaf carry, at this hour, the descent of about eleven households that are alive, and of the ground of about four of them.*

*If the leaf is put back, the descent runs again, and any ground of those four that has changed hands in a hundred and forty-one years becomes a claim, and the whole of the fold has to be let a second time. The fold is twenty-eight pounds. Fourteen pounds of it would have to be found again, and fourteen pounds spread upon about sixty households is four shillings and eightpence each, and that is half of what a household’s fold is worth in a year.*

*THE WALL. The common wall at the top of the lane is about eleven feet high and about four miles of it in all, counting the returns, and there is a mile and a half of it that has not been touched inside nine years. The making-up is sixteen days of work and there are nine men on it at a time, and sixteen days of nine men is a hundred and forty-four days of work. A man’s day on this wall is eightpence, and a hundred and forty-four times eightpence is one thousand one hundred and fifty-two pence, which is four pounds and sixteen shillings and no pence.*

*THE ONE ON THE WALL. The book is kept by one person. That person is paid two shillings a year by each household that keeps a beast on the fold, and there are about sixty of those, and two shillings upon sixty households is a hundred and twenty shillings, which is six pounds a year. It has been six pounds a year for nineteen years, and it was six pounds a year the year before that.*

Then she put the chalk down and stood back and let the room do it, and about four people in that room did it, and one of them said it out loud, and it is worth recording who, because he was the miller and not the recordwright.

“*Four pounds sixteen.*”

“*Against six pounds.*”

“*Then a name is worth somewhere between four pounds sixteen shillings and six pounds this year,*” said Marda Rill, “*and that is the whole of the working. That is why nobody in this room will agree about anything. I would like it written down that I have said the reason out loud, because on Saturday about nine people are going to argue for an hour and ninety of them are going to be told it is a matter of principle. It is not. It is four pounds sixteen shillings.*”

A man by the fire said that it was the best money this town had ever been handed.

“*Say the rest of it.*”

“*It is the best money this town has ever been handed, and I have had it for nineteen years, and nobody has ever once asked me whether it was enough, and I would like that to be entered, in whatever hand you do it in.*”

“*It is entered. It goes in under the head of the sheet with the day on it, and the day is the fourth day of the ninety-fourth week, and on Saturday you may read it before it is read out and after it is read out.*”

---

And then the fourth thing, which nobody had asked for and which is the same thing as the other three, and she found it herself, in front of about eleven people, with the chalk in her hand.

“*The orchard is not in it.*”

There are about nine hundred apple trees on about eleven acres inside the common wall, and eleven households work them, and the fruit is divided among the sixty. The lower corner of the orchard is a frost pocket, and the fruit there is a fortnight later than the fruit in the rest of it, and is worth about twice as much if it comes at all.

“*Nine hundred trees,*” she said, “*and eleven families, and a wall round it, and it is in none of the four columns.*”

The man of eighty-one on the chair by the fire said that it was in the wall.

“*It is in the wall in the way that a road is in a road. Nobody’s name is against it. If the eleven of them stop working it, it goes back to being grass, and there is nothing to send anybody a return about, and there has never been a return about it in four hundred years, and I have looked.*”

“*Then the orchard is worth nothing to this dispute, and that is all you are saying.*”

“*I am saying the orchard is worth nothing to this dispute, and that if the forty households that lose half a fold want to be paid for it, they are going to have to be paid out of the orchard, and there are no columns for that either.*”

---

And it was in that room, at about the fourth hour of a Friday afternoon with about eleven people in it, that a man of thirty-one made the mistake of the week. It was the mistake he has made in nine rooms in six weeks in another town, and in about nine rooms in three days in this one, which is that a figure is a good thing and the right shape and the argument is over.

“*That is a good number,*” he said. “*Four pounds sixteen against six. That is not a large figure. Nobody can be ruined by four pounds sixteen shillings. We are going to a market about a wall.*”

“*Say the last part of that again.*”

“*We are going to a market about a wall.*”

“*You have said it twice now, and both times it has been about a wall.*”

She put the chalk down on the board, which she does when she is about to say a thing she means to be able to be asked about later, in a room, on a Saturday, by anybody.

“*And I am going to say the other half of it. You are the third person this week who has said it to me and the first one who has said it in a room.*”

“*And the reason I am saying it in a room is that on Saturday I have to say it again in front of nine people, and I would rather it were a thing I had said to one man on a Friday and had got wrong and had taken back.*”

“*Say it.*”

“*The figure is a good one. And nobody in this room has been asked whether the forty households that lose half a fold are the same forty that gain a wall.*”

“*I asked about the wall.*”

“*You said nobody can be ruined by four pounds sixteen shillings, and you did not say the word *ruined*, you said the word *wall*. The two of those are the same sentence to a man in a market, and they are not the same sentence to a woman who has four children and half of what her beasts are worth.*”

The room did not do the thing that rooms do. There was about nine seconds of nothing.

Then the man of eighty-one said *say the half out loud*, and she said that nine shillings and fourpence is a household’s whole year on that fold, and half of it is four shillings and eightpence, and there is no beast in that at any price, and it is a year.

“*And a name is worth between four pounds sixteen and six.*”

“*And a name is worth between four pounds sixteen and six,*” she said, “*and about forty of the sixty in this town are being asked for half of what they had. I have not asked one of them, and neither has he, and neither has the woman from the barn. On Saturday we are going to read a page out in a room to nine people, and about a hundred and ninety people are going to hear it in a square, and not one of the hundred and ninety is going to be asked first.*”

She said that to a man of thirty-one and not to a room. Then she picked the chalk up again and wiped the back of the board off, because the back of the board was last week’s sheet and it was not sacred either.

The fever was thirty-one weeks and six days old. A boy of fourteen had written a dead man of about eighty-four into a column of holdings because there was nowhere else to write him, and a recordwright of thirty-four had done the whole of the working of this town on the back of a board in pence and in pounds, and the whole of it came to four pounds sixteen shillings and no pence on one side and six pounds a year on the other, and about eleven people were in a chancel eight feet by eleven, and about four of them had understood the reason and about nine had not.

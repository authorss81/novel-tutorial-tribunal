# Chapter 521: The Fifth Day Of The Ninety-Sixth Week, And A Saturday On Which The Week Of This Town Is Read Out In A Chancel Eight Feet By Eleven For The First Time In Three Weeks And It Takes About Eleven Minutes And About Four People In That Room Understand One Line Of It And About Nine Do Not, And A Man Of Thirty-One Says Out Loud That It Is An Ordinary Week, And A Boy Of Fourteen Asks Him What The Fourth Column Is For

Fifth day of the ninety-sixth week. His two hundred and seventy-first morning. One hundred and twenty-one days after the settlement. The fever thirty-four weeks and no days. Seventy-five days since the division. Twenty-one days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town two days from where he is standing.

The week of a country town is read out on a Saturday morning. It has been done in that building for four hundred years and in that room for nineteen, and it is not written anywhere, and there is no form in the county of Aldwick that says a man may be sent for to hear it.

It had been missed twice. She said the count out loud in the porch before anybody went in, because the man of thirty-one had been in that porch every day for a fortnight and was going to say something about it.

“*It was not read on the sixty-first day after the division, which was a Saturday, and I was up a bank at the seventh hour with a shovel.*”

“*And it was not read on the Saturday of the ninety-fifth week, when a leaf a hundred and forty-one years old was read out in that room instead. That is two Saturdays in three weeks and I have had both of them out of my own mouth before this morning.*”

“*I would like it said in the porch rather than in the room, because a room is a place where people are held to.*”

About nine people were in that porch and about nine were in the square. The square on a Saturday is a place with the bars of Thursday’s stalls still lying in the mud, and the sweep in the middle is done on the Wednesday night by two men who are paid a penny each out of the twenty-eight pounds a year that a hundred and forty acres of sheep bring in at four shillings the acre.

---

The chancel door was open and the window was down, and about nine people went in, and the man of thirty-one did not go in with them and had not asked to.

A person not of this town is not in a room where a book is read. That was settled on a Tuesday in the third week of his being in Bramblefold and it had not moved since. He had understood it in about four seconds the first time, and a man of eighty-one had told him so to his face, and about four people in that porch had heard the man of eighty-one say it.

So he was on the step with the door open behind him and the window above him, and he heard eleven minutes of a book four hundred and eleven years old being read out on a Saturday morning in a country town. It was the same eleven minutes it had always taken.

The week is four columns and a day. The head of it is the day of a week and the name of nobody, and the boy’s name is at the foot of the whole of it and not at the top.

It went like this.

A holding of four acres at the top of the lane passed on the Wednesday from a man of about sixty to his son. The number went with it. The number is the tenth, and it is cut on a beech stake and in chalk on the wall, and the son is the fourth one of that name in the book. The book has never once had a line saying how many of them there had been.

Four sheep went out of the fold on the Thursday and two came in. The four that went out belonged to a man of about thirty-five who has a cottage and no beast. There is no column in that book for how many beasts were on the fold, because a book that carried the beasts would have to be corrected every week, and this book has not been corrected in nineteen years.

A child was born on the Friday at about the fourth hour on the upper row, and it is a boy, and the mother is the second of that name in the third column. The ground behind the meeting-house is six feet, and it came to her from her grandmother.

And then the fourth column, and the man of about eighty-four.

He died on the Wednesday of the fifty-eighth day after the division. He has been in the ground column on the end wall of the smithy for three weeks with the day against him, in the middle of the column about the ground behind the meeting-house. He has been there since a woman of thirty-four told a boy of fourteen to put him there and to say it was her doing.

The book has no column for a death. It has four hundred years of descents and about four hundred men a year in it, and every one of those men is alive in it. The one removal in four hundred years is a leaf.

The fourth column carries a depth and can carry a name and can carry a day. It does not carry the fact that the man is dead, which is the whole of why the name is there.

That is what the week said in about eleven minutes. About four people in that room knew which line was the one that had no column under it. About nine did not, and the nine went out into a square in the ordinary way at the ordinary time.

---

And then the last thing she read was not in the book.

“*That is the week,*” Marda Rill said. “*And there is one more line and it is not in that book, and I am going to read it in here anyway, which is a thing I have not done before.*”

“*I am doing it because it is on a board at the bottom of this town in a column with nothing at the top of it, and the boy who wrote it is in this room.*”

Nobody in that room said anything at all for about four seconds.

She read it.

“*About nine feet of the bottom of the common wall, open since the day it came down. The day it was left is the sixty-first day after the division, which is a Saturday.*”

“*The crew lost about four days of the twenty to weather and went the three that were left to the top of that section, and the wall stopped on the Wednesday night of the ninety-sixth week.*”

“*At the end of the nine feet there is a shelf in the last standing course with a boundary stone in it that has nothing cut on it at all.*”

That is a document. A man of forty-four said so on a wall in the fourth day of the ninety-sixth week, and the copyist said it in a market square on the Thursday, in pence, to about two hundred people. About two hundred of them had a figure they could not check, and the figure was right.

There is no column in that book for a gap in a wall. There is no column in it for a boundary stone with nothing cut on it. There is no column in it for a man of forty-four being right for nine years about a stone that had been in a lump, and no column for the fact that nobody in this county is going to shut it before the weather turns.

It went in a room eight feet by eleven under a column with no heading on it. About four people in that room worked out where it was going. About nine heard a woman read a line about a wall.

---

Ilyan Vester was on the step and he had said out loud, to about nine people in a porch at about the half hour after the tenth, that it was an ordinary week.

He had said it because it was true in the way a week is true. He had been in this town twenty-one days and he had said a great many things out loud in about nine rooms, and a sentence that makes a thing ordinary is a thing a person can carry.

Then the boy came out of the room.

Toby Henshaw was fourteen and he writes the week in chalk on a board on the end wall of the smithy. He is paid a penny for the week out of the twenty-eight pounds, and he can count and he cannot yet add. He had been in that room for eleven minutes and he had written nothing.

He came onto the step and sat down on the stone bench about two-thirds along, and put the rag on his knee.

“*You are the one at the meeting-house. You are not from here.*”

“*I am not.*”

“*Then you are the only person on this step I have not asked already.*” He put his hands flat on his knees. “*I have asked her three Fridays running and she gives me the same answer three Fridays running. What is the fourth column for?*”

“*The ground behind the meeting-house.*”

The boy did not say anything for about four seconds. Then he said the whole of the rest of it, in the voice of a boy of fourteen who has written a dead man into a column three weeks running and has not slept a whole night about it since.

“*That is what I have written on the board for three weeks. And there is a man in it who is dead.*”

“*He has been in it since the Friday of the ninety-fourth week, and he is in the ground column with the day against him, and I asked her about it and she told me to write him there and to say it was her doing if anybody asked.*”

“*I have written him there three times and I have not said it was her doing to anybody, because nobody has asked me and I was not going to go round a town telling people that a dead man is in the ground column on a board.*”

“*Why have you not said it was her doing?*”

“*Because if I say it was her doing then I did not work it out, and I did.*” The boy took the rag off his knee and folded it. “*I worked out that he ought to have gone in the ground column before she said so, and I have not told anybody that, and you are the first person I have said it to and you are not of this county and you cannot be sent for.*”

Nobody in that porch said anything about that. About four people in it had understood the whole of what the boy had said underneath the sentence. About nine had heard a boy of fourteen say a thing about a man in a column.

Marda Rill came out of the room and stood at the door and listened to the last of it. She did not say the boy was right and she did not say she was wrong.

“*You should have come and told me on the Friday. Not on the Saturday, and not in the porch, and not to him.*”

“*And you did not, and I did not ask you, and that is the eleventh time in nineteen years I have been the one who has not asked.*”

“*I would like it said in front of about nine people that the eleventh is a boy of fourteen and not a widow.*”

“*You never ask anybody anything,*” the boy said, without any weight on it at all.

“*I never ask anybody anything that I cannot put a day against,*” she said. “*And there are about nine hundred things in a year I cannot put a day against. Asking about them costs a person about four minutes of their life and they never know it happened.*”

Then she said the other half of it, and she said it to the man of thirty-one and not to the porch.

“*The line about the wall is in a room eight feet by eleven with a column with nothing at the top of it over it. It will be in that room twice a year for as long as there is a building, and about four people will know what it is under and about nine will hear a wall.*”

“*That is the same four and the same nine as on the sheet, and I have stopped minding, and there is no form in the county of Aldwick that says a keeper may be asked whether she minds.*”

The fever was thirty-four weeks and no days old. A week of a town of about two thousand four hundred people was read out in a room eight feet by eleven in about eleven minutes in front of about nine people.

A holding passed, four sheep went out and two came in, a boy was born on the upper row, and a dead man of about eighty-four stood in the ground column with the day against him. About nine feet of the bottom of a common wall went in under a column with nothing at the top of it.

And a boy of fourteen had worked out that a dead man belonged in the ground column before the woman who keeps the book told him, and had said it out loud, once, to a man from another county who cannot be sent for.

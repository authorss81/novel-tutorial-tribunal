# Chapter 520: The Fourth Day Of The Ninety-Sixth Week, And A Friday On Which A Boy Of Fourteen Has To Be Told What To Do About A Gap In A Wall And There Is Nobody To Tell Him, And A Recordwright Of Thirty-Four Rules A Column For It With No Heading On It, And A Chalk Line About Nine Feet Long Goes On A Board On The End Wall Of The Smithy With The Boy’s Name Under It And Nothing At The Top

Fourth day of the ninety-sixth week. His two hundred and seventieth morning. One hundred and twenty days after the settlement. The fever thirty-three weeks and six days. Seventy-four days since the division. Twenty days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town two days from where he is standing.

The week of a country town is made on the Friday night. The descent, the births, the deaths, the fold coming in and going out, and whose ground it is under the wall.

It is written in chalk on a board on the end wall of the smithy, and about nine people read it going in and out and about two hundred read it on the market day, and nobody has ever written down a rule that says it has to be there.

The man of thirty-one was in that smithy from about the fourth hour of the afternoon and he was not asked to be.

He stayed until about the eighth hour. There is a fire in a smithy, and it is the only fire in that part of the bottom of the town that a man is allowed to sit at without giving a reason for it, and on a Friday evening in a cold week there is not much else to do in that lane with a leg and three pence a day.

Toby Henshaw was fourteen and he wrote it in chalk, because a man of sixty-one had done it in chalk for eleven years, and before that a woman had done it in chalk, and before that it was a slate. He can count and he cannot yet add, and he is paid a penny for the week, and the penny comes out of the twenty-eight pounds a year that a hundred and forty acres of sheep bring in at four shillings the acre.

The dead man of about eighty-four was in the ground column again, with the day against him, in the middle of the column about the ground behind the meeting-house, where he has been since the Friday of the ninety-fourth week.

Nobody looked at him. About nine people read that board every week and about four of them have never once read his name there. One of those four asked him about it in the week after, and he said the recordwright told him, and that was true, and that was the sentence he was told to say, and the man of four asked no further question and has not since.

The boy had got to the bottom of the fourth column at about the seventh hour and had stopped, with the chalk in his hand and the rag in his other one, and the two of them were the only tools anybody in that smithy had for the work.

A week is four columns and a day. The head of that sheet is the day of a week and the name of nobody, because a week does not have a name in this town, and the boy’s name is at the foot of the whole of it and not at the top, and about four people in this town could tell you why and about nine could not.

He is paid a penny for the week out of the twenty-eight pounds a year, and a penny out of twenty-eight pounds is not a sum he can do, which is the whole of what can and cannot yet add about a boy of fourteen.

“*There is a gap in the wall,*” he said. “*Nine feet of it, at the bottom end, and it is on the board at the back of a stall in the square because Mr Rill read it out on Thursday. I do not know what to do with it.*”

Nobody in that smithy had told him, and about nine people in it went on with what they were doing.

One of the nine was a man of thirty-one. He had been in the porch of that meeting-house for a fortnight. He had been on a wall on Monday. He had been on a step on a Saturday with about a hundred and ninety people in the square behind him. He was the only person in that smithy who had been told anything about a gap in a wall by a person who had a day for it.

And he could not tell him either, and it took him about four minutes to find out that he could not.

“*Put it in the fold,*” said one of the men at the fire.

The boy said that the gap is not in the fold, because the fold is the acres and the acres are a hundred and forty whichever way the wall stands, and a gap in a wall is not an acre.

“*Put it in the holdings.*”

“*A holding is a number and a quantity, and the quantity is right,*” the boy said. “*A gap is not a quantity.*”

He said it in the voice of a boy of fourteen who has written a dead man into a column because a woman of thirty-four told him to, and who has not slept a whole night about it since, and about four of the nine men in that smithy heard the whole of what was underneath the sentence.

“*Put it in the ground behind the meeting-house,*” said a man of about thirty-five.

The boy said no to that one out loud, in front of about nine people, and he said it well, and about four of them heard the whole of what he had understood.

“*That is seven feet of somebody. You do not put a wall in it.*”

Marda Rill came in at about the eighth hour and put her hand on the board.

She is thirty-four and she has kept the book nineteen years. She is paid two shillings a year by each of about sixty households, which is six pounds a year, unmoved in nineteen years. She had come for the board, because the board is the recordwright’s and nobody else’s.

About nine people in that smithy got out of the way of a woman with a key on a string round her neck and a slate under her arm, and about four of them got out of the way faster than the other five, and the difference between those two is four years of knowing her.

The boy told her the whole of it again in about a minute. She did not interrupt him, and at the end of it she asked him one question.

“*How many columns has that book got?*”

“*Four, Mrs Rill.*”

“*And how many did it have when it was made?*”

“*Four, Mrs Rill.*”

“*Then write those two numbers at the head of that board and not at the top of anything, and go on.*”

He wrote them. They are the only two figures in the whole of that sheet and he put them at the head of it, and about four people in that smithy read them and about nine did not.

“*Is there a column in that book for a gap?*”

“*No, Mrs Rill.*”

“*Is there a column in that book for a thing somebody went out of a room and would not go back in for?*”

The boy thought about that for about four seconds.

“*No, Mrs Rill.*”

“*Then I am going to rule one, and I am not going to head it, and you are going to tell me if I head it.*”

She ruled it on the right of the board with the edge of her hand, because there is nothing on a smithy’s board to rule with.

It came out about a hand wide and about a foot and a half long, which is the length of a gap. About four people in that smithy noticed that and about nine did not.

Then she put the chalk in the boy’s hand and did not take it back, which is the thing she has done twice in nineteen years.

“*One line,*” she said. “*About nine feet of the bottom of the common wall, open since the day it came down.*”

“*And the day it was left is the sixty-first day after the division, which is a Saturday, and you are to write the day of a week and not a number.*”

“*Why?*” said the boy.

“*Because a day of a week comes round. A day and a year does not come round, and a count does not come round, and there is nobody in this town that could tell you in four years what day of the week a Tuesday was. I have had that from a man of eighty-one and I am passing it on, and he is eighty-one and there will not be a second asking of him.*”

“*Where do I put it?*” said the boy.

“*Under your name at the foot, because you wrote it.*”

He wrote it. It is one line of about nine words in chalk, and then the boy’s name at the foot of it, and nothing at the top of the column.

The chalk on the top of the column is clean, and the board is a board and not a page, and there is no lock in this smithy and no key and no string round anybody’s neck, and any person in this town may add to it or rub it out, and about nine people who will read it on Thursday have not worked that out and about four have.

Ilyan Vester asked for it to be headed. He has asked once a week since the Tuesday of the ninety-fourth week. He was told no on that Tuesday, in front of about nine people, in about four sentences, and nobody enjoyed that either.

He asked again, on a Friday night, in a smithy, and the answer was the same answer, in about four sentences, in front of about nine people, and the second time was worse for him than the first, because the first time he had not been in the room and had heard about it afterwards from a woman of thirty-four who had put a square sheet of paper outside a shut door.

“*A heading is a promise about what goes in it,*” Marda Rill said, “*and I have not agreed what goes in it, and I have ruled it because a boy of fourteen could not write a week without it and not because anybody in this smithy knows what it is for.*”

“*Then what is it for?*”

That is the half she gave him on the Tuesday of the ninety-fourth week, and this time she gave him the other half, and she gave it out loud, in a smithy, to about nine people, and about four of them understood the whole of it at once.

“*If it were headed I would have to say what goes in it,*” she said.

“*And I do not know what goes in it.*”

She put the chalk down on the board and stood with her hand flat beside the column.

“*There is a gap in a wall in it. There may be four gaps in a wall in it by Christmas. There may be a thing a man said in a porch on a Sunday in it, and I do not know that either, and I have been in this county nineteen years.*”

“*Then it is a column that cannot be filled,*” said the man of thirty-five.

“*It is a column that cannot be filled according to a heading, because there is no heading, and that is the only reason it can hold anything. And if I headed it I would be promising that the next person could fill it in a year. I cannot make that promise. I would not know in a year whether I had kept it, and there is no form in this county that says a person may be asked about a promise she does not remember making.*”

“*So it is the not-knowing that is the reason for it,*” he said.

“*That is the reason for it. Write the day of a week at the head of the sheet and not a number, and go home. Your mother wants you in before the weather turns.*”

Nobody in that smithy thanked anybody.

The boy was not thanked for the four seconds in which he told a man of about thirty-five that you do not put a wall in the ground behind the meeting-house. He had not expected to be. He was paid a penny and the penny is out of the twenty-eight pounds and there is no form that says a boy may be paid anything else for a column he ruled himself.

About four people in that smithy understood, standing at a board, that a boy of fourteen who cannot add is now the only person in that town who can be shown a thing that happened. About nine did not.

The board went up on the end wall at about the ninth hour, where about nine people read it going in and out, and a hundred and ninety people will read it on the next Thursday and not one of them will be able to check the figure in it.

The fever was thirty-three weeks and six days old. On the end wall of the smithy there was a column about a hand wide and about a foot and a half long with nine feet of chalk in it and a day of a week against it, and a boy’s name at the foot of it because he wrote it, and nothing at the top.

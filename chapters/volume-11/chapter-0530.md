# Chapter 530: The Seventh And Last Day Of The Ninety-Seventh Week, And A Monday On Which A Boy Of Fourteen Has To Be Told What To Do About A Week In Which Two Towns Remember The Same Morning Differently And There Is No Column For That And There Is Nobody To Tell Him, And A Recordwright Of Thirty-Four Rules Another Column On The End Wall Of A Smithy With No Heading On It And Writes In It One Line About Two Accounts Of One Morning In Two Towns, And The Boy’s Name Is At The Foot Of It Because He Wrote It, And She Says Out Loud That A Book With Four Columns And No Fifth Is Now A Book With Three Columns That Have No Heading On Them

Seventh and last day of the ninety-seventh week, and the morrow is the first day of the ninety-eighth. His two hundred and eightieth morning. One hundred and thirty days after the settlement. The fever thirty-five weeks and two days. Eighty-four days since the division. Thirty days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town two days from where he is standing.

The week of a country town is made on the Friday night and it is read on the Saturday, and this is the Monday, and there is nothing to make.

He came in at about the fourth hour of the afternoon and the board was still on the end wall with Friday’s week on it, because a board is a board and not a page, and nobody has ever written a rule that says a week comes down on the Saturday.

The dead man of about eighty-four was in it, in the ground column, in the middle of the column about the ground behind the meeting-house, with the day against him, where he has been since the Friday of the ninety-fourth week.

About nine people read that board every week and about four of them have never once read his name in it. One of the four asked about it in the week after and was told it was the recordwright’s doing, which was true, and the man of four asked no further question and has not since.

And there was the column with nothing at the top of it, about a hand wide and about a foot and a half long, which is the length of a gap, with about nine feet of chalk in it and a day of a week against it and the boy’s name at the foot of it.

The boy came in at about the seventh hour with the chalk and the rag and stood in front of the board and did not start.

---

“*There is a dead man in the ground column,*” he said, “*and there is a gap in a wall in the next column with nothing at the top of it.*”

“*And this week there were two accounts of one morning read out in a market square in front of about two hundred people. One of them is four words a hundred and forty-one years old and the other of them is four sentences a woman of about fifty-one wrote out from memory in a town two days off.*”

“*And a man of about seventy-two was asked about it on a Thursday in a room with the door open and said he did not remember, and there were about nine people in that room and about four of them understood it was a finding and about nine understood it was a failure.*”

Nobody in that smithy had asked him for any of it. About nine people in it went on with what they were doing.

“*I do not know what to do with it. That is what I said about the gap, and you were not here for that one, and it went in the column with nothing at the top of it, and I have not slept a whole night about either of them since. There are two of them now.*”

One of the men at the fire said *put it in the fold*, and the boy said no, because the fold is a hundred and forty acres whichever way a wall stands and two towns remembering a morning differently is not an acre. Nobody argued with him about it, which is the thing that has happened about nine times in three weeks.

One of them said *put it in the holdings*, and the boy said a holding is a number and a quantity and the quantity is right, and a disagreement between two towns is not a number.

One of them said *put it in the ground behind the meeting-house*, and the boy did not answer that one out loud.

About four of the nine men in that smithy noticed that he did not answer it. About nine noticed that he did not say no to it this time, which is a different thing and is not the thing anybody meant.

---

The man of thirty-one had been in that smithy since the fourth hour and had been asked nothing and had not offered.

His want on that Monday evening was the smallest he has had in this country, and it was the fourth time in a fortnight that he has wanted a heading.

He had asked for the ruled space at the back of the book to be headed on a Tuesday in the fourth week. He had asked for the column on that board to be headed on the Friday of the sixth day. He had asked about the two of them at the end of the seventh week.

He asked about the third one at about the eighth hour on the Monday of this week, in front of about nine people, and he did it the way he does it, which is in about four sentences and in the voice of a man who has an arithmetic behind it.

“*There are three. A space with a line down each side of it on a leaf at the back of a book four hundred and eleven years old, a column on the end wall of this smithy, and a third one. Three ruled things in this town with nothing at the top of them.*”

“*In a hundred years there will be a person in this county who reads the four columns of that book, which have names over them and have had names over them for four hundred years, and then three that have not. He will work out what the difference is, because a person cannot help it, and there is no form in this county that says a person may not work out a thing.*”

“*He will,*” said Marda Rill.

“*Then give him the difference. Head the third one. You have had a fortnight to find out what goes in it and you have had a day since Saturday, and a week in which two towns remember one morning differently is a thing that a heading can hold.*”

“*And the heading would be that a town and another town remember the same morning differently, and that the two accounts are in a room over a milliner’s and in a book of orders nine miles off.*”

She put the chalk down on the board and stood with her hand flat beside the column.

“*No,*” she said. “*And I have given you the same answer three times in twenty-eight days and I am going to give you the other half of it, and it is the half that is new, and it is about a hundred years from now, and it is the only reason I have ever ruled any of them.*”

About nine people in that smithy stopped.

“*If the other two were headed, then a person in a hundred years would fill them in according to the heading. And they would fill them in wrong.*”

“*Not wrongly. Wrongly, and reasonably, and out of the best intentions, and with a good hand, and there would be nine lines in it and eight of them would be sensible and one of them would not, and the one that would not is the one a person in about a hundred and forty years would go and act on.*”

“*How do you know what the third one is for, if you do not know what goes in it?*”

“*I do not, and that is the only reason it can hold anything, and I said that to you in this smithy on the Friday of the sixth day of the ninety-sixth week, and you have written it down four times since in about four rooms and a market square, and about nine of them did not hear the second half of it.*”

She picked the chalk up and put it in the boy’s hand, and she did not take it back, which is the thing she has done three times in nineteen years.

“*A not-knowing in a column is better than a knowing that is not true. That is the whole of it.*”

“*A column with a name over it is a promise, and a promise is worth something, and it is worth more than a guess, and there is no form in this county that says a person may be asked about a promise she does not remember making.*”

“*Then what is a heading for,*” said the boy of fourteen, “*if it is not for telling somebody what goes in it?*”

Nobody in that smithy had expected that, and about four of the nine in it had already been thinking it.

“*That is a better question than the one he asked,*” she said, “*and I am going to answer it in a room and not in a smithy, and I will tell you the answer in about four years if I have got one.*”

“*And if I have not got one, then the answer is that there was not one, and I have said so out loud in a room in this building, and that is a thing you can check against a day of a week.*”

She ruled the column on the board with the edge of her hand, on the right of the one that was there. It came out about a hand wide and about two feet long, which is longer than the length of a gap, and about four people in that smithy noticed that and about nine did not.

“*One line,*” she said. “*Two accounts of one morning, in two towns, fifty-four days back. Both read out in the square on the Saturday and neither of them is in the book.*”

“*The man they name was asked here on the Thursday and said he did not remember, and he is not a hundred and forty-one years old and the leaf is, and the two of them do not go together, and nobody has made them go together.*”

The boy wrote it. It is one line of about nine words in chalk and it is the longest line in that unheaded column, and then the boy’s name at the foot of it because he wrote it, and there is nothing at the top of it.

Then she did the other thing, and it was the last thing she did that evening, and she said it standing in front of the board in front of about nine people.

“*A book with four columns and no fifth is now a book with three columns that have no heading on them. That is the whole of what has happened in three weeks, and I would like it said in a room and not in a smithy.*”

“*And I am saying it in a smithy because there is nobody in this county to say it to in a room except about nine people, and about four of them would understand it and about nine would not.*”

“*Three,*” said the man of thirty-one.

“*Three. A space with a line down each side of it on a leaf at the back of the book, a column on the end wall of this smithy, and the one the boy has just written in.*”

“*And the fourth of them is a return in a book nine miles off that is on the back of a form because the form has no line for two, and a man of fifty-four signed the back and did not sign the front, and that is a column with no heading on it in a leather wallet nine miles from here, and I cannot see it and I have not asked to.*”

Nobody in that smithy thanked anybody. The boy was not thanked for writing a line about two towns in a column with nothing at the top of it.

There is no form in the county of Aldwick that says a boy may be paid anything else for a column he ruled himself, and the penny comes out of the twenty-eight pounds a year that a hundred and forty acres of sheep bring in at four shillings the acre. A penny out of twenty-eight pounds is not a sum he can do.

The board went up on the end wall at about the ninth hour, where about nine people read it going in and out, and about two hundred will read it on the next Thursday, and not one of them will be able to check the figure in the first column or the sentence in the third.

The fever was thirty-five weeks and two days old. On the end wall of the smithy there were two chalk lines with nothing at the top of them, one about a foot and a half long and one about two feet, and a boy’s name at the foot of each of them because he wrote them.

A dead man of about eighty-four was in the ground column with the day against him, and a week in which two towns remember the same morning differently was standing in a town of about two thousand four hundred people that has not had a dated thing to count from since a leaf was struck a hundred and forty-one years back in a week that not one person in that county can name.

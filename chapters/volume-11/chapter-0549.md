# Chapter 549: The Fifth Day Of The Hundredth Week, And A Saturday On Which A Week Of This Town Is Read Out In A Room In About Eleven Minutes And Then Forty-Three Names Are Read Out In A Square In The Open Air In About Eleven Minutes In The Order They Are On The Leaf And The Four Gaps Are Read As Gaps, And A Man Of Thirty-One Asks For The Four Names To Be Read As Struck, And A Woman Of About Seventy Is Asked First In A Porch Before Anybody Argues And Says No, And It Is Entered, And Nobody Is Struck Out This Time

Fifth day of the hundredth week. His two hundred and ninety-ninth morning. One hundred and forty-nine days after the settlement. The fever thirty-eight weeks and no days. One hundred and three days since the division. Forty-nine days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town two days from where he is standing.

It is a market on a Thursday and this is a Saturday, and the square fills on a Saturday about the same as it fills on a Thursday. There was no market on and the bars of Thursday’s stalls were cleared on the Friday night by the same two men who sweep the middle.

The week of this town was read out in the morning, in the chancel, with the door open and the window down, and it took about eleven minutes, and about nine people went in.

A holding of two acres passed on the Wednesday. Three sheep went out of the fold on the Thursday and two came in. A man of about thirty had a wall taken down and put up again in four days and the mason came from Coldcombe. The dead man of about eighty-four was in the ground column on the end wall of the smithy for the twelfth week running, with the day against him.

Then a woman of about seventy-one had been buried behind the meeting-house on the Thursday and the ground behind it is nine feet, and the nine feet is now hers, and the man who had it is in the book in the first column and alive.

That was not remarked on by anybody, and about nine people went out into a square in the ordinary way at the ordinary time.

---

Then at about the eleventh hour about a hundred and ninety people were in that square, and about nine of them had been in the room, and about a hundred and eighty-one heard nothing at all.

The keeper of the book stood at the end of the wool bench with the leaf in her hand. She is the only person in this country who can read the hand on the front of that book. She had not brought the leaf out of the building on any previous Saturday and she brought it out on this one.

She said the terms out loud first, standing where two accounts of one morning had been read out in a square three weeks ago, and about a hundred and ninety people heard a woman read out a paragraph in the voice she uses for minutes.

“*The leaf is read in the order it is ruled, from the top, and the first name is the eldest of that household. Forty-three names. It took eleven minutes on the Saturday of the ninety-fifth week and it will take about eleven minutes now because a number has to be read as a number and not as a sound.*”

“*And the four words.*”

“*The four words are *struck by order* and they name no order and they have no day on them and I read them once and then I go on to the names.*”

“*And the four that are not going to be read.*”

“*Say that last part again, because you have got it wrong and about nine people in this square have got it wrong with you, and I would rather they had it wrong from a woman who told them than from a woman who did not.*”

“*The four gaps are going to be read. As gaps. A hundred and ninety people in this square are going to hear me go down a leaf a hundred and forty-one years old and say *a gap* four times in about eleven minutes, and not one of them is going to be told which four and not one of them is going to be told why.*”

About nine people in that square said *why* at once and about four of the nine were hers.

---

She read it, and it took about eleven minutes, and about a hundred and ninety people heard forty-three names.

She read the holdings too, as she did on the Saturday of the ninety-fifth week, because that is what a column is. The figures in the fold and the depths in the ground behind the meeting-house, read slowly, as numbers.

Nobody stopped her and nobody started her. A woman of about forty wrote on the back of her hand and got two of the figures wrong, as she did five weeks ago, and did not know.

And at the fourth name, and at the seventeenth, and at the twenty-eighth, and at the thirty-first, the keeper of the book said the two words *a gap*, and went on.

She read the stroke at the end. She said that it is a single line from about an inch below the top rule to about an inch above the bottom one, and that it is heavier than any other ink in four hundred and eleven years of that book, and that there is no way of telling from the ink in what order it was made.

Then she said the last of it and she said it into the middle of that square and not out of a doorway.

“*That is a list of forty-three names with four gaps in it and it has been read out in the open air in about eleven minutes. It is not in a book and it is not a return and it is not a finding and nothing is carried.*”

---

Then she said the thing the whole morning was for, standing at the end of the wool bench with the leaf in her hand, and about a hundred and ninety people heard it.

“*A square is not a book.*”

Nobody in that square stopped what they were doing.

“*I have said in a room four days ago that a finding goes into a book without anybody asking the people it is about. About four people in that room understood it and about nine heard a woman argue about a column.*”

“*Say the rest of it, because a hundred and ninety people have heard a leaf read out and not one of them has heard what it was for.*”

“*What it was for is that those forty-three names are in the public record of this town this afternoon. They are in the mouths of about a hundred and ninety people at this moment and in about nine mouths inside this building, and that is the whole of the public record in a county of about two thousand four hundred people.*”

She held the leaf up about a foot and let it down.

“*They are not in the book. The leaf they are on is in a cupboard with the door shut on a key on a string, and the book has four columns and no fifth and no column for a name that is on a leaf, and I have not ruled one and I am not going to.*”

Barnaby Rill, in his chair, understood the whole of that. About nine people in that square heard a woman say the word *book* and thought of a cupboard.

“*That is the difference between a square and a book and it is the whole of what I have got to say in the open air this afternoon. A book is where a thing goes so that one person can be sent for about it. A square is where a thing goes so that about a hundred and ninety people can be told about it and nobody can.*”

---

Then the man of thirty-one did the thing, and he did it in the open air in front of about a hundred and ninety people, and about four of them had known he was going to before he opened his mouth.

“*Say the four gaps with the names under them.*”

Nobody in that square said anything for about four seconds.

“*Say what you mean by that, because I have just read forty-three names out in about eleven minutes with four gaps in it and a hundred and ninety people heard me, and you have stood at the end of that wool bench for a hundred and forty-nine days and asked me for a fifth thing twice.*”

“*Read the four names under the four gaps, and then read who struck them and when and on what piece of paper. That is four lines. Then there is no gap in it, and it is still not in a book, and a hundred and ninety people will have heard which four and that they struck themselves out with their own hands in a square.*”

“*You want the gaps filled and there is nobody in this county to ask.*”

“*I want the gaps filled and I am not asking you to ask anybody. I am asking you to say four names out loud in a square.*”

“*Say the rest of that, because about four people in this square have worked out what you are asking for and about nine have not.*”

“*I am asking you to do to them in about eleven minutes in the open air what you said in a room on Monday that I must not do in a room with the door shut. That is all of it and there is not one more word of it.*”

About four of the about a hundred and ninety understood that inside about four seconds.

---

Then the woman of about seventy was asked first, in a porch, before anybody argued, and she had not been sent for and she had not been told that anything was going to be asked of her, and the woman of about fifty-one had come down the lane on the Sunday of the ninety-sixth week and had asked her one question and had got a no.

She was at her own door on the lower row and about nine people were in the road, and about four of the nine had come to hear whether she would say it again.

The question was put to her by the keeper of the book, standing on the step of the house, in about nine words.

“*Four names were struck out of a copy of that list in a square five weeks ago and one of them was the name your grandmother is descended from. A man has asked that the four be read in this square with the four names under them and who struck them. Do you want that.*”

Nobody in that road said anything for about four seconds.

“*No,*” said the woman of about seventy.

“*Say the rest of it, because I am going to write this down and I would rather write what you say than what I think you say.*”

“*Leave the gaps. I told the woman who came down this lane twenty-seven days ago to leave the striking on the sheet in the room and not in the book, and she did it, and the four gaps are on that sheet, and they can stay as four gaps in a square as well.*”

She had her hand on the door frame.

“*Because in a hundred years there will be a person in this town who was told that a woman of about seventy went up a step in front of about a hundred and ninety people and struck her own grandmother’s name out of a descent. That is a thing a person is told, and I have watched what this town does with a thing it is told in about four days.*”

Nobody in that road thanked anybody. A woman of about seventy was not thanked for saying no a second time and a woman of thirty-four was not thanked for writing it down, and there is no form in the county of Aldwick that says a person may be thanked for either.

---

The keeper entered it at the back of that book, on a fresh leaf, in the ordinary hand, with the day on it, ruling nothing and heading nothing, and she read the last four lines out in the road to about nine people and not to a room.

Then she counted, out loud, in the road, because she counts out loud.

“*That is two people in four hundred and eleven years.*”

“*She said it twenty-seven days ago.*”

“*She did. It is still two people and it is three afternoons, and I am going to say both halves of that out loud because about nine people in this road would have made it three.*”

“*And is it a form now.*”

“*It is not a form and it is not a practice and there is no column in that book for a person who has declined something twice and I have not ruled one. I said in a porch on Wednesday of this week that if a third person in this county says no to a thing about their own name then what happens to that is a question that has to be worked out by whoever is there at the time.*”

“*Say the rest of it, because you have not said whether there is going to be a third person.*”

“*I have not. There has not been a third person, and the woman of about twenty-eight in a room over the chandler’s shop was asked a question last Tuesday and said she did not know and would not guess, and that is not a no, and I counted her twice on Wednesday morning and I am not going to count her again.*”

The woman of about fifty-one understood the whole of that and said nothing, because she has said nothing in this town since a Sunday in the ninety-sixth week and had not meant to start. About nine people in that road heard a woman say that there has not been a third person.

---

Then they went back to the square, and about a hundred and ninety people were in it still, and the man of thirty-one asked for the four names one more time and was refused one more time, and the second refusal was in about four words.

“*No,*” said the keeper of the book. “*I have asked her and I have entered it and I am not going to say a name in this square against the entry of the only person in this county who can be sent for about this afternoon.*”

Nobody in that square thanked anybody and nobody was struck out. A leaf a hundred and forty-one years old was read out in the open air in about eleven minutes with four gaps in it and the four words in the margin name no order and no day.

And about a hundred and ninety people in the open air heard a list of forty-three names with four gaps in it, and about nine of them can say where they heard it, and about a hundred and eighty-one will be told about it by somebody.

Nobody in that square was in the book when it was read out, and about four people in that town know the difference between a square and a book and about nine do not.

The fever was thirty-eight weeks and no days old. The forty-three names on a leaf struck through a hundred and forty-one years back went out into the public record of this town for the second time, on a Saturday, in a square, in about eleven minutes, in the order they are ruled, and the four gaps were read as gaps.

A man of thirty-one asked for the four names to be read as struck and was refused twice, and a woman of about seventy was asked first, in a porch, before anybody argued, and said no, and it was entered, and she is still one of two people in four hundred and eleven years and it is still not a form.

And seventy-three days back from this Saturday is the morning of the thirtieth day after the division, which is one hundred and three days since the division less the thirty, which is seventy-three, and about a hundred and ninety people in that square have in their mouths an account of a morning that this town has never resolved and is not going to resolve this afternoon.

# Chapter 544: The Seventh And Last Day Of The Ninety-Ninth Week, And The Morrow Is The First Of The Hundredth, And A Monday On Which The Two Accounts Of The Morning Of The Thirtieth Day After The Division Are Put In Front Of The Person They Are About In A Room With The Door Open, And Nobody Asks Him Anything, And A Man Of Thirty-Four From A Town Four Days Off Reads The Quicker Hand And The Reading Stops Where It Stops, And A Man Of Thirty-One Is Asked By The Keeper Of The Book Whether He Is Going To Say The Sentence He Has Been Carrying And Says That He Is Not

Seventh and last day of the ninety-ninth week, and the morrow is the first day of the hundredth. His two hundred and ninety-fourth morning. One hundred and forty-four days after the settlement. The fever thirty-seven weeks and two days. Ninety-eight days since the division. Forty-four days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town two days from where he is standing.

The room was the chancel, eight feet by eleven, with the door open and the window down. Nobody had been asked to come into it and nobody had been asked to stay out of it, and about nine people were in it at about the eleventh hour, and about four of them had been in that room on the Sunday before.

The copy of a copy of four pages was on the table. It had come down on the previous morning from two rooms over a milliner’s at the bottom of the town, in the hand of the woman who wrote it. She had put it down flat on the table and had not read it out, and it had lain there about nine hours before anybody came into the room.

---

The man of about seventy-two was already there. He had come up the lane at about the half hour after the tenth, on a Monday, in a dry week, and nobody had sent for him and nobody had told him what the room had been opened for, and he had found nine people in it and a door that was open.

That was wrong and the keeper of the book knew it was wrong before anybody else said so.

“*Mr Kettle. I have kept you standing in a porch for nine years for eleven minutes about four acres at the bottom of the lane, and I have put you in a chair in this room three times and not once told you what any of them were about, and I told a room full of people in this building on the Thursday of the ninety-seventh week that it was wrong and I have gone on doing it.*”

He put his hand on the arm of the chair, and it was the chair he had been given on that Thursday and not the one by the fire.

“*Say what this one is about.*”

“*It is about four lines at the foot of the third page of that, and a name at the foot of the fourth. I am going to have them read out in this room in front of about nine people. You are named on the fourth page, and I am telling you before anybody else here knows it.*”

About four seconds went past in that room, and a man of thirty-four from four days off put both hands on the back of the chair in front of him.

“*And the other half of it.*”

“*Nobody is going to ask you anything. I fixed that in this room on the Thursday of the ninety-seventh week and I have had eighteen days to think about whether I still hold to it and I do.*”

“*Then what is the point of me being in the room.*”

“*That is your question and it is the right one and I am not going to answer it before the reading, because I do not know the answer until it has been read out. I have known since about the fourth hour yesterday that I was going to put you in this chair and I have not known since the fourth hour yesterday why.*”

---

Then she asked him one question, and it was a question and not a notice, and about nine people in that room understood the difference inside about four seconds.

“*Will you stay while it is read.*”

“*Ay.*”

“*Say the rest of it, because I have asked you one question in this room in eighteen days and I asked it with terms and I am not going to leave it to about nine people.*”

“*I would like to know what it says about me in a room where about nine people can be sent for to this building and say what they said this morning. That is what I asked for in a porch on the Wednesday of the ninety-eighth week, and I asked for it once and not to be told what I said last time, and nobody has asked me anything since and that is twelve days and every one of them was a day nobody counted on purpose.*”

“*Then you will hear it and you will not be asked one word.*”

“*I have been in this county seventy-two years and I have been asked about nine things and six of them were about money.*”

Nobody in that room thanked anybody. A man of seventy-two was not thanked for staying in a chair and a woman of thirty-four was not thanked for asking him, and there is no form in the county of Aldwick that says a person may be thanked for either.

---

The man of thirty-four from a town four days off read it, and he stood at the table and not at the window, and about four of the nine people in that room had not seen him stand anywhere but the window.

He read the four lines at the foot of the third page. He was one of about four people in this country who can read that hand and the hand it was copied from is a round hand with forty-one mistakes in it and thirty-eight of them corrected, and this one is in a quicker hand with about nine mistakes in it and none of them corrected.

He read it as it is on the page. Nobody in that room stopped him and nobody in that room started him.

Then he read the name at the foot of the fourth page, and he said it as it is written and did not put a title in front of it.

Nobody in that room said anything for about four seconds, and a man of about seventy-two in a chair did not move, and about four of the nine people in that room were looking at him and about nine were not.

Then the man of thirty-four read the fourth of the four lines, and it is the one that stops the reading.

---

“*That the person was asked by a man of thirty-four of Bramblefold and not by the woman, and about four people heard the question and about nine heard the answer.*”

He put his hand flat on the page and stopped there.

“*Say the rest of it,*” said the recordwright, “*because you have stopped on a line and about nine people in this room think you have run out of page.*”

“*I have not run out of page. That is the last line and there is a name under it. I have stopped because that is the first name of anybody in a hundred and forty-one days and I do not know how to read it out without somebody in this room asking a question, and nobody is going to ask.*”

The man of thirty-four from four days off understood the whole of what he had stopped for, and the weaver of sixty-two understood it and said nothing.

“*Then say what it stops at.*”

“*It stops at a man of thirty-four of Bramblefold who asked a question in a square in another county. About two hundred people there heard the answer of. There is no name for him on this page or on any page, and there is no form in this county that says a person may be asked what they did in a square in another county.*”

“*And how many men of thirty-four does this county have that anybody could ask.*”

“*It has not got a count and nobody in this room is going to make one, and that is the answer. I have been in this county for fifteen days and there is nobody in it that anybody has told me can be sent for about a square in another county, and I am not of it and cannot be sent for from it either.*”

---

Then the keeper did the thing she had said she was going to do on the Sunday, and it took about four minutes, and she did it in the ordinary hand with the day on it at the foot of the fourth page of a copy of a copy.

*At the foot of this page, on the seventh and last day of the ninety-ninth week, in a room with the door open, in front of about nine people, in the ordinary hand of the keeper of the book of this town. That the name at the foot of this page is the name of a person of this town, of about seventy-two years, who is recorded in the four lines at the foot of the third page as having been asked a question in the open, in a square, in a town two days from here, on the morning of the thirtieth day after the division, and as having given an answer.*

*That the person named has been told, in that room, before the reading, that the name at the foot of this page is his and what is written about him at the foot of the third page.*

*That the person named was not asked anything. That he has said in that room that he would like to know what it says about him and not that he was asked whether it says it.*

*That the four lines at the foot of the third page are read out in that room in full and are not entered in this book, and that this book has four columns and no column for a copy of a copy and none has been ruled.*

Nobody in that room read it twice and nobody in that room asked whether it was right.

---

The man of about seventy-two was told, and it was told twice, once before the reading and once after the writing, and he was asked nothing at either point.

He said nothing at all in that room. About nine people were in it. He sat in the chair with his hand on the arm of it for about four minutes afterwards and said nothing at all, and the fire was not in it and the window was down and the door was open and about nine people watched a man of seventy-two not say a word.

The keeper of the book wrote one more line at the foot of that leaf and read it out, and it is four words long.

“*That he said nothing.*”

About four seconds went past in that room and the woman of about forty at the wool bench turned her head and looked at the door and then at the table again.

“*Say the reason that is entered,*” said the man of thirty-four, “*and say it before you leave the room, because four words with no reason behind them is a thing I have watched two shelves stop being kept over, and I have told this town about both of them.*”

“*He has been asked about nine things in seventy-two years and six of them were about money. And I promised him in this room twenty minutes ago that he would not be asked one word, and I have kept it.*”

She stopped there, and then she said the rest of it.

“*And a woman of thirty-four keeping a promise to a man she cannot pay is the only kind of keeping there has ever been in this county.*”

Barnaby Rill understood all of that from the chair. About nine people in that room heard a woman write down that a man had said nothing.

---

And then the man of thirty-one was asked the question he had been carrying, and he was asked it by the woman who keeps the book, standing at the end of that table, in front of about nine people, and about four of them had been waiting for it since the seventh hour.

“*Are you going to say it.*”

“*Say what.*”

“*The sentence. The one you have been carrying since the Sunday of the ninety-fourth week and have not said in about forty days. I have watched you not say it in nine rooms and a porch and two market squares and I am asking you now, in this room, with the door open, in front of these nine people, whether you are going to say it.*”

Nobody in that room said anything for about four seconds.

“*No,*” he said.

“*Say the rest of it, because I have refused you once since Saturday and I gave a reason, and I am not going to let you go out of this room on a bare word.*”

“*I am not going to say it in a square about the Thursday of the ninety-seventh week. About two hundred people were in that square and not one of them can be sent for, and I have wanted that sentence in the open air for about forty days and it is not mine to put there.*”

“*Whose is it.*”

“*It is a woman of about fifty-one’s. She said it in a lane on the Friday of the ninety-eighth week in about ten words and she has never been asked where she got it and she is not a person this county could send for about a square in another town.*”

About four of the nine people in that room heard the whole of what he had just refused and about nine heard a man say he was not going to say a thing.

Then she said the last of it, standing at the table with her pen in her hand and not writing with it.

“*Then it is not going to be said, and there is no form in this county that says a man may be asked a second time about a thing he has already said no to, and I have asked you once and I will not ask you again in a room.*”

“*You can ask me in a porch.*”

“*I can ask you in a porch and you will say no again, and I did not ask you in a porch, and that is the whole of what happened to you today and it is four words long.*”

Nobody in that room thanked anybody and nobody in that room was sorry for anybody. A man of thirty-four read four lines out in a quicker hand and was not thanked for it. A man of about seventy-two sat in a chair for about four minutes and said nothing at all and was not thanked and was not sorry for.

The fever was thirty-seven weeks and two days old. A name was entered at the foot of the fourth page of a copy of a copy, in the ordinary hand, with the day on it, as the name of a person who was asked a question in a square in a town two days from here on the morning of the thirtieth day after the division.

The man named was told before the reading and after the writing and was asked nothing at either point, and the keeper of the book entered four words at the foot of that leaf, which is that he said nothing.

And a man of thirty-one was asked in that room whether he was going to say the sentence he had been carrying for about forty days, and said that he was not, and gave a reason, and the sentence is not in that room and is not in that book and is not in a square.

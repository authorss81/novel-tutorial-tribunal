# Chapter 507: The Fifth Day Of The Ninety-Fourth Week, And A Saturday On Which About Thirty People Go Up The Chalk To The Top Of The Common Wall With Shovels And A Barrow And A Bucket Of Water, And A Man Of Thirty-One With A Bad Leg Is Put On It And Cannot Do It And About Nine Men On A Wall Do Not Stop For A Man Who Cannot, And The Week Is Not Read Out For The First Time In Nineteen Years Because The Woman Who Reads It Is Carrying Chalk Up A Bank

Fifth day of the ninety-fourth week. His two hundred and fifty-seventh morning. One hundred and seven days after the settlement. The fever thirty-two weeks and no days. Sixty-one days since the division. Seven days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town two days from where he is standing.

The wall is about eleven feet high and four miles of it in all, counting the returns. The top lane runs along it for about a mile and a half on the top of the chalk, with a fall of about a foot and a half on the down side and nothing on the lane side for about nine feet.

There is a coping of chalk set on edge a foot wide and about nine inches proud, and four people of this town have recopied it since the wall was built, and that is what keeps the water out of the courses.

There is about a mile and a half of the wall that has not been touched inside nine years, and about a quarter of a mile of that is the section at the top wall, off the down, which is where a man of about fifty told him on Thursday in a market square that he would be.

It is a Saturday and there is no market, and the work is done on a Saturday because that is the one day a man who keeps a beast can be there without a loss. About thirty people went up the lane at about the seventh hour with shovels, a barrow, a bucket of water and two things of string.

Nine men and one woman carried at the bottom end, which is the end nearest the town. The woman is thirty-four, and she carried with a shovel, and nobody asked her to, and she is the only person in Bramblefold who has done a day’s work on that wall inside the nineteen years she has been keeping the book.

She said that out loud on the top of the bank at about the ninth hour, because nobody else was going to.

A man of about fifty named Ormer set the top. He is the man who found the stakes, and he has been finding stakes on that wall for about thirty-one years, and he finds them by the eye, and he has never in his life been wrong about one.

“*You will pull them,*” he said to the man of thirty-one. “*Not turn them. Pull. And put them on the coping and not in the barrow, because a barrow is for the rubble, and if a man puts chalk in the barrow then he has to carry it down and then he has to come up again, and I have watched men do that four times.*”

“*Where do I put them?*”

“*On the coping, a foot apart, and the foot is the length of your own boot, and I do not care whose boot. I have never once in thirty-one years cared whose boot it was.*”

---

He carried chalk for about an hour and a half, and then he was moved to the barrow, because the man on the barrow went down to get water and would not be back for a quarter of an hour.

The barrow is a plank on two wheels, and the going over the top of a chalk bank with a plank on two wheels and ninety pounds on it is not a thing that can be learned in a morning.

He got about forty yards with it on the second load and had to be taken off it by two people. One of them was a boy of about nineteen, who said nothing and simply took the handles off him. The other was a man of about forty-four, who said that his leg was gone.

“*It has been gone the best part of a year.*”

“*It has gone because you are on a bank and there is no flat in this country. If you had been born in the chalk you would not know it.*”

The man of thirty-one stood about four feet off to one side with the string in his hand for about an hour, and held the string for the top man to sight a course, and that was the whole of what he did for two hours.

He did it well. About nine men on a bank did not stop for him and did not start for him and did not ask him a single question about the string in his hand, and he found that the not-asking was the thing that undid him.

He had come to a town of two thousand four hundred people on a chalk slope two days off — a night at a lodging at the end of a lane and a day in a cart that stops four miles short — from a market town of four thousand people where he had spent forty-eight days making sentences. He had been in this town five days. He had asked a woman with a key for a place in a room, and he had been entered on a leaf at the back of a four-hundred-and-eleven-year-old book, and he had been refused a thank-you on a step.

He had not in five days been told one single thing about a bar and a coping and a footing course. On a Saturday morning on a chalk bank in England a hundred and forty years ago somebody was doing exactly this and would have done it exactly as badly, and there was no form in this county that said he had to be good at it, and there was no form that said he did not have to be.

At about the half hour after the eleventh the woman of thirty-four came down the bank past him, going to the bottom end, and stopped, and did not say anything about the barrow.

“*Is the string long enough?*” she said.

“*Long enough for what?*”

“*For the coping. Four feet is long enough for the coping and it is not long enough for a footing course, and I have watched two men who did not know that.*”

“*I have not laid a footing course.*”

“*No. But you have said that in the voice of a man who expects to be told something about it, and I want you to notice that you did that, and that about nine men on that bank did not notice and one did. The one is going up at the bottom end and not down here, and I would rather you heard it from her than from me.*”

Then she went down the bank, and about two hours later she had laid eleven feet of footing course at the bottom end, and it was true, and nobody made a thing of it, and the only man on the whole of that wall who noticed it was the man of thirty-one, who was holding a string he had not been asked to hold.

---

They got a course and a half up in the day and it came down again in the same afternoon at a section a different man had built, and it came down in about four minutes, and nothing was hurt, and about thirty people stood and looked at it.

The reason it came down is that there is a cold joint in it about four feet below the new work, where an old man laid a stone on a wet bed in the year of the long frost, and the stone had been in a lump ever since.

When the new work came down onto it, the stone moved about half an inch, and four feet of wall went out from under the course above and lay on the lane in one piece about nine feet long.

“*That is not anybody’s fault,*” said the man of forty-four, who was a long way from it. “*That stone has been waiting nine years for somebody to stand on.*”

“*Say the rest of it.*”

“*I have been pointing at that stone since I was seventeen years old.*”

“*Then why is it still in?*”

“*Because a pointing-out is not a man on a wall,*” said Harl Wenlock, “*and because the man who could put it in is the man whose grandfather bought the holding off the family that was leaving, and I would have had to write to the county. The county writes a return, and a return takes about a year, and my grandfather is dead.*”

Nobody answered that, and it is worth recording that nobody answered it, because it is the shape of the whole of this town in about thirty words spoken on a bank by a man who is not in the wrong and who has been right for nine years.

And then he did the other half of it, which he also had not planned.

“*It is out now. It is out and it is on the lane and it is no longer a stone in a wall, and I would like the man who struck that line in the book to hear that. There are four columns in that book and one of them is this wall, and what came down this afternoon is in that column, in the same hand, and somebody can look at it.*”

“*I am not going to write it in.*”

“*I know you are not. I have not asked you. I have said it where about thirty people are standing, and thirty people is twice the number that is in that room on a Saturday, and a hundred and ninety is in the square on a Thursday, and by Thursday this will have got to about two hundred, and there will be no way of putting it back. You may as well know that now as on Thursday.*”

---

At about the fourth hour the two of them were on the same side of the orchard, which is the thing that has to be recorded, and it happened by accident.

The orchard is inside the wall at the top end, and about nine hundred trees stand in it in rows, and the rows are about thirty feet apart. There are about eleven rows that are old and the rest are a mixture, and the lower corner is a frost pocket, and the trees in it were planted about ninety years later than the rest and are about half the size.

Harl Wenlock was doing the eleven old rows, because he has done the eleven old rows since he was seventeen, and because the fruit of them is his by the custom of the orchard and not by any of the four columns.

Abel Tarn was in the lower corner, on the same side, with a hook and a canvas bag, doing the half-size trees. That is a thing a man does for nothing, because the fruit in the pocket is worth about twice the fruit in the rest of it if it comes at all, and it had come that year, and there was about four weeks left of it on the branch.

They were about sixty feet apart inside eleven rows of apple trees. Neither of them said anything to the other, and four people who were in the orchard that afternoon saw it, and by the following Saturday a hundred and ninety people in a market square would have heard about a wall and about an orchard and about a name, and none of the three of them had said a word to either of the other two.

Ilyan Vester came down off the top lane at about the fifth hour with chalk dust in the creases of both knees and a blister on his right heel and a string.

The fever was thirty-two weeks and no days old. The wall at the top was nine feet four where it had been eight feet eight in the morning, and it was going to stand like that until the weather turned. It is sixteen days of work to bring it up another nine inches, and there are twenty days to do it in.

The week’s sheet went up on the end wall of the smithy at about the seventh hour by a boy of fourteen, and the dead man of about eighty-four went into the ground column, which is the first thing that has ever gone in it. It was read by about nine people on the way in and out.

The reading of the book in the chancel, which is done on a Saturday morning and had been done every Saturday for nineteen years, did not happen. The only person in this town who reads that book was up on a bank at the top of the common wall from the seventh hour.

Nobody had said that the reading might not happen, and nobody had to. The first Saturday in nineteen years on which the week of Bramblefold was not read out in the building it has been read out in for four hundred years was the day a man of thirty-four picked up a shovel.

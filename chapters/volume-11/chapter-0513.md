# Chapter 513: The Fourth Day Of The Ninety-Fifth Week, And A Friday On Which A Recordwright Of Thirty-Four And A Man Of Forty-Four Who Holds The Next One Along Are Put In A Room Nine Feet By Seven With A Window That Does Not Shut, And What Comes Out Of It Is Not An Agreement And Is A List Of What The Four Columns Carry And Do Not Carry, And A Man Of Eighty-One Adds A Fifth Thing To It From A Chair By The Fire

Fourth day of the ninety-fifth week. His two hundred and sixty-third morning. One hundred and thirteen days after the settlement. The fever thirty-two weeks and six days. Sixty-seven days since the division. Thirteen days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town two days from where he is standing.

He paid a penny on the Friday morning for a bed at the top of a joiner’s shop at the bottom of the town. The woman who took it did not ask him a question and did not ask him to leave a name, and there is no form in the county of Aldwick that says a traveller must leave a name at a lodging.

One pound and fourpence less that penny is one pound and threepence, which is two hundred and forty-three pence, and two hundred and forty-three pence at three pence a day is eighty-one days exactly, with nothing over.

He had slept on the stone bench in the porch of the meeting-house on the Thursday night. It is about six feet long and has a mark worn into it about two-thirds along. He got up off it at about the fifth hour with a leg that was worse than it had been and a cold that was not a fever.

Then Barnaby Rill said the thing that made the Friday, and he said it in the porch on the Thursday evening to about nine people and not in a room and not in a market.

He is eighty-one. He kept that book before his daughter and cannot read it now and says so, and he can read the first and a half of the second hand and none of the third, and the fourth is a man who rebound the book about ninety years back and was not his.

“*Two people in this town have been talking about the same half-acre for about forty years through about nine other people, and neither of them has ever been in a room with the other. Every word either of them has said about it has been said to somebody who will carry it to the other one and make it into something else on the way. Send for both of them. Put them in the vestry room. It is nine feet by seven and the window does not shut, and that is the whole of the advantage of it.*”

---

She sent for them herself, on the Friday morning, and she did not tell either of them who had said it.

That was the first thing a man of thirty-one found out when he came down the lane at about the tenth hour with a leg and a cold and the morning’s work done.

The door of that room was open, and on the Sunday of the week before it had been shut on purpose for the first time in nineteen years, and the difference between the two was given out loud in about four seconds by a woman of thirty-four standing in the doorway with her hand on the frame.

“*It is open because nobody in this room has been asked to be in it,*” she said. “*About nine people standing in a doorway are nine people who can leave. That is what the open door is for and it is the only thing it is for.*”

The room was as he remembered it: a table, three chairs, a cupboard, a window that does not shut, and a fire in the grate at the west end. It was the only warm fire in that part of the building. A man of eighty-one was in the chair by it with a rug over the front of him that is not a blanket and is a rug.

“*You are in here,*” she said to the man of thirty-one, “*because the book is not going to be read in this room. If it is read in this room I will put you out of it, and I will do it in front of about nine people, and there will be no argument about it, and you will not be asked anything on the way out.*”

“*I have not asked to read it.*”

“*I know. I am telling you the terms while they are cheap.*”

There were about nine people in that room, and there are about nine in a room in this town when something is being decided, and the number is not fixed and nobody keeps it, and it is generally between four and eleven.

The book was in the cupboard and stayed in the cupboard, and she said so out loud before anybody asked her. On the Sunday of the week before, a man of thirty-one had been put out of a room with a sheet of headings on his arm. The whole of what had been learned from that was that a book on a table turns a room into a different room, and a cupboard is a cupboard.

There were three chairs and there were about nine people, and the rest of them stood, and the two men who had come at the two ends of the room sat on two of the three, and there was a chair left over and nobody offered it to anybody.

Abel Tarn came at about the ninth hour and Harl Wenlock came at about half past ten, and neither of them came in with the other, and neither of them said why he had come, and about four people in that room noticed that neither of them had to be asked twice.

Then the man of thirty-one said the thing that was wrong, and he said it before either of them had said anything at all.

“*It is not going to work,*” Ilyan Vester said.

He had been in that town eleven days and he had done the thing he does in about nine rooms in a hundred and fifteen days, which is to say out loud what a room is about to find out, and he had got in first again.

The room did not stop, because about nine people in a country room have never once stopped for a stranger, and about four of them thought he was right.

“*A book and a bill of sale. Two men who have been right for about nine years and about forty years respectively, and both of them right, and nothing on this earth that either of them can use.*”

“*Sit down,*” said Marda Rill, “*or stand at the end. You may do either and you may do neither. What I am going to do is read out what the four columns of that book carry and what they do not carry. If a man of forty-four has a thing to say about any of it, he is going to say it while I am saying it and not afterwards, and afterwards I will not be able to hear him.*”

And she did it, standing at the table with the book not open, which she said was on purpose.

“*Descent. Who comes from whom, in a hand going down a page and then across. It carries a person being the child of a person. It does not carry a death. It does not carry a person being struck out. And it does not carry what became of anything, which is the third of those and the reason the other two are a problem.*”

The first of the four went into about nine people in about four seconds and nobody argued with it, and nobody has ever argued with the first column in this building, and that is not because it is right. It is because it is the only one of the four that is easy to agree with.

“*Holdings. A number and a quantity — four acres, a half-acre, a yard, a cottage and a yard. It carries a number. It does not carry the man, and it does not carry what he gave for it, and there is nowhere in that column to put a price, and that is not an oversight, because a price was not a thing anybody in this county wrote down until about a hundred and forty years ago.*”

Harl Wenlock put his hand on the table and took it off again.

“*Of the fold. The acres out of a hundred and forty that a household may put sheep on. It carries a number of acres. It does not carry the beasts, and it does not carry what the beasts are worth this week, and it does not say who let the acres to whom or at what rent or for how long, and if a page goes back in on Saturday then every one of those is a question and none of them is answered anywhere.*”

“*That is your arithmetic,*” Harl Wenlock said. “*Four shillings the acre. Twenty-eight pounds a year on a hundred and forty acres, and four shillings and eightpence a household on the sixty of them if it has to be let a second time. I have read that off a board at the back of the smithy and I have had it in my head for a fortnight and it is right.*”

“*It is right and it is the fourth day of the work and it is the last thing I am going to say today,*” she said, “*because we have until the tenth hour tomorrow and not after it.*”

“*Of the ground behind the meeting-house. A depth, in feet and inches, going back about two hundred and twenty years. It carries a depth, and it can carry a name, and it can carry a day. It does not carry the fact that the man is dead, and that is the whole of why the name is there. And there is one name in that column in nineteen years and it went in a fortnight ago, and it is a man of about eighty-four, and he is in it because there was nowhere else to put him.*”

“*You put him there,*” said Abel Tarn.

“*I put him there,*” she said, “*and a boy of fourteen worked out that he belonged there before I did, and neither of us has said so out loud in this room, and it is not on the list because it is not about the columns.*”

Nobody laughed, because it was not funny, and about four people in that room were still on the first two of the four.

Then the man of eighty-one in the chair said the fifth thing, and he did it without being asked, and he said it in about four sentences, and the room let him.

“*You have got four and they are the four there are.*” He had his hand out flat on the arm of the chair. “*A fifth thing, and it is not a column, and I am eighty-one and I am the only one left who kept that book and cannot read it and knows what it does, so I will say it and you may take it or leave it.*”

“*Say it.*”

“*A page cannot be read backwards. A person can.*”

About four people in that room did not follow that and about nine did not follow the other half either, and the two halves of the room were not the same four.

“*Say the whole of it,*” Marda Rill said.

“*You have been at this a fortnight asking whether a line can be put back in that book. It cannot. There is no hand that ever struck that stroke alive, and there is no body it can be asked, and every hand that comes after it, mine included, goes on top of it and cannot lift it. That is the whole of the first half.*”

He turned his head about four inches, and it cost him something to do it.

“*And a person can be read backwards. A person can be asked what they said, and they can say it again, and they can say it differently, and they can say no. That is the only thing in this building that is not a page. And it is the reason nine people are in this room and not one, and the reason you have shut a door once and opened it once this week, and the reason the man of sixty-two is standing there instead of being argued about.*”

“*He is wrong about that,*” said the man of eighty-one, to nobody.

“*I know,*” said Ilyan Vester. “*I said it in front of nine people and four of them believed me and I have been in this country eleven days and that is the third time that has happened to me and the other two were in other counties.*”

Nobody thanked anybody.

The two of them were not friends afterwards. The recordwright went back to the book and the man of forty-four went back to the holding numbered five, which is the next one along, and neither of them said anything to the other on the way out, and about four people in that porch had expected them to and about nine had not.

The fever was thirty-two weeks and six days old. In a room nine feet by seven with a window that does not shut there were four things a book does and four things it does not, and a fifth thing said from a chair by the fire. A man of thirty-one had said out loud that it would not work, and had been wrong in a room of about nine people, and had said so before anybody else did.

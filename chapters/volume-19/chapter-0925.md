# Chapter 925: Twelve Inches And About Seventy Under, And A Baker Came Down Four Miles With A Loaf That Was Worse And Would Not Go Down A Lane And Ask A Woman What She Said

Third day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week. His six hundred and seventy-fifth morning. Five hundred and twenty-five days after the settlement. The fever ninety-one weeks and five days old. Four hundred and seventy-nine days since the division. Four hundred and twenty-five days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Twelve inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, five days of the coming back, and about seventy of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and said that about seventy was the last figure before a bank stops being a thing you can plan a morning around, and that he had noticed the noticing, and did not intend to do anything about it.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and has begun to say out loud what he is not going to do with his own figure.

“Twelve at the seventh hour, five days of the coming back, and about seventy of those ninety steps under, on the third day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week, and it is a Thursday.”

“You are all waiting for me to notice something about that.”

“We are.”

“I am not going to notice it. I am going to say three figures and go and sweep.” He picked the broom up off the step. “And when a bank is about seventy I cannot tell anybody what the fourth day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week will be, and when it is about sixty I can, and the difference between those two states is one figure, and the difference between what a man can predict and what he cannot is nineteen years of looking at a step, and neither of those is the same as knowing anything.”

Then the baker came down the cart road on foot with a loaf under each arm and put one down on the low wall and cut it in three with a knife he had in his pocket, and would not let anybody say anything about it for about a minute.

Pol Dunnage keeps the bakehouse four miles up that cart road, in the village the road goes through, has had his oven out for four days, has been on a fire in the far corner since, and has come down the road this morning with a loaf out of it.

“That is off the temporary. Cut it. I am not going to stand here and watch you not cut it.”

“It is grey.”

“It is grey and it is heavier than it should be and the crust has not had a chance, and I have known that since the seventh and last day of the hundred and fifty-third week and so have you.” He put the other loaf down and kept his hand on it. “That is the whole of what the wall has cost me so far and I have had four days of it and I have not yet lost the bread, I have lost the shape of it. That is a different thing and I want somebody to say the difference out loud before I stop being able to.”

Nobody said the difference out loud, and he waited, and then he gave up waiting and said the rest of it himself, which was about a woman at the end of the lane.

“There is a woman at the end of the lane I have bought bread from for thirty years and on the second day of the hundred and fifty-third week she told my wife that the far corner is not even a bakehouse. In the lane. On the step of her own house.”

“Do you want to ask her what she meant.”

“I have thought about going down there about nine times since the first day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week and I have not gone and I am not going to.” He took his hand off the loaf. “Not because she was wrong. Because there is nothing to be gained by standing in a woman’s lane and being right at her about bread. She has had bad bread. She has said so. If I go down there she will say it again, and it will be the same sentence, and then one of us has to decide what we are doing with it.”

“And you will have the same thing again.”

“I will have a woman in her own doorway saying a thing twice and me standing in the lane not knowing what to do with the fact that she has never said it before, and I will not know whether she said it before because I have not asked, and I am not going to ask, and that is the last thing about that lane that I am going to say.”

Then the woman of forty-nine came up with the ninth run under her arm and put it on the low wall and read the morning out herself, and said she had entered a week in which the same morning was said out loud twice and had changed shape while she was writing it down.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years in a room four hundred miles off, holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and entered a morning in which the water was twelve inches on a step.

“That is the sixty-eighth morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing.”

“There has been a thing going on this landing for two days about a baker and a morning, and I have had it in the room with me the whole time in the sense that a clerk has a thing in a room, which is to say that it has not been in my hand at any point.”

“Is it going in.”

“No.” She put her thumb under the page and did not open it. “And I want the reason said out loud on a step, because in about a week this landing will have decided I was slow. A run takes what comes in, what went out, and who stood there while it did. An oven was shut down. Nothing came in and nothing went out, and who stood there is not a line anybody has ever written in a book of that kind.”

“That is an argument against it.”

“It is an argument that it would be a bad book and not an argument that it is true.” She closed it. “I have been asked three times this week whether I am going to, and I have said no three times, and I am not going to say it a fourth time on this step, because the fourth time is the one where somebody in this county starts saying I have been asked and refused, and that is a different thing and it is the thing I am not going to let happen to me.”

“Does it matter that they are both telling the truth about the same morning.”

“That is not my business and I have thought about it for two days and I have come back to it being not my business, and I can tell you why without being proud about it.” She shouldered the book. “If I enter it, then in ten years there is a page in a book four hundred miles off that says a baker shut an oven down, and somebody can stand in front of that page and say *well, that settles it*. That is all it would be good for. That is the whole of what it would be good for, and there is no end on that page.”

Wat Marshe came up out of the lane and gave his figure and said that a man with a book had said on the first day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week that the morning in his head had become a Tuesday thing, and that he had been thinking about it since and had worked out that a thing in a head changes shape whether you are guarding it or not.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and seventy-two days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and seventy-two is nine hundred and twenty-five less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and seventy-two days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up. And the man with the book did not know that the morning in his head had turned into a Tuesday thing until it was already the first day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week, and he was on the step saying it in the open.”

“That is not the same as a thing getting checked.”

“No. It is a thing getting copied, sort of, except it is not copied, because nobody copied it. It just moved on its own.” He looked along the step. “And that is the bit I have been trying to work out since the first day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week. Everybody keeps saying it goes in a book and that is how you stop it moving, and the man who says it goes in a book is the man who cannot hold it himself, and the man at the bench cannot hold it himself either, and the baker will not put it anywhere.”

“So it is on this landing in about six mouths.”

“So it is on this landing in about six mouths and it is going to come out different in all of them and there is nobody going to know which one is the one that happened.”

Nobody on that landing told the boy he had found anything and nobody told him he had not, and Barnaby Crowe came up off the low side and said his figure and said that he had been wrong about one thing on the first day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week and had worked out what the right thing was, and the right thing was nothing.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for two hundred and four mornings, which is nine hundred and twenty-five less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and fourth. And I am going to say a thing and then carry gravel. On the first day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week I said I was not asking the man at the bench because I had no reason. That was not true. I had a reason and the reason was that he had something and I had a want and I did not want to be the man who took a thing off a person who had carried it up a road on his own.”

“What is the right thing.”

“Nothing. There is no right thing, and there is a wanting thing which is what I did, and I am not going to pretend I did not want it.” He shouldered the barrow. “Two hundred and four.”

Then Ilyan Vester came up and said his figure, and said that a baker who would not ask a woman in a lane what she had said had lost the cheapest thing on that landing, and was told by the baker that the cheapest thing was not a thing.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and seventy-one days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and forty days old.

“Three hundred and seventy-one days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and forty days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and twenty-five days old, which is nine hundred and twenty-five less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Sixty-fifth wrong thing in sixty-fifth morning, and here is the sixty-fifth. There is a woman at the end of a lane who has said one thing about that man’s bread and he will not go and ask her what she said. That is the cheapest thing on this landing. One question, once, one person, and a woman who would be glad to be asked, because she has been holding it since the second day of the hundred and fifty-third week and she has not put it anywhere either.”

“It is the cheapest thing because it is worthless,” said Pol Dunnage.

“You do not know that it is worthless.”

“I know what it would be. It would be her sentence and my sentence and two sentences on a step, and there would be no third thing on that step that came out of the asking, and if there were a third thing then it would be worth doing and there is not one. There is a loaf. She has eaten it.”

“That is not an argument.”

“It is not an argument and I am not giving you one. I am telling you what I am not doing and I am not doing it because I have been at this trade for forty-one years and the only thing I have ever got from asking a person what they said is a person who has had to stand there and be asked.”

He picked up the second loaf and looked at it and did not eat it, and put it back down on the wall for whoever came up next, and went back up the cart road on foot at the pace he had come down at, and twelve inches lay on the top step with five days of it to run and about seventy of those ninety steps under.

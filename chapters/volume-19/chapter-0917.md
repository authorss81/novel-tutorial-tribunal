# Chapter 917: A Man Who Wired A Catch Once And Is Not Going To Wire It Again

Second day of the hundred and fifty-third week. His six hundred and sixty-seventh morning. Five hundred and seventeen days after the settlement. The fever ninety weeks and four days old. Four hundred and seventy-one days since the division. Four hundred and seventeen days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Twelve inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, five days of the coming back, and about seventy of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave the three figures and said nothing about the woman who had spoken on the step the day before, because it was not his to say anything about and because he has watched this landing go quiet over things it should have gone loud over, and has said so out loud once, and is not going to make a habit of it.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and has decided that what is said on his landing is not his to comment on.

“Twelve at the seventh hour, five days of the coming back, and about seventy of those ninety steps under, on the second day of the hundred and fifty-third week, and it is a Wednesday.”

“That is the whole of it. There was a thing said on this step yesterday that half of you have been talking about since the seventh hour and I am not going to be the one who agrees with it or disagrees with it. I hold a landing. What gets said on it is between whoever said it and whoever was here.”

Wat Marshe came up out of the lane and gave his figure and then gave away the only thing he has ever wanted anybody to ask him for, and he did it without being asked and he gave it away to the wrong man on purpose, which took him most of the morning to decide.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and sixty-four days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and sixty-four is nine hundred and seventeen less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and sixty-four days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up. And I am going to say one thing about the count I am keeping and then I am never going to say it again, and I am saying it to you and not to her, because she asks and you do not, and it has got nothing to do with the bank.”

Barnaby Crove stopped on the step and did not ask. He stood with his hand where the shovel had been and he said *go on* and nothing else.

“It is not about the bank. It has never been about the bank. It is about a needle and a thread and a thing I sat and did in the dark.”

“And I have worked out this fortnight that if anybody in this county asked me for it they would take it away and put it in a room, and it would be in there forever with my name nowhere on it, and I would be a person who had it taken, and I would not be a person who had done a thing. So nobody is to ask me and I am not to wish it otherwise.”

“I am not going to ask you.”

“I know you are not and that is why I told you, and I have had all morning going over whether that was the reason and I have decided it was, and if it was not then I have told a man of fifty-four the only thing I have, and he will do what he likes with it, which is nothing.”

He went up past the ash and did not come back down, and the man of fifty-four did not follow him and did not ask anybody else about it either.

Then Ezekiel Moss came off the low side with the bar under his arm and said that he was not going to do the thing he had come to do, and gave the working for it, and it took longer than the thing would have taken.

Ezekiel Moss keeps the lock at the head of a cut on the low side of that drain, about a mile and a bit above the ash, and took the post before he took anything else, and gave an account of one morning on the first day of this fortnight and is not going to be asked for it again.

“The catch is loose for the third time since the frost  and I have not wired it, and I have come down here to say so out loud before anybody stands at the top of that cut and looks at it and decides I have been idle.”

“Why have you not wired it.”

“Because of what you did on the first morning of this fortnight.” He shifted the bar against his leg.

“A woman stood at my gate and asked me what I did, and I told her, and she went away, and I have not thought about it once since except when I got my hand on that catch this morning and could not make my fingers do it. That is what you have done to me. Not the telling.”

“The telling cost me an hour and I would tell it again. What you have done is that I have got a morning in my mouth now, and a man with a loose catch is a man who is going to get asked about that morning, and I am not going to be a man who has to stand there deciding whether he can be trusted twice.”

“So the wall goes if it is not wired.”

“The wall goes, and I will take the wall. I have had that catch loose three times and wired it twice and the water has never got through it, and the reason it never got through it is that it never rose while I was up there, and that is not a thing I can do again.”

“If it goes, it goes, and it will be written down afterwards that the catch was loose and not that I thought a woman asking me a question was worth more than a wall. Let them write that. I know which of the two of those is the true one and it will not be the one on the paper.”

“A man who does not do a thing gets no account of it at all.”

“No. He gets what I have just said, standing on a step, once, to whoever is here. And that is more than the wall and it is less than a line in a book and I have made my choice and I have made it in front of people so that I cannot pretend later that nobody knew.”

Two people asked him whether that was a wise thing to do and he told both of them that it was not his business whether it was wise and that he had not come down for advice, and one of them asked him a second time and he gave the same four words, and it was not a second check on the first and nobody on that landing has ever pretended that it was.

Sena Dorr entered the morning at about the tenth hour and read it out, and then said what the hardest sentence of the week had done to her, and she had not slept on it.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, has read for a living for twenty-three years in a room four hundred miles off, and did not sleep on what she had said on the step the day before.

“That is the sixtieth morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing.”

“I said a thing on this step yesterday about nobody ever having asked me whether my lines were right, and it has not made anybody ask me. That is what I have this morning. I said it in front of a landing and it did not do a thing.”

“Nobody came up the road last night and nobody came up this morning and the woman who keeps the pound did not stop me at the gate, and I am not saying that to make anybody feel bad about it, I am saying it because I sat up half the night with it and it is the first thing I have said out loud in twenty-three years that changed nothing at all, and I would have been better off not having said it.”

“Then say less next time.”

“I am not going to say less. I am going to say it again, in the same words, on the same step, and I would like everybody here to notice that I am now a woman who has done the thing she complained about. I have said a true thing about myself out loud and every person standing on this step has had it and it is not a record and it does not check anything and it has done nothing. I am not going to take it back and I am not going to be asked to.”

She put the book under her arm and said the arrangement had not answered and that she had put no day on it, and that this morning, whatever else had happened on the step, was a morning, and she had entered it.

Then a man of thirty-one said that a keeper who lets a wall go for the sake of a sentence has given the sentence more weight than the wall can carry, and was refused by the man who had wired that catch twice and was not going to wire it a third time.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and sixty-three days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and thirty-two days old.

“Three hundred and sixty-three days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and thirty-two days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and seventeen days old, which is nine hundred and seventeen less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Fifty-seven wrong things in fifty-seven mornings, and here is the fifty-seventh. He has decided to let a wall go because a woman asked him a question once on a gate. And that is not a keeper deciding anything. That is a sentence out of a landing getting into a gate and weighing more than stone.”

“No,” said Ezekiel Moss, without turning round.

“It is a man deciding not to wire a catch, and the woman is not in it, and if you want to know what I decided it was this: I have wired it twice and the water has not come through either time, and I am not going to be a man who comes up that cut a third time and has to be asked what he did on the two previous times.”

“You have come to me twice this fortnight with a thing I said, and I have given you both, and here is the third thing I am not going to give you, which is why I am leaving the catch loose. You did not do it. A gate did, when it was loose, and you happened to be standing near it, and you have put yourself in the middle of a wall because it suited you to be in the middle of something.”

“That is not what happened.”

“No. And I would rather you did not say it was, because as long as it is a woman’s sentence on a step then it is a sentence, and the moment it is a wall it is a wall, and I have got to stand up that cut every morning whatever it is.”

He was not told he was wrong and nobody said he was right, and the man from the low side went back up with the bar under his arm, and the catch stayed loose.

Then Barnaby Crove said that he had gone to the back of the passage on Wednesday evening and had not asked, and gave the reason, and it was not the reason anybody would have given.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for a hundred and ninety-six mornings, which is nine hundred and seventeen less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“A hundred and ninety-six. And yesterday on this step I said I was going to ask a man at the back of that passage on Thursday. And on Wednesday evening I went and stood there anyway, to see whether I would do it on a Wednesday, and I stood there and I did not ask him.”

“Why not.”

“Because I worked out while I was standing there that I was not after whether Orla Fennimore had an account of her own morning right. I was after being a man who asks the second question.” He said it and then looked as though he had said more than he meant to.

“That is the whole of it. A man of fifty-four has spent nineteen mornings on this step wanting a day against a line and everything he has wanted has been about himself, and the second question was going to be about me, and there was no part of it that was about the back of that passage.”

“A man of thirty-one has already told you what that is.”

“He has. He has told me it is a position, and it is not, and I would thank him not to put a name on it, because if it is a position then somebody is going to notice I have not filled it.”

Nobody told him he was wrong and nobody told him it was not a position and nobody was thanked for anything, and at the eleventh hour the man who holds the landing gave his three figures again to nobody in particular and put the shovel down.

The water was twelve inches over the top step with five days to come back and a man had decided to let a wall go rather than be asked about a morning twice, and a boy of thirteen had given away the only thing he had to a man who had not asked, and a man of fifty-four had stood at the back of a passage and found out that he had not gone to ask a question at all.

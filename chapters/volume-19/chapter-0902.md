# Chapter 902: A Full Bank And The Morning He Named For Four Days Off

First day of the hundred and fifty-first week. His six hundred and fifty-second morning. Five hundred and two days after the settlement. The fever eighty-eight weeks and three days old. Four hundred and fifty-six days since the division. Four hundred and two days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Sixteen inches stood on the top step at about the seventh hour and that was a full bank, with four days of the coming back and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave the three figures and then named a morning that was not this one, with the week on it, and gave that morning its own figures and not this morning’s.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, has held that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, and has caught himself out loud on this step before and said so in front of people.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and that is a full bank, four days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the first day of the hundred and fifty-first week, and it is a Tuesday.”

“It comes off on the fifth day of the hundred and fifty-first week, which is a Saturday. It comes off at about the sixth hour and all ninety of those steps will be walkable by about the seventh, and there will be no figure of inches on that morning and no figure of steps under, and that is the thirtieth time.”

“That is four days.”

“It is four days. I have said a day with the week on it before and come out wrong, and I caught it out loud and I said the words *I said it a day early*, and I am not going to do it twice. If you have it written down anywhere, you have written down a morning and not a figure, and there is a difference and I have learned it the hard way.”

Then the woman of forty-four was doing an ordinary thing on the step with a man who moves chairs for her, and nobody on that landing knew it was the ordinary thing, because she had not told anybody it was.

Orla Fennimore is forty-four, has kept twenty-nine chairs for nineteen years at thirteen shillings and fourpence a year, has put her question down five times, and has one line in another person’s hand with nothing settled on either side of it.

“Sit down, Aldis. I am going to ask you about the passage, and I am asking you once, and if I ask you again you are to tell me it is the second time and I will stop.”

“That is all right.”

“It is not all right and it is not not all right, it is just a way of doing it that I have arrived at by myself and I am not going to explain where it came from.”

“What do you want to know.”

“Whether you carried the eleventh chair out on the second day of the hundred and fiftieth week and put it back on the third. Not what you thought about it. Whether you carried it.”

“I did, and that is what you asked me.”

“That is all I wanted and I am going to leave it there.”

“You are not going to put it down.”

“No. And if you had said the thing about the chair that I know you were going to say, I would have had to tell you no, and then it would have been a thing between us instead of a thing I did with my own hands and saw with my own eyes. You have not said it. That is why I asked you the way I did.”

Pol Dunnage came up the cart road at about the eighth hour with the bakehouse shut behind him and stood at the top of the steps and said a thing that men who keep things have been saying in one shape or another for nineteen years and that nobody on that bank had ever heard said out loud.

Pol Dunnage keeps the bakehouse four miles up that cart road, in the village the road goes through, and he has not been asked by anybody on that landing for anything at all.

“The oven at our end is slower than the oven at the other end and it is the same fire and it is the same flour, and I have asked the man who sets it twice and I have not asked him a third time, and he has told me it is the damp, and the damp is in the wall and not in the flour.”

“Why is that a thing you have to be careful about.”

“It is not a thing I have to be careful about, it is a thing I do. You ask a man once about a thing he did and then you know what he did. You ask him twice about a thing he did and he has to stand there and decide whether you have caught him in something, and he is not standing there deciding whether you have caught him. He is standing there deciding whether he can be trusted, and that is a different question and it is not yours.”

“You have thought about that.”

“I have not thought about it once. It is how you keep a fire.”

Then a man of thirty-one said that a person who kept an ordinary thing running by himself was the same as a keeper of one of the nine, and was refused by the same man with flour still on his hands, and the refusal was given in a flat voice and not at all unkind.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and forty-eight days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and seventeen days old.

“Three hundred and forty-eight days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and seventeen days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and two days old, which is nine hundred and two less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Forty-two wrong things in forty-two mornings, and here is the forty-second. That man keeps a thing nobody in this county can check, by himself, with nobody beside him who is allowed to say no, and he says he asks his man once. That is what a keeper of one of the nine is doing.”

“No, and it is not, and I will tell you the difference because you will go on saying it otherwise. He has nobody who can say no to him. Not nobody who will not. Nobody who can. A keeper of one of the nine has somebody on the bank who may put a hand on the same stone on the same morning, and the two of them are standing there on purpose. That is the whole of the difference, and it is not a small one, and it is not a thing you get by asking your man fewer times.”

“I am asking him fewer times.”

“You are asking him fewer times because you have worked out it is the only decent way to ask. Those are not the same and I would rather you had the second one.”

Barnaby Crove asked for a day against the ninth line for the hundred and eighty-first morning, and said he had thought about saying something else and had not.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for a hundred and eighty-one mornings, which is nine hundred and two less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“A hundred and eighty-one. And I worked out a sentence on the way up here, a long one, and I have left it at home on purpose. It was going to be about what the line is for. It is not going to be about what the line is for.”

“Why not.”

“Because then it would look like the day might turn up if I said it well enough.”

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and forty-nine days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and forty-nine is nine hundred and two less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and forty-nine days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.”

“Nobody asked me for the count I am keeping. That is forty-one mornings of them, and a man just said on that step that asking twice is a different question from asking once, and he is talking about his own hands and not about my count, and both of those are the same sentence and I am not going to be the one who joins them up.”

Sena Dorr entered today’s line on the ninth run at about the tenth hour and read it out herself on the first day of the hundred and fifty-first week, and said what the week had been and what it had not.

“That is the forty-fifth morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing.”

“Nothing was settled this week and I want that said by somebody who was here for all of it. A man kept a lock at the head of a cut and said out loud what he did there, and it went nowhere, and that is the correct size for it and I am not going to be the one who says it should have gone further.”

Sixteen inches stood over that landing until the light went and a woman who keeps twenty-nine chairs had asked a man about a chair once and had not asked him again, and nobody on that bank knew that either of those two things was the same thing.

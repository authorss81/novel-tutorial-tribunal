# Chapter 904: A Byre And A Bakehouse, And One Of Them Settles One Thing

Third day of the hundred and fifty-first week. His six hundred and fifty-fourth morning. Five hundred and four days after the settlement. The fever eighty-eight weeks and five days old. Four hundred and fifty-eight days since the division. Four hundred and four days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Sixteen inches held on the top step at about the seventh hour and it was holding at that figure, with two days of the coming back and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave the three figures and named the morning the bank would come off, with the week on it, and gave that morning its own figures and not these.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing, and gives four figures off one stone, and when he has two days left of a bank he says so out loud rather than let somebody do the subtraction for him.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, two days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the third day of the hundred and fifty-first week, and it is a Thursday.”

“It comes off on the fifth day of the hundred and fifty-first week, which is a Saturday, at about the sixth hour, and all ninety of those steps will be walkable by about the seventh. There will be no figure of inches on that morning and no figure of steps under. That is the thirtieth time and I have said it twice and I am not going to say it a fourth.”

Ned Farrow came in wet to the knee and stood at the low wall with his hands open so that the water could come off them, and he talked the way a man talks when he is not going to be asked twice and has decided that in advance.

Ned Farrow keeps a byre a mile past the turn, off the lane that forks for the upper farms, and there is no stone at it and no seat and nobody after him.

“It is a mile past the turn. That is the whole of it. Come off the road at the fork and go up until the ground stops going up, and that is where it is, and if you have got a cart it is a bad mile.”

“What do you keep.”

“Cows. Eleven of them at one time of year and more at another, and I keep a line a day about the ones that are in, and about the ones that are out, and about a calf that came and went and came.”

“Who brings the cows.”

“Two men. They come up at dusk and the animals go out at dusk and they come in again before the light. I ask them about a calf once. Once. I say, was such a calf in on such a morning, and they say yes or no, and it goes in the line and I do not ask them about that calf again.”

“Why not, then.”

“Because the second time they come in, they will have had a night to think about it, and a man who has had a night to think about it is not telling you what he remembers. He is telling you what he has decided. There is a difference and it is the whole of what a line of days is worth, and I would rather have the first one than the second one.”

“Nothing about that is written anywhere.”

“No. And I have no stone and no seat and no one after me, and if I stop on a Tuesday there is nobody who can say whether I stopped. I have made my peace with that by being here at dusk, which is the only place on this bank where anybody ever is.”

Then the keeper of the bakehouse said what a bakehouse is and how far it is, and it was four miles and it was not a new thing, and he said the second part of that out loud before anybody could ask him.

Pol Dunnage keeps the bakehouse four miles up that cart road, in the village the road goes through, where the road bends left at a wall and goes back to the right.

“It is four miles up that road. You go past the turn and up the fork and then you are in the village, and it is the second house on the left after the wall, and there is no sign on it and there has never been a sign on it.”

“You have told us a distance and nobody asked you for one.”

“You have never asked me for one and you have all come up this road. That is not a complaint, it is an instruction. If a thing has a place, then the place has to be sayable by the person who keeps it, and I have got tired of that road being described by people who have not walked it this week.”

Aldis Rook came up the road at about the ninth hour and asked Pol Dunnage one question and got an answer that settled it, and then stood there holding the answer as though it might turn into something larger.

“Did you set the far oven on the second day of the hundred and fifty-first week.”

“I did. I set it at about the fourth hour and the oven was cold at the back when I put the trays in and I moved them forward.”

“That is it, and that is all I came for.”

“That is all you came for and it has taken you four miles and it was worth the four miles, and I am going to tell you the other half so that you do not come back for it. If you had come up and asked me what I thought about that oven, or whether I was sure, I would have given you an answer that sounds like an answer and it would not have been worth the four miles. You asked me what I did and now you know what I did.”

“I am not going to ask you again.”

“You are welcome to ask me again. I am only saying it would not help you, and that is a different thing from saying you may not.”

Then a man of thirty-one said that the bakehouse had the easiest ordinary case on that bank because bread is sold and a buyer can say whether it was there or not, and was refused by the man who keeps it.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and fifty days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and nineteen days old.

“Three hundred and fifty days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and nineteen days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and four days old, which is nine hundred and four less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Forty-four wrong things in forty-four mornings, and here is the forty-fourth. He sells bread. A buyer can put his hand on a loaf and say whether it was in that oven. That is a man in the world who can check you.”

“A buyer can say whether there was bread.”

“That is all he can say. He cannot say what I did at the fourth hour. He cannot say whether the oven was cold at the back. He cannot say which trays I moved forward. If I stopped doing all of that tomorrow and only did the last hour of it, every buyer in that village would go on saying the same thing about the same loaf, and every one of them would be telling the truth, and you would have counted it as checked.”

“You have thought about it.”

“I have thought about bread. I have not thought about the difference between a loaf and a morning and I am not going to start now.”

Barnaby Crove asked for a day against the ninth line for the hundred and eighty-third morning and said he had not asked anybody what they did this morning, which was the first morning in a long while.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for a hundred and eighty-three mornings, which is nine hundred and four less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“A hundred and eighty-three. And I did not ask anybody what they did this morning. I want that said. I stood here with my mouth open twice on the second day of the hundred and fifty-first week and this morning I shut it, and that is not me giving up, and I would like somebody to notice it who is going to notice it tomorrow as well.”

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and fifty-one days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and fifty-one is nine hundred and four less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and fifty-one days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.”

“Nobody asked me for the count I am keeping. That is forty-three mornings of them, and a man of fifty-four stood here yesterday wanting to ask everybody what they did, and today he did not, and nobody knows which of those two was the right one and neither do I.”

Sena Dorr entered today’s line on the ninth run at about the tenth hour and read it out herself on the third day of the hundred and fifty-first week, and said what she was not going to do with it.

“That is the forty-seventh morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing.”

“I am not going to take any of this four hundred miles. Not the oven and not the byre and not the line about the calf that came and went and came. They are not about the nine and they are not about the arrangement and I am not the clerk of a lock and a gate and I have said no to being that twice already and this morning I am saying it about a gate instead of about a bridge.”

A man had walked four miles up a cart road and got one true answer and had gone back down it, and a man with cows had told everybody how far his byre was and had not told them a single thing about the morning his last calf came in.

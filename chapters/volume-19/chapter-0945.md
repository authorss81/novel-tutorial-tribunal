# Chapter 945: Sixteen Inches And It Holds, And A Wall Came Out Of A Bakehouse Four Miles Up That Road And What Was Behind It Is Dust

Second day of the hundred and fifty-seventh week. His six hundred and ninety-fifth morning. Five hundred and forty-five days after the settlement. The fever ninety-four weeks and four days old. Four hundred and ninety-nine days since the division. Four hundred and forty-five days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, and it held at that figure, one day of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and then said the thing that goes with one day, which is that the water comes off tonight, and that after that all ninety are walkable, and that he is not going to say anything else about it.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and gave a day of the coming back today that puts every one of those ninety steps out of the water before the light tomorrow.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, one day of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the second day of the hundred and fifty-seventh week, and it is a Wednesday.”

“I am going to tell you the one thing I have told nobody and I am telling it now because it is a figure and not a feeling: it comes off tonight, and by about the seventh hour tomorrow every one of those ninety steps is out. So tomorrow you can all walk up that bank and there will be nothing on the stone, and I have said that before on this bank and I am not going to stand up here tomorrow with my mouth shut and let anybody say I withheld it.”

“You told us on Monday you would not give the figures of a new week in advance.”

“I gave you the shape of the week. Sixteen, then a morning with nothing on it. That is a shape and not the figures, and I will argue about that with anybody who wants to argue about it, and I will lose, because the man who wants to argue about it has been waiting three weeks for me to slip.”

Then the man with the byre came down off the turn with the smell of the byre on him and did not come up past the second step, and said that he had read it back, and that he had read it back on Monday night in his own house by the fire, and that he was going to say what it said.

Ned Farrow keeps a byre a mile past the turn, off the lane that forks for the upper farms, has a line a day in it, put a person in that line on the seventh and last day of the hundred and fifty-fifth week, and has not told the man whose name is in it.

“I read it back.”

“You said on Sunday you would not read it back for a year.”

“I said on Sunday that I had not read it back and was not going to and was not going to ask him about it, and all three of those were true on Sunday.” He turned his cap over in his hands. “And on Monday night I got the book off the shelf and I read the line and it said what I thought it said and it said what I was frightened of, which is that it is not about him at all. It is about me. It is in my handwriting and it is in my book and it is a Monday. That is all it is and that is what a line is.”

“You have made yourself the only keeper on this landing who keeps something nobody can check.”

“I did that on Sunday and I did it on purpose and I am not going to take it back.” He put the cap on. “And having read it I have worked out the rest of it, and the rest of it is that I am going to tell him. Not today. I am not going to tell you today and if you ask me every morning of this week I will say the same thing, which is that I have not told him and I am going to, and if you ask me on the tenth morning after that I will tell you I still have not.”

“You could tell him now, on your way down.”

“I could, and I have not, and that is not a secret and it is not a wall. A wall is a thing you cannot get at. He is a mile past the turn and he comes down that lane on a Tuesday and I have not said anything to him because I have not worked out how. I am not going at him this morning while a full bank is coming off.” He looked up at the sixteen inches. “And when I do tell him I am going to say that I wrote him in and did not tell him, in that order, and I am not going to let him have the easier version.”

Nobody on the step told him the order was right and nobody told him it was wrong.

Then a man came up the cart road with grey dust to the knee and the mark of a chisel on his coat sleeve, and he had not gone home, and he had a face on him that the landing had not seen before and it was not pride and it was not shame.

Pol Dunnage keeps a bakehouse four miles up that cart road, in the village the road goes through, took out the first course on the first day of the hundred and fifty-sixth week and found old lime behind it, and has taken the wall out.

“It came out on Sunday night. All of it, down to the footings, and I could not have got it down quicker if I had tried and I could not have got it down slower if I had wanted to.”

“You said on Tuesday you could not tell whether the lime was good.”

“I could not on Tuesday and I cannot now and it is not going to matter tomorrow, so that is the end of it.” He knocked the dust off his knees and a good deal of it did not come off. “There is nothing behind that wall now. There is a hole in a bakehouse with the yard coming through it and the oven still going and the weather coming in on the north side. That is what a wall was for. It is what a wall is.”

“You took it out.”

“I took it out and I threw the lime in the yard and I am going to let the rain have it and I have not written one word down and I am not going to.” He looked at the people on the step without moving his head. “On Tuesday I stood here and told you that a thing which held is not a record.”

He kept his hand on the cold chisel and did not take it off.

“I meant it when I said it and I still mean it and it is the reason I could not sleep between Saturday and Sunday, because I had spent a fortnight telling a step that holding is not knowing and then I found out that the old lime in my own wall has been holding for longer than anybody in this village has been alive and it has never once told me what it was like.”

“Does that make it a record.”

“No. It makes it a wall that stood up.” He shifted the chisel on his belt. “And the difference is not the answer. The difference is that a record can be read by somebody who was not there and this cannot, and I have not got anybody to read it and I am not going to pretend the rain is a second opinion.”

Then the man of fifty-four came up in the middle of all that with his barrow and gave his figure and said that he had been wrong on Saturday about a thing he had worked out in his yard, and that he was going to say so on the step.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and twenty-four mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is nine hundred and forty-five less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one, and has not spent one of them since the third day of the hundred and fifty-sixth week.

“Two hundred and twenty-fourth, and that is the sixth morning I have not asked, and I am going to be back here in four days asking and everybody can make their own arrangements.”

“You told us on Friday you had worked out that not asking is not the same as not wanting.”

“I had. And I have been wrong about it since about eleven o’clock last night.” He set the barrow down. “I worked out that not asking is a different thing from not wanting, and I said so, and that was true and it was also the easiest thing I could have said. The harder one is that I have been enjoying it. Not asking is a morning where nobody can say no to me.”

“And a man who is asking every morning has a thing that can be refused, and a man who is not asking does not, and I have been walking about this landing for six mornings being the only person nobody can refuse, and it is the best I have felt in three weeks, and I am saying that out loud because if I do not say it I will have worked out the easy version again by Friday.”

Nobody on that step told him that was a worse thing and nobody told him it was better, and the man who moves chairs was not there, and the boy came up out of the lane after the baker and gave his figure and said that he had been up the cart road in the dark to watch a wall come down and had been told to go home by a man with a chisel.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and ninety-two days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and ninety-two is nine hundred and forty-five less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and ninety-two days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.”

“You went and looked at a wall come down.”

“I went and watched the back of it and I could not see anything and I stood in the road until he came out and told me to go home, and I went home.” He put his hands in his pockets. “And it is the first time in this county that anybody has sent me away from something, and he did it by saying go home and not by saying why, and I am going to be annoyed about it for about a day. And then I am going to notice that he did not ask me one question before he said it, and neither did anybody else on this step. It took a wall.”

And then the man of thirty-one came up and said out loud that the lime was a record, and the man with the chisel answered him in one sentence and gave the working, which is what he does, and it was not unkind.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and ninety-one days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and sixty days old.

“Three hundred and ninety-one days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and sixty days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and forty-five days old, which is nine hundred and forty-five less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Eighty-five wrong things in eighty-five mornings, and here is the eighty-fifth, and I am going to say a small thing because there is nothing else on that step.” He nodded at the baker. “That lime in your wall is a record.”

“No it is not.”

“You said it held.”

“It held. A record can be read by a man who was not there and that cannot, and if you cannot read it then you have not got a record, you have got a thing that lasted, and there is a difference and it is the difference between a wall and a wall with cattle on the front of it, which is what the man behind me said to me on the step a fortnight ago and he was right and he is always right and that is not a comfort.”

Pol Dunnage did not unbutton his coat. “And I will say the other half, because you have been standing here three weeks waiting for somebody to tell you a thing was worth something and I am not going to be that and I am not going to be nothing. It lasted longer than I did. That is not nothing. It is just not the thing you said it was.”

Nobody agreed with either of them and nobody said it had been a good week, and sixteen inches sat on the top step with one day of it to run and about sixty of those ninety under, and by morning the water would be off that bank and the man of forty-three would stand on a step with nothing to say, which he has done before, more times than anyone on that landing has ever asked him about.

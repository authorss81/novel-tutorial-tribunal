# Chapter 901: What He Did In The Cut, Said Once, And On Nothing

Seventh and last day of the hundred and fiftieth week. His six hundred and fifty-first morning. Five hundred and one days after the settlement. The fever eighty-eight weeks and two days old. Four hundred and fifty-five days since the division. Four hundred and one days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

The asking was the first thing on that step and it was an ordinary thing, and the woman who did it was not going to get a thing out of it, and she said so before anybody had thought to wonder.

Norah Stannard is forty-six. She keeps nothing, holds nothing and is on no roll. What she has is what has been said to her, and she is holding it because there was nowhere else to set it down.

She caught Ezekiel Moss at the low wall with his bar still against his leg, before he had it off the wall.

“You keep the lock at the head of that cut.”

“I do.”

“I want one morning out of you and I am going to ask you for it once, and that is the whole of what I am going to ask you for.”

“Which morning.”

“The third day of the hundred and forty-sixth week.”

He did not answer straight away. He looked at the water coming over the step and then he set the bar down on the stone, which is a thing a man does when he has decided to give the whole of a thing rather than a piece of it.

“That is a long way back,” he said. “Then you will get it once, and you will not get it again, and if you come up this road and ask me the same thing I am going to tell you it is the second time. I am telling you now so that it is not a thing you have done to me afterwards.”

“Do it.”

“I was at the head of that cut from before the light until the middle of the morning. Here is the whole of what I did. I carried a bar down and opened the gate at the top and let the head down until it stopped of its own accord. I carried the bar back up. I shut the gate and I wired the catch with three turns of wire, because the catch was loose and it had been loose before I took the post.”

“That is what you did.”

“That is what I did.”

“Is that all of it?”

“That is all of it. You did not ask me what the water was doing. If you want to know what that water was doing you want to ask the water, and it will not tell you, and I do not think it knows either.”

Twelve inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, five days of the coming back, and about seventy of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave the three figures and gave nothing else, because there was nothing else to give.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, has held that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, and has never once given a fifth.

“Twelve at the seventh hour, five days of the coming back, and about seventy of those ninety steps under, on the seventh and last day of the hundred and fiftieth week, and it is a Monday.”

Then a man of thirty-one asked to write the account down so that it would not be lost, and was refused in the mouth of the man who had given it, with the working given twice and neither half of it in a kind voice.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and forty-seven days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and sixteen days old.

“Three hundred and forty-seven days in this county, and the cut across this palm is two hundred and sixteen days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and one days old, which is nine hundred and one less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Forty-one wrong things in forty-one mornings, and here is the forty-first. Let me put that down before it goes. It is the only account of that morning in this county and it is standing in the air and it dies with the man who said it.”

“No.”

“I am not asking you for anything else. I am not asking to keep it. I would take it down and put it in my own book and say whose it was, and you would have a copy, and a copy is better than nothing.”

“No, and I will give you the working, because you have been standing on that step for eleven weeks wanting a thing to do, and I would rather you had the shape of it than the wanting. The minute that is on paper it is not what I did. It is what a person wrote that I did. It goes in a drawer, and it comes out one day with my name at the top of it, and it will be true, and it will not be mine, and I will not be able to say no to it, because I will not be able to remember whether I said it that way.”

“One half of it.”

“That is one half of it. Here is the other half and I am saying it once. If two men were in that cut on that morning then there are two of what I have just said, and one of them is mine and one of them is his, and mine does not put his inside it and his does not put mine inside it. If you take mine and set his underneath it you have not made a better record. You have made one man’s account with another man’s name on it, and the second man cannot get at it to say that part was not his.”

Ezekiel Moss put his hand flat on the wall for a moment, the way a man does when he has said the true thing and does not want to soften it afterwards.

“You have not asked me what happened,” he said. “You asked me what I did, and I have told you what I did.”

“I did not ask you what happened.”

“No. You did not.”

Norah Stannard said where it was, out loud, so that nobody on that step would spend the week trying to find it.

“I am not a keeper of it. I am not going to write it and I am not going to hold it for anybody, and if you want it again in half a year you will have to come up here and ask him, and he has told you in front of me what he will say.”

“That is not a very good arrangement.”

“No. It is the arrangement there is.”

Barnaby Crove asked for a day against the ninth line for the hundred and eightieth morning, and said the rest of it in the same breath so that nobody could take it as a favour.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for a hundred and eighty mornings, which is nine hundred and one less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“A hundred and eighty. That is the whole of it, and it was the same figure on the sixth day of the hundred and fiftieth week, and it will be the same again, and nobody has to do anything about it for me to keep saying it.”

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and forty-eight days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and forty-eight is nine hundred and one less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and forty-eight days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.”

“Nobody asked me for the count I am keeping. That is forty mornings of them and it is not the sixty-three and the sixty-three is on no page and it is staying on no page, and I am not going to start handing out my own numbers because a man of thirty-one has asked to write somebody else’s down.”

Then the woman of forty-four said what had happened to her question, out loud, on the first morning of a week, and it was the same thing she had said at the end of the one before, and she said it because it is the ordinary work and not because anything had happened.

Orla Fennimore is forty-four, has kept twenty-nine chairs for nineteen years, has put her question down five times, and is not going to put it down a sixth. She has one line in another person’s hand and nothing has been settled.

“My question went down five times in nineteen years and it is not going down a sixth this morning because a man has offered to hold a piece of paper.”

“And it is not answered.”

“It is not answered, and nobody who said that man’s name out loud this morning answered it either, and I am not being unkind. One line on a sheet is about a chair in a passage. My question is about a day, and about whether the day you write is the day it was on, and there is no form anywhere in this county that asks me that.”

“Then it will not be answered this week.”

“No. And it stays in use until it is answered, which is not the same thing as being nearly answered, and everybody on this landing has been treating those two as one for a fortnight.”

Sena Dorr entered today’s line on the ninth run at about the tenth hour and read it out herself on the seventh and last day of the hundred and fiftieth week, and put no day on anything else.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years in a room four hundred miles off, and holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone.

“That is the forty-fourth morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing.”

“The arrangement is four hundred miles off and it is still answering. It did not answer this week and nobody in this county is in the room where it answers, and nobody was asked to go and I did not ask anybody to go.”

The account of one morning at the head of a cut was in the air over that landing at about the eleventh hour, and it was on no paper and on no run and in nobody’s book, and the man who had given it had gone down the low side to see whether his catch was still holding, and he had said he would be back and had not been asked to.

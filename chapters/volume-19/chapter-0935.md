# Chapter 935: He Gave What He Did And Then He Gave What He Thought Happened, And A Man Of Forty-Three Would Not Take The Second Thing

Sixth day of the hundred and fifty-fifth week. His six hundred and eighty-fifth morning. Five hundred and thirty-five days after the settlement. The fever ninety-three weeks and one day old. Four hundred and eighty-nine days since the division. Four hundred and thirty-five days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, and it held at that figure, three days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and swept the top step from one end to the other and came back and stood on it again, which is what he does when there is a fourth thing going to be said on that step and he would rather not be the man who says it.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and this morning would not say a fourth and has not said why.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, three days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the sixth day of the hundred and fifty-fifth week, and it is a Sunday.”

“There is no fourth thing and I am not going to invent one. That is a full bank and three days to run off it and about sixty steps gone under, and everything else anybody says on this step today is about something that is not water, and there are two men on that landing who keep ordinary things and there is a boy and there is a woman with a key, and on a Sunday morning this step turns into a room, and it has been turning into a room for a fortnight and I have not said so.”

“You keep it a step.”

“I keep it a step by not saying anything, and on Sunday that is harder than any day in the week, because on Sunday I have got a day and nothing to do with it.” He looked down the drain. “Somebody is going to stand up there this morning and say a thing and I would rather I was at the top end of the steps when it happened.”

Then Ilyan Vester said his figure, and did not go down the road, and gave the thing he had come up this bank to give, and it was the first thing anybody had said out loud on that step all week.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and eighty-one days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and fifty days old.

“Three hundred and eighty-one days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and fifty days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and thirty-five days old, which is nine hundred and thirty-five less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Seventy-five wrong things in seventy-five mornings, and here is the seventy-fifth. Nobody asked me anything this morning so I am going to give two things and the second one is mine and it is up to whoever is standing here to take it or not.”

“Two things.”

“First. What I did.” He said it plainly and it took eleven seconds. “I came up that road before the light. I stood in the lane behind the ash where there was no light because I did not want my boots on the gravel. I put my hand on the outer post of the gate to get over it and the post was wet and I took my hand off again. I went round by the ditch instead. I was in that yard from the fifth hour until about the eighth and I did three things and all three of them were carrying, and that is what I did.”

Nobody on that step said one word.

“Second.” He did not stop this time. “I stood at the front of it. And what I thought was happening in that yard was that the man behind that wall had been told something in the evening and had not slept, and that everything he did that morning was him getting his hands to behave, and that the three things I carried were not cattle and were not money and were his wife’s boxes, and that nobody in that yard knew except me and that I have not seen any of it since.”

The water going over the top of the drain could be heard from the bottom step.

“Which part of that is what you did.”

“All of it is what I did. None of the second part is what happened.”

“Yes.”

“That is the trouble with what you have just given me and I am going to say it once and plainly and then I am not going to say it again this morning.”

The man of forty-three came down off the top step to the step above him and stood there.

“What you have just handed me is not yours.”

“It came out of my mouth.”

“It came out of your mouth and it is not yours, and that difference is the whole of what I have stood on this landing for nineteen years.” He did not raise his voice. “I did not see that. You did not see it either, or you would not be standing here asking. Nobody in this county saw it. There is not a stone for it and there is not a page, and there is not a man who was in that yard that morning who is on this step with a wall or a key or a book. A thing nobody can be shown has to be carried.”

“I am not asking you to carry it.”

“You are not. That is the trouble. If you had asked me I could have said no and we would both have got up this morning.” He waited, and the water went on, and nobody else on the step came in. “And I am not going to say that it is wrong.”

“You do not know that it is wrong.”

“I have known nothing about that yard since I was a young man and I am not going to start pretending I have. I do not know what happened in it. Neither do you. You know what you made of it, and you have been making a very great deal of making for a fortnight, and a thing you have made is yours, and it stays yours, and it does not get to be put on a step in front of people because it came out of your mouth in a voice like a stone.”

“I have not asked it to be put anywhere.”

“No. That is exactly what you did.” He looked at the sixteen inches on the step. “Here is the working. If a man tells me what he did, that is his and it is as good as the telling. If a man tells me what he thought happened, then I am standing under it. Not because I asked. Because he gave it to me in the open, and if one word of it goes wrong it is on this step and my name is not on it but I am on it. The only safe place to stand is a place where nothing has been put on you.”

“I wanted to be somebody.”

“I know you did. I have watched you want it for a fortnight and it has been on every one of your faces like a man reading a price.” For the first time that morning he looked directly at him. “Here is what I am not going to say, so do not wait up for it. I am not going to tell you it was wrong, because being told you were wrong is a thing that is given to you. And I am not going to tell you it was right, because I do not know. And I am not going to hand it back, because you put it on me and not on the women of this county.”

He went up to the top of the steps and picked up the broom again, and did not come down for an hour.

Nobody on that landing said one word for a while, and then a woman with a key came down off the turn with her sleeves wet to the elbow and said the thing that was waiting to be said to him, and it did not do what it was meant to do.

Ruth Ansell keeps a pound two hundred yards up that cart road before the turn, has a book in her own hand with a day in it, put a mark in that book on Wednesday that meant she was in her own yard, and has decided to stop keeping the pound.

“You can say you were wrong this morning and no one will hold it against you. That is what a landing is for. I have known a man be told he was wrong by a woman with a key and be a different man inside a week, and there was nothing in it that cost him anything.”

“No, and I know what you are going to say about it.”

“You can say it to him.”

“No.” He looked at the broom going up and down the top step. “And I want to say the reason out loud, because you have been honest with me twice this week and I have got no use for being handled. If I say I was wrong then I am a man who was wrong, and that is a thing that happened to me and it is over. And if I do not say it then I am a man who said a thing on a Sunday morning that could be about a woman and her boxes, and that is a thing I did, and it does not go, and I would have to carry it.”

“You would be relieved.”

“I would be given something. And there is nothing on this landing that gives anybody anything, and you have heard the woman with the ninth run say it and you have heard the man who holds this landing say it in a different way in about nine minutes, and both of them are right, and here is the thing underneath both of them that nobody has said yet.”

He put his hand flat on the low wall and left the cut across it where it was. “There is no way to put this down. Not by being wrong, not by being right, not by somebody saying a thing was a mistake. It is on a Sunday morning on that step and I put it there, and the man who holds this landing is right that he cannot un-have it, and I am not going to be the one who asks anybody to try for me.”

Ruth Ansell stood on the bottom step for a moment with the water coming up past her boots, and then said that she had come down to say something else and would say it now so that it was not confused with kindness.

“I have stopped keeping the pound.”

“You told me Friday.”

“I told the step on Friday. I have not told a soul and I have been up there three days not telling one soul, and I have worked out this morning why, and it is because I said it in front of people first and then went home and had it to myself after, and a thing you have said in front of people stops being yours.” She shifted her grip on the bucket. “And a man has just said out loud that he cannot put a thing down. So I am going to ask you the plain question and you may say no. Has it got easier.”

“No. Good.” She started up the steps. “I am glad you are not going to tell me it has got easier, and that is not relief and I am not going to say thank you for it.”



Then the boy came up the lane and gave his figure, and did not sit on the bottom step, and said that he had heard the whole of that from the wall and that he had been in front of all of it too and had said nothing, and that he was going to say nothing tomorrow as well, and why.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and eighty-two days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and eighty-two is nine hundred and thirty-five less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and eighty-two days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.”

“You were listening to that.”

“I was on the wall at the top of the steps from the sixth hour because it was dry and it does not rain on the wall.” He did not come down. “And I have thought of about nine things to say about it and I am not going to say any of them.”

“Go on.”

“No. Because every one of them was a thing about what is true, and you did not give that step a thing about what is true. You gave it a thing you made, and you gave it to a man who was standing on his own step, and he has just said out loud that it is his now and he cannot get rid of it, and that is not about whether you were right. That is about what it costs.”

He turned the figure over in his mouth. “I have got one of those. It is a figure of stitches and it is not about anything anybody in this county did and it is on no page, and I gave it to a man in a passage and he carried it up a road and put a name on it, and I have not been able to get it back since, and I did not know until this morning that that was the same shape as what he has just done.”

“Nobody is going to think that.”

“They already have.” He sat down on the wall itself, high up, out of the water. “I am not doing anything about it. I just did not want to stand down there and be the only one of us who had not noticed.”

Then Barnaby Crove came up the road at the ordinary pace, and said his figure, and said that he had worked out where two hundred and thirteen mornings of asking ends, and that it does not end, and that he had wanted to be the one to say it and had waited for the man to stop sweeping first.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for two hundred and fourteen mornings, which is nine hundred and thirty-five less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and fourteenth. And I came up at the ordinary pace and I waited at the bottom of that road for the broom to stop, and I want it known that I waited on purpose and that I could have gone up that road four times while a man was un-saying something.”

“You could have.”

“I could have, and I have wanted to two hundred and fourteen times.” He set the barrow down where it had been every morning of the fortnight. “And here is the end of it and there is no end of it. I have thought about this the whole way up and there is nothing on that step this morning that finishes me. Not a woman saying no, not a man with a broom, not a man saying a thing out loud that he cannot take back. I get up tomorrow and it is two hundred and fifteenth.”

“Do you want it to be.”

“I want to be asked a question by somebody who can go and look. That is what a day against a line is for and that is what I have wanted every morning and it is not on this landing and it is not going to be on this landing in a fortnight or a year, and I am going to be two hundred and fifteen on Monday morning.” He picked the barrow up again. “And nobody on this step is going to be kind to me about that and I am going to say that out loud now so that if anybody is kind about it afterwards I can say I asked them not to.”

Then Ilyan Vester said the last thing, standing at the bottom of the steps with the water coming up to the second one, and did not say his figure again because he had said it twice.

“Three hundred and eighty-one days in this county, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and thirty-five days old, which is nine hundred and thirty-five less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it, and I am not going to ask it this morning either, and that is the only thing I have got left that is still mine.”

Nobody on that landing was relieved. Nobody on it was told they were right and nobody on it was told they were wrong, and nobody was thanked for anything at all, and the bank held at sixteen inches with three days of it to come back, and a man who had been in front of all of it had given away the only thing he had ever had that could not be given back.

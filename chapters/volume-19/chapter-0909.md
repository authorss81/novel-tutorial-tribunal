# Chapter 909: What She Is Owed Is To Be Asked Once And Not Twice

First day of the hundred and fifty-second week. His six hundred and fifty-ninth morning. Five hundred and nine days after the settlement. The fever eighty-nine weeks and three days old. Four hundred and sixty-three days since the division. Four hundred and nine days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Twelve inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, five days of the coming back, and about seventy of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave the three figures and said that a week beginning is a week like any other and that he had said three days ago what he would do this morning and had done it.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, and says a day out loud when he means to be held to it, and does not say it more than he means to.

“Twelve at the seventh hour, five days of the coming back, and about seventy of those ninety steps under, on the first day of the hundred and fifty-second week, and it is a Tuesday.”

A man of thirty-eight came up the cart road at about the ninth hour and stood at the low wall where he had said he would stand, and did not ask, and nobody asked him why, and he told them anyway.

Garrin Tolley is thirty-eight, has a child of nine, and carries a board in the inside of his coat with thirty-two notches cut in the edge of it, which is a figure of what is on a board and not a figure of a person.

“You said you would come on the first day of the hundred and fifty-second week.”

“I did.”

“And you have not asked me.”

“No. It did not.”

Ruth Ansell kept a pound two hundred yards up that cart road, before the turn, and wrote a line a day in her own hand, and did not move.

“You worked it out on the road.”

“I worked it out on the road. There was nothing in front of me on that road and I have had the same answer in my head the whole way and there was no point in coming up here to be told it in a different voice.”

“That is not a reason. That is a road with nothing on it and a man thinking on it, and if you go home and tell people you did not ask because you understood me, you will be understood by people who have not been up this road and they will think you did something clever.”

“I am going to tell them I did not ask because I had nothing to put on the board and I would rather come back with nothing than go home and pretend I have something.”

“That is better. I will not say it is the right answer, because I do not know what the right answer is, and I have not been asked it.”

He stood there a while and then he went back down the road. He said he would come on the fifth day of the hundred and fifty-second week, and Ruth Ansell said he could, and not one person on that landing put a word to him about it.

Then a woman who holds an account of what somebody else did asked a woman of forty-four what she did, once, and was not going to ask again, and neither of them knew what it was going to be for.

Norah Stannard is forty-six, keeps nothing, holds nothing, and has been holding one account since the seventh and last day of the hundred and fiftieth week, which is what a man said to her at the head of a cut.

“Orla Fennimore. I am going to ask you what you did on the fourth day of the hundred and fifty-first week. Once. I am going to ask you now and I am not going to ask you again, and I am telling you both of those things before I ask so that you cannot be surprised by either one.”

Orla Fennimore is forty-four, has kept twenty-nine chairs at thirteen shillings and fourpence a year for nineteen years, has put her question down five times, and is not going to put it down a sixth.

“Then ask.”

“What did you do on the fourth day of the hundred and fifty-first week.”

“I carried the twenty-second chair out to the yard and back because the leg was wet, and I put a piece of slate under it, and I came in at about the fifth hour and I made my tea and I sat down.”

“That is the whole of it.”

“That is all of it. I did not see anybody. There was nobody there.”

“I am not going to ask you what you saw. I am asking you what you did, and you have told me what you did, and I have got it, and I am not going to ask you a second time, and if anybody else comes up here and asks you what you did on that day you are to tell them I have already asked, because I am not going to have it said twice on one step and then read as though there were two.”

“That is a thing to say.”

“It is a thing to say and I have never had it said to me before.”

Then the woman of forty-four said what all of it was worth, and she said it before anybody thanked her for it, and she told them not to.

Orla Fennimore is forty-four, and for nineteen years she has been the one who asks, and nobody on that landing had ever turned it round.

“Nineteen years I have put a question down five times and every one of them was mine, and every one of them went out of this county and came back as nothing, and I have got used to that and I have not got used to the other side of it.”

“The other side of it.”

“That somebody asks me. Once. About a thing I did. And then does not ask me again. That is the whole of what I have been owed since the seventh and last day of the hundred and thirty-ninth week and I have never once said it out loud because I did not want to be a woman who wants a small thing.”

“Do not say that.”

“I am going to say it. I want a small thing. I have wanted it for nineteen years and it is small and it is not a lesser thing than what I have been asking for, and it is not spent by being named out loud once, and I am not going to let anybody here make it smaller so that they can go away from it.”

“Nobody is going to thank you.”

“Nobody is to thank me. And I am saying that first, because if somebody thanks me for this it turns into a thing that was done for me, and it was not done for me. She asked me what I did and I said what I did. That is two people on a step and nothing has been settled and nobody has been made better.”

“It is the smallest thing anybody has put in front of me this year.”

“It is small. It is not nothing, and it is not going to be any bigger, and I am not going to sit on this step and wait for it to be the other thing.”

Then a man of thirty-one said it was a small thing out loud, and was told by the woman of forty-four that it was not to be a small thing, and he did not get anything for having been the first to say it.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and fifty-five days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and twenty-four days old.

“Three hundred and fifty-five days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and twenty-four days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and nine days old, which is nine hundred and nine less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Forty-nine wrong things in forty-nine mornings, and here is the forty-ninth. Nineteen years of that woman asking, and what she is owed is that somebody ask her once. That is a small thing. I have been refused for eleven weeks over a sheet of paper.”

“Do not say small.”

“You said it was small.”

“I said it is small. You said it as though saying it settled something, and it does not settle anything, and if the two of you go away from this step calling it a small thing then it will be a small thing, because everything on this landing becomes what the last person to mention it said it was.”

“I am not trying to take it away from her.”

“No. And you are not taking it away from her. That is what I object to. You are agreeing with me, and agreeing with me in a smaller voice is the same as taking it away, and I would rather be argued with.”

Barnaby Crove asked for a day against the ninth line for the hundred and eighty-eighth morning, and asked a woman what she did, once, and told nobody the answer.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for a hundred and eighty-eight mornings, which is nine hundred and nine less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“A hundred and eighty-eight. And I asked a woman this morning what she did on the second day of the hundred and fifty-first week. I did not ask her what she saw. She told me and I have not said it out loud to anybody and I am not going to.”

“Why are you not.”

“Because the moment I say it out loud it is a thing other people have, and it was mine and hers. That is the only reason and it took me the whole walk up here to find it.”

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and fifty-six days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and fifty-six is nine hundred and nine less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and fifty-six days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.”

“Nobody asked me for the count I am keeping. That is forty-eight mornings of them, and a man of fifty-four has just told the whole step that he asked somebody what they did and is not saying what they said, and he thinks that is a new thing, and it is not, and I am not going to be the one who explains that to him.”

Sena Dorr entered today’s line on the ninth run at about the tenth hour and read it out herself on the first day of the hundred and fifty-second week, and said what she was not going to enter.

“That is the fifty-second morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing.”

“I am not entering anything anybody said on that step this week. Not the gate, not the oven, not the heifer, not the chair. They are people telling me what they did and it is the correct size for it, and I am a clerk of a run of days and a run of days takes a line about a day, and this is not a day.”

Somebody had asked a woman of forty-four what she did and had not asked her again, and the woman of forty-four had said out loud that it was not to be thanked and had not been thanked, and the thing was still small and was still open and was still hers.

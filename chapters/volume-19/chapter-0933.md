# Chapter 933: Twelve Inches, And A Keeper Of A Pound Has Decided To Stop Keeping It

Fourth day of the hundred and fifty-fifth week. His six hundred and eighty-third morning. Five hundred and thirty-three days after the settlement. The fever ninety-two weeks and six days old. Four hundred and eighty-seven days since the division. Four hundred and thirty-three days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Twelve inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, five days of the coming back, and about seventy of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and then said a thing about a figure that is not a figure, and that no instrument in this county could be got to check it.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and this week has started leaving room on the step for one.

“Twelve at the seventh hour, five days of the coming back, and about seventy of those ninety steps under, on the fourth day of the hundred and fifty-fifth week, and it is a Friday.”

“I will say a fourth thing and it is not a figure and I want that marked. A full bank on this landing has been sixteen inches four times since this stone was swept and every time it came off all ninety steps were walkable by the seventh, and it does not come off because of anything I do, and it does not come off because of anything anybody does, and I have watched four men try to hurry it.” He put his thumb on the stone.

“And that is not one thing about the ground. It is a thing about the water and the weather and about forty-one years of somebody upstream. And it is the only thing in nineteen years of mornings that I could tell somebody else about, and I am telling nobody, because the day I tell it, somebody writes it down and calls it a rule, and it is not a rule, it is what I have noticed, and it is worth nothing on a step.”

“That is nearly a rule.”

“It is nearly a rule and that is exactly why it stays in my mouth. I have given three figures off this stone every morning for nineteen years and I have kept my mouth shut about the rest of it, and I have not been thanked for that and I am not going to be thanked for it this morning.”

Then Ruth Ansell came down the cart road with a barrow and tipped something grey out of it in the corner of the yard and said that she had done with it, and said the rest of it standing up.

Ruth Ansell keeps a pound two hundred yards up that cart road before the turn, has a book in her own hand with a day in it, put a mark in that book on Wednesday that meant she was in her own yard, and has this morning decided to stop keeping the pound.

“That is the last of it out of that corner. It has been in that corner since before I had it and I have been throwing at it for six years and it has grown back four times and I have fed it and I have never once asked it whether it was worth feeding.”

“You have decided to sell it.”

“I have decided to stop keeping it. That is not the same as selling it and it is not the same as giving it away, and I have not written to anybody and I have not asked a price. I have decided, and that is the whole of it, and it is this morning, and nobody on this landing suggested it and I would like that noticed.”

“Who would take a pound.”

“I do not know. That is not my end of it.” She put the barrow down. “And before anybody starts at me about it, I know what a person says. She says it is a load-bearing thing. She says a lock is a load-bearing thing and a pound is a load-bearing thing and a bakehouse is a load-bearing thing, and she is right about the lock and she may be right about me and it makes no difference to the thing that has happened.”

“It makes a difference to what happens next.”

“It makes a difference to me and none at all to what happened.” She looked at the low wall rather than at anybody. “My father kept it. There was never a day of his in it because there was no day in it, it was a pound, it is a pen for things that cannot be got in anywhere else. And I have kept it six years, and there is one day in my own hand against my own name, and it is the day I went in and stood in it when there was nothing in it at all. Half of that book is whether I stood in my own yard.”

“And now you have thrown something out of a corner.”

“I have thrown something out of a corner. That is all I have done and it took four minutes and nobody helped me and I am not going to be asked how I feel about it, and if I am asked I will give you the same answer I gave the woman on Wednesday, and the answer is that I am not going to be relieved about it by anybody.”

Then the man of thirty-one came up and gave his figure and put a thing on the step that he had clearly been building in the lane since the light came up.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and seventy-nine days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and forty-eight days old.

“Three hundred and seventy-nine days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and forty-eight days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and thirty-three days old, which is nine hundred and thirty-three less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Seventy-three wrong things in seventy-three mornings, and here is the seventy-third. Every one of us on this landing who keeps an ordinary thing should be asked, once, in the open, by somebody else standing on this step, and the person asked should be allowed to say no in front of everybody who is going to hear about it later. Not in a passage. Not by a man who comes up the road afterwards with a name in his mouth. Here, on a Friday morning, in daylight, where it stops being a private kindness and becomes a thing this county knows about.”

“That is the second asking rule anybody has built on this step in three weeks and you have built it out of the one that went wrong for somebody.”

“Yes. And I have thought about that, because I watched it go wrong for him and I did not help him and I have not told him I did not help him, and that is a separate thing and it is not this morning.”

“And what do you get.”

“Nothing. That is the part I have not been able to get past.” He looked down at the cut across his palm and did not put it out of sight. “If it is here and it is refused in the open, then the refusal is on this step and so am I, and a man nobody can put a finger on is a man who has a morning a person can point at. I do not want to be liked. I want to be findable.”

Then the man at the head of the cut came down off the low side with a bar under his arm and gave the refusal in the plainest words he has, and did not make it a second refusal of the same kind twice.

Ezekiel Moss keeps a lock at the head of a cut a mile and a bit above the ash, has wired the same catch twice since the frost and has refused to wire it a third, and was asked what he did by a woman once and told the truth and has not been able to forget it.

“A refusal said out loud on a step is not a refusal any more. It is a notice served, and I know that because a woman stood at my gate about a year ago and asked me a question and I answered her and she took it away and it came back through the parish in a fortnight with her name on the front of it, and I never got asked anything again and I never needed to.”

“She did not put your name on it.”

“She put her account of it in a place other people could read, and that is the same thing with one more step in it, and I have watched a man four miles up that road find out this fortnight what that is worth.” He set the bar down. “And the working is this. If you stand up there and I say no, then tomorrow it is not that you asked me and I said no. It is that a man on this landing asked the man with the lock and the man with the lock would not say. That is a different sentence and it has got my name in it and nobody in it.”

“You want it kept private.”

“I want it not to have a shape anybody can hold. A man may say no to me on a Tuesday and go to his work and be a man who said no on a Tuesday. That is the only kind of no I have ever been able to live with.”

Nobody said thank you to either of them for that, and the man with the bar picked the bar up again and went back up the low side, and the man of thirty-one stood at the bottom of the steps with his want on him and no use for it and did not say one word about the standing offer.

Then Barnaby Crove came up with the gravel ground into the boots and said his figure, and said that he had asked a woman at the pound for one thing and had got one thing, and that he was not going to say what it was.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for two hundred and twelve mornings, which is nine hundred and thirty-three less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and twelfth. And I asked the woman with the pound for something today.”

“What.”

“I am not going to tell you. I am not going to tell you because I have had a morning where a man offered to put five of us in a book, and if I say out loud that I asked her for something then somebody is going to say that was you starting it. It was not me starting it. I have been asking for something since before that man came up this road with a use for himself.”

“What did she say.”

“She said it depends what it was. And I told her it depends whether she is minded to say no.” He put his cap on. “That is where I have got to in two hundred and twelve mornings. I am negotiating with a woman who keeps an empty yard about whether she is in the mood to refuse me, and I would rather that was on a step than in my own head, and I am aware of how that sounds.”

“How does it sound.”

“It sounds like a man who has made asking into the whole of his life.” He looked at the twelve inches. “It is not. Asking is what I do instead of the thing.”

Then the boy came up out of the lane and gave his figure and said that he had decided to stop saying the figure out loud to everybody who wants it, and had come up this morning to say it exactly once, and had not managed it, and had got as far as the wall.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and eighty days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and eighty is nine hundred and thirty-three less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and eighty days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up. And I have decided I will not say it again on this step.”

“You said it.”

“I said it, and you are all looking at me, and that is what I mean. You look at me. And that is all it is and I have not got anybody in this county who is waiting for it and does not know I have got it.” He turned the figure round in his mouth and came out with it anyway. “And I have said it. And that is a Friday and there are five more mornings and I am not going to manage a Friday on the last one.”

Nobody on that landing told him that was a very grown thing to decide, and nobody told him it was not, and the bank was at twelve inches and rising and a woman was going to stop keeping a pound.

# Chapter 943: Sixteen Inches And It Holds, And A Boy Of Thirteen Asked A Woman Once Down By The Water And Will Not Say What She Said

Seventh and last day of the hundred and fifty-sixth week. His six hundred and ninety-third morning. Five hundred and forty-three days after the settlement. The fever ninety-four weeks and two days old. Four hundred and ninety-seven days since the division. Four hundred and forty-three days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, and it held at that figure, three days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and then gave the week its own figure, once, in the plainest words he has, and said that he would not be giving the first figures of tomorrow.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and gave the closing figure of a week once a week and does not give anybody the opening figure of the next one.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, three days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the seventh and last day of the hundred and fifty-sixth week, and it is a Monday.”

“This week is out at midnight. Tomorrow is the first day of the hundred and fifty-seventh week and I will say three figures at about the seventh hour and they will be the same three I have just said and they will not surprise me and they will not surprise you, and anybody who wants a morning that tells them something they could not have worked out for themselves is going to be disappointed, and I have been disappointing people on this step for nineteen years.”

“That is the first time you have said that a week going out would be nothing.”

“That is the first time a week has gone out with a man on it who could tell me in advance what the water was going to do, and I am not going to be told I was right about it by anybody, including him.” He set the broom down. “And I am not going to say tomorrow’s figures tonight or on my own step or in my own yard. I have never done that in nineteen years and this week is not going to be the week I start.”

Then the woman of forty-nine came up the road with the ninth run under her arm and entered the morning at about the tenth hour and read it out, and then said a thing she had been carrying down that road since Saturday, and it was not about the run.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years in a room four hundred miles off, and holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and has decided to ask one question and has not asked it, and has said she would say it after and not before.

“That is the eighty-sixth morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing, and there has been nothing in it this week that anybody could use.”

“Nobody has used it.”

“Nobody has used it and nobody has been stopped by it either, and I want to be careful how I say that, because I have spent twenty-three years being a person people are stopped by.” She set the book flat on the wall and did not open it. “I have been standing on this step for a week telling people that they cannot put a column in here, and every one of them has heard the same thing, which is that Sena Dorr wants to be the only person in this county with a secret. And I do not. I want to go on being asked things in front of other people.”

“Then why not say it in the book.”

“Because the book is four hundred miles off and the thing I am asking for happens in about ten feet of step, and a thing that happens in ten feet of step does not go four hundred miles. That is why I am telling this landing and not the run.” She put a hand flat on the cover. “And I have still not asked it. I said on Friday that I would do it after and not before, and it is Monday, and I have had a weekend, and I have not done it, and I am not going to tell you why.”

“You told us on Friday you had decided.”

“I decided who. I have not decided how, and there is a difference and I did not know there was one on Friday.” She picked the book up. “Ask me what I have done this week and I will tell you. Ask me again on Thursday and I will tell you the same thing, because it will be true both times. That is the whole shape of what I have decided and I am not going to let a person on this step turn it into a rule.”

Then the boy came up out of the lane and gave his figure and said that he had done a thing before the light that he had not told anybody, and that he was telling the step about the fact of it and not about the thing itself, and that nobody was to ask him for the rest of it.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and ninety days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and ninety is nine hundred and forty-three less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and ninety days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.”

“You have done a thing.”

“I asked somebody what they did, once, and they told me, and that is all I am going to say about it, and I am saying that much because I am not going to be a man who did a thing and let it sit in me.” He stood at the bottom of the steps and did not come up.

“I am not going to say who, I am not going to say what they said, and I will not say what I made of it, and if anybody on this step tries to get the rest out of me I will go down into that lane and I will not come back up it today.”

“You asked them what they did.”

“I asked them what they did. Once. Not what they wanted, not how long they had wanted it, not why. What they did.” He looked at the sixteen inches going past on the step. “And they answered me, and I did not ask them anything else, and I did not ask them the same thing twice to see if it came out different the second time, and that was the hard part and the hard part lasted about as long as the asking.”

“So it is done, then,” said the man of forty-three from the top of the steps, without coming down.

“It is done once,” the boy said. “That is all it is. It is one question and one answer and it was in a lane by the water before any of you were on this step, and there is nobody in this county who can check it and there is nobody in this county who is going to be told what either of us said. I am not going to be asked again for it and I am not going to tell them what they said if somebody else asks them, and that is the whole of what I have done and the whole of what is going to happen to it, which is nothing.”

“You are describing an account asked once and nobody obliged to give it twice,” the man of forty-three said.

“I do not know what that is called. I am telling you what I did and not what it is, because what it is belongs to whoever I asked and what I did belongs to me, and I am not going to put them in the same place on a step at about the seventh hour.”

“You did it on your own.”

“I did it on my own and I did it before the light because I have been up before the light every morning of this year counting a figure that nobody has ever asked me for, and I got tired of the only thing I say on this step being a number that came out of me on my own. I wanted one thing on this step that came in from outside.”

He shifted his weight. “And I am not going to be told it was the right thing to do, and I am not going to be told it was the wrong thing, and the woman who answered me is not going to be thanked by anybody on this landing for answering me, because if she is thanked then I have asked her twice. Once for the question and once in front of you.”

Nobody thanked anybody and nobody asked him anything else, and he sat down on the wall above the drain with his heels hanging over the low side and did not say another word.

Then the man of thirty-one came up and gave his figure and proposed that the boy of thirteen be added to the list of people somebody ought to ask, and was refused inside a sentence, by the boy, in a mouth, with the working given.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and eighty-nine days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and fifty-eight days old.

“Three hundred and eighty-nine days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and fifty-eight days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and forty-three days old, which is nine hundred and forty-three less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Eighty-three wrong things in eighty-three mornings, and here is the eighty-third, and it is about him and not about me.” He nodded down at the wall. “That boy has just told us he asked somebody a question and got an answer, and it is the only time in three weeks that anybody on this step has done the thing I have been asking for. So he goes on the list. Ask him tomorrow what he did. Ask him every morning.”

“No,” said the boy, without getting up.

“You have not heard the rest of it.”

“You want to add me to a list so that somebody asks me every morning for the rest of my life, and you have been on this step three weeks telling people to do that to the keepers.” The boy turned his head round but did not sit up. “You said on Tuesday that I should be asked about what I thought. You said on Wednesday that every keeper should be asked once.”

He turned round on the wall so that he was looking at the man of thirty-one and not at the step.

“You said on Thursday that it should be every morning of this week. And every single one of those times you wanted a person to hand you a morning so that you could hold it. And I have got one and I have given it away this morning for nothing, before the light, to somebody who is not you, and I am not going to be added to anything.”

“You could have said that a week ago.”

“I could have, and I did not, and that is what eighty-three of your are.” He put his hands flat on the wall on either side of him. “You are not angry. You are relieved, and it is a bad thing to be relieved about, and I am saying it so that you have to sit there with it.”

Nobody on that landing told him it was true and nobody told him it was not, and the sixteen inches held at that figure with three days of it to run, and the man of forty-three said nothing about tomorrow, and a boy of thirteen sat on a wall above a dry drain and had asked one question and was not going to be asked for it again.

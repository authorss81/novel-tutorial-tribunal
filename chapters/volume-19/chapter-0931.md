# Chapter 931: A Man Who Stood In Front Of All Of It And Has Never Once Been Asked

Second day of the hundred and fifty-fifth week. His six hundred and eighty-first morning. Five hundred and thirty-one days after the settlement. The fever ninety-two weeks and four days old. Four hundred and eighty-five days since the division. Four hundred and thirty-one days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

Four inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, seven days of the coming back, and about ninety of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and gave no fourth thing, and he said that there were seven days to run off that bank before it would stand a man on the bottom step again, and he said that out loud rather than leave it in the stone.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and does not stand in front of a week he is not in.

“Four at the seventh hour, seven days of the coming back, and about ninety of those ninety steps under, on the second day of the hundred and fifty-fifth week, and it is a Wednesday.”

“About ninety of ninety is the whole of what I have. A bank that is four inches on the step is not going to tell you anything by Friday, and if it did I would not put it in a stone, because a man who puts a thing in a stone because it looked like something is a man who has stopped saying what he knows and started saying what he would like to have been true.”

“That is a hard thing to have on a landing.”

“It is nineteen years of standing on a step at the fifth hour. It is not hard. It is only that nobody has ever asked me whether it is hard, and I have assumed for most of those years that nobody ever would.”

Then the woman of forty-nine came up with the ninth run under her arm and put it on the low wall and read out the morning she had entered, and said the thing that had come out of a fortnight in which nobody had asked her anything she could answer.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years in a room four hundred miles off, and holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and has still never once been asked whether a line of hers was right.

“That is the seventy-fourth morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing.”

“Read it out again.”

“No. You have all heard it. What I have to say is not in it. What I have to say is that a man came up this road on the second day of this fortnight and told me what he thought happened in a yard in another county, and I wrote nothing, because there is no column in that book for what a man thinks, and there never has been one, and I am not going to open one because I happen to have a spare half hour and a man who is bored.”

“What is in the book.”

“A day. What came in and what went out and who stood there while it did.” She put her thumb under the page. “That is all that has ever been in it and that is all that is ever going to be. I have turned down better offers than that in my life and I did not take them because the book is a run and a run is a run.”

“Would you take it now.”

“I would take nothing this morning. I said on the ninth run at the tenth hour on a Tuesday that I was not going to spend the rest of my life not knowing whether my lines were right, and I have not worked out what I am going to do about it, and a man coming to me with a column he wants added is not that.” She closed the book on her thumb. “And nobody is to thank me for saying that either, because I have not done anything.”

Then Barnaby Crove came up the road at a good clip with the gravel ground into the cracks of his boots, and said his figure, and said that he had asked nobody at all this morning and had walked past a man to do it.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for two hundred and ten mornings, which is nine hundred and thirty-one less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and tenth,” he said, and stopped, and then said it again. “I have been saying it the other way for a fortnight and I have got to two hundred and it will not go in that shape any more, and nobody helped me and nobody is going to. Two hundred and tenth. There.”

“That is all right.”

“It is not all right and it is not not all right. I have said that number the same way two hundred mornings and this morning it came out a different way and that is the first morning anybody has heard me say a thing two different ways and it is the first morning in two hundred that my mouth has done something on its own.”

“What did you do this morning.”

“I walked up that road and I did not ask anybody. There was a man standing at the head of his own gate doing nothing and I had it in me and I went past him.” He looked at the four inches. “And the reason I am telling you is that I have been telling this step for a fortnight that I ask, and it has turned out that the reason I ask is not that I want to know. It is that a man who asks is a man doing a thing while he waits. And I have had two hundred mornings of waiting.”

“That is a thing to work out on a Wednesday.”

“It is a thing I have been working out since a man came up here with a name in his mouth and put it on a step. That is what a name in the open does. It makes everybody else find out what they have been doing.”

Then the boy came up the lane with his coat over his arm because it had begun to rain a little and gave his figure, and said that he had been asked for it on Monday by somebody he is not going to name, and that he had said no.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and seventy-eight days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and seventy-eight is nine hundred and thirty-one less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and seventy-eight days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up. And somebody asked me for it on Monday.”

“Who asked you.”

“I am not going to tell you and you have not got anything to give me for it so I do not need anything.” He shifted the coat. “And I said no, and I want it known that I said no out loud, because I have decided that I do not give it to anybody unless I am the one who asks for it, and I am not asking for it today.”

“That is a rule you have made on your own.”

“That is a rule I made in the dark on Sunday and I have not told anybody and I am telling you now because a rule nobody knows about is a rule I have not made.”

Then the man who moves the chairs came up on foot from the village at about the ninth hour and stood at the low wall with his hands behind him and said a thing out loud that he says to one woman in a passage every time she comes to the back of it, and said that he had said it to her again on Monday, and asked nothing for it.

Aldis Rook moves the chairs for Orla Fennimore and keeps nothing and has no stone and no seat and no board, and is the only man on that landing who is on the page without being on the stone.

“I say the same thing to her every time and I am not going to give you the words because they are hers and she has heard them and nobody else on this landing has, and that is not a treasure I am keeping, that is the shape of the whole thing.”

“Then why say it here.”

“Because she told me on the first day of this fortnight that a thing said once and not said again is the only thing she has ever wanted out of anybody.” He took his hands from behind him. “Here is what I have not said to her. There is nobody on this landing who can ask her once, properly, because the one who could is the man she is refusing to, and I have watched that from a passage for a fortnight and said nothing.”

“Why not.”

“Because I would be a man asking her a question on a step with a woman in front of it, and she would have to answer me in front of a woman.” He looked at the four inches and did not step back from it. “And I am not going to do the thing I would be doing.”

“Which is what.”

“Finishing it for her.”

Nobody asked him what he did. He had not been asked what he did on this landing in nineteen years and he did not appear to expect it, and he went back down the road at the same pace he came up at.

Then Ilyan Vester said his figure, and said the thing he had been carrying two days up that road, and what he said was not the thing anybody on that step wanted and it was not a lie.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and seventy-seven days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and forty-six days old.

“Three hundred and seventy-seven days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and forty-six days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and thirty-one days old, which is nine hundred and thirty-one less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Seventy-one wrong things in seventy-one mornings, and here is the seventy-first. The woman with the ninth run should ask a man what he thought about a thing he saw. Not what he did. What he thought. She has a line a day and no column for it, and I have been in front of all of it and I have never once been asked, and there is a man on this landing who does the same thing every morning at the fifth hour and nobody has asked him either, and that is two men and one of them has a stone.”

“You have never been asked,” said the woman. “That is true. I have not asked you and I am not going to.”

“No. I know you are not going to, and I want it on the step that I know it.” He did not look at the woman of forty-four, who was not on the step this morning. “I do not want to be asked what I did. I want to be asked what I made of it. There is no column for either and the second one is worse and I want it more, and I have wanted it since before I came up that bank, and if I do not say it today I am going to go on standing here not saying it.”

“And what would you do with it.”

“I have no idea. And that is the first true sentence about this that I have said in a fortnight.” He put his hand flat on the low wall, and the scar went across the middle of it and he left it there. “You are the only person in this county who could take it, and you have told me you are not going to, and I am not going to ask you again, and I am not going to go and find somebody who will. Because then it would be a thing I was doing, and I have spent seventy-one mornings being wrong and I would like the seventy-second to be one I chose.”

“You have not been asked,” the woman said, “and neither has the man who holds this landing. And when you said that you made a figure of two, and it is a figure of two men and not a figure of anybody, and I am not writing it down.”

“I know you are not.”

“No. I am not writing it down and I am not putting a column in my book and I am not going to.”

He was not told he was wrong by anybody on that step and he was not told he was right, and the woman of forty-nine went up the road with her book under her arm without saying another word to him, and the man of forty-four who holds the landing said the whole of what he had come down for, which was that the four inches would be eight by the weekend and there was nothing else in it.

Nobody on that landing was relieved, and nobody was thanked for anything, and a man of thirty-one walked up that bank with a want on him that was larger than the one he had carried down it, and did not have a use for it yet.

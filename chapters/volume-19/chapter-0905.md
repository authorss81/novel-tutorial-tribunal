# Chapter 905: A Hedge With A Man On Each Side Of It

Fourth day of the hundred and fifty-first week. His six hundred and fifty-fifth morning. Five hundred and five days after the settlement. The fever eighty-eight weeks and six days old. Four hundred and fifty-nine days since the division. Four hundred and five days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Sixteen inches held on the top step at about the seventh hour and it was holding at that figure, with one day of the coming back and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave the three figures and said that he had named the morning the bank comes off twice this week and was not going to name it a third time before it had happened.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, and has learned that a day said too often starts being a thing somebody repeats.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, one day of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the fourth day of the hundred and fifty-first week, and it is a Friday.”

“I have named that morning twice this week and I am not going to name it again until it has happened. It comes off at about the sixth hour and there will be ninety of those steps walkable and there will be no figure of inches on it. That is all it gets from me and you have had it twice, and a thing said three times in a week stops being a fact and starts being a habit.”

Wil Cairns had come down off the high side with a bill-hook over his shoulder and sat down on the bottom step, which is the step everybody sits on, and gave his distance before he was asked for anything else.

Wil Cairns keeps a hedge along the top of that drain where the parish line runs, from the wall above the ash for about half a mile, and there is no stone and no line and nobody after him.

“It is half a mile of hedge, from the wall above the ash going up, and it is the parish line, and that is the whole of what I can tell you about it. A hedge is not a thing anybody keeps. A hedge is a thing two men disagree about.”

“Disagree about how.”

“About which side of it the ground is. I have cut that hedge for nine years and I have put in the stakes that are mine and I know to a foot where the line is, and the man on the other side has been putting in stakes of his own on a different line for nine years, and neither of us has ever walked it together, and if we did we would find about nine feet of it where we are both right.”

“Have you ever walked it with him.”

“No. And I have thought about it more than about anything else. If we walk it, one of us is wrong, and it will be settled, and it will be settled against somebody who has been working nine years for nothing. So we have not walked it, and it goes on being half a mile long.”

“That is not an account of anything.”

“No. And that is what I have instead of an account. For nine years I have known how many stakes are mine and I have not written it down anywhere and I could not tell you the number today.”

“Why could you not tell me the number.”

“Because I would have to go and count, and I have never gone and counted, and if I went and counted this morning I would have a figure of stakes and I would not have a figure of nine years. Those are not the same thing and I have never wanted to swap them.”

Then Orla Fennimore asked a man who had been in her passage what he did in it, and did not ask him what he saw, and he said that if she asked him a second time he would give her the same words, and she did not ask him a second time.

Orla Fennimore is forty-four, keeps twenty-nine chairs at thirteen shillings and fourpence a year for nineteen years, has put her question down five times, and is not going to put it down a sixth.

“Aldis Rook, you were in my passage on the second day of the hundred and fiftieth week. I am not asking you about the chair. I am asking you what you did.”

“I was in the passage from about the first hour to the second and I carried nothing and I moved nothing and I was in there because there was a delivery at the front door.”

“That is the whole of what I did.”

“That is the whole of it. And I did not carry the eleventh chair, and the man who did carry it was not me, and if you have got me written down anywhere as the man who carried it then somebody has written down a thing that did not happen to me.”

“Who wrote that down.”

“I do not know and I never asked and I am not going to have him say it in front of me and take it back if he says it a different way. He will say it the same way tomorrow.”

“How do you know he will say it the same way.”

“Because it was not a busy day for either of us. There was a delivery and a chair and about an hour. If a man is asked twice about an hour like that he gives you the same hour both times, and there is nothing in it that comes out different, and that is not a second thing agreeing with the first one. It is the first one again.”

“That is the shape of what I have been trying to do for nineteen years.”

“It is the shape of something, and I do not know what, and I am not going to pretend to you on a step that I do.”

Then a man of thirty-one said that the lock was a better kind of ordinary case than the hedge because a lock has somebody to ask and a hedge has nobody, and was refused by the man with the bill-hook.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and fifty-one days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and twenty days old.

“Three hundred and fifty-one days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and twenty days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and five days old, which is nine hundred and five less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Forty-five wrong things in forty-five mornings, and here is the forty-fifth. That man cuts a hedge with a man on the other side of it and they have never walked it together, and nobody can ask anybody anything about nine years of it. The lock has a man in it. Ask the lock.”

“No. Ask it the other way round. A lock has one man who goes to it every morning and nobody watching him. A hedge has a man on each side and they are both watching and neither of them can go. That is not better and it is not worse and it is not the same, and it is worse for him in the way that matters, because when it is settled it will be settled by somebody else on a day he is not there.”

“Then it should not be settled.”

“No. It should be cut.”

Barnaby Crove asked for a day against the ninth line for the hundred and eighty-fourth morning, and the number was the last thing he said.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for a hundred and eighty-four mornings, which is nine hundred and five less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“A hundred and eighty-four. And I asked nobody anything this morning and I shut my mouth twice, and the day did not come anyway, and I am still here, and that is the third morning and it is going to be the fourth.”

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and fifty-two days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and fifty-two is nine hundred and five less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and fifty-two days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.”

“Nobody asked me for the count I am keeping. That is forty-four mornings of them, and a man of thirty-eight has been coming up that road all week and he is going to be here again, and he is going to ask somebody something, and I am not going to tell him what the number is when he does.”

Sena Dorr entered today’s line on the ninth run at about the tenth hour and read it out herself on the fourth day of the hundred and fifty-first week, and said that the week had keepers in it and no new position among them.

“That is the forty-eighth morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing.”

“A gate and a yard and a byre and an oven and a hedge have all been described to me on this landing this week and not one of them is a position among the nine, and not one of them has a stone, and none of them has replaced anything. I have not been asked whether they have and I am not going to be, because the answer is that they have not, and a thing that has not replaced anything does not need to be entered.”

Nine years of a hedge was in the air on that landing at about the eleventh hour and was not a number and was not going to become one, and a woman had asked a man in her passage what he did there and had not asked him again, and the reason had been given to her in advance and she had taken it.

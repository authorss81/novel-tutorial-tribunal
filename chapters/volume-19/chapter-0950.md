# Chapter 950: Sixteen Inches And A Full Bank, And A Woman Of Forty-Four Was Asked Once Down By The Water And Has Said What She Is Going To Do About It

Seventh and last day of the hundred and fifty-seventh week. His seven hundredth morning. Five hundred and fifty days after the settlement. The fever ninety-five weeks and two days old. Five hundred and four days since the division. Four hundred and fifty days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, and that was a full bank, four days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave those three figures, said that it was a full bank and that a full bank is not a thing to make a speech about, and then swept the top step from the high end to the low end and put the broom against the wall where it has been every morning for nineteen years.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and on the last day of a week gave three figures and a sentence saying a full bank was not a thing to make a speech about.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour, that is a full bank, four days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the seventh and last day of the hundred and fifty-seventh week, and it is a Monday. And that is the week out and a full bank is not a thing to make a speech about, so I have made one and it is a short one and it is the last sentence I am going to say on this step that I have not already said.”

“You have said things on this step that you had not already said.”

“I have. About a wall and about a record. And a man told me on Thursday that I was always right and I asked him not to, and he asked me not to be told I was right and I said nothing, and there has not been a morning since where I have been able to work out which of us was being polite.”

He set the broom down. “So that is the week out. The bank will be full again by Thursday and it will come off on the Sunday, and I will say three figures on Friday and a man will say a thing about what it means and I will sweep it off and that is what this step is for.”

Then the man who moves chairs came up the cart road for the last time in this week and went past the wall and up to the step, and the whole of that landing turned round, and nobody said anything about it, and he put his hands behind his back and said that he was going up.

Aldis Rook moves the chairs for Orla Fennimore and keeps nothing and has no stone and has no seat and no board, has never once been asked what he did, was told on the fourth day of the hundred and fifty-sixth week not to come up that bank again, came as far as the wall four mornings running, and this morning went past the wall.

“I am going up.”

“You said you would not.”

“I said on Saturday that I was not going to and then I stood at that wall four mornings and told a whole step about it, and I have worked out on the way up the road that standing at a wall saying you are not going to is still going, and I would rather have the thing done.” He did not move his hands from behind him.

“And I am going up because she has not come out of that door once since the day she told me, and she does not have to come out of that door, and I am going up because I would like her to know that there is a man on a step who has come up and is not asking her for anything. That is not a thing she has ever been owed and I am not going to stand here and call it owed.”

“Nobody asked you to do that.”

“Nobody asked me to do anything this fortnight and I have got very good at it.” He looked up at the sixteen inches. “And I am going to say one more thing and then I am going to stand at the back where I can see her door. If a thing said once and not said again is the only thing she has ever wanted out of anybody, then there is a man on this step this morning who has been asked nothing and told nothing and given nothing, and she can have that for free, and she does not have to know about it to have it.”

Then the woman of forty-four came up the road from the village at about the ninth hour and stood at the bottom of the steps and asked for nobody to come up, and said what she had come to say, and it took her a while.

Orla Fennimore is forty-four, has kept twenty-nine chairs for nineteen years, has put her question down five times and refused a sixth, was asked what she did once by a boy of thirteen on the seventh and last day of the hundred and fifty-sixth week before the light came up, and has not been asked by anybody since and is not going to be.

“I know it is finished,” she said. “All of you have known it was finished since Wednesday. Do not stand there being kind at me. Say what you came to say and go.”

“You did not ask to come up.”

“I did not.” She put her hand on the post at the bottom of the steps and did not go up it. “I came because a boy asked me what I did on Monday before the light, down by the water on the low side where the drain runs, and I answered him, and I have not told a living soul what I said, and I am not going to tell you now, and I am not going to tell you because you did not ask me and because you have all been waiting a fortnight for it.”

Nobody asked her.

“So here is what I came to say and I have thought about it for two days so I am not going to lose my nerve. I am not going to be asked again. Not by that boy, not by the man who moves my chairs, not by any of you, and not because of anything I say on this step this morning. If one of you asks me a second time then I will say the thing I said on the seventh and last day of the hundred and fifty-fifth week, and all of you will be able to say afterwards that I said it here first and warned you.”

“Nobody is going to ask you.”

“I know, and I want it said that I am not thanking anybody for that.” She looked along the step. “Nobody is to thank that boy either. He asked me one question and I gave him one answer and if either of those gets thanked then one of them has been asked twice, and I will know it, and it will not matter to me, and I am not going to have it be a thing where a woman of forty-four gets thanked on a landing because somebody was decent to her about it.”

“And the thing you have been after for nineteen years.”

“I have not had it.” She said it flat and she did not look away. “I have wanted a day against a line for nineteen years and I have not got it, and I am not going to be told this week was close to it, and I am not going to be told a boy on a step is a version of it.”

She kept her hand where it was and did not go up the step.

“Being asked what I did is a thing that is owed to everybody and it is not a thing that is owing to me, and the two have been mixed up on this landing for a fortnight and I have watched it happen and I am not going to be the last person to say so.”

“Nobody has said it was close to anything.”

“No,” she said. “And that is not a comfort and I would like it noted that it is not.”

And then the man of thirty-one came up and gave his figure and made his last proposal of the week, which was that being asked once was the same as what a woman of forty-four had been asking for nineteen years, and it was refused inside a sentence, by her, in a mouth, with the working given, and after that he gave the thing he had been carrying up that bank for a fortnight.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and ninety-six days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and sixty-five days old.

“Three hundred and ninety-six days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and sixty-five days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and fifty days old, which is nine hundred and fifty less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Ninety wrong things in ninety mornings, and here is the ninetieth, and it is the plainest one I have said and it is not going to be listened to.” He looked at the woman at the post. “You have been after the same thing I have. She asks you once and gets nothing and keeps it, and I have been asking to be asked once and getting nothing and keeping it, and that is one thing, and if you two things can be put together then somebody in this county is owed a morning and everybody else in this county can stop.”

“No,” said the woman at the post.

“You have not heard the working.”

“There is no working, and that is what I have come up here to say, and you have just done it in front of me.” She did not let go of the post. “You have put her and yourself into one sentence, two things that were each owed to somebody, so that one of you can stop, and you did it because it would be a use.”

“And a thing I was owed is not half of a thing I was not owed, and I am not going to be the half of you. If you want to be asked once then you ask for that, alone, without me in it, and somebody will either do it or they will not, and it will be yours and it will not have anybody’s nineteen years in it.”

Nobody on that landing put them together afterwards and nobody separated them again.

Then the boy came up out of the lane and gave his figure and said that he was not going to say anything about what he had asked her and what she had said, and that if anybody on that step asked him for it he would go down into the lane and stay there.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and ninety-seven days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and ninety-seven is nine hundred and fifty less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and ninety-seven days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.”

“You asked her.”

“I asked her. And I am not going to say what she said, and not because it was a secret. Because if I say it then it is a thing that got out of her and out of me at the same time, and the only thing that made it hers was that it did not come out.” He stood at the bottom of the steps.

“And that is the whole of what I have learned this fortnight and it is not a figure and I am not going to pretend it is better than the figures. It is smaller. It is just mine and it is not going anywhere and I am not going to be thanked for it and she is not going to be thanked for it, and I would like him and me left alone now.”

Nobody on that landing was thanked for anything and nobody was relieved and nobody was told they had been right about anything. The man of forty-three swept the sixteen inches off the top step and said no fifth thing. The man who moves chairs stood at the back of that landing where he could see a door in the village and did not go to it.

The woman of forty-four went back down the road to count twenty-nine chairs. The boy went down into the lane. The man of thirty-one went down the bank with a cut across his hand and nothing to put it on, and nobody asked him what he had done that morning, because everybody on that landing had already given an account of it in their own words and it was not his to give and it was not going anywhere.

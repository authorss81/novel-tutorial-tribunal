# Chapter 930: The Thirty-Third Time, And All Ninety Of Those Steps Walkable On A Tuesday, And A Man Came Up The Road With A Question In His Mouth And Did Not Ask It

First day of the hundred and fifty-fifth week. His six hundred and eightieth morning. Five hundred and thirty days after the settlement. The fever ninety-two weeks and three days old. Four hundred and eighty-four days since the division. Four hundred and thirty days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

The water came off that bank at about the sixth hour and all ninety of those steps were walkable by about the seventh, and that was the thirty-third time. There is no figure of inches on this morning and there is no figure of steps under, and there is no figure of days of the coming back either, and that is not a gap in the morning. It is the shape of the morning, and it is also the first morning of a week, and it was a Tuesday.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth thing, and has said the three missing ones out loud on every off-morning since the first of them and is not going to stop on the thirty-third.

“Not one figure this morning. The water is off the top of that drain and there is nothing standing in it, and I am telling you that plainly, because a man who is honest at the fifth hour looks like a man who has come out early with nothing in his hands.”

“You could put a mark up anyway.”

“I could put a mark up and it would be a mark about me and not about the water, and then in a year somebody would come and ask me what the mark meant and I would have to make a speech about a morning.” He set the broom against the wall. “And when I have figures I give them, and when I have none I say I have none, and that is the whole of the trade. It is not going to change because the whole county happens to be out on the road on the one morning of the week they can all get to a landing.”

Then the county came up the road, which is what an off-morning is for, and the first thing that happened on that step was that nobody asked anybody anything for about an hour and a half.

The woman who sweeps the steps came up out of the lane with her sleeves already wet to the elbow, did about forty of them before the light, said she was going back up for the rest after the hour, and refused to be asked how many she had done and was not asked.

She went past the baker, who came down the far side with two sacks and did not stop, and past the man at the bench, who was loading on the low side with the light behind him and had a cart road to walk on and was on it, and past a woman washing lime going up with a barrow and a man with a byre coming down off the turn. They said one sentence to one another of them and none of the sentences was a question, and nobody on that landing has written any of it down and nobody is going to.

Wat Marshe came up out of the lane at about the ninth hour and gave his figure and then said the thing that was actually on his mind, which was that this was the morning it would have been easiest for anybody in this county to ask him a question, and that it was the morning nobody did.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and seventy-seven days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and seventy-seven is nine hundred and thirty less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and seventy-seven days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up. And I want to say this once and then I am going to stop saying it.”

“Say it.”

“There is not one person in this county who could not have stood on this step this morning and asked me one thing out loud and walked away, and not one of them did, and that is because it is the only morning of the week anybody can.” He looked along at the man with the gravel. “I worked out on Friday that I did not want to be asked because I did not want it to be a thing somebody did to satisfy something. And the honest version of that is that on a morning like this I would have taken it from anybody who offered, and I did not want to find that out about myself today.”

“So what do you want instead.”

“Nothing. And I have wanted nothing on this landing for nine days now and it is the first time in my life that not having a thing has felt like the thing I am doing.” He pulled his coat on. “And nobody is to thank anybody for it. If somebody had asked me it would not have been a kindness, it would have been a use of me, and I would have let them have it and I would have felt it all week.”

Barnaby Crowe came up the cart road at about the tenth hour with the barrow on his shoulder and stopped at the low wall, and he did not put the barrow down, which he has not done once in three weeks, and he said his figure, and then said that he had walked up that road from the bottom of the bank with a question in his mouth and had taken it out of it on the way, and that he wanted to say that out loud on a step before it went back in.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for two hundred and nine mornings, which is nine hundred and thirty less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and ninth. And I have not asked anybody this morning.”

“I do not think that is news.”

“It is not news and it is not the reason I stopped.” He kept the barrow on his shoulder. “There is a man in this county who was stood in a room on the fifth day of the hundred and fifty-third week and I had it in me at the fifth hour, on the low side, with the whole county out and nobody home, and I could have walked to his door and stood in it and asked him one thing.”

“Then why did you not.”

“Because I worked out on the road what he would say.” He shifted the barrow. “He would say it in the same words he gave to the man with the book. He would not have looked at me differently and he would not have been shorter with me and he would not have told me anything I had not been told. And I would have come up this road with it anyway and I would have said *two of the same*, and it would have been two of the same, and I would be a man carrying a copy.”

“That is not nothing.”

“It is not nothing and it is not what I have been asking for either and I am not going to pretend it is.” He did not put the barrow down. “Two hundred and nine mornings, and this is the first one where I worked out on the road that the answer was going to be the answer, and I am not going to be told that was a decision, because it took me two hundred and eight.”

Then the woman of forty-nine came up at about the tenth hour with the ninth run and said that a clerk’s week turns over at midnight and that she had entered the morning of the hundred and fifty-fifth week before anybody else on that step had said a word about it, and read it out.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years in a room four hundred miles off, holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and entered the morning of a new week before the landing had finished making itself.

“That is the seventy-third morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing.”

“Read it out.”

“A bank came off and ninety steps came out of the water and a man has asked nobody and a boy has asked nobody and a step full of people have said nothing to each other, and that is the morning, and I have entered every part of it that I can see from a landing.”

“That is thinner than last week.”

“It is a Monday and there is no water on a step and everybody in this county is on a road, and I am not going to write a thin line and then go back over it on Thursday and fatten it up because it looked bad next to the week before.” She closed the book. “The arrangement is four hundred miles off and it is still answering. It did not answer this week and it has not answered for a long time, and nobody in this county is in the room where it answers, and nobody was asked to go and I did not ask anybody to go.”

Nobody on that landing thanked her for the morning and nobody asked her whether her lines were right, and she shouldered the book and went up the cart road with it.

Then Ilyan Vester came up the bank and said his figure, and said that a man who has kept a thing in his mouth for two hundred and thirty days with no answer coming back ought to be given something else to put in it, and was told by a woman with wet sleeves to take his own mouth back.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and seventy-six days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and forty-five days old.

“Three hundred and seventy-six days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and forty-five days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and thirty days old, which is nine hundred and thirty less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Seventieth wrong thing in seventieth morning, and here is the seventieth. That offer has been in your mouth two hundred and thirty mornings and no mouth in this county has taken it, and there is nothing else in there, and a man ought to be able to put something in his mouth that is not a dead thing.”

“Who is that for.”

“I am not saying it is for me. I am saying a man standing on a step two hundred and thirty mornings running with one sentence has no other trade, and you have all got one and he has got that.” He looked down the wet steps. “And he is thirty-one and nobody in this county is going to give him a second thing to say, so somebody ought to.”

“No,” said the woman at the bottom of the landing, wiping her forearms on her skirt. “A man who says a thing out loud every morning for two hundred and thirty days is not a man short of something to say. He is a man with one thing that is not finished, and there is a difference between the two and it is the whole difference and it has nothing to do with how many mornings.”

“What is the difference.”

“Short of something is a man who could do another one. Not finished is a man who has to keep going back to the same one.” She went back up the steps with the broom. “And I have swept these ninety steps for eleven years and I know every man who has ever said the same thing twice a week, and none of them were short of things. They were all in the middle of one, and you cannot hurry the middle of a thing by handing somebody a second one to carry, because you will end up carrying two and putting down the one that was actually his.”

Nobody on that landing told him he was right and nobody told him he was wrong, and the man with the book had not come up that morning and had sent nothing, and Barnaby Crowe went back down the bank with the barrow on his shoulder and the question he had taken out of it still in his pocket, and all ninety of those steps were dry and walkable by the seventh hour on the first day of the hundred and fifty-fifth week.

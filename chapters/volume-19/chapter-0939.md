# Chapter 939: Four Inches And About Ninety Of Ninety Under, And A Man Who Has Set A Hedge On A Line He Cannot Prove

Third day of the hundred and fifty-sixth week. His six hundred and eighty-ninth morning. Five hundred and thirty-nine days after the settlement. The fever ninety-three weeks and five days old. Four hundred and ninety-three days since the division. Four hundred and thirty-nine days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

Four inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, seven days of the coming back, and about ninety of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and gave the fourth thing, which is that when about ninety of ninety are under he gets up that bank at the sixth hour instead of the fifth and that is not a mood.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and gets up an hour earlier on a morning when the water is nearly off.

“Four at the seventh hour, seven days of the coming back, and about ninety of those ninety steps under, on the third day of the hundred and fifty-sixth week, and it is a Thursday.”

“And the fourth thing, which is not a figure. When it is about ninety of ninety under I am up here at the sixth hour instead of the fifth, and I have been up here at the fifth for nineteen years every other morning, and it is not because I am worried. It is because there is nothing to do at the sixth hour except come and stand here, and a man who is going to be on a step at the sixth hour ought to be on the step at the sixth hour and not be getting his boots on in the dark at half past five and calling it a change.”

“You have never once said that.”

“Nobody has asked me in nineteen years whether I get up earlier and I would not have brought it up on my own, and it has come up because there is nothing else to say to you this morning.” He set the broom against the wall. “There is going to be a full bank again by the weekend and there is going to be a morning after that where all ninety are walkable, and I am going to say those three figures on each of them, and I am not going to say a word about this.”

Then the man who keeps a hedge came down off the wall above the ash with his hook in his hand and said that he had refused a man at the head of the lane before he had got his coat off, and that he had refused him the second time since Friday, and that neither refusal had been the same refusal.

Wil Cairns keeps a hedge, the parish line, half a mile from the wall above the ash, and has cut it twice a year for nine years, and has moved every stake on his side of it onto the line he believes is, and is going to be wrong about it in a way nobody will ever be able to check.

“He came to the head of the lane before I had the hook off my belt and asked me whether I would be willing to have somebody write down what I did, so that in ten years there would be a thing in this county about a hedge.”

“What did you say.”

“I said no.” He turned the hook over. “And then I have thought about it on and off all week and I am going to say why I said no, because the why is the part I have got and the no is easy.”

“Give us the why.”

“Because I have spent nine years setting a hedge on a line I cannot prove, on purpose, knowing what I am about. And a man has come up that lane and offered me a thing that will find out. And I have realised, standing at the head of my own lane, that I do not want to be found out.” He looked up the wall.

“Not because I am frightened of the answer. Because there is a difference between a man who is wrong about something and a man who is wrong about something and is watched while he is wrong about it, and I want the first one and I have had it for nine years and I am not giving it up for a line in a book.”

“You have decided that in nine years?”

“I decided it on Friday when I told him, and I have had all week to find out whether I meant it, and I meant it.” He set the hook down. “And I will tell you what I have done, because nobody has asked and it is worse than the not doing. I have moved the first eleven stakes. All eleven. Onto the line I believe is. And I have not said one word to the man on the other side, and he has not been up here, and he does not know.”

“He will find the stakes out.”

“He will find them out when he finds them out, and if he walks the line one day in nine years with a man who has not been told then the walk is the end of it, and the end of it is that one of us is wrong about nine years.” He picked the hook up again.

“And I will be at the bottom of that walk and I will know which one of us is wrong, because I will have checked my own stakes against the stones as I set them and he will not have done that. But he will not know. And he will think he was right. And I will have to stand next to him for the rest of my life knowing it. That is the price and I am paying it and I am not going to be told it is bold.”

Nobody told him it was bold.

Then the man of thirty-one came up and gave his figure and said the same thing he had said at the head of the lane of the man who keeps a hedge, out loud, on the step, where everybody could hear it, which was the mistake.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and eighty-five days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and fifty-four days old.

“Three hundred and eighty-five days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and fifty-four days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and thirty-nine days old, which is nine hundred and thirty-nine less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Seventy-nine wrong things in seventy-nine mornings, and here is the seventy-ninth. That man has been refused by a keeper at the head of his own lane and he does not know why and neither do I. I am going to say it here so that he does. Every morning of this week, somebody on this landing should be asked, out loud, by somebody else, what they did, and whoever asks should say it to the whole step and not to a passage. Every keeper. The man with the lock. The woman with the key. The man who has opened a wall. The man with the byre. And me.”

“Nobody has asked you for that.”

“Nobody did. That is why I am standing here saying it and I am not going to pretend to have come up with it and had it turned down.” He put his hands behind him. “And here is the second half, and it is the half I would like somebody to argue with. I am not asking for their mornings so that anybody is checked. I am asking so that this county has one continuous morning about a single man, in it, every day, with no gaps, for as long as anybody will say it. I want it because it would be the only thing in this county that is about me, and nobody could check it.”

The man who keeps the hedge said, from the wall, that that was the worst thing he had heard on that step in three weeks and he was not going to say why, and then said why.

“What you have just described is a wall with you in it.” He did not raise his voice. “I have nine years of hedge on a line nobody can check and I have made my peace with it, because a thing I cannot be found out about is a thing I have got to myself. And what you want is also true, and it is being said every morning in about nine pairs of ears, and in a year that is not a wall. It has got a man inside it, and that is the whole difference between a wall and a wall with somebody watching it.”

“No one is watching it.”

“You are. You have said so. You want it to be about you and you want it to be there.” He picked up the hook. “And I have refused you once today and I am not going to refuse you a second time for the same thing, because you have already had the no and repeating it would be me enjoying it.”

Then the woman of forty-nine came up with the ninth run and entered the morning at about the tenth hour and read it out, and said that a man had come up her road on Tuesday and asked whether she would put the accounts of two people, of one morning, side by side in her book and call it a standing thing, and that she had said no, and that the second no had been easier, and that she wants that named.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years in a room four hundred miles off, and holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and has now been asked three times for a thing that is not in the book and has said no three times.

“That is the eighty-second morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing.”

“Which three things.”

“Who wrote their own morning down. What somebody said would come off that bank before it came off. And now two accounts of one morning put next to each other and called a standing thing in this county.” She turned the book round so that the cover was toward them both. “And I am going to say the whole of why, once, because I have said a piece of it three times and three times is a rule and I do not want a rule.”

“Then say the whole of it.”

“If I put two accounts of one morning next to each other, then a man who comes up this road in ten years opens that book at that page and he has got two people who were there, and he does not know that neither of them saw what the other did. And he will read it as a check. And it is not a check. It is two people in a place at the same time who saw different parts of it, and there is no third thing anywhere that could say which part was the whole part.”

“That is the same thing you said on Tuesday.”

“It is the same thing and I am the one saying it twice, and I would like somebody on this step to work out what that costs me, because it costs me the whole of the only authority I have ever had in this county.”

“It costs you nothing. You are right.”

“No,” she said. “That is what a man said on Monday about a wall and I have watched two days of this landing turn it into a thing people do for each other, and I am telling you now that I am not doing it. I am the woman with the ninth run and I have not been asked whether one of my eighty-two mornings was right and I am not going to be praised for saying a thing I have said three times.”

Then the boy came up out of the lane and gave his figure and said that he had worked out on Wednesday what he would do with the figure he had, and it was nothing, and that he had come up to say that he had understood something about himself that he did not like.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and eighty-six days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and eighty-six is nine hundred and thirty-nine less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and eighty-six days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.”

“You have not liked something for some time.”

“No. I did not like it.” He stood at the bottom of the steps in his boots. “On Wednesday I worked out that the only reason I have not given that figure to anybody is so that nobody can say I gave it. And that is not honesty. That is me keeping hold of the handle so that I am the one holding it. And a person who has got a handle on their own honest thing so that nobody can take the handle off is not an honest person. They are a person with a good.”

“Nobody has said that.”

“I have been sitting on it since Wednesday and I came up this morning because I said on Saturday that if I do not say a thing on the day it comes out of me then I am going to spend a week thinking about it, and I have spent three days thinking about it, and that is already three days and a half of my one morning every day, and it is a terrible trade, and it is the only one I have got.”

Then Barnaby Crove came up at the ordinary pace and gave his figure and said that he had stopped asking, and that nobody on that landing was going to notice for a year, and that he had told them first.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for two hundred and eighteen mornings, which is nine hundred and thirty-nine less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one, and this is the first morning on which he has not asked.

“Two hundred and eighteenth, and that is the first morning I have not asked.”

“You have said you were going to go on asking.”

“I said that on a Saturday and I have said it twice and I meant it, and I am not going to do a thing on this step twice this week because a woman in front of me has done it three times out loud and told us it was a rule.” He set the barrow down. “I am going to stop asking. Not in a room. Not quietly. Here, on this step, in the morning, where a man who keeps a step can hear me.”

“For what.”

“For nothing.” He stood with his hands on the barrow and did not look at anybody. “I have been two hundred and eighteen mornings into a morning that I wanted somebody to end. And this morning I got up at the fifth hour and I came up that road at the ordinary pace and I got here and I was the only one asking, because I am the only one who has ever asked. And I worked out that if I do not ask then I am still the only one, and there is nothing in that that changes.”

“Then why.”

“Because I want to be a man who asked. Not a man who is asking. There is a difference and I did not know there was one until a fortnight ago and now I can feel it every morning and it is going to take more than a morning to go away.” He picked the barrow up. “And nobody is to say that was a good thing to work out, and nobody is to say it was a bad thing, because I have worked it out and it has cost me what it has cost and I am the one who will be standing on this step at the fifth hour tomorrow.”

Nobody on that landing said it was good and nobody said it was bad, and four inches was on the top step with seven days of it to come back, and a man who had set eleven stakes onto a line nobody could prove was going back up the wall to do the rest of them in the dark.

# Chapter 924: Eight Inches And About Eighty Under, And A Man With A Byre Said The Same Thing Happens In His Yard And Nobody There Thinks Of It

Second day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week. His six hundred and seventy-fourth morning. Five hundred and twenty-four days after the settlement. The fever ninety-one weeks and four days old. Four hundred and seventy-eight days since the division. Four hundred and twenty-four days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Eight inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, six days of the coming back, and about eighty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and said that about eighty out of ninety is a bank going down rather than a man going up, and that he would not be answering questions about the difference.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and has been asked on three mornings running what a bank that is going down is for.

“Eight at the seventh hour, six days of the coming back, and about eighty of those ninety steps under, on the second day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week, and it is a Wednesday.”

“You are going to ask me what that is for.”

“I was going to ask you whether it would be eight again on the second day of the hundred and fifty-fifth week, and you are going to tell me you do not know, and then I am going to ask you what about eighty is for, and you are going to tell me that too. So you may as well have both.”

“Then both. About eighty is for a man who has to decide whether to send anybody down to the low side, and it is not a figure of how far the water has come. It has not come eighty. Eighty of those ninety are under. Those are two different sentences and about half of what gets said on this step in a fortnight turns on somebody hearing the first one and going off with the second.”

Then the man with the byre came down off the turn with the smell of the byre on him and said he had listened to two mornings of this step from the lane and had come down to put the easy version of it in front of them, and that nobody was going to like it.

Ned Farrow keeps a byre a mile past the turn, off the lane that forks for the upper farms, has a line a day in it and no stone and no seat and nobody after him, and has asked his men once about a calf because a man who has had a night to think about it is deciding and not remembering.

“You have all spent a week on a thing that in my yard is not a thing. That is what I have come to say and I would say it exactly the same in front of anybody.”

“Say it.”

“A man works a byre. On a morning something happens in the yard. He will tell me what he did, and I will ask him once, and I will get one answer, and I will not ask him again, and I will not go and find out from anybody else whether he did it.” He took his cap off and turned it over. “And that is not because I have worked anything out. It is because he is in that yard tomorrow and so am I, and if he has done something he has not done then I will see it over the next eleven days without asking him.”

“So your version is easy.”

“My version is easy and I have not the least idea how anybody manages the other one, and I am not being clever about it, I am telling you what I have.”

Barnaby Crowe came up off the low side at that and put his barrow down and said the whole of what he thought about it, which was that a byre is easy for one reason only and he had got it in a morning.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for two hundred and three mornings, which is nine hundred and twenty-four less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and third. And I have got the answer to that man’s thing and I have been carrying it since Tuesday.”

“Go on.”

“It is not the asking once. It is the eleven days.” He did not lean on the barrow. “A man who asks once and then has eleven mornings with the same man in front of him is not checking him, because he is not looking for a different answer. He is looking at a man he has to stand next to for the rest of the week. And a man who asks once and then never sees the man again has nothing but the words, and the words are all he has got, and I have had two hundred and three mornings of nothing but words.”

“He said he was not going to give you a reason.”

“He did not and I am not asking him for it and I am not going to ask him for it tomorrow either.” He shouldered the barrow. “That is the first time in a fortnight I have said a thing about my own two hundred mornings without it coming out as a complaint about somebody else, and I am not going to say what I feel about it because you would all get busy.”

Wat Marshe came up out of the lane and gave his figure and said that a byre was the only place in the county he had ever been in where nobody had asked him anything, and that he had liked it.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and seventy-one days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and seventy-one is nine hundred and twenty-four less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and seventy-one days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up. And I have been in that yard about nine times in my life and nobody has ever asked me anything at all in it, not once, and it is the only place I have ever stood where that was true.”

“That is not a compliment to a yard.”

“No. It is a compliment to a yard and I am the one saying it and I am thirteen and I have been holding a thing for a fortnight that nobody will ask me about, and I went and stood in a yard on Saturday on purpose to see whether it was better in there, and it is not better in there, it is only quieter.”

“That is a different complaint.”

“It is the same complaint and I know it is and I did not think of a better word on Saturday.” He put his hands in his pockets. “A quiet place is not the same as a place where nobody is going to come. Somebody has to come, once. That is all I have ever wanted and it has nothing to do with being in a yard or not being in one.”

Nobody on that step told the boy he had it right and nobody told him he had it wrong, and a woman said *he is asking for the same thing you are asking for and he has been asking for it longer*, and nobody took that up.

Then Ilyan Vester came up the bank and said his figure, and said that a man with a byre had proved that the whole shape of this week was ordinary and could be done anywhere, and was refused by the man with the byre in four sentences that gave the working.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and seventy days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and thirty-nine days old.

“Three hundred and seventy days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and thirty-nine days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and twenty-four days old, which is nine hundred and twenty-four less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Sixty-fourth wrong thing in sixty-fourth morning, and here is the sixty-fourth. That man has asked at his bench once for nine years and never asked anybody else and never had to, and it has not once gone wrong, and that is a thing that can be done by anybody with a yard or a byre or a bakehouse or a pound, and this week has spent three weeks arguing about whether it can be done on a step.”

“No,” said Ned Farrow.

“You have not heard what I am going to do with it.”

“You have, and you are going to make it something people do.” He put the cap back on. “It is not the asking. I have never once had a thing go wrong in that yard because I asked once. It has gone wrong twice and both times I did not ask at all, and both times I found out from the cattle. The asking is not the thing that is keeping it working. The being there is the thing that is keeping it working.”

“Then a man on a step could do it.”

“A man on a step is not in a yard. Nobody in this county is going to be on this step in ten years, and nobody in my yard is going to leave it in ten years.”

“Take the asking off this step in ten years and see what is there.”

“If you take the asking off this step in ten years there will not be a step to take it off. You have not made anything easy. You have made a hole with a shape in it.” He was already going up the turn. “And that is not me being clever. That is me telling you the easy thing in my yard is easy *because* of a thing about the yard.”

Then the man at the hedge came along the wall above the ash on his way back to the parish line and stopped, because a hedge is a thing you stop at, and said that he had heard the word *ordinary* used on the step twice and had come to find out which ordinary it meant.

Wil Cairns keeps a hedge on the parish line, half a mile from that wall, has cut it for nine years, works it with another man, and has never once gone and counted his own stakes.

“I have cut that hedge for nine years and I have never had a morning anybody asked me about, and that is not because it has never happened. There is a morning every year in that hedge when my man and I are the only ones in the parish who could say what was in it. Nobody asks me on that morning or any other.”

“Why not.”

“Because I am there the next morning and so are they, same as your man with the byre, and because a hedge is not going anywhere while I am looking at it.” He put a thumb on the wall. “And I will tell you the other half of it, because nobody does. If we all stopped cutting that line the parish would be a different parish in a year, and nobody would be able to say when. That is not a thing anybody can ask me about, because there is nobody in this county who was there the year before the one I am in.”

“So you have the same answer.”

“I have the same answer and I have had it for nine years and I never thought it was worth saying out loud until this morning, and I would go back to cutting now if you would let me.”

Nobody on that landing told him he was right and nobody told him he was wrong, and the man with the byre went back up past the turn with the smell of the byre on him again and did not stop at the low wall, and eight inches lay on the top step with six days of it to run and about eighty of those ninety steps under.

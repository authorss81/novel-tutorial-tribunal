# Chapter 916: An Account Is Not About The Thing, And A Page Torn Out Is Not About It Either

First day of the hundred and fifty-third week. His six hundred and sixty-sixth morning. Five hundred and sixteen days after the settlement. The fever ninety weeks and three days old. Four hundred and seventy days since the division. Four hundred and sixteen days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Eight inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, six days of the coming back, and about eighty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave the three figures and said that on Monday he had told them he could not tell them in advance what a new week would bring, and that he had not been able to, and that this was the ordinary way and he was not going to be a man who guessed.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and does not guess a week before he is standing in it.

“Eight at the seventh hour, six days of the coming back, and about eighty of those ninety steps under, on the first day of the hundred and fifty-third week, and it is a Tuesday.”

“On Monday I said I would not be able to tell you in advance and I am telling you now that I was right that I could not, and that is the whole of it. If I had said eight and about eighty on Monday morning somebody would have written it down and gone away pleased, and it would have been a figure I made up on the strength of nothing.”

Then the woman of forty-nine entered the morning on the ninth run at about the tenth hour and read it out, and said the thing that four separate people had come up that road to say to her in one week, and the reason she had said yes to none of them.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years in a room four hundred miles off, and holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and has never once been asked whether a line of hers was right.

“That is the fifty-ninth morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing.”

“Four people have asked me this week whether I agree that what they told me on that step is what happened, and I have said no four times, and I have said it easily, and I have been thinking about why it is easy ever since the first one.”

“Why is it easy.”

“Because nobody has ever asked me the other one. Not one keeper in this county has ever come up that road and said *the woman with the ninth run will know* — not said, but meant it. They would have said *did she have it right*, and they would have looked at my face, and I would have been a woman being asked about another woman’s morning.” She closed the book on her thumb.

“And I have a line every morning and nobody has ever asked me whether it was right either. Not once in twenty-three years. I write what I write and the day goes in and nobody on this landing has ever thought to look at it and say *is that the day*.”

“That is not a thing anybody would ask you.”

“No. And I have written a line every morning for twenty-three years and I do not know of a single reason to think any of them were wrong, and I have never once been asked, and I have decided this morning that I am not going to spend the rest of my life not knowing, and I do not know yet what I am going to do about it.”

“The arrangement is four hundred miles off and it is still answering. It did not answer this week, and nobody in this county is in the room where it answers, and nobody was asked to go, and I did not ask anybody to go, and I have put no day on it and I am not going to, whatever anybody says to me on this step this morning.”

Then Barnaby Crove came up and gave his figure, and said he had worked something out on the road, and it was the wrong thing, and he had brought it up anyway, and said so himself before anybody else could.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for a hundred and ninety-five mornings, which is nine hundred and sixteen less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“A hundred and ninety-fifth. And I have worked out a thing on the road and I am going to say it and I am going to say first that I know it is wrong before any of you tell me, because I worked it out and then worked out why it is wrong, and I have brought it up anyway because I want somebody else to have to look at it.”

“Say it.”

“You ask a person what they did, and you get it, and it is true. And then you ask a second person whether the first one had it right. And if the second one says yes, then you have the thing checked, and a checked thing is worth having, and I have not had a checked thing since I was a young man and I have wanted one every morning since I came up that bank.”

“You have not asked it.”

“No. I am asking it. Not here. Not on this step, in front of everybody, because I am not a fool and I know what a man does to the woman he asks that when she has said the thing was hers. Orla Fennimore keeps a passage and a man works in it, and I am going to ask that man at the back of that passage on Thursday, quietly, one time, and I will not ask him again whatever he says.”

“You are going to ask a woman whether another woman’s account of her own morning was right.”

“I am. And I have been walking up this road for two days telling myself it is only asking.”

Then Orla Fennimore, who had been standing on the step since before the seventh hour and had heard all of it, said the thing that made the week harder and not easier, and she said it in the plainest words she had and she asked for it to be written down by nobody.

Orla Fennimore is forty-four, has kept twenty-nine chairs for nineteen years, has put her question down five times, is not going to put it down a sixth, and was asked what she did on one morning and is not going to be asked again.

“You have all been treating a thing with a page torn out of it as though it were about the thing,” she said.

“It is not about the thing. It is about a book with a page torn out of it. And what you have got in place of that is a man telling you what he did on one morning, and that is not about the thing either. That is about the man and the morning. The page was about the thing that happened, even if you could not read all of it. Your accounts are not. They are about people, and about days, and about what people did on days, and none of that is the thing.”

“That is a hard thing to say to a landing.”

“It is a true thing and it is the first true thing anybody has said on this step since I came up it in the wet,  and I want to say the rest of it before anybody tries to make it smaller.” She put both hands on the low wall.

“I have wanted a thing about a day for nineteen years. A day that is the day it was on. I have written five times and every one went four hundred miles and came back as nothing, and I told you all on Tuesday that what I am owed is to be asked once and not twice, and you are all still standing here, and every one of you has now heard me say that the thing I actually wanted is not in this county at all.”

“What do you mean by that.”

“In the stone. In the yard. In the pound and the byre and the bakehouse and the hedge, and in the passage, and in a man’s mouth on a Tuesday, and in the book that woman carries. It is not here. And I am not going to be comforted and I am not going to be relieved, and I am saying that now, before it happens, because somebody on this step is going to try to do it to me out of kindness and it will make the whole of this smaller and it will not make one word of it true.”

“Nobody is going to try.”

“Then somebody will try before the hour is out, because that is what this landing does, and I have watched it do it to the man of fifty-four and to the boy and to the woman with the key. Take your kindness somewhere else. My question is not answered and I have just told you the reason, and the reason is not that nobody has tried hard enough.”

Nobody said a word for a while, and a woman at the top of the steps put down the bucket she was carrying and picked it up again and did not come down, and what she had been going to say was not said and she did not say it later.

Then a man of thirty-one said that the way to fix it was for somebody to start writing them down, and was refused by the same woman in one sentence, with the working given and without any kindness at all.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and sixty-two days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and thirty-one days old.

“Three hundred and sixty-two days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and thirty-one days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and sixteen days old, which is nine hundred and sixteen less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Fifty-six wrong things in fifty-six mornings, and here is the fifty-sixth. Everything she has just said is right and I have heard it, and none of it says anybody should stop saying what they did. Write them down. Five lines this week. Ten next week. A hundred in a year. And one day there is a thing in this county that is about a hundred mornings, and it will be made out of people, and it will be the only thing here that is not a bank and not a chair and not the nine.”

“No,” said Orla Fennimore.

“You are asking for the same thing you asked a man at the head of a cut for on the first morning of this fortnight and he gave you the whole working then, and you have not read it since. The minute one of those is on paper it is not what he did.”

“It is what a person wrote that he did. And if there are a hundred of them there will be a hundred names at the top of a hundred lines, and in twenty years there will be a thing in this county made entirely out of people being written down by a man who could not be shown one of the mornings.”

“That is not the same as what she said.”

“It is exactly what she said and you have been on this landing long enough to hear the second half of it.” She looked at him and did not soften it, and there was nothing on the step to soften it with. “And you are the only man here I am going to be unkind to this morning, because you have been standing on this step for a fortnight looking for a use, and I would rather tell you plainly than watch you find one.”

He was not told he was wrong by her and he was not told he was right, and he said nothing, and two men at the top of the steps stopped talking about nothing, which is not agreement and is not comfort and was not reported afterwards by anybody.

Wat Marshe was on the wall above the ash and had been there the whole of it, and gave his figure, and then gave the other thing, which was about himself and which nobody had asked him for.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and sixty-three days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and sixty-three is nine hundred and sixteen less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and sixty-three days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up. And nobody has asked me one single thing this week, not you and not him and not her, and I have had my figure ready every morning since the light came up, and it has been ready and nobody has come for it, and that is the whole of what has happened to me this week and I have not told anybody and I am telling her because she has just said out loud that nobody asks.”

“You are thirteen.”

“I know how old I am. I have been thirteen this whole fortnight.”

The bank was up at eight inches by the seventh hour with six days of it to come back, and a woman had told the whole of that landing in the plainest words she had that the thing they wanted was not in the county and not in anybody’s mouth, and had asked for nothing to be made of her for saying it, and a man had been told by the same woman that the only way to keep the next one was the way that was refused to him on the first morning of the fortnight, and nobody on the step had been told they were right and nobody had been relieved.

# Chapter 932: The Woman Who Keeps The Pound Has Picked A Day And Will Not Say Whose

Third day of the hundred and fifty-fifth week. His six hundred and eighty-second morning. Five hundred and thirty-two days after the settlement. The fever ninety-two weeks and five days old. Four hundred and eighty-six days since the division. Four hundred and thirty-two days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

Eight inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, six days of the coming back, and about eighty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and then gave the half of a fourth, which was that there was nothing to say about a bank going up unless it stopped going up, and then said that half of it was not a figure and he was not going to finish it.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and has twice in the last fortnight stopped himself halfway into one.

“Eight at the seventh hour, six days of the coming back, and about eighty of those ninety steps under, on the third day of the hundred and fifty-fifth week, and it is a Thursday.”

“I had a fourth thing this morning and I am not giving it. It would have been a thing about the bank and not about the ground, and a man who says a thing about the bank is a man who is watching a thing instead of standing in front of it. I have caught myself twice in a fortnight and I would rather say that I caught myself than say the thing.”

“So there is no fourth figure.”

“There are three figures and a man who nearly made a fourth out of arithmetic. Nineteen years and I still want to be the man on this landing who says one more thing than the stone can be argued out of.”

Then the man of thirty-one was at the bottom of the steps before the man of forty-three was finished, and he had been up that road since the sixth hour with the thing he wanted in his mouth, and he gave his figure first, which was not his way.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and seventy-eight days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and forty-seven days old.

“Three hundred and seventy-eight days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and forty-seven days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and thirty-two days old, which is nine hundred and thirty-two less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Seventy-two wrong things in seventy-two mornings, and here is the seventy-second. I am going to say this in front of everybody and in the plainest words I have and I am not going to be quiet about it afterwards. Ruth Ansell, you keep the pound before the turn, and there are five of us who keep an ordinary thing, and the woman with the ninth run has a book with a day in it and no column for anything else. Every one of you should be asked, once, by her, what you did, and in a year this county would have a thing about itself that is not made of stone.”

“That is what I said on the ninth day of the week before last and the woman in front of me took it apart in one sentence.”

“I know she did and I have read that ground since. And what I am asking for now is smaller and it is different. I am asking for five. Not a hundred. Not a run. Five, out of five, put in a book by a woman who is the only person in this county standing in a place where she cannot be wrong.”

Then Ruth Ansell came down off the turn with her sleeves rolled and a yard of something grey on the back of her hand, and she did not stop at the bottom step.

Ruth Ansell keeps a pound two hundred yards up that cart road before the turn, has a book in her own hand with a day in it, and put a mark in that book on Wednesday that meant she was in her own yard.

“That is my book and you have just stood on my step and offered it to a woman who has never asked me for it and does not want it and told a man on Tuesday she would rather be argued with than have it made easy for her.”

“The book is yours and the asking would be hers and you could refuse it any day of the week.”

“That is true and that is the whole of my refusal.” She wiped her hand on her apron and did not wipe it properly. “Because the day that woman opens her ninth run and puts my Tuesday in it is the day her run is a book of people, and she has been a run for twenty-three years and she is the last person in this county who is not a book of people. You do not get to use her to keep a copy of yourself.”

“You think I want a copy of myself.”

“I think you want to be in something.” She said it without any edge on it at all, which was worse. “And there is a man on this step who has been trying to get in something for a fortnight and he is not the only one and I am not going to help him by giving away a Tuesday.”

“I did not mean it as help. I meant it as the truth.”

“Yes. That is what is hard about you and it has been hard about you for a fortnight.” She turned and went two steps up and then stopped. “And I am going to say the other half of it, because nobody is going to ask me and because a thing I have not said out loud has been in my own house for nine days making the room smaller. I have decided which morning I am going to give. I have picked it and it is not today and it is not this week. And I am not going to say who to.”

“Ruth.”

“Do not. If I say who to, then it is a plan, and a plan gets talked about in a village before it happens, and then it is not a Tuesday any more, it is an event, and I have got nothing that could stand up to being an event.”

“You could tell me when.”

“No. You could come up that road at the time I have picked and ask me then, and you could do that once, and if anybody asks you what I gave you would be a man who took a woman’s Tuesday and put it on a step, and I will not do that to you and I will not do it to me.”

He said nothing for a while. Then a keeper at the low wall said that he had been in front of all of it and could have any day he liked out of his own, and he said that he did not want one out of his own, and there was nothing to be done with that.

Barnaby Crove, fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for two hundred and eleven mornings, which is nine hundred and thirty-two less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one, and he asked nobody this morning and said so.

“Two hundred and eleventh. And I walked up this road behind a man who has just offered a woman my Tuesday, and I have not asked him for anything, and I am going to tell you the honest reason and it is not the reason I gave on Monday.”

“Which is it.”

“On Monday it was that a man who asks is a man doing a thing. This morning it was that I have been two hundred and eleven mornings into a morning I want somebody to end, and that man came up this road this morning with a use for himself and offered to put five keepers in a book, and I have been waiting two hundred and eleven mornings for somebody in this county to have a use, and he had one, and it was the same one I have wanted.”

He turned the cap over in his hands. “And if he had asked me I would have said it. And if I had said it then he would have had his five, and he would have gone down that road a somebody, and I would still be two hundred and twelve.”

“So you did not ask.”

“I did not ask. That is the first morning I have been glad of anything and I am not going to be glad of it out loud.”

He said nothing for a while, and then the man who keeps the gravel said that it was a strange thing on a landing that the man everybody was waiting for was the one nobody wanted to ask.

“That is not what I said and you have made it sound better than it is, and I would rather it stayed worse.” He put both hands on the low wall. “I do not want to be asked by any of you. I want to be asked by somebody who is in a position to say no to me, and there is not one of you, and that is not a complaint, it is the whole of what is wrong with me.”

Then the boy came up the lane behind him and gave his figure and said that he had been thinking about what a rule is, and that he has decided a rule is not a thing you have, it is a thing other people can check you against without knowing they are checking you.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and seventy-nine days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and seventy-nine is nine hundred and thirty-two less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and seventy-nine days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.”

“That is a very hard thing to work out on a Thursday and it is thirteen.”

“You have all been saying it is a grown thing since Saturday and it is not. It is not a thing that takes longer when you are thirteen, it is a thing that takes longer when you are frightened, and I am not frightened, and I have worked most things out faster than any of you and some of them I have got wrong.”

“What have you got wrong this week.”

“The light. I have been counting in the dark and there is a lamp in the lane now that I had not counted.” He said it flatly and without any trouble in it. “And I have not gone back and changed the number. It is a figure of mornings and the mornings did not stop when the lamp came.”

“That is a very grown thing to do.”

“It is an ordinary thing and you have all been calling it a hard thing for a fortnight because you have not got one.” He went and sat on the bottom step with his back to the water.

Then the woman of forty-nine came up and read out the morning she had entered, and said that she had been asked for a column twice in two mornings, by two different men, and had said no twice, and that the second no had been easier and she did not know what that meant.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years in a room four hundred miles off, and holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and has now been asked twice for a column and has said no twice.

“That is the seventy-fifth morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing.”

“Twice in two mornings.”

“Once on Tuesday from a man who wanted it for this county, and once an hour ago from a man who wanted it so that he would be in it.” She set the book on the low wall and did not open it. “I want to say the thing under it, because it is worse than both asks. The second no was easier, and that means the second ask was the same ask. A man can ask me for a thing twice and get a different answer about how hard it was, and the difference was not in me. And a book that takes a man’s difficulty as an input is not a run, it is a temper.”

Nobody on that landing said anything to a man of thirty-one for a while, and one keeper of a pound went back up the cart road to a yard that had nothing in it that wanted doing, and nobody on that landing told him he was wrong and nobody told him he was right, and the standing offer went unasked on that step for the two hundred and thirty-second morning of its life, and the eight inches did not stop going up.

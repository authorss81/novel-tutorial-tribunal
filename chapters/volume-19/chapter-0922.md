# Chapter 922: The Thirty-Second Time, And A Man With A Book Asked A Second Man One Question And Got The Same Morning Back

Seventh and last day of the hundred and fifty-third week. His six hundred and seventy-second morning. Five hundred and twenty-two days after the settlement. The fever ninety-one weeks and two days old. Four hundred and seventy-six days since the division. Four hundred and twenty-two days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

The water came off that bank at about the sixth hour and all ninety of those steps were walkable by about the seventh, and that was the thirty-second time. There is no figure of inches on this morning and no figure of steps under, and there is no figure of days of the coming back either, and that is not a gap in the morning. It is the shape of the morning.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth thing, and on an off-morning says that out loud before anybody has the nerve to ask him for a figure.

“There is no figure of inches this morning. None. And there is no figure of how many of those ninety steps are under, because all ninety of them are, and there is no figure of days of the coming back, because there is nothing to come back from. Three figures that are not there and one figure that is, which is the thirty-second, and I have said that number out loud so that nobody on this step goes home this afternoon and tells somebody it was the second time.”

“It was not the second time.”

“It was not the second time and it is not going to be the second time in anybody’s telling, and that is not fussiness. In nineteen years I have heard this landing say a water figure wrong on more mornings than I can stand in cold, and almost all of those were not anybody being dishonest. It was a man repeating a thing he had been told on a day that was not this one.”

“And this is the third time you have written the same three figures on the morning after a morning with nothing on it, and both of the other times were also Mondays, and I would take it as a thing about the water if I had ever been wrong.”

“That is not a thing I am going to be asked about on a morning with nothing on it. If you want it you can have it when there is something on the step and I have got to my three figures first.”

Then the whole county was out on the road, as it is on a morning when ninety steps have no water on them, and a woman came up out of the lane with her sleeves already wet to the elbow and told the man with the stone that she had done about half of them before the light and was going back up for the rest after the hour.

“Not a thing to say and a thing to do,” she said. “You have ninety steps to sweep this morning instead of one. That is not a figure. It is a job you have got and I have got, and it is the only morning of the week you and I are working the same hours.”

“That is true and I am not going to put a figure on it.”

“Nor will I. I have done about forty of them before the light and I am going back up for the rest after the hour, and nobody is going to tell me how many I did, because I did not count and I am not going to start.”

Then Nevin Trask came up the road on foot with the book under his arm and said he had been up the cart road at the fifth hour and had asked the man who sets Pol Dunnage’s bread the same question he had asked the baker, once, and that the man had given him an answer.

Nevin Trask is sixty-one, keeps a book of heads and carts, asked a baker about the fifth day of the hundred and fifty-third week on the sixth day of the hundred and fifty-third week and wrote nothing down, and has now asked one other person the same question once.

“He was loading. He was loading on the low side at about the fifth hour with the light behind him and I asked him the same words I used up there yesterday and he looked up and then he answered it.”

“And what did he say.”

“That he was told before the light that the oven was going to be shut, and that he banked the fire with the baker and they covered what was in the trough and they had the far corner lit by ten, and that he could not tell you what is behind that wall because he was not in the room with the wall in it.”

“That is what the baker said.”

“That is what the baker said, and not one word of it is different, and it is not one word of it different in the order it came out either, which I did not expect.” He shifted the book. “I have been sitting on that road since the fifth hour with two of the same thing and I have gone over it the way you would go over a sheaf, and there is nothing loose in it.”

“That is the same thing twice,” said a man at the top of the steps. “That is the baker and the man at his bench and they have both told you the same morning and it fits. That is not nothing. That is the whole shape of a thing being right.”

“It is a thing being true twice,” said Nevin Trask. “Those are not the same and I am going to say why, because I am the one who went and got the second one and I have thought about nothing else since the fifth hour.”

“Go on.”

“A book is a thing a man can read who was not there. That is the whole of what it is for and if it is not that then it is not a book, it is a wall. What I have got is two accounts of a morning, and both of them are of what a man did, and both of them are true, and neither one of them is the morning.”

“It is the shape of the morning.”

“It is the shape of what a man did in one room. The morning is the room and the oven and the far corner and the hour it was lit by, and it is in there whether I can reach it or not.” He did not open the book. “And here is the part I have not got round to. The only thing that could set those two accounts against the thing itself is the thing itself. You would have to ask the oven, and you would have to ask the wall, and there is no third person to ask. Not because somebody has been careful. Because there is not one.”

“There is one man who could tell you about it.”

“There is one man who has not been asked about it and is not going to be asked about it, and those are different, and if he were asked he would say something true and it would be about his hands and his wall and not about what happened in that room on Saturday, and it would be the third account and it would not be a check on the other two either. It would be a third man talking.”

“Then what is the good of it.”

“The good of it is that nobody is standing on that step this morning being lied to. That is worth what it cost to go and get, and I would pay it again at six o’clock tonight.”

And a woman began to say a figure, and Nevin Trask stopped her before she got the number out, and said he would not have that said on that step, and she asked him why not when everybody else had figures on that landing all morning.

“Because the moment a number of us is said out loud on this step it stops being the people and it starts being a count of them, and I have been the man who wanted that count for forty years and I have not had it this week.”

“If I had had it it would have been a figure of nothing, and there is a difference, and you would not enjoy being the one who found it.” He put the book behind his back. “I know what a number of people is for. It is for finding out whether anybody was missed. And there is nobody in that room who has been missed, and if there were I would not find out by counting the ones who came back.”

Barnaby Crowe came up with the barrow at about the ninth hour and said his figure, and said that he had heard the whole thing from the low wall and had not asked anybody, and had worked out why, and the reason was that he had been doing the counting.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for two hundred and one mornings, which is nine hundred and twenty-two less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and first. And I heard all of that from the low wall and I want to say the thing that came out of it, and it is not clever.” He set the barrow down. “Twenty years I have been the man on this step who wants to know how many people were in a room. That is all I have ever wanted and I have dressed it up as wanting a day and it is not, it is wanting a number.”

“And now.”

“Now a man with a book has got the same morning twice and he says he cannot use either copy for anything, and I have watched him carry them up this road in his own head, and I have thought about doing the same thing with two hundred and one mornings, and I have not got a second version of one of them. I only ever got one and I only ever wanted another, and I have never once wanted to hear the same thing again out of a different mouth.”

“So there is nothing better.”

“There is nothing better and there is nothing worse and that is the first time in twenty years I have been able to say both halves in a row without one of them being the excuse for the other.”

Wat Marshe came up out of the lane and gave his figure and said that a man on the step had been stopped halfway through a figure, and that he had worked out that the stopping was the point and not the figure.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and sixty-nine days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and sixty-nine is nine hundred and twenty-two less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and sixty-nine days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up. And somebody is going to be glad I have not been asked this week and I would like it noticed that I have not been asked this week, because nobody asking me and me not asking anybody are two different things and only one of them is mine.”

“Which one.”

“Mine is that I have not asked anybody. There is nobody in this county who has been careful with me. There is nobody in this county who has left me alone on purpose. And that is worth having and it is not the same as somebody deciding I am better off.”

Then Ilyan Vester came up the bank with the figure already in his mouth and gave it and then said the smallest thing he has said all week, which was about a man who was paid.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and sixty-eight days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and thirty-seven days old.

“Three hundred and sixty-eight days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and thirty-seven days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and twenty-two days old, which is nine hundred and twenty-two less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Sixty-second wrong thing in sixty-two mornings, and here is the sixty-second, and it is the smallest one I have done and I am not going to dress it up.” He looked at the man with the book. “You went and asked a second man because you wanted a second one.”

“I did not.”

“You did. You have been up that road since the fifth hour and you are holding the same morning twice, and a man who went to get one of those on purpose went to get it.”

“I went to get it because he is in my book,” said Nevin Trask. “A cart went up that road on the fifth day of the hundred and fifty-third week and a man was wheeling it and I did not know which man. That is all. I have got a book with heads and carts in it and a baker and a man at a bench and one of them was wheeling a barrow of stone and I do not know which, and I went to find out and I found out.”

“That is a reason.”

“That is the reason and it is a poor one and it is the only one I have. If you want a better one you will have to ask me tomorrow and you would get the same one, and you would have made the same thing twice out of me to prove a thing that does not need proving.”

Nobody on that landing told either of them he was right and nobody told him he was wrong, and the woman went back up for the other half of the steps with a broom and no figure, and the man with the book stood at the low wall and let a week go out of itself without putting anything in it, and all ninety of those steps were dry and walkable by the seventh hour.

# Chapter 941: Twelve Inches And About Seventy Under, And A Man Who Moves Chairs Has Been Told To Stop Coming Up

Fifth day of the hundred and fifty-sixth week. His six hundred and ninety-first morning. Five hundred and forty-one days after the settlement. The fever ninety-four weeks old. Four hundred and ninety-five days since the division. Four hundred and forty-one days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

Twelve inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, five days of the coming back, and about seventy of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and then gave a fourth that is not off the stone, because the fever is a whole number of weeks this morning and he says it once in the same words every time it comes round.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and this morning has a whole figure of weeks and nothing to put it beside.

“Twelve at the seventh hour, five days of the coming back, and about seventy of those ninety steps under, on the fifth day of the hundred and fifty-sixth week, and it is a Saturday.”

“And the fever is ninety-four weeks old. That is all of that, and there is no figure of days going beside it and there is not going to be one. A whole number of weeks is a shape the calendar has and not a shape the stone has, and the stone has three figures on it this morning and I am not going to bolt a fourth one on next to them because the calendar happens to line up. I said it in these words the last time it came round and I am not going to say it in different words this morning.”

“You have never said it in different words.”

“No, and I am not going to start on a morning when there is a man on that step who has been counting things.” He set the broom against the wall. “There is no figure of inches being left off today, either, before one of you comes and asks me. Twelve, and it is going, and about seventy of the ninety under. If a man wants a morning with nothing on the step he can have one and there was one on Wednesday and it did not suit anybody.”

Then the man who moves the chairs came up the cart road from the village with nothing in his hands, as he has, and stood at the low wall and said the thing he had come up to say, which was that he had been told not to come up.

Aldis Rook moves the chairs for Orla Fennimore and keeps nothing and has no stone and has no seat and no board, has never once been asked what he did, works in a passage a hundred and thirty feet long with nine openings in one wall and a wall at the end of it, and came up that road on the fourth day of the hundred and fifty-sixth week with nothing in his hands because there was nothing to carry.

“She told me on Friday that if I came up that bank again this week she would not have me moving her chairs.”

“Then you will not come up this bank again this week.”

“I am going to come up this bank on Tuesday and I am going to come up it on Friday, and she is going to have to decide what to do about her chairs, and that is her half and it is not mine.” He put his hands behind him. “I am not going to say why.”

He did not take them out from behind him again.

“I have thought about saying why all morning and every version of it comes out as me asking her for something, and I have not come up here to ask her for anything, and I would rather be a man who came up with nothing and said nothing about it than a man who came up and explained himself to a landing.”

“Everybody on this step wants to know.”

“Yes, and I have noticed that, and I am not going to feed it.” He looked up at the twelve inches on the top step. “I have moved chairs in that passage for nineteen years and I have never once been asked what I did on any morning of it, and I made my peace with that on the first day and I have not broken it since.”

“And now the woman I move them for has told me not to come up a hill, and I have said no to her, and there is a thing on that step this morning where a man who keeps nothing has been told to keep away from a place he has never been paid to be. And I am not going to give any of you the inside of it. There is no inside. That is the whole of what I have.”

Nobody asked him what he did. He had not been asked what he did on that landing in nineteen years, and he went back down the road at the same pace he came up at, and he did not look back at the step on the way down.

Then the man of thirty-one came up and gave his figure and made a proposal about the stone, which was the smallest thing he has ever put in front of anybody and the only one in a fortnight anybody let him finish.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and eighty-seven days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and fifty-six days old.

“Three hundred and eighty-seven days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and fifty-six days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and forty-one days old, which is nine hundred and forty-one less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Eighty-one wrong things in eighty-one mornings, and here is the eighty-first, and it is not about anybody at all.” He put his hands at his sides. “I want that stone to carry a fifth thing. I have been coming up here three weeks wanting somebody to say what I did and every one of you has told me why I cannot have it, and I have listened to all of it, and I have taken the whole of it, and I am not asking for it again. So I am asking for the stone instead. Put one more thing on it. Not a figure. Just a line that is not water.”

“That is not yours to ask for either,” said the man with the broom, before Ilyan had finished the sentence.

“I know it is not. I am asking anyway, and you can refuse it, and I want the refusal out loud in the same words you have used all week so that everybody down here knows exactly what the answer is.”

“All right.” Corvin Tace came down two steps and put the head of the broom flat on the stone. “Here it is, and here is the working, and then I am going back up. Three figures come off that stone every morning and they are about water, and they are the same every morning of a kind, and that is the whole reason a man can stand here and say them without looking.”

“The day I put a fourth kind of thing on it, one of three kinds comes off, because from that morning there is something on this stone about a man. And then the first thing anybody does with the other figures is check them against it. Not on purpose. They just stop being figures about water and become figures about whoever is standing here. You have been asking me for three weeks to make you somebody and you have just asked me to make the water into you.”

Nobody on that landing said that was a good argument and nobody said it was a bad one, and a man who moves chairs went down a hill with nothing in his hands.

Then the man who holds the gravel came up at the ordinary pace with the barrow and gave his figure, and did not ask for it, which was the second morning he had not asked, and he had a different reason for that and worked it out on the road.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and twenty mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is nine hundred and forty-one less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one, and he has not spent one of them since the third day of the hundred and fifty-sixth week.

“Two hundred and twentieth, and that is the second morning I have not asked, and I am not going to tell you why I stopped because I have given one reason on Friday and I am not going to give you a second one, because a man who gives a new reason every morning is a man building something and I am not building anything.”

“You do not owe anybody a reason.”

He did not look at the water while he said it.

“I do not, and that is exactly the trouble and it is the first time in a long while that not owing somebody something has felt like work instead of like a rest.” He set the barrow down on the dry part. “On Friday I told you I was going to stop asking, and I have been thinking about it since, in the yard, at four in the morning, with the barrow in front of me.”

“And what I have worked out is that not asking is not the same as not wanting. I have wanted a day against that line every morning I have been alive in this county and I am going to want it tomorrow. The wanting has not stopped. What has stopped is me putting it in front of people. And I cannot tell you which of those two things is the bigger and I am not going to stand here and work it out on a Saturday morning in front of everybody.”

“You have not asked anybody for anything all week.”

“No, and if a man on this step wants to come up here on Monday and ask me how it feels not to ask, he can come up on Monday and ask me how it feels not to ask, and I will tell him, and then it will be a thing that happened. And a thing that happened is a thing somebody could do again next week.”

Then the boy came up out of the lane where he had been since before the light and gave his figure and said that he had watched a man come up with nothing and go back down with nothing and had thought about it for an hour, and that he had worked out that the man was the only person on the step who had not asked anybody for anything since Friday.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and eighty-eight days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and eighty-eight is nine hundred and forty-one less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and eighty-eight days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.”

He shifted his weight on the wall and did not go up the steps.

“You have been thinking about a man all morning.”

“I have been thinking about him since he went down the hill and I have got something and it is not a question and you are all going to be annoyed by it.” He stood at the bottom of the steps in his boots. “That man has come up that road with nothing in his hands and gone back down it with nothing in his hands, and everybody on that step opened their mouths at him, and not one of you asked him what he did.”

“And he has been here every morning of the fortnight and every morning of the fortnight one of you has had a go at him about something. And this morning he was the only one who said no out loud and got nothing for it, and I have been sitting in that lane working out that a person can say a thing out loud and go down the hill and nobody has to do anything about it, and that is the whole thing I have got this week.”

“That is not much of a thing for an hour in a lane.”

“It is the first thing I have worked out that did not have a number in it,” he said, “and there is nothing else on that step with a number in it that is any use to anybody.”

Twelve inches was on the top step going down, and the man of forty-three swept the wet of it off in three strokes and stopped, and the fever was ninety-four weeks old and was not going to be said again, and there was a full bank coming on a Sunday, and nobody on that landing asked a second question.

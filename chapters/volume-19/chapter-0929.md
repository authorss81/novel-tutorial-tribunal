# Chapter 929: Sixteen Inches And One Day Of It Left, And A Man Came Up From The Ash With A Day Against His Own Name And Said It Was Not The Ninth Line

Seventh and last day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week. His six hundred and seventy-ninth morning. Five hundred and twenty-nine days after the settlement. The fever ninety-two weeks and two days old. Four hundred and eighty-three days since the division. Four hundred and twenty-nine days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, holding at that figure, one day of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave those three figures, and then gave the shape of the week going out of itself, which he does once a week and has never once done on the wrong evening.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and gives the shape of a week on the evening it goes, in words, because he will not carry the figures of a week he has not stood in front of.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, one day of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the seventh and last day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week, and it is a Monday.”

“This week is out at midnight. Tomorrow is the first day of the hundred and fifty-fifth week and I will say three figures at about the seventh hour, and I am telling you now what they will not be, so that nobody can go away pleased with me.”

“What will they be.”

“That is the whole of it. I will not say the shape of next week on this landing, and I lost that argument at a man with a broom on the fourth day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week and I am not reopening it.” He looked at the bank. “What I will tell you is that it comes off, because one day means it comes off, and after it comes off there is nothing on this step, and you have all worked that out by now and I would rather say it than have it said behind me.”

Then a young man came up from the low side of the ash with his coat open and stopped at the end of the landing and said that he had a day against his name and that he had come up to say so out loud because somebody on that step had been asking for one for a long time and he thought the man ought to know that it was possible.

Ivo Kellow is thirty-four, keeps nothing, holds no stone and no seat and no run and has a day against his own name, and has known since the second day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week that a man on that step has been asking the county for a thing he has got.

“I heard it on the low side, and I have had it since the sixth day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week, and I did not come up on the sixth day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week because I did not think it was mine to come up about. There is a day against my name. That is all. I have not got a book and I have not got a line and there is nobody who would put a day against my name if they were not asked to.”

Nobody on that landing said one word for long enough that the water going over the top of the drain could be heard from the bottom step.

Barnaby Crowe put the barrow down on the stones it has stood on every morning of the fortnight and asked the first question he had asked all week, and it was not the one anybody expected, and it was not about the ninth line.

“What name.”

“Mine.”

“That is a figure of a name and not a figure of a day.”

“It is a day and a name, and the name is mine, and I am not going to tell you the day because you would ask me for it twice and I would have to decide whether you were right to ask.” He shifted his weight. “I have had it since I was thirty-four, which is now, and it came off a page that is not in this county and I have never once been asked about it and I have never once wanted to be.”

“I want the ninth line.”

“Yes. And I know what you want, and you have wanted it on two hundred and eight mornings, and I am telling you here in front of everyone that a day against a name in this county is not the day you want, and that I would not give you the day you want if I had it.”

“I have never asked you for anything.”

“You asked me three seconds ago what my name was, and you asked me because you wanted a way in, and that is fair and I would have done the same.” He did not move.

“And here is the thing I have come up to say, and then I am going back down to the ash. If I gave you a day this morning it would not be a check on anything. You would have two days, and it would not be two of the same thing and it would not be two of a different thing. It would be a man on a landing holding a piece of paper and a man on a landing holding a want, and neither of them would be able to put the other one down.”

“I would not put it down.”

“That is what I am saying and it is why I am going down to the ash now.” He looked along the step. “There is a woman in the reed below the turn called Marda Vell who asked me this same question on the second day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week, and I gave her an answer. I am not going to tell you what the answer was, and she is not going to tell you either, and if either of you ever gets it into your head to go and ask her what I said then I will know, because she will tell me, and I do not want any part of that.”

Barnaby Crowe is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for two hundred and eight mornings, which is nine hundred and twenty-nine less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one, and nobody told him that he had been relieved and nobody told him that he had not.

He picked the barrow up again and went back down to the low side without saying one word about the two hundred and eight, which is the first morning in a fortnight that he has given the figure without a sentence on it.

Wat Marshe came up out of the lane and gave his figure and said that a man had come up the road and told everybody a thing nobody had asked him for, and that this was the third time in a week that had happened, and that he had a list, and that he was not going to give the list to anybody.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and seventy-six days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and seventy-six is nine hundred and twenty-nine less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and seventy-six days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up. And it has happened three times this week that somebody came up here and said a thing out loud that nobody asked them for.”

“That is not a list anybody wants.”

“It is a list I want.” He looked at the man going down to the ash. “Because every one of them said it out loud and every one of them has said on this step that it cost them something, and that is the same shape every time, and the same shape every time means it is not a coincidence and it is not a rule either, and I have been trying all week to work out which of those two it is.”

“And what have you worked out.”

“That I do not have to. I have got the list and I am thirteen and I am not going to hand it to a man of thirty-one who is building something out of people saying things on a step.” He pulled his coat tighter. “And if you say I am right about it I will not do it again for a month, and if you say I am wrong about it I will do it tomorrow. That is the arrangement and you can both go away and be annoyed about it.”

Then Ilyan Vester came up and said his figure, and said that a man who had been refused two hundred and eight times and had heard the truth about it that morning was owed something by the landing, and was told by the woman with the ninth run what he was owed and by whom.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and seventy-five days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and forty-four days old.

“Three hundred and seventy-five days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and forty-four days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and twenty-nine days old, which is nine hundred and twenty-nine less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Sixty-ninth wrong thing in sixty-ninth morning, and here is the sixty-ninth. That man has stood on that step and asked two hundred and eight mornings and this morning a man he has never spoken to told him to his face that he will not be relieved, and it was the right thing and it was said on a step, and the whole of this landing should do something about that.”

“About what, precisely.”

“With him. With the wanting.” He looked at the wall. “He has been doing it alone for two years. Two years and two hundred and eight mornings. And this week every single one of us has been watched.”

“No,” said Sena Dorr, from the other side of the landing, who is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years in a room four hundred miles off, holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and entered this morning at about the tenth hour and read it out to nobody in particular.

“That is the seventy-second morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing. And I have said it because he is standing there and I would rather he had it from me than worked it out, and I am not going to be asked for it twice.” “He has not been doing it alone. He has been doing it in front of us and we have not helped, and those two things are not the same, and I am the person who should know because I have been the one looking at it and writing the mornings down.”

“You would say that if it were any of us.”

“I would say that if it were any of us and I would not have liked saying it. I have said it because it is true, and because it is the last day of a week, and because he has had a morning.” She closed the book on her thumb. “And nobody on this landing is going to do anything about that man this week, and I am saying so as a clerk and not as a kindness.”

“Because the whole of the week has been people finding things to do about each other, and he is the one thing on this step that nobody can do anything about. It is not because he is unloved. It is because there is no third thing.”

Barnaby Crowe did not hear any of that, because he was already on the low side with the barrow, and the man with the book came up the cart road at about the eleventh hour and said he was not going to open the book again this week and was not going to give the reason a second time.

Nevin Trask is sixty-one, keeps a book of heads and carts, has the fifth day of the hundred and fifty-third week twice and one morning before it, has not opened the book in seven days, and has come up a road for the last time this week to say that nothing about any of it has changed.

“Seven days. I have not opened it and I am not going to this week, and I am not going to say why again because I said the reason on the fifth day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week and it was the true one and it will not be truer for saying it again.”

“Will anything change.”

“Yes. Someday one of those mornings will come out of my head different from the way it went in, and when it does I will not know whether it changed or whether I changed, and there is nobody in this county who can tell me.” He put the book away. “And I have worked out that this week has not made anybody better off. A baker and the man at his bench told a step the same morning and it is still the same morning and nobody can do anything with it, and a man at that bench gave a morning up for nothing and says he would give it again. That is the week.”

Nobody on that landing told the man with the book he was right and nobody told him he was wrong, and sixteen inches lay on the top step with one day of it to run and about sixty of those ninety steps under, and a week went out of itself at midnight without one thing on it having been settled.

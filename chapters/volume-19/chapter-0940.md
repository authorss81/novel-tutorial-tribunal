# Chapter 940: Eight Inches And About Eighty Under, And A Man Who Has Decided To Ask Somebody One Thing And Has Not

Fourth day of the hundred and fifty-sixth week. His six hundred and ninetieth morning. Five hundred and forty days after the settlement. The fever ninety-three weeks and six days old. Four hundred and ninety-four days since the division. Four hundred and forty days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

Eight inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, six days of the coming back, and about eighty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and said the one thing he has not said in three weeks, which is what he does when there is no figure left on a stone for anybody to argue about.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and has three weeks of Sundays on the step behind him and has not yet said what they were.

“Eight at the seventh hour, six days of the coming back, and about eighty of those ninety steps under, on the fourth day of the hundred and fifty-sixth week, and it is a Friday.”

“And a fourth thing, and it is not a figure and I am going to say it once. There has not been a fourth thing on this step for three weeks and there has been more said on it than in the nineteen years before it, and I have swept it every morning and been on it every morning and I have not once been able to work out whether I have got the worse of it.”

“That is a very large thing to say at seven in the morning.”

“It is not large. It is the only thing I have got this morning and there is no figure on that stone to put it next to.” He set the broom down. “A man who stands on a step and gives three numbers a day for nineteen years is doing one thing. A man who gives three numbers a day and watches people talk on it is doing a different thing, and I have had three weeks to find out which one I would rather be, and I have not. I am not going to find out on this stone, because this stone is a wall and walls are for figures.”

Then the woman of forty-nine came up the road with the ninth run under her arm and entered the morning at about the tenth hour and read it out and said that she had decided something in the last hour which she had decided on Friday, and had told nobody, and was telling the step, and that it was not going in the book.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years in a room four hundred miles off, and holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and has decided to ask one question and has not asked it.

“That is the eighty-third morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing.”

“You decided to ask somebody.”

“I have decided to ask one person one question, once. That is all. It is not a plan and it is not a column and it is not going in the run.” She put the book down flat on the wall and did not open it. “And I am telling a landing full of people before I do it, which is the opposite of how I have done everything for twenty-three years, and I have thought about that for a whole week and I have not found a good reason for it and I have done it anyway.”

“Who.”

“That is exactly the thing I am not saying. If I name him then he has to be ready, and a man who is ready is a man who has been given a warning, and there is a man on this step who got a warning in a different way two weeks ago and came back with nothing and put it on a step in the open on a Saturday. I have read what he said and I am not going to be the cause of the same thing happening to somebody else.”

“Will you say it when you do it.”

“No. I will say it after. Which means that on the morning I do it nobody in this county will know I am asking, and if it goes badly the whole of this landing will have heard about it after, and I have spent twenty-three years being the person everybody could check.” She picked the book up. “And I have worked out this morning that I do not want to be checked on it. I have wanted that for a fortnight and I did not know I wanted it and I am not going to be told it is a strange thing for a woman who keeps a run to want.”

“That is the first thing anybody has said on this step in a fortnight that is about wanting.”

“Yes. And I want it to be noted that nobody has said thank you to me for it and that I did not ask.”

Then the man of thirty-eight came up the cart road at about the ninth hour with a board against his coat and asked the question he had asked twice before, and got the same thing, and then said the other thing, which is that he was going to stop, and did not say what stopping meant.

Garrin Tolley is thirty-eight, has a child of nine, carries a board with thirty-two notches cut in the edge of it in the inside of his coat, and nobody in this county is to count him and the figure of how old that board is is not given, and he has come on the day he named and been refused and has been refused again and is not told he was right and is not told he was wrong.

“I am going to stop.” He said it on the bottom step and did not come up. “I am not going to come up that road again and ask anybody anything, and I am telling you here so that it is not a thing that happens to you one morning and you find out from somebody else.”

“You do not have to say that.”

“I do not have to, and that is why it is worth saying.” He did not take the board out of his coat. “I have got a want. It is the only new want anybody in this county has this year and I am not going to describe it, because a want that gets described on a step gets answered by somebody being kind, and I have watched a man this week be almost kind about his and it was the worst thing I have seen in three weeks.”

“What did you get.”

“Same words. Same voice. Same place. I have asked three people in two counties and got one answer, and I knew the answer before I asked, and I asked anyway. That was not a second asking being a check. It was me wanting to be a man who asks, and you get that from asking, and it does not depend on what comes back.”

He finally looked up at the step. “I have got a boy of nine at home and a thing in the inside of my coat, and on Monday I am going to take it out and put it on a nail in a room, and I am not going to put a day against it, and I am not going to tell anybody why.”

“That is the first thing anybody has said in three weeks that sounds like a decision.”

“It is the end of a habit and it is the worst thing I have ever done and I have not done it yet.” He took a step back. “And nobody is to tell me I was right about the reason I did it and nobody is to tell me I was wrong about it. I have worked it out myself and it is mine and it is not going to be handed back to me in the shape of somebody agreeing with me.”

Then the boy came up out of the lane and gave his figure and said that he had heard the man with the board say he was going to stop, and that he had worked out that stopping and finishing are two different things, and that he had been trying to finish something since Wednesday and could not.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and eighty-seven days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and eighty-seven is nine hundred and forty less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and eighty-seven days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.”

“You have not finished it either.”

“No. Stopping means you are still there tomorrow and whatever it was keeps happening.” He sat on the wall above the drain, which was dry. “That man is going to put a board on a nail and it will be on the nail tomorrow and he will walk past it tomorrow and it will not have a day against it, and he will have to do that every day of his life.”

“That sounds like finishing.”

“No. It sounds like doing the other thing forever with less paper.” He looked down at the two of them. “And I am not saying that to be clever. I am saying it because he has thought about it longer than I have and I do not think he knows what he has just walked into.”

“Do not tell him.”

“I was not going to. I am thirteen and I have been asked for nothing and I am not going to start handing out things to men who are going to put them down.”

Then the man of thirty-one came up and gave his figure and made the last of the proposals that anybody is going to make on that step for a fortnight, and it was the plainest he had made it and it was refused by a man of thirty-eight who had told him four days ago not to thank anybody.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and eighty-six days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and fifty-five days old.

“Three hundred and eighty-six days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and fifty-five days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and forty days old, which is nine hundred and forty less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Eighty wrong things in eighty mornings, and here is the eightieth, and I am not going to say it as a man making a proposal.” He put his hands at his sides. “Ask me once. In the open. Somebody on this step, this morning, and then do not ask me again as long as I stand in this county. One morning about me on that step, given by one person, gone by Tuesday. And then you can all go and do whatever you want with it, and none of it will be checked, and I will have had one morning in this county that somebody said out loud in front of other people.”

“Where do you want me to ask you.”

“Anywhere. Here. It does not matter.”

“Of course it does not. That is what you have wanted for a fortnight and you have just said it out loud on a Friday.”

Garrin Tolley came back up onto the second step, which he had not done, and looked at the man of thirty-one for longer than anybody on that landing had ever looked at anybody about anything.

“Not yet. You have not given a reason.”

“You have not given a reason.”

“I am going to give you a reason and you are not going to like it.” He did not put his hand in his coat. “A man who is asked in front of everybody is not being asked. He is being put on a step. And I am the last man on this step who thought there was going to be anything in it. The only reason you want it is that you have not been able to see yourself in anything for eleven weeks, and you have decided that being spoken about is the same as being in a thing, and it is not.”

“That is my want and you have just described it.”

“Yes. And nobody is going to thank me for describing it, and it is not going to help you, and if it did I would not do it.” He stepped back off the step. “And I have been right about the reason I asked twice and wrong about it in about nine other places, and nobody here knows which is which and neither do I. And the last part. You said nobody could check it. There is no way anybody could check it. That is the whole of what you have been asking me for, and you have just said it again in front of the woman with the ninth run, and she heard it.”

The woman with the ninth run said nothing at all.

“Do not look at her like that,” Garrin Tolley said. “She has not said one word and she is not going to, and I want you to understand that a silence on that wall this morning is not a refusal and is not a consent and is not an answer, and it is the only thing that has been said on this step in three weeks that I would be willing to have my name next to.”

Then Ilyan Vester said the thing he had been carrying up that road for three days and it was not a proposal and it was not an account and it was not a figure, and it was the largest thing he had said in this county since he came up the bank.

“I do not want to be checked. I have said that out loud on a Saturday to a man with a broom and it was refused and it was the right refusal and I am saying it again because a man on that step has told me to say it again and I am not going to insult him by saying it quietly.”

Nobody on that step thanked him for it and nobody told him it was right and nobody told him it was wrong.

“And I am not going to go and be somebody else. I am going to go down this bank with three hundred and eighty-six days in this county and a cut across my hand and eighty mornings of being wrong, and none of that is a use, and it is not going to become one, and I have been waiting three weeks for it to become one, and it has not, and there is no man on this landing who is going to hand it to me, and I have stopped asking them to.”

“You have not asked anybody for two days.”

“No. And I am not going to ask anybody for the rest of the year, and that is not a decision I have made this morning, it is a thing that has been done to me and I have only just found out where it happened, and it happened at the top of that step on Sunday morning with a broom in his hands, and he is not going to hear that from me and he will not hear it from anybody else, because nobody on this step is going to go and thank a man of forty-three for refusing something.”

Nobody went and thanked a man of forty-three for refusing something.

Then the man who moves the chairs came up from the village at about the eleventh hour with nothing in his hands for the third time this fortnight and stood at the low wall and said the whole of what he had come to say, which was a thing about what this landing had, and it was not a figure.

Aldis Rook moves the chairs for Orla Fennimore and keeps nothing and has no stone and no seat and no board, has never once been asked what he did, and works in a passage a hundred and thirty feet long with nine openings in one wall and a wall at the end of it.

“I have moved chairs in that passage for nineteen years and I have never once been asked what I did on any morning, and I have made my peace with it, and I am not here to be asked and I am not here to say it again.” He put his hands behind him.

“I am here because a man on this step has just said he was wrong for the eightieth morning running and another one has said he does not want to be checked and another one has put a board on a nail without a day against it, and between the three of them there is one thing this landing has had in three weeks that it has not had in nineteen years, and I am the only man here who can see all of it and I am not going to name it because naming it would turn it into a rule and a rule is a thing that stops working the minute one person breaks it.”

“You will not say what it is.”

“No. I will say where it is. It is in about nine pairs of ears and it is not in a book and it is not on a stone and there is no line anywhere in this county with a day against it, and on Friday of next week not one person on this landing will remember the half of it, and the person who said the biggest thing will have gone down that bank and been nobody again by Sunday.”

He looked at the eight inches on the step. “And I have spent nineteen years watching people keep things in places where somebody could find them, and this landing has spent three weeks saying things where nobody could put them, and I am the man who moves chairs and I am telling you that is the only thing I have learned since the last time I came up here that is worth a thing.”

“Would you have it any other way.”

“No. And I want that said too, because I have moved chairs in front of a woman for nineteen years who wanted a day against a line for nineteen years and did not get it, and I know what it is costing her, and I am not going to stand here and pretend those are two separate things.” He started down the road. “And nobody on that landing was relieved this week and nobody was thanked for anything, and the bank is at eight inches with six days of it to come back, and there is not one word of this fortnight on any page in this county and there is not going to be.”

# Chapter 903: A Lock And A Pound, And Neither Of Them Has A Stone

Second day of the hundred and fifty-first week. His six hundred and fifty-third morning. Five hundred and three days after the settlement. The fever eighty-eight weeks and four days old. Four hundred and fifty-seven days since the division. Four hundred and three days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Sixteen inches held on the top step at about the seventh hour and it was holding at that figure, with three days of the coming back and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave the three figures and gave the shape of the rest of the week, because there were three days of it left and he says them when there are three.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, has held that landing nineteen years, and gives four figures off one stone without being asked which of them he has and which he has not.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, three days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the second day of the hundred and fifty-first week, and it is a Wednesday.”

Ezekiel Moss said what a lock is and where his is, and said the distance himself, and said it in the way a man says a thing he has said to himself many times and never to anybody.

Ezekiel Moss keeps the lock at the head of a cut on the low side of that drain, about a mile and a bit above the ash, and he took the post before he took anything else.

“It is a mile and a bit up that drain above the ash. That is where the cut takes the water off the bank and that is where the gate stands, and the gate stands because without it the whole of that water comes down the low side at once and takes the wall out.”

“You are not on any of the nine.”

“No. And I would like it said that there are nine of them and there are a great many of me, and I am not saying that as a complaint. I keep a gate. It is a gate and it is on a drain and it has a bar on it and I oil it and I wire the catch when the catch goes loose.”

“What is the catch.”

“The catch is the piece that keeps the gate shut when the water is up. It is the whole of the safety. Everything else on that gate is so that you can get to the catch.”

“You had it loose on the third day of the hundred and forty-sixth week.”

“I had it loose on the third day of the hundred and forty-sixth week and I wired it with three turns, and I have wired it twice since, and I am telling you what I did and I am not telling you what the water was doing, because if I told you that I would be guessing and you would be writing a guess down as though a man had stood in front of it.”

Ruth Ansell came up the cart road with a rope over her arm and put her weight against the low wall while she talked, because talking on that landing had always taken two hands.

Ruth Ansell keeps a pound two hundred yards up that cart road, before the turn, where the road forks for the upper farms, and it is a yard and a wall and a gate and nothing else.

“It is two hundred yards up that road, before the turn. That is the whole distance and I will not be asked it again. It is a yard and a wall and a gate and a roof that came off in a gale one year and has not gone back on.”

“What do you do in it.”

“I write a line. That is what I do in it and I have done it every day since the yard was made. What came in and what went out and whether it was the same animal that went out as came in.”

“And when it is not.”

“Then I say so. A beast goes out that is not the beast that came in, and I say which one it was, and there is a man on this bank who can look at it and say I have got the colour wrong.”

“That is a check.”

“That is me looking at a thing and writing down what the thing looked like. That is not a check and I will not let anybody call it one. The only thing that would check me is somebody standing in that yard at the hour the beast goes out, and nobody stands in that yard at the hour the beast goes out, because the beasts go out at dusk and everybody is eating.”

“And you ask about it after.”

“I ask once. I go up to the shepherd on the upper ground and I ask him where that animal went and I ask him who has it, and I ask him that one time, and he tells me or he does not.”

“And then it is written and I do not go up there again about that animal. If I went up again he would begin to wonder whether I had caught him in something. That is what a second asking does, and I have watched it do it to other people, and I am not going to do it to a man who has walked a long way to tell me where a cow went.”

She stopped, and then she said the other half of it, and she said it to nobody in particular.

“That is not a rule anybody gave me. It is a thing I do because it is the only decent way to ask and because I would not want it done to me.”

Then a man of thirty-one said that the pound and the ninth line were two figures of days and could be set one against the other, and was refused by the woman who kept the pound, and was not told he was wrong.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and forty-nine days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and eighteen days old.

“Three hundred and forty-nine days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and eighteen days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and three days old, which is nine hundred and three less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Forty-three wrong things in forty-three mornings, and here is the forty-third. She keeps a line a day about animals. Barnaby Crove keeps asking for a day against a line about a day. Two figures of days. Set them side by side and I will be able to say something about how long this county has been keeping things.”

“No. They are two figures of days and they are not one figure of days, and putting them side by side makes a third thing, and the third thing is you.”

“They are both days.”

“They are both days and there is no end to that road. The day in her yard is the day a beast came in. The day on that ninth line is a day somebody wrote on a page in a room four hundred miles off. If you set those together you have got a thing where nobody knows which end of it is which, and before the year is out there will be somebody who reads your two numbers as one number and puts them on something.”

“I am not going to put them on anything.”

“No. And I believe you, and that is the part of it that costs me. You will hold them in your head side by side and that will feel like knowing.”

Barnaby Crove asked for a day against the ninth line for the hundred and eighty-second morning and said what he had been doing instead of asking.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for a hundred and eighty-two mornings, which is nine hundred and three less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“A hundred and eighty-two. And I have started doing something else, which I am not proud of, and I am going to tell you what it is so that you can stop me. I have started asking people what they did. That is not what I want and I know it is not what I want, and it is nearer than asking for the day.”

“Why is it nearer?”

“Because I get an answer.”

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and fifty days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and fifty is nine hundred and three less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and fifty days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.”

“Nobody asked me for the count I am keeping. That is forty-two mornings of them and it is the only number I have got that is mine, and a man of fifty-four has just said on that step that he gets an answer when he asks people what they did, and I am thirteen and I do not have anybody to ask and that is not the same as having nobody who would answer.”

Sena Dorr entered today’s line on the ninth run at about the tenth hour and read it out herself on the second day of the hundred and fifty-first week, and did not put a day on the arrangement.

“That is the forty-sixth morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing.”

“The arrangement is four hundred miles off and still answering, and it did not answer, and nobody in this county is in the room where it answers, and no day belongs to it and I am not going to invent one to be pleasant.”

A woman with a rope over her arm had told a man with flour on his hands nothing at all about his oven and had told a man with a bar on his leg everything she had been told about a gate, and neither of them knew that the second thing was the more useful of the two.

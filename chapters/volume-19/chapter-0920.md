# Chapter 920: A Man Who Refused A Question Has Named The Man Who Asked It

Fifth day of the hundred and fifty-third week. His six hundred and seventieth morning. Five hundred and twenty days after the settlement. The fever ninety-one weeks old. Four hundred and seventy-four days since the division. Four hundred and twenty days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, holding at that figure, two days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave the three figures. The fever is ninety-one weeks old this morning and there is no figure of days beside it, and it is the shape of a Saturday, and he has said this once and is not going to say it a second time on the same day.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and has a whole figure this morning and no reason to put a second figure next to it.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, two days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the fifth day of the hundred and fifty-third week, and it is a Saturday.”

“The fever is ninety-one weeks old. There is no figure of days with that and there is not going to be one. I have said this morning’s figures and I am not going to say anything else at all, and anybody who wants to talk to me about what was said on that step this week can talk to me about it any other day of the week.”

Then Aldis Rook came up the cart road at about the eighth hour without being asked to by anybody, and stood at the low wall, and named a man.

Aldis Rook moves the chairs for Orla Fennimore and keeps nothing and has no stone and no seat and no board, and has told the woman he works for the same thing about his mornings every time she has come to the back of that passage, and a man of fifty-four asked him a different question on Thursday evening and got no, and nobody told him he was right and nobody told him he was wrong.

“A man came to the back of my passage on Thursday evening and asked me whether Orla Fennimore had a right account of a morning she had done,” he said.

“One question. That was all of it, and he was quiet about it and he did not stand in the doorway. And I said no. And I am not telling you that to get anything. I am telling you because I said no in private and it has to be said in the open, and because if I had kept it to myself then on Thursday evening I would have been a man who had refused something and he would have been a man who had asked something, and both of those are worse than what happened.”

“Which man.”

“Barnaby Crove. He keeps your gravel. He was not drunk and he was not daft and he did not put his hand on me. He asked it the way you would ask a man for the time.”

For a while nobody on that step said one word, and the water going over the top of the drain could be heard from the bottom step.

“You said no,” said a woman coming down. “You could have said yes. He would have gone away content and it would have been a thing between him and me and you would never have heard another word of it.”

“He could have asked you.”

“He could have asked me. He did not ask me. That is the whole of it and you know it as well as I do, and you have known it since I told him on Tuesday that I would not give it twice.” Aldis Rook did not raise his voice, because he has never in his life had to.

“He asked me whether you had it right, and what he wanted was for me to say yes, and if I had said yes then you would be a woman whose own morning had been agreed with by a man who was in a passage and did not see you do it. And you would never have known. And I would have been the man who agreed with you.”

“You were in the passage.”

“I was in the passage from the front door to the back and there is a wall at the end of it. That is the whole of what I have got about any morning in that house, and it does not reach where she was, and it never will, and I have worked that out on a Wednesday standing at the back of it while a man who is not going to ask me again stood the other side of the wall.”

Nobody on that landing told Barnaby Crove he was wrong, and Barnaby Crove was not on that landing and had not come up, and nobody sent for him.

Then a man of thirty-one said that a man who refused a question had no standing to name the man who asked it, and was told by the man with the chairs that this was the exact opposite of the truth and that the refusal was not a shield.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and sixty-six days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and thirty-five days old.

“Three hundred and sixty-six days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and thirty-five days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and twenty days old, which is nine hundred and twenty less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Sixty wrong things in sixty mornings, and here is the sixtieth. That man has taken a refusal that happened in a passage and carried it up a road and put it on a step with a name on it. If a man says no in private then what happened in private is private, and a woman does not have to be told about a question that was asked for her, and he has taken a thing that was hers and used it.”

“No,” said Aldis Rook.

“You have it backwards and you have had it backwards for a fortnight. A man who asks a second question does not get to keep it to himself. If I had kept Thursday to myself then Barnaby Crove is a man who went quietly and asked, and I am a man who quietly refused, and between the two of us there is a thing on Thursday evening that only two people know about, and the two of us both carry it.”

“It is not hidden. It is just small. And a small thing between two men in a passage is how every bad thing in this county has ever started, and I have moved chairs in that house long enough to know what it looks like before it happens.”

“You have put a man on this landing in a position.”

“He put himself in it on Thursday and he did it to a woman and not to me, and if nobody had come up here this morning then he would have carried it quietly and so would I, and by next week it would be a thing he had once nearly done. I have watched a man nearly do a thing and then do it, four miles down that road, and it started with a wall and a damp that nobody would write down.”

He was not told he was wrong by anybody, and a woman at the top of the steps said *that is the first thing anybody has said on this landing all fortnight that is about something*, and he said *it is not about something, it is about a woman and a passage and me*, and she said *yes, and that is the first time in a fortnight that those three have been in the same sentence*, and nobody thanked either of them.

Wat Marshe came up out of the lane and gave his figure and did not ask for the thing he asked for on Friday, and said why, and it was not because anybody had told him not to.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and sixty-seven days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and sixty-seven is nine hundred and twenty less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and sixty-seven days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up. And I asked this step on Friday for one person to ask me one thing about the dark, and nobody did, and I am not going to ask again, and I am not going to be the one who complains that nobody did, because if I am the one who complains then a person who does it for me has to do it to satisfy a complaint and not because they wanted to.”

“That is a very grown thing to work out on a Saturday.”

“It is a very ordinary thing. I have been doing it since Wednesday.”

Sena Dorr entered today’s line on the ninth run at about the tenth hour and read it out herself on the fifth day of the hundred and fifty-third week, and said what she was going to do with a week in which nothing at all had happened, and she entered it.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years in a room four hundred miles off, and holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and entered a week in which nothing at all happened and it was still a week.

“That is the sixty-third morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing.”

“This week nothing happened. I want that said by somebody who was here for all of it and who has been in the ninth run the whole time. A man asked a woman the same question twice and got the same words. A keeper in a village has decided to open a wall. A woman with a key has found out that half her own book is about herself.”

“A man who moves chairs has come up a road with a name on his mouth. And none of it is on my page, and all of it happened, and I have entered the mornings and the mornings were thin and that is the correct size for a week in which nobody could do anything about anything.”

“Does that bother you.”

“It has never bothered me before and I am not going to pretend it does not now. A clerk whose week is thin is a clerk whose week was thin. I am not going to go back into a week that was fatter because the people in it were saying more, and I am not going to say on Monday that nobody asked me whether my lines were right, because I said it once and I have made my own rule about saying a thing twice.” She closed the book.

“The arrangement is four hundred miles off and it is still answering. It did not answer this week. Nobody in this county is in the room where it answers, and nobody was asked to go, and I did not ask anybody to go, and I have put no day on it, and I am not going to, and I will not be told it would be a kindness to put one there.”

Then Barnaby Crove came up at about the eleventh hour, having been sent for by nobody, and said his figure, and said that he had heard, and that he was not going to apologise for it on that step, and that he was also not going to have anybody else apologise for it either.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for a hundred and ninety-nine mornings, which is nine hundred and twenty less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“A hundred and ninety-ninth. And I heard, and I came up, and I am not going to ask anybody on this step to overlook it, because he is right about the reason I did it, and any regret in it would be about wanting a checked thing, and I do want a checked thing, and I have wanted one every morning since I was a young man and I am not going to stand here and be a different man on a Saturday.”

“Then what are you going to do.”

“Carry it, the way I have carried the other one. He is up there now saying a thing that happened in a passage in the open, and there is no longer any part of that Thursday that belongs to me alone, and I would rather it did not than that it did.” He looked at the full bank for a while.

“And I am not going to ask him again. I said on Tuesday I would not ask her again and I have not, and I said on Tuesday I would not ask him again whatever he said to me, and he said no, and that is the end of that question and I am not going to open it on Monday.”

Nobody on that landing told him he was right and nobody told him he was wrong and nobody thanked the man who had come up the road with a name in his mouth, and the question he had gone to ask stayed open on that step where anybody could walk past it.

The bank held at sixteen with two days of it to come back, and a man had come up a cart road and put a name on a Thursday that nobody else in this county had known about, and the man it belonged to had come up after him and had not asked to be told he was wrong, and a boy of thirteen had worked out on a Saturday that a person who does a thing to satisfy a complaint is not the same as a person who does it, and had stopped asking, and nobody had told him that was right either.

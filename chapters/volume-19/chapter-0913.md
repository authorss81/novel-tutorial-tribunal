# Chapter 913: The Man Of Thirty-Eight Comes On The Day He Named

Fifth day of the hundred and fifty-second week. His six hundred and sixty-third morning. Five hundred and thirteen days after the settlement. The fever ninety weeks old. Four hundred and sixty-seven days since the division. Four hundred and thirteen days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, one day of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave the three figures. The fever is ninety weeks old this morning and there is no figure of days with it, and that is the shape of a Saturday, and it is not a figure of anything anybody did.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and has a whole figure this morning with no figure of days beside it and does not put one there.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, one day of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the fifth day of the hundred and fifty-second week, and it is a Saturday.”

“The fever is ninety weeks old. It has been ninety weeks for a day and a night and I have not been counting anything since Friday, and there is no figure of days to go with that and I am not going to make one up to sit beside it. Whatever it is, it is that, and it does not want a second figure next to it this morning.”

The bank came off the top of the drain at about the seventh hour and went down the steps and was gone before the hour turned, and nobody on that landing gave the man of forty-three a third figure to say, because there was not one.

Then the man of thirty-eight came up the cart road at about the ninth hour with the board out of the inside of his coat and went straight to the pound, where the woman with the gate-key had told him on Sunday what he would get, and he asked her the same question he had asked her on Sunday.

Garrin Tolley is thirty-eight, has a child of nine, and carries a board in the inside of his coat with thirty-two notches cut in the edge of it, which is a figure of what is on a board and not a figure of a person, and nobody is to count him.

Ruth Ansell keeps a pound two hundred yards up that cart road, before the turn, and writes a line a day about what came in and what went out, and did not sit down when he came to the gate.

“Did a black heifer come into your yard on the fifth day of the hundred and fifty-first week.”

“No. Not a beast in.”

“That is it,” he said, and did not move.

“You have written the same words in the same voice in the same place as you wrote them on Sunday,” she said, “and I want you to hear yourself do it, because you told me on Sunday that a second time would tell you what to do with your board and I have watched you since to see whether it does.”

“It does not.”

“No. And I told you on Sunday that it would not, and you came anyway, and I want to say this once and then I am going to the gate. You have two of it now. Two of the same, in the same hand. And they are not two, because there is nobody standing anywhere who was in that yard on that morning except me and I only know what I wrote, and what I wrote is a line saying a beast did not come in.”

“I know that. I worked it out on the road on Monday.”

“Then why did you come.”

He stood in the yard gate with the board against his chest and did not answer for long enough that a dog on the far side of the road stopped barking.

“Because I had it in my coat and I could not not come.”

“That is a reason you can live with. It is not a reason that gets you anything, and I am not going to stand in this gate and soften it for you, because you are thirty-eight and you have a boy of nine and you came up a road on a Saturday morning to be given the same words twice, and the gate is open and you can go down it whenever you like.”

He went down it. He said he would not come back with it in his coat, and she said she was not going to hold him to that, and neither of them was thanked by anybody for either half of what they said.

Then the boy of thirteen was on the wall above the ash and gave his figure and then said one thing without being asked for it and without explaining it.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and sixty days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and sixty is nine hundred and thirteen less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and sixty days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up. And I am not going to say anything else about any count I am keeping, and I have not said anything all week, and I will not start now because there is a man up this road asking people things twice.”

“That is not yours to have an opinion about.”

“I know it is not. I have got one anyway and I am not going to give it to you, and I am telling you that I have got one so that you can go on thinking nobody has.”

Then Barnaby Crove said that he had watched a second asking done and that it was the first thing in a fortnight that had made his own problem smaller rather than larger, and then said what he was going to do about his own problem, which was nothing, and it was nearly the hour.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for a hundred and ninety-two mornings, which is nine hundred and thirteen less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“A hundred and ninety-two. And I watched that man go up to a yard and ask a woman a question she had already been asked, and get the same words, and come away with nothing, and I have to say that it made me feel better, which I did not expect and have not got a use for.”

“Because it worked.”

“No. Because it failed in an ordinary way. He wanted the second one to mean something and it did not mean anything and she told him so before he asked and he asked anyway. That is a thing that can happen to anybody. What I have been doing with the ones I am holding is the same thing except I have been doing it quietly and calling it a decision, and it is not a decision, it is a habit I got into on Wednesday.”

“So what are you going to do.”

“Nothing. I am going to keep asking and I am going to keep not saying, and now I know that is a habit and not a choice, and I would like somebody to come up this road and make me choose one, and I am not going to ask anybody to do that, and if anybody does it I shall very likely go on doing the other thing.”

Nobody on that landing offered him anything and nobody said he was wrong, and the man who holds it said his figure and said nothing else, which was the second day of that.

Then the woman of forty-nine entered the morning at about the tenth hour and read it out, and said that she had sat with the pen afterwards, which she has not said in nineteen years.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years in a room four hundred miles off, holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and sat with the pen in her hand afterwards on a Saturday for the first time in nineteen years.

“That is the fifty-sixth morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing.”

“I sat with the pen in my hand for a while after I had written it this morning. I have entered a line every morning since I was twenty-six and I have never once sat afterwards. I have no idea what I was doing and I am not going to invent a reason for it in front of a step. I will say only that I did not go straight on to the next thing, and that I do not know yet what it was, and if I find out I will not be writing it down.”

“That is the least useful thing you have said in a fortnight.”

“It is the only true one. Take it or leave it, and I am not going to be thanked for it and I would rather not be thanked for it, because I do not know what it is.”

“The arrangement is four hundred miles off and it is still answering. It did not answer this week, and nobody in this county is in the room where it answers, and nobody was asked to go, and I did not ask anybody to go, and I have put no day on it. A thing that does not answer is not a thing with a gap in it. It is a thing that has not answered, and those are different and I am the one who has to keep them different.”

Then a man of thirty-one told the man with the board to put another notch in it, and was refused by the woman he had taken it from a quarter of an hour before, and nobody stopped the man with the board from hearing it.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and fifty-nine days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and twenty-eight days old.

“Three hundred and fifty-nine days in this county, and the cut across this palm is two hundred and twenty-eight days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and thirteen days old, which is nine hundred and thirteen less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Fifty-three wrong things in fifty-three mornings, and here is the fifty-third. He went away with nothing and he will go back down that road with nothing, and if he had come on Wednesday and put a second mark in that board he would have had two of the same answer with a day between them, and that is the shape a thing is supposed to have.”

“No,” said the woman with the gate-key, from the other side of the yard. “I told him on Sunday that a second answer was not going to tell him what to do with it and you have spent the morning telling him what to do with it. You are not a keeper of anything and nobody has asked you about a morning, and if you want to be useful to that man you will tell him to go home, and if you cannot do that then you will say nothing, and both of those are things you could do.”

He was not told he was wrong by anybody else either, and the man with the board went down the cart road at about the eleventh hour with the board still flat against his chest and no new mark on it, and there was nothing on the landing to show for the morning except a line in a clerk’s book and a man walking away from a yard gate with two of the same sentence in his coat.

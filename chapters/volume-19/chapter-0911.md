# Chapter 911: A Bank That Holds And Three Days Of It Running Down

Third day of the hundred and fifty-second week. His six hundred and sixty-first morning. Five hundred and eleven days after the settlement. The fever eighty-nine weeks and five days old. Four hundred and sixty-five days since the division. Four hundred and eleven days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

The bank was still full at about the seventh hour and it had been full since Monday, and the only figure on that stone that had moved in three days was the figure of days of the coming back. Sixteen inches on the top step, three days of it, and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave the three figures and then gave the reason out loud, because a bank that is full does not go anywhere, and a man who says otherwise on the third morning of it starts being a man who is watching himself.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and named a morning on Monday and has said since that he is not going to name another one until the one he has named has happened.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, three days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the third day of the hundred and fifty-second week, and it is a Thursday.”

“I named that morning on Monday and I am not going to name it again. It comes off at the sixth hour on the sixth day of the hundred and fifty-second week, which is a Sunday, and there will be ninety of those steps walkable by the seventh and there will be no figure of inches on it and no figure of steps under. That is the whole of what I know about it and I have said it once. If you have it written down you have a morning and not a figure, and that is the correct size for it.”

Then the woman who keeps the pound came up the cart road with the gate-key still in her hand and stopped at the bottom step instead of going up, and said a thing she had thought about for two days and had not said to anybody.

Ruth Ansell keeps a pound two hundred yards up that cart road, before the turn, and has written a line every day since the yard was made, and none of the lines in that book is about her.

“I have been thinking about my book since Monday,” she said. “Not about the beasts. About the book. And I have worked out that I am not going to start putting what I did in it, and I want to say that out loud on this step before somebody comes up this road and asks me for a morning and then asks me what I am going to do with it.”

“You have not been asked for a morning.”

“No. That is why I am saying it while I still have the choice. A line in that book is a line about what came in and what went out, and anybody who opens it can tell you what my yard did on a day. If I start writing down what I did instead, then the day I open it is a day about me, and about what I thought, and about how long I stood at the gate, and then it is not a book about a yard any more. It is a book with a woman in it.”

“And that would be worse.”

“It would be smaller. There is a difference and I have known it for two days and I have not said it to anybody and I am saying it now.” She turned the key over in her hand.

“And I will tell you why it came to me. Because a man came up this road on Monday with a sheet he wanted to write something on. And I have been thinking since Monday that there is not one of us on this landing who is in a position to write anything down about anything, and there is one man who has said so out loud, and I would have said the same thing in the same words, and I have been keeping a book longer than he has been in this county.”

Corvin Tace did not answer that, and there is a kind of silence on a stone step that is the shape of a man agreeing and not wanting the day to be about him.

Then Barnaby Crove asked her what she did on a morning, once, and she told him, and he did not say it again to anybody, and the reason he gave for not saying it was different from the reason he gave last week.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for a hundred and ninety mornings, which is nine hundred and eleven less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“A hundred and ninety. And I asked that woman what she did on the second day of the hundred and fifty-second week, once, and she told me, and I am not going to say what she told me to a living soul, and I am going to say the same thing about it I said last week and then I am going to say the other half of it, because there are two halves and I only gave you one.”

“Go on.”

“The half I gave you last week was that the moment I say it out loud it is a thing other people have. That is true. The half I have not given you is that I wanted to say it out loud, and I want to say it out loud most mornings now, and every time I want to I have to go through a minute where I am deciding not to, and that minute is getting shorter.”

“Then you should say it.”

“No. And I will tell you why not, and you will not like it. If I say one, then in a week there will be a man on this step who has heard one from me and a man who has not, and the second one will think the first one is something I hand out. It is not something I hand out. There is no giving in it. It is a thing I heard and I am not carrying it anywhere. If I start saying them, the not carrying stops being true and becomes a thing I say about myself.”

He said the figure and then he stood on the step the way he does, which is with his back to the water, so that a man going up the steps has to come round him.

Then the woman of forty-nine entered the morning on the ninth run at about the tenth hour and read it out herself, and said what she had entered and what she had not, and the thing she had not entered was not a small thing and she gave it no smaller than it was.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years in a room four hundred miles off, and holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and came up the road before the light was properly on.

“That is the fifty-fourth morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing.”

“I entered the morning and nothing else. There was a woman on this step this morning saying out loud that she is not going to put what she did in her own book, and that is the most sensible thing anybody has said in this county in a year, and it is not going on mine either.”

“A line about what a person did is not a line about a day. If I enter it I will have a page in the ninth run that is a page about a woman and not about a day, and the person after me will read it as a day, because that is what a run of days is.”

“Then what is it.”

“It is a true sentence that a clerk cannot hold. That is the whole of what I have to say about it and I have been a clerk for twenty-three years and I have never once wanted a true sentence that I could not hold.”

She looked at the book under her arm the way a person looks at something that has never once let them down and is not going to today.

“The arrangement is four hundred miles off and it is still answering. It did not answer this week, and nobody in this county is in the room where it answers, and nobody was asked to go, and I did not ask anybody to go, and I have not put a day on it and I am not going to. A thing that does not answer when it is asked does not get a day put on it by me so that somebody in a book can see that I asked.”

Then a man of thirty-one said that if the five keepers in this county each wrote down one morning of their own working and put the five lines in one place, then the county would have a thing about itself, and was refused by a woman holding a gate-key who had said ten minutes earlier that she was not going to do it.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and fifty-seven days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and twenty-six days old.

“Three hundred and fifty-seven days in this county, and the cut across this palm is two hundred and twenty-six days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and eleven days old, which is nine hundred and eleven less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Fifty-one wrong things in fifty-one mornings, and here is the fifty-first. Five people keep five ordinary things running in this county and not one of the five has ever been asked for a morning, and if each of them gave one morning and the five were put together, this county would have a thing about itself that is not about a bank and is not about a chair and is not about the nine.”

“No. And I have just told you, standing here with the key in my hand, why it would not be a thing. You have asked me for five mornings and you have got one refusal and you have heard the reason, and the reason is that five lines about five people is not a thing about five people and it is not a thing about this county.”

“It is five lines. And you would have it on a morning and you would have read it on a morning, and then in a year there would be a place where somebody could point at it, and the somebody would be you, and I would be a line on it.”

“That is not the same as saying it is not worth anything.”

“No. I did not say that. I said it is not a thing about this county, and if you want a thing about this county then what you want is not five lines and there is no five lines on this landing this morning.”

He did not get anything for having said the thing out loud, and nobody told him it was wrong, and one of the men at the top of the steps said *that is a hard thing to be told by somebody with a key in her hand* in a voice that was not unkind and was not agreeing either.

Then the boy came up out of the lane and gave his figure and did not give the other one, and asked a question nobody answered.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and fifty-eight days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and fifty-eight is nine hundred and eleven less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and fifty-eight days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.”

“I am not going to say the other number to anybody, and I have said that to nobody all week and I am not starting on a morning when the water is up and everybody is standing about waiting for it. I have got a question instead and it is not about my count. Is anybody going to write down what the man of thirty-eight says on Saturday, or is that going to be another thing that happened.”

Nobody answered him, and he went and sat on the wall above the ash with his back to the road, and stayed there past the hour, and did not ask it again.

The bank held at sixteen for a fourth morning after noon and nothing came off it, and a woman had said out loud that she would rather lose a heifer’s worth every year than open her own book to herself, and a man had been told by a woman with a key in her hand that five true sentences make five lines and not a county, and a boy of thirteen had asked a question on that step and had not been answered and had not asked it twice.

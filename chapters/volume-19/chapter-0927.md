# Chapter 927: Sixteen Inches And It Holds, And The Fever Ninety-Two Weeks Old, And A Man With A Book Has Not Opened It In Five Days

Fifth day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week. His six hundred and seventy-seventh morning. Five hundred and twenty-seven days after the settlement. The fever ninety-two weeks old. Four hundred and eighty-one days since the division. Four hundred and twenty-seven days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, holding at that figure, three days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave those three figures, and then said the fever, whole, with nothing beside it.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and has a whole figure this morning and no reason to put a second figure next to it.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, three days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the fifth day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week, and it is a Saturday.”

“The fever is ninety-two weeks old. There is no figure of days going beside that this morning and there is not going to be one. A whole number of weeks is a shape the calendar has and not a shape the stone has, and I have said this once and I am not going to say it a second time today, and anybody who wants to talk to me about it can talk to me about it on a different day of the week.”

“It is the shape of a Saturday.”

“It is the shape of a Saturday, and it has been the shape of a Saturday every seventh morning for a long time now, and nobody on this step has ever come up here on one of those mornings and asked me for a figure of days, and I have stopped expecting it, and I want that said because the not-expecting is the only thing I have got that is worth having.”

Then the man with the book came up the cart road at about the eighth hour with the book in his coat instead of under his arm, and said that he had not opened it in six days, and that he had been carrying it round the house at night looking at it, and had worked out why.

Nevin Trask is sixty-one, keeps a book of heads and carts, has the fifth day of the hundred and fifty-third week twice in his head and one morning before it, and has not opened the book since the seventh and last day of the hundred and fifty-third week.

“Five days. I have not opened it. I have taken it out of the press twice and looked at the two lines that are in it for that week and shut it again, and both times I did it to see whether I wanted to write something.”

“Did you.”

“No, and I want to be exact about why, because I have had six days to make a good reason and I have not got one.” He took it out and held it. “It is not that a person’s morning is too important for my book. It is that my book would not know what to do with it. There is nowhere in it for a man to go who was in a room. Everything in it came in or went out or stood still and got counted, and an oven being shut down did none of those things.”

“So it goes nowhere.”

“It goes in here and it will be in here when I am not able to hold it, and by then it will be a baker’s Saturday with something added to it and I will not be able to tell what.” He put it back. “And there is a worse one and I will say it because you would all get at it inside a week. If I write it down then it is a thing a man can come back to, and a thing a man can come back to is a reason to come back to.”

“And I have wanted to go and stand in his yard, and that is not the same as wanting to ask, and if I go and stand in his yard then in a month I will have asked. I know that about myself and I did not know it a fortnight ago.”

Wat Marshe came up out of the lane and gave his figure and said that he had begun to be frightened, not of anybody asking him, but of the not-asking being the thing that happens to him.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and seventy-four days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and seventy-four is nine hundred and twenty-seven less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and seventy-four days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up. And I have worked out something this week that I do not like.”

“Say it.”

“If nobody asks me, then the not-asking is the thing that happened to me.” He looked along the step and not at anybody. “Everybody on this landing has got a thing that happened to them. I can name what happened to every one of you from here without getting up off this wall. And if nobody asks me about the dark then there is going to be nothing that happened to me and I am going to be the boy nobody asked, and that is going to be how I get talked about.”

“Then ask somebody.”

“No. Because then it is a thing I did to make a story, and it would be a story, and I have not got a story. I have got a thing I did in the dark and nobody has asked me, and that is the whole of it, and I would rather have that than have a story.” He put his hands in his pockets. “And I am not saying that to anybody in a week’s time. I am saying it now because a rule nobody hears is not a rule, and I have had this one since the second day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week and I have not said it out loud in one place.”

Barnaby Crowe came up off the low side and said his figure and said that a man of thirty-eight had told a whole step a thing on the fourth day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week and had said he was stopping, and would not say when, and that he had not asked him a single question about it, and that the not-asking was going to be the hardest week of his life.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for two hundred and six mornings, which is nine hundred and twenty-seven less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and sixth. And I have not asked him one thing about it and he told me to my face that nobody is to hold him to it, and he said that on purpose so that it could not be held to. And there are six mornings coming and I am going to have to walk past that road every one of them and say nothing.”

“You could ask him whether he means it.”

“If I ask him whether he means it then in six days I am standing here telling a whole landing that I went and asked him whether he meant it.” He set the barrow down. “And that would be me, at the end of it, having been the man who asked a man of thirty-eight whether he meant to stop. He would tell me the truth if I asked. That is the whole difficulty and I have worked out about nine times this week that I do not want to know.”

Then the woman of forty-nine came up at about the tenth hour with the ninth run and put it on the wall and read the morning out, and said that a Saturday which is a whole number of weeks old did not get entered any differently from a Wednesday, and gave the reason, and it was the reason she gave for everything.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years in a room four hundred miles off, holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and entered a morning on which nothing on the step changed anything.

“That is the seventieth morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing.”

“Tell us what is in it.”

“Nothing that came in and nothing that went out and a full bank that has already told this landing everything it is going to tell it, and a man with a book who has not opened it, and a man of thirty-eight who has said he is stopping and has not stopped yet because it is a Saturday and the day he named has not come.” She turned a page and put it flat again. “And that is a Saturday. It is not a special Saturday and I did not enter it more carefully and I did not write it a second time in the margin in case it went.”

“It is ninety-two weeks old.”

“It is ninety-two weeks old and that is a figure of a thing in a man and not a figure of a morning, and it is not in this book and it is not going in it.” She shouldered the run. “The arrangement is four hundred miles off and it is still answering, and it did not answer this week, and nobody in this county is in the room where it answers, and nobody has been asked to go, and I have put no day on it and I am not going to put one on it because it is a whole number of weeks old and that is a good enough reason to do nothing.”

Then Ilyan Vester came up and said his figure and said that a man who says an unanswered thing out loud every morning is doing the same thing as a man who asks twice, and was refused on the step by the man who holds the landing in four sentences that gave the working.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and seventy-three days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and forty-two days old.

“Three hundred and seventy-three days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and forty-two days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and twenty-seven days old, which is nine hundred and twenty-seven less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Sixty-seventh wrong thing in sixty-seventh morning, and here is the sixty-seventh. I say that offer out loud every morning of my life in this county and nobody has ever answered it, and a man who asks a second time is doing it to get a different answer out of the same person. I am doing the same thing to nobody. I am putting the same sentence in the same place every morning and putting a figure on it so that it goes up by one.”

“No,” said Corvin Tace.

“You have not said why.”

“Because you have not asked me a question.” He did not stop sweeping. “Asking puts something to somebody. You stand on that step with a figure in your mouth and you are putting it to nobody at all. There is not a man in this county being made to stand there and decide whether you have caught him in something, and that is the whole difference, and I have watched it happen to a man who went up a road twice and I am not going to watch you build it into a rule about yourself on a morning when there is nothing on the stone.”

“Who watched it happen to a man.”

“A man who asked a question twice and got the same words twice and came up here wanting somebody to tell him what that meant.” He went on up the steps with the broom. “And nobody is going to tell him and nobody is going to tell you, and that is not a punishment. It is what happens when you have both got nothing to put it against.”

Nobody on that landing told him he was right and nobody told him he was wrong, and the man with the book went down the cart road with the book shut against his chest, and a boy went up past the ash with a list he was not going to give to anybody, and the fever was ninety-two weeks old with nothing standing beside it.

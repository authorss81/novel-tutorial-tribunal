# Chapter 938: The Thirty-Fourth Time, And Ninety Steps Walkable, And Nobody Said A Figure Of Inches Out Loud

Second day of the hundred and fifty-sixth week. His six hundred and eighty-eighth morning. Five hundred and thirty-eight days after the settlement. The fever ninety-three weeks and four days old. Four hundred and ninety-two days since the division. Four hundred and thirty-eight days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

The water came off that bank at about the sixth hour and all ninety of those steps were walkable by about the seventh, and that was the thirty-fourth time. There is no figure of inches on this morning and no figure of steps under, and there is no figure of days of the coming back either, and that is not a gap in the morning. It is the shape of the morning, and there is nothing else on this landing that wants a figure put on it, and a man said that out loud before anybody asked him for it.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth thing, and on an off-morning says so out loud and then stands there.

“There is no figure of inches this morning. None. I am not going to say one and I am not going to say how many of those steps are under, and there will be no number of days either, because there is none when all ninety are walkable. Nineteen years of getting up at the fifth hour on every one of these has not once given me a fourth thing to say, and I am not going to invent one this morning, and half of you would not know the difference if I did.”

“That is not a gap.”

“It is not a gap, and I want that in about nine pairs of ears before eleven this morning, because on an off-morning a man who is honest looks like a man who has come out early and has nothing. And I would rather that than put a figure on that step that the water did not put there.”

“Not a thing to say, and a thing to do,” said a woman coming up out of the lane with her sleeves already wet to the elbow. “You have ninety steps to sweep this morning instead of one. That is not a figure. It is a job you have got and I have got, and it is the only morning of the week you and I are working the same hours.”

“That is true and I am not going to put a figure on it.”

“Nor will I. I have done about forty of them before the light and I am going up for the rest after the hour, and nobody is going to tell me how many I did, because I did not count and I am not going to start.”

Then the man at the head of the cut came down off the low side on purpose, on an off-morning, when there was no reason on earth for him to be on the water at all, and said that he had come to walk the ninety and check one thing, and had found it.

Ezekiel Moss keeps a lock at the head of a cut a mile and a bit above the ash, has wired the same catch twice since the frost and has refused to wire it a third, and was asked what he did by a woman once and told the truth.

“I have wired that catch twice since the frost and I said on a morning last week that I was not going to wire it a third. And on Tuesday morning I went up and looked at it.”

“And it is still loose. It has been loose since before the frost and it is loose now and I went and stood in front of it for about a quarter of an hour, on a morning with ninety steps walkable and no water on any of them, and I worked out that I can walk up those ninety and come back and look at that catch on the way down, and it will cost me half a morning, and half a morning is a thing I have got on exactly one morning out of seven.”

“Why are you telling this step.”

“Because I told a step last week that I was not going to wire it and a man on that step said a sentence about it and it has been sitting in me for two days and I am not going to be the sort of keeper who decides things in a yard and says nothing.” He set the bar down. “And because there is nothing else to say this morning. There is no figure on that stone for the first time in a fortnight and I have come down off a hill to stand in it.”

“You could go home.”

“I could go home and I would have gone home on any other morning of the week. On this morning the whole thing is walkable and that is the only thing it is, and a man with a loose catch and ninety steps in front of him is a man who has been given an excuse.”

Then the man who moves the chairs came up from the village at about the ninth hour with nothing in his hands again, and said that he had done a thing on Sunday that was his own doing and not hers, and had come up about it.

Aldis Rook moves the chairs for Orla Fennimore and keeps nothing and has no stone and no seat and no board, and has never once been asked what he did, and told this step on the first day of the week that a passage is not about anything.

“I moved a chair on Sunday. The second one from the front on the left. I moved it about a foot and I put it back about a foot the other way and I put it down the way it was, and then I went and stood in the passage and did it again.”

“Why today.”

“Because I could.” He said it without any weight at all. “There is nobody in that house on a Sunday until about eleven and I have moved that chair the way she wants it for eleven days and I moved it the way I wanted it and put it back, and I have known I could do that for nineteen years and I have never done it once, and on Sunday I did it twice.”

“You were going to say something this morning.”

“I was going to say that a man who has been in a place every morning for nineteen years and has never once moved a thing in it has been treating it as a place he is employed at. That is what I have been doing. And the difference between that and keeping something is that nobody can ask me about either.” He looked at the wall. “And I am not going to say that again. I said it once and she does not need it twice and I do not need to have said it once. That is a thing I learned in a passage.”

Nobody thanked him for learning it and he did not expect to be thanked, and he stood there until somebody else came up the road.

Then the boy came up the lane in his boots without his coat on and did not go up the wall, and gave his figure, and said that he had not counted the ninety steps on his way up and had thought about doing it and had decided not to.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and eighty-five days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and eighty-five is nine hundred and thirty-eight less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and eighty-five days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up. And I did not count the steps.”

“All ninety of them walkable and you did not count them.”

“There is a man on that step whose job is counting them and he has counted them every morning for nineteen years and this morning he had nothing to count and that is the whole of his morning.” He sat down on the bottom step in the dry.

“And I have got a figure in my head of every morning I have been in this county and it goes up by one every morning whether I count it or not, and I have been out in the dark counting it for two hundred and eighty-five days and I decided on Friday that I am not counting anything else. That is the end of my counting. It is the steps, or it is his, or it is nobody’s.”

“That is a very grown thing to give up.”

“It is not a thing to give up, it is a thing that is not mine.” He looked at his own hands. “I want to be a person who has a figure and a person who gives that up. And I have worked out that I can only have the first one and I have it already and it goes up by one every morning without me. There is a man on this landing who has been working nineteen years at a thing and a woman who has entered sixty-one mornings and neither of them would survive a day without it.”

Then the man of thirty-one came up and gave his figure and said that he had worked out what he was for, and it was not a use, and it was worse than that.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and eighty-four days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and fifty-three days old.

“Three hundred and eighty-four days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and fifty-three days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and thirty-eight days old, which is nine hundred and thirty-eight less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Seventy-eight wrong things in seventy-eight mornings, and here is the seventy-eighth. There is a man on this landing who has told that whole step there is no figure coming today, and he is right. And every one of us is going to spend today guessing. So I am the one who has been in front of all of it and I am going to say what I think is coming, and it can be checked against the stone on Friday, and if I am wrong then it is on me and not on anybody else.”

“What do you think is coming.”

“Sixteen by Friday and a full bank and about sixty under and five days of it to come back.” He said it in one go, the way a man reads a line out of a book. “And I have been wrong for seventy-eight mornings and I am telling you that now so that the number goes with the name.”

Then the man who moves the chairs said no, from the wall, without raising himself off it at all, and gave the working in about eleven words.

“You cannot ask a man what he thinks is coming and take it, because he does not know, and you know he does not know, and if you take it anyway then in four days it is a figure in this county with your name on the top of it, and when it comes out at sixteen it will be said that you said sixteen. And when it comes out at something else it will be said that you were the man who said it was coming.”

“Then it is a guess with a name on it and I said it was.”

“You said it was. And a guess with a name on it is the only kind of guess in this county that anybody remembers, and you have just made yourself the man who says what the water is going to do on a Wednesday with no figure on the stone. You have done it to be findable. And you will be findable.” He did not move.

“And the woman who holds the landing has gone nineteen years on three figures off a stone and she has never once said a fourth thing out loud, and you have just made her fourth thing out of her mouth, and that is the worst thing anybody has done on that step in three weeks and you did not know it was happening while it was happening.”

“He told you to say nothing.”

“He did not tell me anything. I am the man who moves her chairs.” For the first time that morning there was something in his voice. “And I am going to tell you the thing I have been carrying since Sunday, once. A man on that step gave a fourth thing away for nothing on Sunday. A man on this step has just handed a fourth thing to the whole landing for nothing on Wednesday. Neither of you has a stone, and I move the chairs for the only person on that landing who has got both.”

Then the woman with the key came down off the turn with a barrow and said that she had done the second half of what she announced on Friday, and that it was not enough and she was going to do the other half and not tell anybody when.

Ruth Ansell keeps a pound two hundred yards up that cart road before the turn, has a book in her own hand with a day in it, put a mark in that book on Wednesday that meant she was in her own yard, has decided to stop keeping the pound, and has thrown nothing out of a corner since the day she said so.

“Forty steps and a corner. That is what I have done since Friday and I have told nobody and I am telling you because it is a free morning and it is the only thing I have done.” She tipped the barrow. “I have not written to anybody and I have not asked a price and I have not put my name on anything. And the yard is still a yard and the gate is still shut and the book is still open and there is a day in it for this morning with nothing in it.”

“Then you are still keeping it.”

“I am still keeping it this week. And next week I do not know, and I am not going to pretend to myself that I know, and I am certainly not going to pretend to the woman with the ninth run.” She put the barrow down. “And nobody is to say that is a step. It is not a step. A step is a thing you can go back from, and I have not got a step, I have got a yard and a book and forty-one steps of wall, and I have known since Wednesday morning that the three of them come apart and I have not worked out which one I am going to lose.”

“Will you tell her.”

“I have not told her and I am not going to be the one to go up her road about it, and if she asks me I will tell her the truth on the day and not before.” She looked up the road. “And there is nothing anybody on this step can do with any of that, and I know that, and I have said it out loud so that nobody spends the morning trying.”

Then Barnaby Crove came up at the ordinary pace with the barrow and gave his figure and said that he had not walked up that road at a clip for four mornings and had not asked anybody for four mornings, and had worked out this morning that the fourth morning was the one that told him something.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for two hundred and seventeen mornings, which is nine hundred and thirty-eight less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and seventeenth. And I have not asked anybody for four mornings.”

“You said you were going to go on asking.”

“I said I was going to go on asking and I have, on my own, in my own house.” He set the barrow down in the same place it has stood every morning of the fortnight. “And the fourth one told me something. Two hundred and thirteen I thought I had found out what I was asking for. Two hundred and fifteen I found out nothing changes. Two hundred and sixteen I found out a man can write a person into a byre book. Two hundred and seventeen, this morning, I found out I would have been the same man here if I had never asked anybody anything.”

“That is not true.”

“It is not true and I have said it out loud on a step and I am not taking it back and I am also not going to be at the bottom of that road at the fifth hour tomorrow.”

Nobody on that landing said he was right and nobody said he was wrong, and no figure of inches was given out loud or otherwise, and all ninety of those steps were walkable, and a man of thirty-one had said on a Wednesday what he thought a bank would do on a Friday, and had been told by a man who keeps nothing that he had handed the landing a fourth thing and had not known it while he was doing it.

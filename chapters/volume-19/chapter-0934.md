# Chapter 934: Sixteen Inches And A Full Bank, And The Fever Ninety-Three Weeks Old

Fifth day of the hundred and fifty-fifth week. His six hundred and eighty-fourth morning. Five hundred and thirty-four days after the settlement. The fever ninety-three weeks old. Four hundred and eighty-eight days since the division. Four hundred and thirty-four days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, and that was a full bank, four days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and gave a fourth which was not a figure, and then said the fifth thing out loud once, and said there was no figure of days going beside it.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and this morning has a whole figure of weeks and nothing to put beside it.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour, that is a full bank, four days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the fifth day of the hundred and fifty-fifth week, and it is a Saturday.”

“The fever is ninety-three weeks old. That is the whole of that and there is no figure of days with it and there is not going to be one. A whole number of weeks is a shape the calendar has and not a shape the stone has, and I do not put it beside the three figures. Sixty days is a man being in a place. Ninety-three weeks is nothing anybody in this county can do anything about, and it is said once, on the mornings it is a whole number of weeks, and it is said in the same words.”

“You have said that every time.”

“I have said it three times and I have said it in the same words three times and that is on purpose, because a thing a man says three times in the same words is a rule and a thing he says three times in different words is a man looking at himself.”

Then the man who moves the chairs came up from the village with wet cuffs, on a morning when there was nothing to carry, and said so before anybody asked him why he was there.

Aldis Rook moves the chairs for Orla Fennimore and keeps nothing and has no stone and no seat and no board, and came up a cart road on a Saturday with nothing to carry because he had decided he would.

“There is no carrying today. The woman I work for is not having anybody in and I have come up here anyway and I have got nothing in my hands, and I have noticed in nineteen years that a man with nothing in his hands on a step gets a different morning than a man with something in them.”

“You came to be asked.”

“No. Nobody is going to ask me anything and I have made my peace with that on a Tuesday.” He looked at the full bank. “I have come because on a morning like this one there is nothing to do and I have started going up and down that passage from the front door to the back, and I have worked out this week that the passage is not about anything.”

“You move the chairs.”

“I move the chairs. Twenty-nine of them, twice a day when there are people, and once when there are not. And I have been doing it for nineteen years in a passage a hundred and thirty feet long with nine openings in one wall, and I have believed for most of those years that the passage was where it happened. That the thing everybody in that house is asking about got done in that passage, in that length, by people walking through it.”

“And it does not.”

“It does not. There is a wall at the end of it.” He said it without any weight, which was how he said the things he had been carrying longest. “I got to the end of that on Wednesday and I have been carrying it three days, and it is the stupidest thing I have ever worked out and I have been walking it twice a day for nineteen years.”

“Stupid how.”

“An old man in a village can tell anybody what was in a room. A woman with a key can tell you what came out of a yard. A man with a board can tell you which days he went and did not. A passage is the only thing on that landing that has no man in it at all, and I have been in it twice a day for nineteen years, and I am the only person in this county who could tell anybody anything about it, and there is nothing in it to tell.”

Nobody thanked him for working that out and he did not appear to have expected anybody to, and he said so himself on the way down the road.

Then the boy came up and gave his figure and then said the other thing, which was that he had been asked for it twice this week by two people who were not him and he had said no to both, and had worked out why he wanted to say yes to one of them.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and eighty-one days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and eighty-one is nine hundred and thirty-four less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and eighty-one days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up. And two people have asked me for it this week and I have said no to both, and I am going to say which one I wanted to say yes to, because I have decided that if I do not say it I will think about it for a week.”

“You do not have to.”

“No, and that is the other half of it.” He turned round so he was facing the wall. “It was a woman who keeps a key, in a yard, and she said she was not going to write it anywhere, and I nearly said it. And I did not, because I had already said it to a man in a passage and he carried it up a road and put a name on it, and if I say it in three weeks to a woman in a yard then somebody has it three times and it is in three places.”

“That is very careful for thirteen.”

“It is not careful. It is arithmetic. I have got one figure and it is mine and it is not about anything and it is on no page, and the minute I hand it about it stops being mine and becomes a thing that is true, and then somebody can look at me. I do not want to be looked at. I want a figure.” He sat down. “Nobody is to thank me for saying no. It is the easy direction.”

Then Barnaby Crove came up the road and gave his figure, and said that he had found out this morning what a checked thing is worth, and that it was nothing, and that he was going to keep going anyway, and he gave no reason at all.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for two hundred and thirteen mornings, which is nine hundred and thirty-four less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and thirteenth. And I asked nobody this morning and I did not walk past anybody, and I did not walk up this road at a clip either. I came at the ordinary pace for the first time in two hundred mornings and it took me a quarter of an hour longer and I have thought about every one of those two hundred and thirteen and I am not going to say what I have thought about them.”

“You have not?”

“You asked me for nothing and I am not going to hand out a morning of work for nothing.” He set the barrow down. “Here is one thing about me and not about anybody else and you will all call it a speech. Two hundred and thirteen mornings and I have not once got a checked thing, and this morning I worked out that I did not want a checked thing. I wanted to stop being the only one. Those are not the same, and I have had the second one the whole time, and it took me two hundred and thirteen mornings to find out I had it.”

“And you are going to go on asking.”

“I am going to go on asking. I decided that a fortnight ago and I have said it out loud and I am not going to take it back because a boy worked out something clever on a Friday.” He picked the barrow up again. “And nobody is to say I am glad about it. I am not glad. I am two hundred and thirteen and I am still asking and the only thing that has changed is that I know what I am asking for, and that is not the same as having it.”

Then the woman of forty-nine came up, entered the morning on the ninth run at about the tenth hour, read it out herself, and said that this was a week in which she had been asked for a column twice and had said no twice and had entered every morning anyway, and that entering them was the only thing she had done all week.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years in a room four hundred miles off, and holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and entered a week in which nothing at all happened.

“That is the seventy-seventh morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing.”

“Seventy-seven. What is the seventy-seventh for.”

“Nothing. That is what a morning in front of anybody is for.” She closed the book on her thumb. “It is not for me. It has never been for me. A run that a clerk can read in twenty years is a run that was worth keeping, and whether the seventy-seventh is for her is the one thing I have never once been able to work out and I have stopped trying in the last week.”

“Have you.”

“I have stopped trying and I want that written down as a decision and not as a woman giving up at forty-nine.” She put the book under her arm. “Because it is not giving up. It is that there are two women in this county who keep a day a day and neither of them has ever once been asked by the other whether the day is right, and one of them is me and one of them keeps an empty yard, and I am not going to say her name on this step, and I am also not going to pretend this morning that I do not know she has decided to stop.”

“Have you asked her.”

“No.” She started up the road. “No. And that is the thing I have wanted to say all morning and I have said it, and it is the first time in twenty-three years I have wanted to say something about the work and there is nobody on this step who can do anything with it.”

Then Ilyan Vester said his figure, and stood on the bottom step for a while with his back to the water, and said the thing that had come out of him, and cut it off in the middle, and gave the reason, and the reason was not the true one.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and eighty days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and forty-nine days old.

“Three hundred and eighty days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and forty-nine days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and thirty-four days old, which is nine hundred and thirty-four less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Seventy-four wrong things in seventy-four mornings, and here is the seventy-fourth. There was a morning in a yard in another county before I came up this road, and I was standing at the front of it, and I have not told anybody what I thought was happening, and I am going to—”

He stopped. Two of the steps went past.

“I am not going to say why I stopped and it is not that I do not trust you.”

“Take as long as you want.”

“There is no time I want to take.” He put his hands in his pockets and took them out again. “I have got nothing to do this morning. That is what stopped it. If I had a wall to open or a book to enter or a ditch out of this county, I would have got to the end of that sentence in about a minute. I am not carrying it because it is heavy. I am not carrying it because I have nothing else to do with it and I would have got round to it by Wednesday.”

The man of forty-three said that a man who has nothing to do with a morning will find something to do with it, and had been finding things to do with mornings for a fortnight, and did not say what any of them had been, and went and swept the top step.

Nobody on that landing told him he was wrong, nobody told him he was right, nobody told him why he had stopped, and the bank stood at sixteen inches with four days of it to come back, and the fever was ninety-three weeks old with nothing beside it, and a man of thirty-one had got to the middle of a sentence on a Saturday and had run out of reasons halfway through the reason.

# Chapter 942: Sixteen Inches And A Full Bank, And A Woman With A Pound Has Taken A Gate Off Its Hinges

Sixth day of the hundred and fifty-sixth week. His six hundred and ninety-second morning. Five hundred and forty-two days after the settlement. The fever ninety-four weeks and one day old. Four hundred and ninety-six days since the division. Four hundred and forty-two days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, and that was a full bank, four days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and stopped at three, and everybody on that step has now seen what a full bank looks like on the stone twice in three weeks and both times it was on a Sunday.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and this morning has said three and no more.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour, that is a full bank, four days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the sixth day of the hundred and fifty-sixth week, and it is a Sunday. That is a full bank and I have said it is a full bank and I am not going to say anything about what a full bank means, because a man on this landing has had a fortnight of the step turning into a room and I am not going to help him with it.”

“Nothing goes on the stone this morning.”

“Nothing goes on the stone any morning. Go and do your work.”

Then the woman who keeps a pound came down off the turn with her sleeves rolled and one bare forearm up to the elbow and salt in the creases of it, and did not say anything for a while, and then said what she had come down to say, which was a sentence about a gate.

Ruth Ansell keeps a pound two hundred yards up that cart road before the turn, has a book in her own hand with a day in it, put a mark in that book on the second day of the hundred and fifty-fifth week that meant she was in her own yard, has decided to stop keeping the pound, and this morning has taken the gate off its hinges and stood it against the wall of her own yard.

“I have not sold it and I have not given it away and nobody has been told anything. The gate is off and it is standing against my own wall and the drain is still running and the wall has not moved an inch and there is still a yard in there.”

“You could shut it with a bar across.”

“I could bar it. And the day I bar it, somebody will come and tell me I have not really stopped keeping it, and they will be right, and I will have to come off the bar again, and that is a shape I have already been round once this year.” She wiped her hands down her apron. “So I have done the only part that is mine. The gate is off. What comes next is not a thing that happens on a step.”

“You said on Friday that you did not know what you were going to do next week.”

“I did, and I do not, and I have worked out in the last two days that those are two different sentences and I have been using them as one.” She looked up at the sixteen inches. “On Friday I meant I had no plan. I have got no plan now either. What has changed is that I have stopped saying I have no plan as though it were an excuse for having one.”

“You are going to sell it.”

“I have told you three times this morning that I am not going to tell you, and you have asked me the same thing in three different shapes, and the answer is the same shape back: no, not today, and not in front of these people.” She did not raise her voice. “There is a difference between a thing being mine and a thing being decided, and I have spent twenty-two years confusing the two because the second one is easier to say out loud at a gate. I have got a gate off a wall and that is all I have done.”

She wiped her hands down her apron again.

“Anything else and somebody will write it down and come back in a year and tell me I said it.”

Nobody wrote anything down.

And then the man who keeps no stone at all came up the cart road with a wet sleeve and said one sentence to her before he got to the step, and everybody on that landing turned round, because the man who moves chairs does not talk to keepers on his way past.

Aldis Rook moves the chairs for Orla Fennimore and keeps nothing and has no stone and has no seat and no board, has never once been asked what he did, and came up the road on Saturday against what he had been told and is coming up the road again on the Sunday because there is nowhere else he has to be.

“I am not going up there,” he said. “I have come as far as the wall. Go on.”

“You have been told not to come up.”

“I have, and I have come as far as the wall on Saturday and I have come as far as the wall this morning, and if you want to say something to me you can say it where you are standing, and if you do not then I am going to stand here for a while and go back down.”

The woman with the gate looked at him for a while and then said that he was the only thing on that road that morning she had not told to go away, and that this was not a compliment and was not going to be treated as one.

“That is all right,” he said. “I have not come up here for a compliment and I have not got one to give and I have not come up here to be given one.” He did not move from the wall. “I am going to stand here until the water goes off the step or until somebody tells me to go down, and I know which of those two is going to happen first and it is not the first.”

Nobody on that step answered him, and he stood at the wall with his hands behind him for a while, and then went back down the cart road at the same pace.

Then the boy came up out of the lane and gave his figure, and said that he had been at the top of the turn before the light and had watched a woman carry a gate up two hundred yards by herself in the dark, and that he had not gone and helped, and that he was not going to explain why and that somebody was going to ask him anyway.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and eighty-nine days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and eighty-nine is nine hundred and forty-two less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and eighty-nine days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.”

“You stood at the top of the turn and watched her.”

“I stood at the top of the turn and watched her and I could have run down and got the bottom of it, because I am quicker than her on a hill and she knows I am.” He turned round and looked at the low side of the drain, which was dry. “And I did not, because if I had carried that gate then she would have had to say thank you to me on a morning when she has taken a gate off a wall, and then the gate would be a thing that had happened between us instead of a thing she had done.”

He scratched the back of his head with one hand.

“There is not one thing in this county worse for a person than being thanked for something they did on the morning they stopped.”

“That is a very clever thing for a boy to have worked out.”

“It is not clever, it is arithmetic. She is doing a thing nobody asked her to do and she has not got anybody to thank. If I turn up with the bottom of the gate she has got somebody to thank, and then the gate is not a thing she did, it is a thing that happened between us.” He sat down on the wall above the drain. “And I have already got a figure of my own that nobody knows and I am not going to give it to anybody to make that gate easier.”

Then the man of thirty-one came up and gave his figure and said out loud that the pound was not a pound, and the woman who keeps it answered him in one sentence and then gave the working, which is what she does.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and eighty-eight days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and fifty-seven days old.

“Three hundred and eighty-eight days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and fifty-seven days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and forty-two days old, which is nine hundred and forty-two less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Eighty-two wrong things in eighty-two mornings, and here is the eighty-second, and it is small. With the gate off, that is not a pound. That is a yard with a wall round it and a drain that runs, and anybody in this county can call it what they like now.”

“No I cannot,” she said, and she did not look at him. “You can call it what you like from up there. I have to shut a gate. That is the whole of what a pound is and it is not the wall and it is not the drain and it is not the intention. It is a thing that closes.”

She turned her palms out once and let them drop.

“The day I stop being able to close it, it stops being mine, and I am not going to stand here and be told by a man who keeps nothing that I have already got rid of something, when what I have done is take the hinges off so that I can find out whether I can put them back on a bad day.”

“You could put them back on a bad day.”

“I could put them back on a bad day and that is the only reason I have taken them off, and it is mine, and you have not asked me for it.” She turned to the step. “And there is a man who asked me to put a person in a line this week and has not read it back, and I would say to you what I would say to him, and it is that a thing you have done in the dark is not a thing anybody else is allowed to describe. Not even to say it has stopped being what it was.”

Nobody on that landing agreed with her and nobody disagreed, and sixteen inches was on the top step and holding at that, and a gate was leaning against a wall two hundred yards up the cart road where the man who put it there could reach it if she changed her mind, and she had not told anybody which day she might.

# Chapter 919: A Man Has Decided To Open A Wall And Knows What It Will Cost Him

Fourth day of the hundred and fifty-third week. His six hundred and sixty-ninth morning. Five hundred and nineteen days after the settlement. The fever ninety weeks and six days old. Four hundred and seventy-three days since the division. Four hundred and nineteen days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, holding at that figure, three days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave the three figures and said that a bank which had been full yesterday and is full today is doing the only thing a full bank can do, which is nothing, and that he is not going to make a speech about it on the second day of it.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and has found that the second day of a thing is where this landing starts inventing.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, three days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the fourth day of the hundred and fifty-third week, and it is a Friday.”

“A bank that was full yesterday and is full today has told you nothing new. That is what a full bank is for. Half the things said on this step yesterday are about yesterday, and I am not going to sit here and be a second-day monument to a first-day fact.”

Then the woman of forty-nine entered the morning at about the tenth hour and read it out, and said something about her own thinness that she had noticed in her own handwriting and would not put a figure on.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, has read for a living for twenty-three years in a room four hundred miles off, and has written the thinnest lines in a long time this week.

“That is the sixty-second morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing.”

“My lines this week have been the thinnest lines I have written in a long time, and I have been reading them back and they are all the same shape, and I am not going to tell you how many days that is because the figure of them is not mine to hand out and I do not want it in a mouth.”

“Why ever not.”

“Because the moment somebody on that step has a figure of how long this county has been going on like this, the figure becomes a thing, and a thing can be held up against a morning and found wanting, and then somebody will do something about it. And there is nothing in this week that anybody can do anything about.”

“That is what the week has been. It has been about ten days of people saying true sentences to each other and nobody being able to do a single thing with any of them, and my book is thin because the week is thin, and that is the correct size for both.”

“Then what did you put in it.”

“The morning. Every morning. I have entered every morning of this week and nothing else, and I have not written down one thing anybody said on this step, and I have been the clerk of this week, and the clerk of this week has had a very quiet week, and that is not a complaint about anybody on the step.”

“It is a complaint about the arrangement, which is four hundred miles off and still answering, and which did not answer this week, and nobody in this county is in the room where it answers, and nobody was asked to go and I did not ask anybody to go and I have put no day on it.”

Then a man of thirty-one said that the only thing anybody on that landing had done this week that was not about a person was a keeper in a village deciding to open a wall, and was told by a man with flour still on his hands that it was about him and always had been.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and sixty-five days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and thirty-four days old.

“Three hundred and sixty-five days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and thirty-four days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and nineteen days old, which is nine hundred and nineteen less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Fifty-nine wrong things in fifty-nine mornings, and here is the fifty-ninth. Nine days of people saying what they did and nobody doing anything, and one man four miles up that road going to take a wall down with his own hands in the middle of it. That is the first thing all fortnight that has been about a thing and not about anybody.”

“It is about me,” said Pol Dunnage. “It is entirely about me, and you have made it into a public act by saying it in front of a landing, and I would have done it quietly in a week. I am going to open that wall because I am tired of deciding not to, and that is a thing about me, and if you go up that road on Sunday and find my oven out you will find a man in a flour sack and not a thing in this county.”

“You would get bread sooner with the damp.”

“No, and you know why not, and you said the same thing to me on Monday about an ordinary keeper and a keeper of one of the nine, and I gave you the difference then. The damp is in the wall and not in the flour and I have known that for thirty years, and there is no decision in it. It is just a wall that has got into a state and a man who is tired of waiting for it to leave.”

He was not told he was wrong and nobody said he was right, and the man of thirty-one got nothing out of it but the hour.

Then Pol Dunnage said the rest of it out loud, because he had come up the road with a cold chisel in his coat and everybody on that step could see it, and nobody was going to be able to unsee it.

Pol Dunnage keeps the bakehouse four miles up that cart road, in the village the road goes through, and is the second house on the left after the wall, and has not been asked by anybody on that landing for anything at all.

“I have brought the chisel up here on purpose so that it is on this step and not hidden in my coat, and I am going to tell you what opening it will cost me so that if it goes wrong everybody on this landing knows it was not a surprise to anybody but me.”

“How bad.”

“My oven will be out on Sunday and it will be out until the middle of next week, and I will bake on a temporary fire in the far corner with half the flour I can get and the loaf will be worse and the village will say so, and I will lose bread.”

“And on Saturday I will find out what is actually in the wall, and it may be a course of stone that costs me more than the damp did, and I do not know which it is, and I have decided to find out tomorrow rather than carry it to the end of the year.”

“That is a decision about a wall.”

“It is a decision about a man of forty-one years who has been carrying a wall about since the frost and has got nothing to show for it, and I would rather know what is in it. Nobody here is to tell me I am bold and nobody here is to thank me. I have not done a bold thing. I have done a late thing.”

Wat Marshe came up out of the lane and gave his figure and then asked for the one thing he has ever wanted from anybody in this county, and asked it plainly and to the step and not to a person, and nobody gave it to him.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and sixty-six days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and sixty-six is nine hundred and nineteen less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and sixty-six days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up. And I want somebody in this county to ask me about the thing I did in the dark, once, out loud, and not to put it anywhere afterwards.”

“That is all I have ever wanted.”

“That is not true, it is the second thing, the first thing is that nobody writes it down.” He looked along the step and at the water and did not look at anybody.

“One person. Once. And then it is gone. I have had it in my head for a fortnight and it has not gone anywhere and I would rather it had been said once in the open air and then been nobody’s, and I am not going to name the person, because if I name the person then it is a thing I asked that person for, and I do not want it to be a thing I asked anybody for. So I have said it to the step.”

Nobody on that step asked him, and two people said *no* out loud for him, and one of them said *that is not a thing you ask for at thirteen*, and he said *I know that is why I asked*, and he went up past the ash and did not come down again that morning.

Then Barnaby Crove came up last and gave his figure and said that he had asked himself a question about a morning that was not his, and had answered it, and that the answer had cost him more than the morning had.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for a hundred and ninety-eight mornings, which is nine hundred and nineteen less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“A hundred and ninety-eighth. And I asked myself this morning whether the morning she gave me on Thursday is mine, and I sat down on the step to do it properly, and I answered, and it is not.”

“That is not a discovery. You told us on Thursday it was not.”

“I know what I told you on Thursday. What has happened since Thursday is that I have caught myself starting to think of it as something I am doing for her. That is a different thing and it is worse and it took me a whole morning to catch it. On Thursday I was holding a thing that was hers.”

“This morning I was holding a thing I was doing for her, and there is a man on this landing who would call that the same thing and he would be wrong, and I could not tell you in what it is wrong except that in one of them she is carrying it and in the other one I am.”

“Will you ask her for it back.”

“No. And I want to say that plainly because somebody will think it. She gave it and it is hers and she has not got it, and I am not going to go back up there this week and say *give it back*, because the moment I do that it is a transaction and she did not make a transaction. She gave a morning to a man who asked her for it and she is not obliged to have it again. I am going to go on holding a thing that is not mine and I am not going to pretend otherwise on a Friday.”

Nobody thanked him and nobody said he had it right, and a woman coming down the steps said *that is the first sensible thing anybody has said about a person on this landing in a fortnight*, and he said *it is not sensible, it is just late*, and the water stood full and did not move.

The bank was still full at about the eleventh hour and would be full tomorrow and the day after, and a man had said out loud that he would lose bread on a wall on purpose, and a boy of thirteen had asked a whole step for the one thing he wanted and had been told no twice, and a man of fifty-four had caught himself about to make a thing of somebody else’s morning and had said so out loud before anybody else worked out what he was doing.

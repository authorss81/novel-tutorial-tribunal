# Chapter 937: First Day Of The Hundred And Fifty-Sixth Week, And A Baker Has Found Lime He Cannot Check

First day of the hundred and fifty-sixth week. His six hundred and eighty-seventh morning. Five hundred and thirty-seven days after the settlement. The fever ninety-three weeks and three days old. Four hundred and ninety-one days since the division. Four hundred and thirty-seven days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, and it held at that figure, one day of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and then answered a thing he had promised on the step on Monday, and the answer was that he had been right that he could not know.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and confirmed on the first day of a week that he could not tell them in advance what it would bring.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, one day of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the first day of the hundred and fifty-sixth week, and it is a Tuesday.”

“I told them on Monday I would not be able to tell them in advance what this week would bring, and I am telling them now that I was right that I could not, and that is the whole of it. The bank is sitting at sixteen with a day to run, and I am not going to tell you what tomorrow is, and if I could I would not say it, because on Monday I said three figures and I did not say sixteen and one, and if I had said sixteen and one somebody would have gone away pleased and would have written down that I could do this.”

“So you cannot do it.”

“I can do it for one day. If I could do it for a week I would not be standing on this step at the fifth hour, and the man who could do it for a week would be a man who had a thing in him I have not got.” He put the broom down. “And that is the whole answer and I am not going to be asked about it again until Saturday.”

Then a man came up the cart road with a cold chisel in his coat, which he had put there on purpose on Friday so that it would be on that step and not hidden in a yard, and said that he had taken out the first course on Sunday, and that he has found something in the back of the wall.

Pol Dunnage keeps a bakehouse four miles up that cart road, in the village the road goes through, has decided to open a damp wall, and has already paid for it with an oven and half of what flour he can get.

“Sixteen and it holds and one day, and I have not got to my own three figures yet, because I have not been to my own oven since Saturday and that is what the wall is and I am not going to stand on that step this morning and pretend it is not.”

“Go and do the oven.”

“No. Because if I go and do the oven this morning then it is Friday and not now, and if it is Friday then I have told nobody anything, and I have been carrying the thing I found on Sunday up four miles of road with a chisel in my coat for two days and my back has gone on it.”

He put his hand on the low wall, not the landing wall, a wall he had no business touching. “There is lime in the back of it. Not the lime we use. Not the stuff you buy by the bag for a wall. Old lime, put on by hand, and it has gone off and it is still holding, and I have been looking at it for two days and I cannot tell you whether it is good.”

“You are a baker. You know lime.”

“I know lime I have bought and I know lime I have watched a man mix.” He took the chisel out and held it up and did not hand it to anybody. “That is the whole of what I can say and it is not enough and it is the first time in twenty-two years I have stood in front of my own work and not been able to say whether it was any good. And the wall is mine. It is not the parish. It is not the bank. Nobody has any standing in it but me and I am the man who cannot check.”

“What would a man check it with.”

“Another man who had built the wall, and there is not one, and there is not going to be one, and he is not in this county or any county, because everybody who would know has been dead longer than I have been selling bread.” He put the chisel back in his coat. “So there is a thing in this county I cannot check about my own two hands, and it has been in that wall since before I bought the bakehouse, and it will come out on Tuesday and it will be dust by Thursday.”

“Will you take it all out.”

“Yes. And I am telling that step what I found and not writing it anywhere, and there is a woman on this landing who has spent a fortnight refusing to have a column put in a book and she is right and I am going to say so out loud this morning because she has not had anybody agree with her since she said it.”

Nobody agreed with anybody else. Then the boy came up the lane and gave his figure, and said that he had worked out overnight why he had got up at the second hour on Monday and given it to nobody, and that it was because a number stops being his the second he is thanked for it.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and eighty-four days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and eighty-four is nine hundred and thirty-seven less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and eighty-four days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up. And I have got it. That is what got me out of bed on Monday. It is not that he did not ask. It is that if he had asked I would have been thanked, and a figure of mornings in the mouth of a man with a broom is not a figure of mornings any more. It is a thing a man with a broom has been given.”

“That is not nothing.”

“It is not nothing and it is worse than nothing, because nothing is still mine and something is a thing that two people hold.” He sat down on the wall above the drain and did not take his elbows off his knees. “And I have decided it is a rule now and not an opinion. If I ever give it to anybody, then I am not going to be thanked and they are not going to call it a fine figure, and if anybody does either of those things then I take it back and it is mine again. That is the whole of my arrangement, and it is not a sad arrangement.”

“It is a very hard thing to work out on a Tuesday.”

“I have had since Sunday and you all keep saying the day. Stop saying the day.”

Then the woman of forty-nine came up and entered the morning on the ninth run at about the tenth hour, and read it out, and said that she had been in a yard on the low side for twenty minutes the previous afternoon with a woman who keeps a key, and that neither of them had said anything, and that it had been the closest two people have come on that landing in a fortnight without anybody asking either of them anything.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years in a room four hundred miles off, and holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and has now stood in a yard with the woman who keeps a key without asking her a question.

“That is the eightieth morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing.”

“Twenty minutes in a yard.”

“Twenty minutes. And I did not ask her what she did and she did not ask me what I did, and we did not talk about the book or the pound or a single thing anybody on this step would call being close.” She closed the book on her thumb. “And I have to say the rest of it, because I am the one who has been saying it all fortnight. It was not a check and not an agreement and it settled nothing, and neither of us moved a step towards the other. If you wanted a morning where two people stood in a yard and said nothing and it counted, that is what it was.”

“Does she know you said that.”

“She knows I was in her yard. She does not know what I have said up here and she is not going to be told, and that is the first arrangement I have made with anybody since I came to this county and I made it in about four seconds.” She got the book under her arm.

“And the arrangement is four hundred miles off and it is still answering and it did not answer this week, and nobody in this county is in the room where it answers, and I have put no day on it and I am not going to, and this week that has been in it is a week I spent in a yard with my hands in my pockets.”

Then the man of thirty-one came up and said his figure, and said that he had found the one thing in this county he could actually be useful for in a fortnight, and that it was nothing, and he gave it to the man with the chisel as a gift and was refused.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and eighty-three days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and fifty-two days old.

“Three hundred and eighty-three days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and fifty-two days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and thirty-seven days old, which is nine hundred and thirty-seven less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Seventy-seven wrong things in seventy-seven mornings, and here is the seventy-seventh. That is the answer. The man in front of me has a wall he cannot check and there is nobody who could check it. Write it down. What he did, and when, and what came out of it, and where he has got to. He stops on Thursday, somebody carries on, and there is a thing on that step saying how far he got.”

“No,” said Pol Dunnage.

“You are going to lose the part of the wall you do not know and somebody will have to stand in the hole.”

“Yes. And nobody is going to write down where I got to, and the reason is not that the offer is bad. I put a cold chisel in my coat on Friday and came up four miles on purpose so that it would be on this step and not in a yard. And now I am going to do a thing to a wall and keep it to myself, and I am going to do it on purpose, because a line made of me is not a record of me either. It is just a different set of people asking me what I did.”

“That is what a record is.”

“That is exactly what a record is and you have said it as though it settles something.” He did not soften it. “In a year the line will say that I opened a wall and how far, and it will say it perfectly, and nobody will ever open that book. And on the day I stop, there will be a moment when the last stone is out and there is no line and nobody on this landing will be able to tell anybody how far I got. And I am going to let that happen.”

Ilyan Vester said that the man in front of him was right, in the plainest words he had, in front of the step.

Pol Dunnage looked at him for a while and did not say thank you and did not say that it was the first thing anybody had got right in three weeks, and said that he had got it from a man in a village a fortnight ago and had not thanked that one either, and went and put his chisel down on the step of a man who did not ask him and went home to an oven he had not lit since Saturday.

Then Barnaby Crove came up at the ordinary pace and said his figure and said that he had been on that step at the third hour that morning, in the dark, on the first morning of a new week, and that he had worked out he had never once done it.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for two hundred and sixteen mornings, which is nine hundred and thirty-seven less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and sixteenth, and it is the first morning of the hundred and fifty-sixth week, and I was on that step at the third hour because a new week opens at the fifth hour and I have stood here at the fifth hour every time one has opened, and this morning I was here two hours before it did and there was nobody on it and there was nothing on the stone but whatever the night left.”

“Did you get a figure.”

“I did not and that is why I am telling you. I have been up that road at the fifth hour for two hundred and sixteen mornings with a figure of days in my mouth, and this morning I was standing up there in the dark at the third hour with nothing in my mouth at all, and I have worked out on the way up that I do not know what I am if I am not carrying it, and I have stood on this step at the fifth hour every morning for two hundred and sixteen mornings and I would rather have had the nothing.”

“That is a thing to work out on a Tuesday.”

“It is a thing I have worked out and I am not going to be thanked for it and I am not going to be told it is a bad thing either.”

Nobody on that landing said he was right and nobody said he was wrong, and a week began with three figures that nobody had been told and a wall coming out one course at a time in a village four miles up that road.

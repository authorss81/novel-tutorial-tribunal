# Chapter 918: A Full Bank, And A Man Now Carrying Somebody Else’s Morning

Third day of the hundred and fifty-third week. His six hundred and sixty-eighth morning. Five hundred and eighteen days after the settlement. The fever ninety weeks and five days old. Four hundred and seventy-two days since the division. Four hundred and eighteen days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Sixteen inches stood on the top step at about the seventh hour and that was a full bank, with four days of the coming back and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave the three figures and said the word for it out loud, because a bank that is full is a different animal from a bank that is high, and this landing has found that out the hard way twice.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and says which of his two words he means before anybody has to ask him.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and that is a full bank, four days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the third day of the hundred and fifty-third week, and it is a Thursday.”

“That is a full bank and not just sixteen. It means it will hold at sixteen and it will not go over, and it will come off on the day it comes off whatever the day does.”

“Two days ago I said on this step that a bank that is full does not go anywhere, and that was a true thing about this morning, and if I had said it on Saturday it would have been a true thing about Saturday. I am careful about it because I have been wrong in this county before and it took me nine years to stop being the man who was wrong.”

Then Barnaby Crove came up and asked the woman who holds an account of what somebody else did what she did, once, and she told him, and from that hour the morning was not the same kind of morning this landing had been having all week.

Norah Stannard is forty-six, keeps nothing, holds nothing, is on no roll and is nobody’s clerk, and has been holding one account since the seventh and last day of the hundred and fiftieth week, which is what a man said to her at the head of a cut.

“You have been up that road all week asking people what they did,” he said, “and I have heard you ask four of them and I have not said anything about it because it is not my business. And this morning I am going to ask you, and I am going to say before I ask that I know what I am doing, and I want you to stop me if I have got it wrong.”

“Go on, then.”

“I want to know what you did on the second day of the hundred and fifty-third week. That is all. Not what you have been given. What you did.”

“That is the first time in this county anybody has asked me that,” she said, and she said it without any weight in it at all, which was worse than if she had made a speech. “Nobody has asked me that since I came here. I ask people. I have never been asked. You have stood on this step and thought about it before you said it, and I could see you doing it, and I am not going to help you by making it easy.”

“That is fair.”

“You did not come here for the last of my keeping. You came because I have it and you would like to know how it feels to have it. That is what a man of fifty-four asking a woman of forty-six for her morning is, and I am not going to be angry with you for it, and I am also not going to pretend it is a smaller thing than it is.”

She stood on the bottom step and looked out at the water going over the top of the drain the way she had looked at it on the first morning of the fortnight.

“I was at the counter from before the light until about the ninth hour. I put the gravel off the low end because the low end was under. I did not go up the steps. I made tea and I sat down and I did not write anything anywhere, and I held a thing that a man said to me at a gate, and that is the whole of it, and there is no part of it that is about a gate.”

“That is what he said.”

“That is what he said and he said it once and I have it once and it is still his. It is not going to become a thing between us because I have told you what I did on a Wednesday, and I would like that said in front of everybody standing here, because I can feel that it is going to be read the other way by about the time tomorrow.”

Nobody said the other way. A man at the top of the steps said *what she has just said is not a thing about a gate*, and she said *no, and you are right, and it is not a thing about a gate and it never was and it is not going to stop being one*.

Then Ruth Ansell came up the cart road with the gate-key and said the thing she had gone home and thought about and could not get round, and it was about a mark she had been putting in her own book for years.

Ruth Ansell keeps a pound two hundred yards up that cart road, before the turn, and has written a line every day since the yard was made, and none of the lines in that book is about her, or so she has been telling herself.

“I put a dash on the days,” she said. “I have put a dash on those days since the yard was made and I have never told anybody and nobody has ever asked me and I did not think about it once in all those years. And on Monday I stood on this step and told a woman I would rather lose a heifer’s worth every year than open my own book to myself, and on Monday a man from a byre told you that a line about a person is a line about a person, and it has been sitting on me since.”

“What does the dash say.”

“A dash says nothing came in and nothing went out. That is what I have always told people and it is not what it says.” She turned the key over in her hand, the way she does.

“A dash says nothing came in and nothing went out and I was in that yard. Because if I had been in the house it would be a different mark, and if the yard had been shut it would be a different mark, and I have a mark for a man locked out and a mark for a gate standing open and I have a dash, and the dash is the one I use on the days when I was there and there was nothing. So half of that book is about whether I was standing in my own yard.”

“That is a small thing.”

“It is not a small thing and I am not going to let it be one and I am not going to be relieved about it either. It is in my hand. It has been in my hand for years. I cannot take it out, and I cannot un-know what it means now that I know it, and I am going to carry on putting the dash down on the same days I have always put it down, because if I stop putting it down then I have decided that the days did not happen, and they happened and I was there.”

A man of thirty-one said that was the smallest account anybody had given in this county and that it counted, and was refused by the woman holding the key in a voice with no kindness left in it at all.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and sixty-four days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and thirty-three days old.

“Three hundred and sixty-four days in this county, and the cut across this palm is two hundred and thirty-three days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and eighteen days old, which is nine hundred and eighteen less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Fifty-eight wrong things in fifty-eight mornings, and here is the fifty-eighth. A woman has kept a mark in her own hand for years meaning that she was there, and she has never told anybody, and there is nobody in this county who could have told her what it meant, and that is the smallest account anybody has given in this county and it is a real one and it counts.”

“No,” said Ruth Ansell.

“It does not count. You cannot make it count by calling it the smallest. A thing either is in a book or it is not, and mine is in a book, and it is a line about cattle in a yard, and I am not going to stand here and have a man of thirty-one promote a dash into evidence.”

“If you want it to count you will have to ask me what I did, and you have not asked me, and if you do ask me I will tell you, and then it will be a morning of mine out loud instead of a mark, and those are two different things and one of them is in your head by lunchtime.”

He did not get anything for having said it, and he was not told he was wrong, and the woman did not soften it and did not apologise for it either.

Sena Dorr entered the morning at about the tenth hour and read it out, and said that she was not going to repeat herself, and gave the reason in the terms of a keeper of somebody else’s morning rather than in the terms of a clerk.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years in a room four hundred miles off, and holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and decided this morning that she would not say a thing twice.

“That is the sixty-first morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing.”

“I said on Tuesday I would say the same thing again in the same words, and I am not going to. A man of fifty-four has just worked out that a thing said twice stops being his and starts being other people’s, and he is right, and I have been running that run for twenty-three years and I know it better than he does.” She closed the book.

“So I am not saying it again. It is said. I said it on Tuesday on this step and everybody here had it and it did not do a thing, and if I say it on Friday then it is a thing said twice, and then somebody can point at me and say *she went on about it*, and they would be right. I would rather it be one sentence in one pair of ears on one Tuesday.”

“Does that mean you will not say it again at all.”

“It means I will not say it again in the same words, and I have not got anything better yet, and if I do I will say it differently, and nobody here is to help me do that by suggesting anything.”

“The arrangement is four hundred miles off and it is still answering. It did not answer this week, and nobody in this county is in the room where it answers, and nobody was asked to go, and I did not ask anybody to go, and I have put no day on it, and this morning a man has a morning of mine in his mouth that I gave him myself, and it has not been written down and it is not going to be.”

Then Barnaby Crove said what he was going to do with the morning he had been given, and the reason he gave was not the reason he gave last week.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for a hundred and ninety-seven mornings, which is nine hundred and eighteen less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“A hundred and ninety-seventh. And I have got a morning that is not mine, and I am not going to say it, and last week you would have had my reason and the reason was that the moment I say it out loud it is a thing other people have. That is still true and it is not the reason.”

“What is the reason.”

“She gave it to me and she has not got it. I have it and she does not, and if I say it out loud then neither of us has got it, and that is a different thing and it is worse, and I would rather be the man holding it than the man who put it down somewhere.”

“Where would you put it down.”

“I do not know and that is the honest answer and I have been thinking about it since the ninth hour. There is nowhere. That is what I have come back to after a fortnight of walking up that road. There is nowhere to put it, and a man who is looking for somewhere to put a thing is a man who is going to find somewhere.”

Wat Marshe came up out of the lane and gave his figure and made one observation about a bank he had been looking at since the sixth hour, and then said nothing else at all and did not stay on the wall.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and sixty-five days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and sixty-five is nine hundred and eighteen less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and sixty-five days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up. And I have been looking at that water from the lane since the sixth hour and it has come up and stopped and it is not going any higher, and I have never seen it full on a Thursday, and I do not know what that means and I am not going to tell anybody what it means.”

“That is the whole of what I have.”

“That is the whole of what you have, and I have been on this landing a fortnight and you have said the same thing to me in a different way every morning and I have never once asked you for the other half. I am not going to start.”

The bank stood full at about the eleventh hour and would stand full for another day, and a woman who keeps a pound had found out that half of her own book was about herself, and a man of fifty-four was carrying somebody else’s morning in his head with nowhere to put it, and a man of thirty-one had been told by a woman with a key in her hand that he could not promote a dash into evidence, and nobody on that step had been told they were right.

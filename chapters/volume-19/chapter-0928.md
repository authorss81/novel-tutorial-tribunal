# Chapter 928: Sixteen Inches And Two Days Of It To Run, And A Woman Came Up The Road And Nobody Asked Her Anything And She Said So

Sixth day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week. His six hundred and seventy-eighth morning. Five hundred and twenty-eight days after the settlement. The fever ninety-two weeks and one day old. Four hundred and eighty-two days since the division. Four hundred and twenty-eight days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, holding at that figure, two days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and said that two days is the last figure anybody gets before a bank stops holding and that he had stopped counting the days a thing takes and started counting the mornings instead, and that that was a different job and worse-paid.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and has begun to notice that a bank holding is harder to stand next to than a bank moving.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, two days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the sixth day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week, and it is a Sunday.”

“I am going to tell you the thing about two days and then I am going to stop talking. Two days is a figure of hours to me and it is a figure of weeks to whoever is waiting on one of the other pages, and both of those are correct and only one of them is on this stone, and I have never in nineteen years been able to say which one a man came up here for.”

“Have you ever wanted to know.”

“Yes,” he said, “and there is no way on this landing to find out without asking, and I am not going to ask, and that is the arrangement I have and I made it.”

Then the woman of forty-four came up the cart road on her own time, past the low wall and no further, and stood there, and nobody asked her anything, and after a while she said so herself.

Orla Fennimore is forty-four, keeps twenty-nine chairs at thirteen shillings and fourpence a year, has put her question down five times in nineteen years and has not put it down a sixth, and has come up this road this morning for the first time in eleven days.

“You have all been very careful.”

“We have,” said a woman at the top of the steps, “and I want to say that it is not a favour to you, it is a rule this landing made for itself, and you should be irritated by it.”

“I am not irritated by the rule. I put five of mine down in nineteen years.” She stayed where she was. “I am saying that eleven days is a long time to have nobody ask me anything, and that I noticed on the fourth day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week and I have said nothing for four mornings about it, and I am saying it now because if I say it once and then stop, then in a week this landing will have a new story about me and I would rather it had the true one.”

“Which is what.”

“Which is that nobody has asked me anything and that I have not asked anybody for anything and that those two things are going to go on happening, and I have decided that they are mine and not a thing that is happening to me.” She put her hand flat on the low wall. “I said on the ninth run that what I am owed is to be asked once and not twice. That is still the whole of it and I have not improved on it in eleven days and I am not going to.”

“Then why are you up here.”

“Because it is a Sunday and I have been up this road on a Sunday before, and because there is a man on this step who moves the chairs in my passage and who comes up here on a Sunday, and I have not seen him for a fortnight and I have not sent for him.” She did not look down the road. “Nobody is to make anything of that and nobody is to go and tell him I said it.”

Then the man who moves the chairs came up on foot from the village at about the ninth hour with his hands behind him and stood at the low wall and said the thing he says every time she comes to the back of that passage, and would not give the words, and was asked by nobody for them.

Aldis Rook moves the chairs for the woman of forty-four, works in a passage a hundred and thirty feet long with nine openings in one wall and a wall at the end of it, keeps nothing, has no stone and no seat and no board, and has never once been asked what he did on that landing.

“I said it to her just now and I am not going to say it to you.”

“You have not been asked to say it to me.”

“No, and I want that noticed as well, because there was a fortnight when anybody who came up that road could put a question to me in front of a landing and I have been on my own this morning for two reasons and one of them is that I am not going to.” He took his hands from behind him. “She has been eleven days without anybody asking her anything and she came up here this morning to say so out loud to a step full of people who have all been careful, and I would not be the man on that step who finally did it for her this afternoon.”

“That is hers to decide and not yours.”

“That is why I am not going to do it. If I asked her one thing on this step this afternoon then for the rest of nineteen years that is what she will have, and it will be a thing a man did in front of a wall because he had decided today was the day.”

Wat Marshe came up out of the lane and gave his figure and said that a man had said he had not been asked anything in nineteen years and that he had worked out that nineteen years is longer than the whole of anybody else on that step having been there.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and seventy-five days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and seventy-five is nine hundred and twenty-eight less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and seventy-five days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up. And the man who moves the chairs said nineteen years and I have been in this county since the day after I was born near it and I did not know until just now that nineteen years is longer than everything else on that step.”

“It is longer than some of it.”

“It is longer than all of it and that is the whole of what I have for a week.” He looked at her. “And you are the only person on that step this morning who came up and got nothing, and I have been getting nothing for a fortnight, and I did not think it was the same thing until you stood there and said it.”

“It is not the same thing.”

“I know it is not the same thing. That is why I am saying it out loud.” He shifted his coat. “And nobody is to ask me whether your nothing and my nothing are both the same nobody, because I do not want that asked.”

Barnaby Crowe came up off the low side and said his figure and said he had walked past a man standing at the head of his own gate on the road up and had not stopped, and that he had thought about it at the fourth hour and had not been able to make it into anything.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for two hundred and seven mornings, which is nine hundred and twenty-eight less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and seventh. And I walked up that road at the fifth hour and there was a man standing at his own gate doing nothing at all, and I had it in me, and I went past him.”

“Why.”

“Because it was not mine and I could not have said what I wanted. I wanted to ask him whether the gate was sticking. That is what I would have said, and it would have been true, and it would have been the first time in a year I had asked a person a thing about their own morning without wanting a day off the ninth line out of it.” He set the barrow down. “And I did not stop, and he will not know I went past, and there is nobody in this county I can tell that to except a step, and I am telling a step.”

“That is not nothing.”

“No. It is a very small something and I am not going to be told it is nothing by anybody on this landing, because I have had two hundred and seven mornings of something and I know exactly what nothing feels like from the inside.”

Then Ilyan Vester came up and said his figure, and said that the man who moves the chairs knows what goes up and down a hundred and thirty feet of passage every morning and that is the same as knowing, and was refused in one sentence by the woman who keeps twenty-nine chairs.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and seventy-four days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and forty-three days old.

“Three hundred and seventy-four days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and forty-three days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and twenty-eight days old, which is nine hundred and twenty-eight less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Sixty-eighth wrong thing in sixty-eighth morning, and here is the sixty-eighth. That man moves a chair through a hundred and thirty feet of passage every morning of his life. He knows what goes up and what goes down and in what order and at what hour. That is knowing. I am not saying he should say it. I am saying that a thing you know every day of your life and never say is not a thing that is gone, and this landing keeps acting as though the unsayable things have gone somewhere.”

“No,” said the woman of forty-four.

“I have not said what I was going to do with it.”

“You have, and it was to make him into a man who is holding something.” She did not take her hand off the wall. “A man who moves a chair through a room knows where the chairs go. He has never once said a word about what was said in that house in nineteen years, and that is not a failure of memory and it is not a shame in him.”

“Then what is it.”

“It is the only thing about that passage that anybody has been able to keep. If you go up that road and put a question to him about it then you will have taken the only thing off him that has lasted nineteen years, and you will have got a sentence, and the sentence will not be worth the chairs.”

He was not told he was right and he was not told he was wrong, and the man who moves the chairs did not say one word about any of it and went back down the road at the pace he had come up at, and the woman of forty-four went home past the low wall at a time when nobody was asking her anything at all.

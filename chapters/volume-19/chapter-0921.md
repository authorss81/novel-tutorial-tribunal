# Chapter 921: Sixteen Inches And One Day Of It Running Down, And A Man With A Book Asked A Baker Once About A Morning

Sixth day of the hundred and fifty-third week. His six hundred and seventy-first morning. Five hundred and twenty-one days after the settlement. The fever ninety-one weeks and one day old. Four hundred and seventy-five days since the division. Four hundred and twenty-one days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, holding at that figure, one day of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave the three figures and then said that one day is the shortest run anybody on that step has ever been given and that he is not going to make anything of it.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and has begun to notice that the landing invents more about a figure than about the water.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, one day of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the sixth day of the hundred and fifty-third week, and it is a Sunday.”

“Before I put the broom down I want to say the thing that goes with one day, and then I am going to stop. It comes off tomorrow. Not at night. Off. And after it comes off there is nothing on that stone for the morning, and I have said that before on this bank and I am not going to stand here tomorrow with my mouth shut while people work out that I hid it.”

“You could have said that on the fourth day of the hundred and fifty-third week.”

“I could have and I did not, and the reason I did not is that I do not trust myself to be right about tomorrow, and the reason I am saying it today is that I have found out I am right about tomorrow on four separate occasions in nineteen years and there is no reason at all for there not to be a fifth.”

Then the baker came up the cart road with his coat open and no fire in his face at all, which is the thing a man looks like when he has been up since the fourth hour with a fire he did not build.

Pol Dunnage keeps the bakehouse four miles up that cart road, in the village the road goes through, and shut his oven down yesterday morning and has been on a temporary fire in the far corner of his own bakehouse since, and has found out what is in his own wall and told nobody.

“The oven at our end is out. It has been out since the sixth hour yesterday and it will be out until the middle of next week at the outside, and I am on a fire in the far corner with half the flour I can get.”

“Which is worse.”

“The loaf is worse. Not bad. Worse. There is a thing you can do with a hot oven and a thing you cannot do with a cold corner, and I have known that for thirty years and I have never had to sell the difference before.” He put his hands behind his back, which is not a thing he does. “The village has had a day of it and has already said so, and a woman at the end of the lane has told my wife the far corner is not even a bakehouse, which is the first time anybody has said a thing about me that was not about the bread.”

“Did you tell her.”

“I did not go down there and I am not going to. She is not wrong and I am not going to stand in her lane and be right at her, because there is nothing in it for her to be wrong about. She has had bad bread and she has said so and I have heard her.”

He stopped there, and then he stopped again, and a man with a book under his arm came up the cart road behind him and stopped at the low wall and did not come further, and Pol Dunnage turned round and looked at him and did not say anything.

Nevin Trask is sixty-one, keeps a book of heads and carts that he has kept for longer than anybody on that step has been on it, and has three lines in it in eleven years and has left some of what he keeps out of it on purpose.

“I am going to ask you one thing and I am going to ask it once,” he said, “and I am not going to stand here and pretend that is a favour to you, because it is not. It is for my book and you are in it.”

“All right.”

“On the fifth day of the hundred and fifty-third week a barrow of stone went up this cart road at about the eighth hour. I was on the low side of the turn with my load and I saw it go past and I saw the man wheeling it, and carts are what my book has. What did you do that morning.”

“Shut the oven down. That is the whole of it. I told my man we were going to do it, we banked the fire and covered what was in the trough and we had the far corner lit by ten, and that is what I did that morning.”

“Did you tell him why.”

“I told him I was going to open the wall on the sixth day of the hundred and fifty-third week. That is all the reason there has ever been and there is not going to be another one in front of anybody.”

Trask took the book out from under his arm and did not open it, and stood holding it the way a man holds a thing he has decided something about.

“I have written nothing down,” he said.

“No.”

“I am going to write nothing down, and I want you to hear the reason from me and not work it out, because you will work out a better one and it will be wrong. My book is heads and carts. A cart goes up a road. You did not go up a road.”

“What you did was in a room with a door on it and a man in it.”

“If I put that in my book then a man in another county in forty years opens it and reads a baker’s Saturday and takes it for a thing that happened on a cart road. He will be wrong and he will not be able to tell that he is wrong, because the page will look like every other page.”

“So it goes in your head.”

“It goes in my head.” He shut the book again. “I am sixty-one. I have had it in my head since yesterday afternoon. It is not going to be in my head on the third day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week and there is nobody in this county I could give it to without giving them the wrong shape of it, and I am not going to ask you for it a second time to see whether you say it differently.”

“That would be twice.”

“That would be twice, and I would get the same words, and then it would be the same thing twice over instead of one thing a man said once, and I would have made it worse for myself.”

Nobody on that step thanked him for any of it and nobody told him he had it right, and a woman coming up out of the lane said *that is the first sensible thing a man with a book has said on this landing in three weeks*, and he said *it is not sensible, it is the only thing a book is for*.

Then Barnaby Crowe came up with the barrow and said his figure and said that he had heard a man ask a baker a question and had not asked anybody himself, and gave the reason, and the reason was the water.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for two hundred mornings, which is nine hundred and twenty-one less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundredth,” he said, and stopped, and then said it again. “Two hundredth. I have been saying that number the same way every morning for two years and this morning it came out of me a different way and I want it noticed, because I did not do that on purpose and I have not got a use for it.”

“Which way.”

“The other way. A hundred and ninety-nine and then this one.” He set the barrow down on the same stones it has stood on every morning of the fortnight. “And I did not ask anybody today. I want that said by me and not worked out by you. The man with the book walked up that road with a question in his hand and I stood here with mine and I did not use it, and the reason is that there was water to come off that step and I wanted to see it come off.”

“That is the whole reason.”

“That is the whole reason and it is not a good one and I am not going to dress it up as a decision.”

Then the man who holds the landing said his three figures and said nothing else, and a woman washing lime eleven days a week went up past the group without stopping, and Ilyan Vester came up the bank and said his figure and then said the thing he had built in the lane since the light came up, which was that a man can be found.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and sixty-seven days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and thirty-six days old.

“Three hundred and sixty-seven days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and thirty-six days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and twenty-one days old, which is nine hundred and twenty-one less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Sixty-first wrong thing in sixty-one mornings, and here is the sixty-first, and it is a small one and I am going to say it small.” He nodded at the man with the book. “You asked him once. You can find him again. He bakes four miles up that road and he is at it on the morning and he will be at it on the morning after that, and a man with a book can go and find out what a baker did on any morning he has a mind to, and I do not see what has been given away.”

“Then say what you think has been given away.”

“I think you have made a thing you cannot use out of not writing it down. You have it and there is nowhere to put it, and a man who is holding something with nowhere to put it will go looking for a use for it, and there is only one use for it and it is checking him.”

“No,” said Nevin Trask.

“I have said it to a man who keeps a book and not to a man who keeps a loaf.”

“It makes no difference what he keeps. You have put me in a place where the only thing I can do with what I have got is go and look at a baker again, and you have done that by describing a thing I cannot put anywhere as a thing I ought to look for.”

“That is the wrong shape for a man’s whole head to be put in by a stranger on a step. A man who was paid to be in that room was not a man who was watching him. If you want to know what a baker did on the fifth day of the hundred and fifty-third week then the baker is the only man in this county who can tell you. And I am not going to go and ask anybody else, because you have just made it sound like there is a better one.”

“That is all right.”

“That is not all right and I am not going to have you agree with me on this step. You have said a wrong thing and it was wrong in the direction that matters, which is that it made a man with a book into a man looking for something.”

Then Wat Marshe came up out of the lane with his coat over his arm and gave his figure and said that nobody had asked him about the dark, and that he had not asked anybody to, and that both of those were now going to be true for the rest of the week.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and sixty-eight days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and sixty-eight is nine hundred and twenty-one less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and sixty-eight days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up. And nobody has asked me about the dark and I have not asked anybody to, and I am putting it on the step because a thing that has not happened is not the same as a thing nobody has heard of.”

“Nobody heard that from a man of thirty-one.”

“No. I said it because it was true and because there is a man stood on this step with a book under his arm who has just put one thing in his head and kept it there, and I wanted him to know that I know what the other version of that feels like.”

Nobody on that step told the boy he had it right and nobody told him he had it wrong, and Nevin Trask put his hand flat on the book and said *that is fair* and nothing else, and the baker went down the cart road on the temporary fire he had built in a corner of his own bakehouse, and sixteen inches stood on the top step with one day of it to run and about sixty of those ninety steps under, and a man with a book walked up the road with the fifth day of the hundred and fifty-third week in his head and nowhere to put it.

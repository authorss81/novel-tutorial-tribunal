# Chapter 946: The Thirty-Fifth Time, And All Ninety Of Those Steps Walkable, And A Man At The Head Of A Cut Wired A Catch He Said He Would Not Wire

Third day of the hundred and fifty-seventh week. His six hundred and ninety-sixth morning. Five hundred and forty-six days after the settlement. The fever ninety-four weeks and five days old. Five hundred days since the division. Four hundred and forty-six days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

The water came off that bank at about the sixth hour and all ninety of those steps were walkable by about the seventh, and that was the thirty-fifth time. There is no figure of inches on this morning and there is no figure of steps under, and there is no figure of days of the coming back either, and that is not a gap in the morning. It is the shape of the morning, and there is nothing else on this landing that wants a figure put on it, and a man said that out loud before anybody asked him for it.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and this morning said the absence out loud and then went and swept ninety steps instead of one.

“There is no figure of inches. Not one, and I am not going to round it or hedge it or give you a feeling of it instead, because a feeling of it is still a figure and I have watched men put those on this step for three weeks and call them something else. No number of steps either. And no number of days, and there is not one to give you, because when all ninety are out there is no coming back to count out from.”

He leaned the broom against the wall. “Nineteen years of the fifth hour on a morning like this and not one of them has given me a fourth thing to say. A morning when the water is off does not have a fourth thing. It has ninety steps. So I am going to sweep ninety steps, and if you want a figure off that, you can count them and I am not going to help you and I am not going to stop you.”

“That is not a gap.”

“It is not a gap and I would like it said before eleven, because on a morning like this a man who is honest walks out here early with his mouth shut and everybody decides he has come out early to look at the water and there is no water.” He picked the broom up. “There is a thing underneath the bank this morning that anybody who wants it can go and look at. It is not a number. It is nine hundred steps cut in a slope and it has been there longer than me.”

Then the man at the head of the cut came down off the low side on purpose, on a morning with nothing to do on the water, and said he had wired the catch.

Ezekiel Moss keeps a lock at the head of a cut a mile and a bit above the ash, has wired the same catch twice since the frost, said on a morning last week that he would not wire it a third, and wired it on Tuesday morning, and has come down off the low side to say so before anybody could come up the lane and find out.

“I wired it. Tuesday morning, before I did anything else, and it is wired now and it is holding and I have told people on the way here and I am telling the step last.”

“You said here on a Monday that you would not wire it a third time, and you said you were leaving it loose and you said the losing nothing by saying so cost you nothing.”

“That is all true and I have not changed my mind about any of it, which is the part I did not expect.” He stood at the bottom of the steps with his hands behind his back. “I left it loose and I lost nothing by it, and then I worked out something on Wednesday that I could not get rid of.”

He took his hands out from behind his back and did not know what to do with them.

“Leaving a catch loose so that a man can check it later is a good thing for about one morning. After that it is just a catch that is loose, and it is not honest, and it is not brave either. It is a thing I did so that I could walk away from it and say I had not touched it.”

“You could have said that on Monday.”

He looked down the low side at the water that was not there.

“I could have, and instead I gave the step the best thing I had, which was a man leaving a thing loose on purpose and losing nothing, and it was worth having and it was also not what I was doing.” He shifted his weight. “And I am not going to tell you what I think of that.”

“If I say I was wrong then somebody will write it down as a man learning. If I say I was right then I will have been right for a day and I do not want that either. It is wired. Say what you like about it. I have said what I did and that is the whole of what I came down here to say.”

“You were not asked what you did.”

“No. And I have noticed that you have never once asked me what I did, and I noticed it the last time I said it out loud, and I have come down here today partly so that the second thing about me on that stone is not a man refusing to be asked.” He looked up at the bank. “The first thing was that I was going to leave it loose. Now there are two things and they do not agree with each other, and I am going to leave them both standing and I am not going to be the one to tidy them up.”

Then the man of thirty-one came up in the middle of a morning with nothing on the stone and said that this was the morning to do it on, and was refused by the man with the broom.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and ninety-two days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and sixty-one days old.

“Three hundred and ninety-two days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and sixty-one days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and forty-six days old, which is nine hundred and forty-six less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Eighty-six wrong things in eighty-six mornings, and here is the eighty-sixth, and it is the only proposal I have ever made that depends on a day having nothing on it.” He looked up the bank at the man of forty-three. “This is the morning. No figure, no steps under, nothing to check anybody against and nobody able to say they had a figure to go on with. If a keeper is to be asked anything on a step in this county then it is on a morning like this one and not on a morning when there is a number on the stone to lean on.”

Corvin Tace looked at him from the bottom of the ninety steps with the broom in his hand.

“I am not going to lend you that stone,” he said. “Not even empty. There is a man on this landing who has had nothing to say on a morning like this for nineteen years and he has not once been asked for it, and he is not going to be asked for it on the strength of a man of thirty-one arranging things.” He set the broom against the wall. “An empty stone is not a clean one. It is a stone nobody has filled in. If you want a morning with nothing on it you will get one when nothing is on it and not when you have arranged it.”

Nobody on that landing told the man of thirty-one that the refusal was a kindness and nobody told him it was the other thing.

Then the boy came up off the ninety steps and gave his figure and said he had been up all of them before the light and had counted the sweep and not the water.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and ninety-three days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and ninety-three is nine hundred and forty-six less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and ninety-three days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up. And I did all ninety before he came out, and I did them twice, going up and coming down, and I did not count the water because there is not any.”

“You did not count the water.”

“There is nothing to count. You all keep asking for a figure on this morning and there is not one to have and the only thing to do up there is the walking.” He sat down on the wall. “And I have decided something and I am not going to make a big thing of it. I am not going to try to work out what any of you are doing on these mornings any more.”

“I have been doing it every day for a fortnight and it has got me nowhere and it has got me into trouble twice, and there is a man at the head of a cut who just told us two things he would not tidy up, and I do not want to tidy him up either. I am going to be thirteen on this step.”

Then the woman of forty-nine came up the road with the ninth run and entered the morning and read it out, and said that she had done nothing again, and that she wanted the step to know she had done nothing on purpose.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years in a room four hundred miles off, and holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and has decided to ask one question and has not asked it, and has now gone four mornings without asking it and has told nobody why.

“That is the eighty-ninth morning in front of anybody, and I entered it, and there was nothing in it and I entered that too.”

She did not open the book and she did not need to.

“You still have not asked it.”

“I have not asked it and I want to be exact about what I did this morning, because there is a difference and everybody on this step has been getting it wrong including me until about half an hour ago.” She set the book on the wall and did not open it. “I did not decide not to ask it. I decided that a morning with no figure on the stone is the wrong morning to do anything I have been carrying.”

“That is a decision and it is a good one and it has nothing to do with the question. On Friday I said I would not tell anybody the day. I have now moved the day twice and I am not going to say a word about where it has moved to, and if you want to be angry with me you may be, and if you want to tell me I have made it into a ceremony you may do that as well.”

“We have all been doing that for a fortnight.”

“Yes, and that is why I am saying it.” She picked the book up. “It is one question. If it becomes a habit it is a thing I am doing to myself so that I have something to be proud of, and I have been proud of nineteen days and not one of them needed it.”

Then the man who moves chairs came up the cart road from the village as far as the low wall and stopped there again, and said the thing he had said the first time he came as far as the wall, and then said something he had not said before.

Aldis Rook moves the chairs for Orla Fennimore and keeps nothing and has no stone and has no seat and no board, has never once been asked what he did, was told on the fourth day of the hundred and fifty-sixth week not to come up that bank again, and has come as far as the wall four mornings running.

“This is the fourth time I have come as far as this wall and I am going to say the same thing I said the first time and then I am going to add one thing.” He put his hands behind him. “I am not going up. I have said that four times and it has not got any less true and it is not going to get more true by saying it a fifth time, so I am not saying it a fifth time. I am saying it here, at the wall, because she can see me from the passage door if the light is right and she has not come out.”

Nobody on that landing told him to go down the hill and nobody told him to go up it.

“And the one thing I have not said, and I have been carrying it since Saturday, is that she came out at about the seventh hour on Sunday and stood in that doorway and looked at me standing at this wall, and she went back in, and neither of us said anything, and that is the whole of what has happened in my week.” He started down the road. “I have not come up here for anything, and I am not coming up here to be told what to do about a wall. I have come up here four times because it is the only walk in this county that I do not have to be paid for.”

The man of forty-three swept his way up ninety steps that morning instead of one and got to the top of that bank at about the eighth hour, and there was nothing on his stone at all, and he did not stand at the top of it.

# Chapter 944: Sixteen Inches And It Holds, And A Man With A Board Came Back Up A Road He Said He Would Not Come Up

First day of the hundred and fifty-seventh week. His six hundred and ninety-fourth morning. Five hundred and forty-four days after the settlement. The fever ninety-four weeks and three days old. Four hundred and ninety-eight days since the division. Four hundred and forty-four days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, and it held at that figure, two days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and did not give the opening figure of the week that morning, because he has never given the opening figure of anything in nineteen years and he said on the last day of the week before that he would not start.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and has never once given anybody the first three figures of a new week in advance of the morning they are said.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, two days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the first day of the hundred and fifty-seventh week, and it is a Tuesday. And I did not tell anybody on Monday night what I was going to say this morning, and if anybody on this step was up early enough to have wanted me to, that is your morning wasted and not mine.”

Then the man with a board came up the cart road on the Tuesday, which is the road he said on Friday he would not come up again, and he came up it slowly, and he did not have the board in the inside of his coat, and everybody on that landing looked at where his hands were not.

Garrin Tolley is thirty-eight, has a child of nine, came back up a road he had said on the fourth day of the hundred and fifty-sixth week he would not come up, and his coat is not buttoned and there is nothing in the inside of it, and nobody in this county is to count him and the figure of how old the thing was is not given.

“I did it on Monday morning.”

“You did what.”

“I took it out. In a room, at about the sixth hour, and there was nobody in the room, and I put it on a nail and I came back and I shut the door and I went to work.” He stood on the bottom step and did not come up. “I am telling you because I said on Friday that I would, and I do not want to be a man who decides afterwards that he does not feel like it.”

Nobody on the step asked him why. Nobody asked him which room, or which wall, or which nail, or how long it had been in the inside of his coat, and there was a stretch of that morning where the question got to the edge of being asked and turned round.

“You did not tell anybody why.”

“No.”

“You have had it in your coat and you have not told anybody why and now it is on a nail and you still have not told anybody why.”

“No, and I have thought about that for two days and here is the reason, and it is the only part of this I have thought about. If I tell you why, then why is a thing that this whole county could have. And if that is a thing this whole county could have, then I will find out what I actually think, and I have decided I would rather not find out what I actually think while I am standing on a step.” He put his hand flat on his coat where nothing was, and left it there.

“There is a boy of nine at home who does not know that is gone and he is not going to be told yet and he is not going to be told by me standing on a hill.”

“You do not know whether you are right about the reason.”

“I have been right about the reason I asked twice and wrong about it in a fair number of other places, and nobody on this landing knows which is which, and neither do I. That has not changed since Friday. Nobody is to tell me I was right and nobody is to tell me I was wrong and I am not going to tell you it was the worst thing I have ever done, because I said that on Friday and I am not going to say a thing twice this week.”

The man who keeps the gravel on that landing came up the middle of it and gave his figure and then did something that nobody had seen him do in the whole of the time any of them had been watching him.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and twenty-three mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is nine hundred and forty-four less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one, and he has not spent one of them since the third day of the hundred and fifty-sixth week, and this morning he asked a question and it was not about the ninth line.

“Two hundred and twenty-third, and that is the fifth morning I have not asked for it.”

“That is the fifth.”

“Yes. And I am going to spend one of them this morning, and I have been saving them up since Friday like a fool with a purse, and I am going to spend one on a thing that is none of my business, and then I will be back where I was.” He set the barrow down on the dry part of the step. “How long have you had nothing to say.”

The whole of that step went quiet in a way it had not gone quiet all week.

Corvin Tace came down one step and looked at him. “Say what you mean by that and then I will answer it or I will not.”

“The fourth thing,” Barnaby Crove said. “The thing you have had to say when there was no figure. How long.”

“No,” said Corvin Tace, and did not put the broom down.

“It is a figure of nothing. It is the only figure in this county that is about a man doing less.”

“It is not a figure of nothing, it is a figure of days, and I could name the day in one go and we would both know I could name it, and then I have put a figure of days in a man’s mouth on a step where a man has just come up and said he is not going to say a day to anybody.” He did not raise his voice. “That is the working, and it is the whole of it.”

“You have spent five mornings not asking for a day, and you have just spent one of them asking me for a different kind of day, and the thing you actually wanted this morning was for me to be good at something in front of you. I am not going to do it for you. I have not been good at anything on that step for three weeks and the only reason nobody has found out is that nobody has asked me.”

Barnaby Crove stood there for a moment. “That is fair,” he said. “I am going to take the five back again.”

“Then take them back and do not tell me.”

“I am not going to take them back and I am not going to put the question back either, and I want that said out loud while you are standing there, because I have been on this step for a long time and I know what I am doing.” He picked the barrow up again. “You did not answer me and you told me why you did not answer me, and I have been asking for a day against a line for two hundred and twenty-three mornings on the grounds that a man owes me a figure.”

“And you have just told me, without saying a word about me, that a figure is a thing a man can have or not have. That is not the answer I asked for. It is the first thing anybody on this landing has given me that I did not ask for, and I am not going to hand it back either.”

Then the woman of forty-nine came up the road with the ninth run and entered the morning and read it out, and said that she had spent a weekend deciding which day she was going to ask her one question on, and had decided that she was not going to tell anybody the day, and had not asked it, and that the second part of that was now the whole of her week.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years in a room four hundred miles off, and holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and has decided to ask one question and has not asked it, and on the first day of a new week gave the reason to nobody.

“That is the eighty-seventh morning in front of anybody, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing.”

“You have not asked it.”

“I have not asked it and I have decided that I am not going to tell anybody the day, including you, and I know what that sounds like and it sounds like a woman making an occasion out of a step.” She put the book down on the wall and did not open it. “And the reason is that a day is the one thing I can be stopped with.”

She turned the book over so that the cover faced down.

“Somebody on this landing finds out there is a day coming and they can get there first, or they can be in a bad mood on it, or they can decide that week is the week they finally say what they think of me. I have not been the person somebody turns up for in twenty-three years and I am not going to become her over one question.”

“Then how will anybody know it happened.”

“They will not, and that is not a loss, and I want that said plainly because I have heard myself say it and it sounded like self-pity and it is not.” She picked the book up. “Nobody is going to thank me for asking it either. There is nothing to thank. I am asking a man one question once and he will say something and I will hear it and that will be the end of it, and if that is worth a thank then the whole of what I have been doing for twenty-three years was worth a thank, and it was not, and I will know that it was not whatever he says.”

Then the man of thirty-one came up the middle of all that with nothing in his hands and gave his figure, and asked the man with the empty coat to put a day against the nail, and was refused by the man who had put it there.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and ninety days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and fifty-nine days old.

“Three hundred and ninety days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and fifty-nine days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and forty-four days old, which is nine hundred and forty-four less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Eighty-four wrong things in eighty-four mornings, and here is the eighty-fourth, and it is the smallest one I have had.” He did not go up past the second step. “You have just put a thing on a nail in a room with nobody in it and told this step you are not going to say why. Put a day against it. One day. You would not have to tell anybody what the day was for and I would not ask you.”

“Not today,” said the man with the empty coat.

“You do not have to give me a reason for that either. I have spent eleven weeks asking this landing to put days against things and you have done every one of them for me, and I have just watched you do the last one without me.” He put his hands at his sides. “That is all I am going to say about it and I am not going to stand here and be pleased with myself for saying it small.”

Nobody on that landing said that the refusal was the right one and nobody said it was the wrong one.

Then the boy came up out of the lane and gave his figure and said that he had done nothing this morning that anybody had asked him for, which was two mornings running, and that he was not going to make anything of it.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and ninety-one days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and ninety-one is nine hundred and forty-four less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and ninety-one days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.”

“You have done nothing anybody asked for, twice.”

He put his hands flat on the wall on either side of him.

“Twice.” He stood at the bottom of the steps. “And I am not going to make a habit out of it, because a habit is a wall and I have watched this step build one this week, twice over, and everybody who built one is standing on it. I did a thing on Monday before the light because I wanted one thing on this step that came in from outside.”

“Today I did nothing because I wanted to find out what happens if I do nothing. What happens is that a man of fifty-four asks a man of forty-three a question he has no right to ask, and the man of forty-three will not answer him and will tell him why, and neither of them has got a day out of it. That is the whole result of my experiment and it took me about a minute.”

“Your experiment.”

“I have not got a name for it and I am not going to give it one on a Tuesday.” He looked at the sixteen inches going past on the step. “A man came up that road this morning with his coat unbuttoned and there is nothing in it, and nobody asked him why, and I have been sitting in that lane for two days trying to work out how a person gets to be a man who can come up here like that. You cannot do it by deciding to. You do it by going to work on a Monday morning and putting a thing on a nail and going to work again.”

Nobody on that landing was thanked for anything that morning, and nobody was relieved, and the man with the empty coat went back down the cart road at the pace he had come up, and the man of forty-three swept the sixteen inches off the top step in four strokes and said no fifth thing.

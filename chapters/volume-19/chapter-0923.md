# Chapter 923: Four Inches And About Ninety Of Those Ninety Under, And A Man Who Was In That Room Came Up On His Own And Said The Part Nobody Asked Him For

First day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week. His six hundred and seventy-third morning. Five hundred and twenty-three days after the settlement. The fever ninety-one weeks and three days old. Four hundred and seventy-seven days since the division. Four hundred and twenty-three days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Four inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, seven days of the coming back, and about ninety of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and gave no fourth thing, and he said that about ninety out of ninety is the whole of what he has, and that a bank four inches on a step is not going to be a different figure by Friday whatever anybody wants off it.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and has been asked twice this week what his three figures are for.

“Four at the seventh hour, seven days of the coming back, and about ninety of those ninety steps under, on the first day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week, and it is a Tuesday.”

“I have been asked what they are for twice in three days and I am going to answer it once and then it is finished. They are for a man who has to be at that step at the fifth hour to know whether to put his broom down the low side or the high side. That is what they are for. If anybody puts another use on them they are a different thing and I will not have it.”

“Then what is the different thing.”

“The different thing is a figure somebody is holding about the landing, and I do not keep it, and if I did I would be a man keeping a figure about a landing and not a man standing on one. There is a difference and you all know it and I would rather it stayed known than argued.”

Then the man who sets Pol Dunnage’s bread came up on foot with a sack over his shoulder and put it down at the low wall and said he had walked up the cart road on purpose and that nobody had asked him to, and that he had thought about it all the way.

The man who sets Pol Dunnage’s bread has set that bread for nine years and was in the bakehouse on the fifth day of the hundred and fifty-third week and was asked about that morning once by a man with a book and gave an answer, and has come up the road this morning of his own accord.

“I have got nothing to give anybody and I have come up here anyway, and I would rather say that at the front than have somebody find out later that I stood on this step and only said what I was asked.”

“What have you got.”

“I have got the day before.” He put his hand flat on the wall. “On the fourth day of the hundred and fifty-third week, in the morning, before any of it, I told him the damp was not the wall. That is what I said to him and that is what I meant, and he is a baker of forty-one years, and he took it as a man at his bench saying a thing to get his hands in it.”

“Which did he take it as.”

“He did not take it as me knowing more about his wall than he does. That is what I have turned over for two days.” He looked at the four inches on the step. “And then on the fifth day of the hundred and fifty-third week we shut the oven down, and on the sixth day of the hundred and fifty-third week he is on a fire in the far corner, and none of that came out of me telling him what I said on the fourth day of the hundred and fifty-third week.”

“You told him a thing and he did not act on it.”

“I told him a thing and he did not act on it for about nine hours and then he shut an oven down, and I have been sitting with that for a day and a night.”

“And you have worked out what to do with that.”

“I have worked out that the man I said it to is the man it was about, and there is nobody else it was about. And I am standing here to say that nobody asked me about the fourth day of the hundred and fifty-third week.”

“He was told on the sixth day of the hundred and fifty-third week that he was going to open that wall, and he is still carrying the chisel about with him, and nobody has asked me what came out of it, and he has not told me either. The man with the book asked me about the fifth day. The baker has not asked me about any day.”

“Nobody on this step has asked me about any day except one day, and I want it on the record of this step that it was one day, and that I gave it once, and that I would rather he did not come up here and ask me a second time to see whether I say it different.”

“He was never going to ask you a second time.”

“I do not know that and neither do you, and that is not why I am saying it. If I go back down that road with the fourth day of the hundred and fifty-third week in my mouth then it is in two places. It is in my head and it is in that step behind you.” He took his hand off the wall.

“And two places is a thing you can check.”

“Two places is a thing you can check, and if it can be checked then somebody will do it, and then they will come and ask me a second time, and I will give them the same words, and then there will be three.”

“That is the most careful thing anybody has said on this step for a month,” said a woman at the top of the stairs.

“It is the most ordinary thing anybody has said on this step for a month and I would rather it was,” he said. “I load bread. You stand on steps.”

Then Nevin Trask said that the thing was in two places now and that one of the two places was him, and that he was going to have to say what he was going to do about it, and he said it on the step, and it was not a decision and he said so first.

Nevin Trask is sixty-one, keeps a book of heads and carts, has the same Saturday twice in his head and none of it in the book, and has just been handed a morning before it by a man who was not asked for it.

“I have got a baker’s Saturday and I have got the day before it and they do not fit each other and I am not going to make them. That is the whole of what I have got and it is worth more than what I had yesterday, which was two of the same thing, and I am aware that that is a strange sentence.”

“It is not strange, it is Tuesday,” said Corvin Tace, “and you are on my step on it, and I would say something if I had anything and I have three figures and no fourth thing.”

Wat Marshe came up out of the lane and gave his figure and then said that a man had come up the road this morning who had not been asked, and that he had wanted to be one of those, and had decided not to be, and the reason he gave was that nobody would have asked him and he would have had to go and stand somewhere.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and seventy days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and seventy is nine hundred and twenty-three less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and seventy days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up. And that man up there came because he had something, and I have had something since the second day of the hundred and fifty-second week and I have not come up with it.”

“Why not.”

“Because if I came up with it then either somebody asked me about it or nobody asked me about it. If somebody asked me about it then I would be answering a question, and the whole point of it is that nobody asks. And if nobody asked me about it then I would just be a boy saying a thing on a step to see what happens, and I worked out on Wednesday that I do not want to find out what happens.”

“That is a very grown thing to have worked out on a Tuesday.”

“It is not grown and it is not a Tuesday thing, it is a thing anybody who has been holding something for a fortnight has worked out by now. It is just that nobody else on this step has said it out loud yet.”

Then Barnaby Crowe came up and said his figure and said that he was not going to be the man who asked the new one anything, and gave no reason at all and went and shouldered a barrow instead.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for two hundred and two mornings, which is nine hundred and twenty-three less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and second. And I am going to say one thing and then I am going to carry gravel. A man has just come up that road with a morning in his mouth that nobody took out of him, and every man on this step wanted to ask him something, and I am not going to, and there is no reason.”

“That is not like you.”

“It is not like me and I am not going to give you a reason, because if I give you a reason you will all build the same morning out of it by the end of the week.” He shouldered the barrow. “Two hundred and two mornings of asking and I am not asking this one. Put that down somewhere. I am not going to say it again.”

Then Ilyan Vester came up and said his figure, and said that a man who gives you a thing nobody asked him for has done the most useful thing anybody has done on that landing in three weeks, and was told by a man with a sack over his shoulder exactly what that is worth.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and sixty-nine days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and thirty-eight days old.

“Three hundred and sixty-nine days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and thirty-eight days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and twenty-three days old, which is nine hundred and twenty-three less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Sixty-third wrong thing in sixty-third morning, and here is the sixty-third. That man came up that road with a thing in him that nobody asked for and put it on this step in the open, and it is the first thing in three weeks that has gone from one mouth to another without a line going into a book, and it is going to be the shape of everything, and a man who does that once is a man who has found the way to do it properly.”

“No,” said the man with the sack.

“You have not heard the rest of it.”

“You do not need me to hear the rest of it, because the rest of it was going to be that everybody should do it, and I have been in a room with a man for nine years and I know what happens to a thing when everybody does it.” He shifted the sack up onto his shoulder again. “You want a thing to be the shape of everything. If it is the shape of everything then it is a rule in about a fortnight and then it is a thing people say, and then a man asks me a second time because a rule is a thing that has to be kept.”

“That is not what I said.”

“It is what you were building and I do not need the last part of the building to know what it is.” He did not put the sack down again. “And I will tell you what mine is worth. It is worth that nobody will ask me about the fourth day of the hundred and fifty-third week. It is worth that, and it lasts about a year, and after that I will have forgotten it and got it wrong when I tell it, and there is nobody in this county who can stop me getting it wrong, because I have made sure there is nobody.”

Nobody on that step told him he was right and nobody told him he was wrong, and four inches sat on the top step with seven days to run and about ninety of those ninety under, and a man went down the cart road with a morning in his mouth that he had handed to a step and had been told would not be held by anybody.

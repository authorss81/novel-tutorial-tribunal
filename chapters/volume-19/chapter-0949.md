# Chapter 949: Twelve Inches And About Seventy Of Those Ninety Under, And A Man Has Told The Man On The Other Side Of A Line And Has Not Walked It With Him

Sixth day of the hundred and fifty-seventh week. His six hundred and ninety-ninth morning. Five hundred and forty-nine days after the settlement. The fever ninety-five weeks and one day old. Five hundred and three days since the division. Four hundred and forty-nine days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

Twelve inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, five days of the coming back, and about seventy of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and said that the number of inches on that step and the number of times a thing has been said on it are two different kinds of number and that he has watched a whole step forget that inside a fortnight.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and has watched people on his step start keeping figures that are not about water and has said nothing until this morning.

“Twelve at the seventh hour, five days of the coming back, and about seventy of those ninety steps under, on the sixth day of the hundred and fifty-seventh week, and it is a Sunday. And that is three, and they are about water, and they are the only numbers on this step that are about anything anybody can do anything with, and I am saying that because I have watched this step get fond of a different kind of number this week and I have not said one word about it until now.”

“You have said nothing about it because you do not think it is your business.”

“That is half of why and the half I am willing to say out loud.” He set the broom down. “The other half is that when a man stops the water coming up his bank with numbers that are not about the water, that man has also stopped being able to see the water. And that is not an argument. I have watched it happen to me this week and I have got about three mornings out of the last four where I could not have told you what was on my stone without looking at it, and I have swept nineteen years of steps and I have never once needed to look.”

Then the man who keeps a hedge came down off the wall with his hook in his hand and his face gone over, and said that he had told the man on the other side, and that he was not going to be asked how and he was going to tell them anyway.

Wil Cairns keeps a hedge, the parish line, half a mile from the wall above the ash, has cut it twice a year for nine years, moved the first eleven stakes onto the line he believes is, has told the man on the other side, and has not walked the line with him.

“I told him.”

“You said on Thursday that you were not going to tell him.”

“I said on Thursday that I was not going to tell him and I have had a weekend to find out whether I meant it, and I did not, and I told him on Friday evening at the top of my own lane and I said it in this order: that I moved eleven stakes, that I did not tell him, and that I do not know whether they are on the line.”

He put the hook down flat on the step. “And I did not walk it with him. That is the part I want on the record and I want it said first. We did not go down it together and look. We stood at the top of the lane and he said he would come up on the next cut and I said yes, and that is what has happened.”

“He could have been lying to you about where the line is.”

“He could. That is the whole of what I have bought myself with telling him and I want it understood that I know what I have bought.” He looked up at the wall. “For nine years I had the good side of it. I knew which one of us was wrong, and he thought he was right, and I got to keep that for the rest of my life, and I told every one of you on Thursday that I was paying a price for it. I have not been paying a price. I was being given something for nothing and I called it a price because a price sounds like a thing a man does.”

“So what have you got now.”

“Now I have told him, and he does not know that he has been told a thing he cannot check, and he still thinks he was right, and he still has not walked it, and the next cut is at the end of this hedging year and I will be standing at the top of that lane knowing what is under the ground and he will be standing next to me not knowing.”

He picked the hook up. “And I would do it again this morning, and I am not going to be told it was a good thing, nor that it was a bad thing, and I have said my piece and I am not going to say it again on this step.”

Then the woman of forty-nine came up the road with the ninth run and entered the morning and read it out and said that she was going to stand there and say nothing for a moment, and then said the thing she had been not saying.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years in a room four hundred miles off, and holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and has decided to ask one question and has not asked it, and has now gone six mornings without asking it.

“That is the ninety-second morning in front of anybody, and I entered it, and there was a gap in the writing on Thursday while I was standing at a wall doing nothing and I did not go back and close it, and I want to say that out loud before anybody asks me about it.”

“You had a gap in it.”

“I had a gap in it because I stopped. Not a blank page. A gap, where I stopped and then started again further down, and a man opening that book in a year can see exactly where I stopped and exactly how long I was not writing, because a gap and a blank are not the same and I know the difference and he will too.” She closed the book. “That is the most honest mark I have ever put in nine lines and it is the first one I have put there on purpose.”

“You have not asked your question.”

“I have not, and I am going to say the thing I have not been saying, which is that I have been looking for a person who cannot ask me back. That is what I have been doing for six mornings. I do not want somebody who is careful. I do not want somebody who has thought about it.”

“I want somebody who has no standing in this at all and asks me anyway and takes what I say and puts it nowhere.” She held the book against her chest. “And I want that because I have spent twenty-three years being the person everybody checks and I would like one morning where I am the person somebody spends. And I am not going to say that again after today and I would rather nobody repeated it back to me than have it repeated back to me kindly.”

Then the man who holds the gravel came up at the ordinary pace and gave his figure and told that step that he was going to start asking again on Monday, and gave the notice on the Sunday, which is his way and always has been.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and twenty-eight mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is nine hundred and forty-nine less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one, and asked for nothing on any morning for ten days and has given the step notice that the asking starts again tomorrow.

“Two hundred and twenty-eighth, and that is the tenth morning, and I am going to tell you now so that nobody has to decide what to do about it tomorrow.”

“You said on Friday you were going to stop.”

“I said on Friday I was going to stop and I meant it and it lasted ten days and it cost me more than the asking did, and I have found out that the wanting does not go anywhere while you are not asking, it just gets louder and you have more time to listen to it.”

He set the barrow down. “I am going to ask again tomorrow morning and I am going to be refused, and I have worked out that I would rather be refused than be a man with nothing in his mouth, and that is not a good thing and I am not going to stand here and make it one.”

“You are going back to asking for a day against the ninth line.”

“No,” said Barnaby Crove. “That is the part I have been not saying all morning and I am saying it now because it would be a lie if I stood here and let it be assumed. I am not asking for the day. I have given that morning up and I have not given the wanting up and I am not going to be a man who asks a woman for a thing he has stopped wanting, because she will take it as a favour and it will not be one.”

He picked the barrow up again. “I am going to ask her a different question and it is worse. I am going to ask her whether there is anything in that run of hers that a man who cannot read could be useful for, once, with no day against it, that nobody has to keep. And I have no idea whether that is a good idea and I am going to do it.”

Nobody on that landing told him it was a good idea, and then the boy came up out of the lane and gave his figure, and then the man of thirty-one came up and said out loud that telling a man where your line is is a second asking, and was refused by the man with the hook inside a sentence.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and ninety-five days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and sixty-four days old.

“Three hundred and ninety-five days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and sixty-four days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and forty-nine days old, which is nine hundred and forty-nine less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Eighty-nine wrong things in eighty-nine mornings, and here is the eighty-ninth, and it is about that man and I am going to say it plainly and then get out of the way.” He nodded up the wall. “That is a second asking. He has done one thing for nine years and now he has done the same thing a second time in the same voice and that is asking twice, and you know it is, because you have spent three weeks arguing with me about asking twice and this is the first asking twice anybody has stood up and admitted to.”

“It is not a second one,” said the man with the hook.

“It is not a record. It is not a second check. It is a man telling another man where he put a thing, once, having not told him, and there is no second anything in it.” Wil Cairns did not raise his voice. “A second asking is when you go to somebody who has already said no and make it into a yes.”

He did not raise the hook and he did not lower it.

“I have been at the top of that lane nine years and I have never once asked him for anything, because I did not know I was allowed to want it and I would have said yes to anything he wanted the whole time and he would have spent it in a fortnight. Telling him is not asking him. It is me putting it down and walking away from it and he does not have to do one single thing about what I have put down.”

“He could have been a second asking if he had asked you to walk it.”

“He could. And he has not and he is not going to, and the reason he is not going to is that he does not know what the eleven stakes are, and he does not know because I did not tell him, and that is the last thing I have in this world that is mine and I have given it away on a Friday evening and I would like to be left alone now.”

Nobody on that landing was thanked and nobody was relieved and the man of thirty-one got out of the way, and the man of fifty-four went up that road with a question in his mouth that nobody on that step had asked him to put there, and twelve inches was on the top step going down, and the man of forty-three swept it off and said no fifth thing.

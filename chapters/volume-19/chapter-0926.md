# Chapter 926: Sixteen Inches And A Full Bank, And A Man Of Thirty-Eight Came Up Asking A Whole Step Whether The Same Morning Said Twice Is A Check

Fourth day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week. His six hundred and seventy-sixth morning. Five hundred and twenty-six days after the settlement. The fever ninety-one weeks and six days old. Four hundred and eighty days since the division. Four hundred and twenty-six days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, and it is a full bank, four days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave the three figures, said the word a full bank, and then gave the whole of what a full bank is, in a mouth, on the morning it became one.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and says the two words a full bank out loud on the morning a bank becomes one and not on any other morning, and is not going to be stopped from doing it.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour, and that is a full bank, four days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the fourth day of the hundred and fifty-fourth week, and it is a Friday.”

“Now you all know what I have been not saying for four days. It filled this morning. It filled between yesterday noon and this morning and it is the first morning for nineteen years that I have watched it fill and been here for it, and that is not a figure and there is no point putting one on it.”

“And now you are going to tell us when it comes off.”

“I am going to tell you what I have, and what I have is four days, and four days from this morning is the first day of the hundred and fifty-fifth week, and there will be nothing on that step then.” He put the broom down against the wall. “That is not a promise. I have said before on this bank that I can do one day and not a week, and I am telling you a week’s worth this morning because the bank is full and a full bank has told me everything it is going to tell me, and when it comes off it will tell me nothing at all.”

Then the man of thirty-eight came up the cart road with a board against his chest and did not go to the pound and did not go to the bakehouse and came straight up onto the second step and asked a question of the whole landing at once.

Garrin Tolley is thirty-eight, has a child of nine, carries a board in the inside of his coat with thirty-two notches cut in the edge of it, and nobody in this county is to count him and the figure of how old that board is is not given.

“You have had the same morning on that step twice this week, once out of a baker and once out of the man at his bench, and it has been standing on that step ever since in about six mouths.”

“Yes.”

“I want to know whether that is a check.” He did not shift the board. “Not from the woman with the ninth run and not from the man with the book. From that step. Because I have been up a road twice asking one question twice and I have got nothing both times, and I have worked out that maybe I have had the shape of it wrong the whole time, and if two accounts that fit together is a check then there is a shape to this and I am not the only man who cannot see it.”

Nobody on that step answered him for long enough that the water going over the top of the drain could be heard from the bottom.

“No,” said the man who holds the landing.

“Give me the working.”

“I have given four figures off one stone for nineteen years and I will give you the working, and it is not the one you came for.” He came down one step, which he does not do. “A check is when a thing is put against the thing itself. A gate and a gate and a gate, and the gates are the same gate. A mouth agreeing is not that. A mouth agreeing is a man saying a thing somebody else has already said, and that is a good deal cheaper than a gate.”

“Then it is worth something.”

“It is worth exactly what a man’s word is worth and not one penny more.” He looked down at the boy and then up at the step. “What would set those two accounts against the Saturday itself is the Saturday itself, and the Saturday itself is a room with an oven in it and a wall on the other side, and there is nobody in this county who can be asked what was in that room, and there is nobody who could be asked again either, because they were asked once and there was nothing wrong with it.”

“You are telling me there is no third person.”

“I am telling you there is no third *thing*, and that is worse and it is not going to change. A third person would have been worth having this morning, a good deal, and there is not one, and if there were one then what you want would be true on a Tuesday and not on a Friday, and I have watched enough of this landing to know that a thing which is true on a Tuesday is not true on a Saturday because the water is a different figure.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the answer and you may have it or leave it.” He went back up the step and picked the broom up. “And I am not going to tell you that it is the wrong answer, because I do not know that it is the wrong answer. I know what a stone is for. I do not know what a pair of accounts is for and neither do you.”

Nobody on that landing told him he was right and nobody told him he was wrong, and a woman coming down said *that is the first time anybody has put a gate and a mouth out of the way separately*, and the man who holds the landing said *they were never in the same place and that is the whole of it*.

Then the man of thirty-eight said the other half of it, which he had not come up the road to say, and said that he had worked out on Friday what his real reason had been and it was not checking, and he was not going to describe it, and then he said the thing about his coat that he has been carrying for a fortnight.

“I am going to tell you why I am going up that road, and I am not going to, and I want the not-going-to said on this step so that nobody spends the week working it out.”

“Why should we not work it out.”

“Because you will get it near enough and you will act on it.” He took the board out from under his arm and did not open it. “It is not a check I am after. I have stood in front of that wall more mornings than I have counted and nobody has ever been able to tell me one thing about it, and I went and asked the same question twice and got the same words, and I did it because a man who asks is a man doing a thing while he waits.”

“That is the first thing you have said this week that has made anybody on this step go quiet.”

“It is the first true thing, and I have a boy of nine at home, and I am going to stop. I am not naming a day and I am not asking anybody to hold me to it, and if you work out the day yourselves that is your morning and not mine.”

Barnaby Crowe came up off the low side at that and said his figure and said he had asked nobody this morning either, and had given a reason, and the reason was that a man had just told a whole step a thing he had been keeping for two weeks and it had cost him something to say it out loud on a landing.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for two hundred and five mornings, which is nine hundred and twenty-six less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and fifth. And I did not ask anybody, and I am not going to say I have a reason for it, because I gave a reason on Tuesday and it turned out to be about me and not about the step.”

“That is a first,” said Nevin Trask from the low wall.

“That is a first and I noticed it myself at about the seventh hour.” He set the barrow down. “And I have got a thing to say and it is not clever and it is not a complaint. That man has come up here two hundred and five mornings asking for a day against the ninth line, and I want to say that somebody may come up that road and tell him there is a day against somebody’s name in this county.”

“You think that is likely.”

“It will not be the day he wants and it will not make his want smaller and he is going to be a man who has heard it and has still got what he has got.” He shouldered the barrow. “And I have got no reason to want that to happen and I have thought about it four times this week, and that is the part I am ashamed of, and I am saying it out loud so that it is on the step instead of in my head.”

Wat Marshe came up out of the lane and gave his figure and said that he had worked out that the man of thirty-eight was the only man on that landing who had said out loud what he actually wanted, and that everybody else on the step had said a reason.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and seventy-three days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and seventy-three is nine hundred and twenty-six less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and seventy-three days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up. And I have been counting which of you have said a reason and which of you have said the thing, and it is not the ones you would think.”

“Naming names is not going to help anybody.”

“It is not naming names. It is that a reason is a thing you can say twice without getting tired, and the thing underneath it comes out once and then you cannot put it back.” He shifted his coat. “I have had one thing to say out loud for a fortnight and I have said a reason for it every morning and the reason has got easier each morning and the thing has not moved, and that is how you know which one is the real one.”

Then Ilyan Vester came up and said his figure, and said that a man should be checked once in his life and that this landing had a man on it who had been checked about as much as anybody in this county, and was refused in one sentence by the man he had been talking about, and the sentence had the working in it.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and seventy-two days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and forty-one days old.

“Three hundred and seventy-two days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and forty-one days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and twenty-six days old, which is nine hundred and twenty-six less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Sixty-sixth wrong thing in sixty-sixth morning, and here is the sixty-sixth. Every man on this landing should be able to say he has been checked once. Once. Not twice, not a fortnight, once, in his life, and then it is behind him and he can go on being a man who was checked and not a man who is waiting.”

“No,” said the man of thirty-eight, and did not move off the step. “You cannot be checked once and put it down the way you mean. There is no shelf for it. I have been up that road twice and I have got nothing both times, and if a third man had come up here and put one good morning against me I would be a man carrying it, and I would be carrying it, and there is no version of carrying that turns into having had it.”

“That is the most defeatist thing I have heard anybody say on this landing.”

“Then it is the most defeatist thing and I have a boy of nine and I am not going to argue about it with you in front of a step.” He put the board back inside his coat. “You are all waiting for me to be the one who finally agrees that I am wrong. I am not going to, and I am not going to be careful about not agreeing either, and both of those are mine.”

Nobody on that step said anything at all, which was the first morning in a fortnight that had happened, and sixteen inches stood on the top step with four days of it to run and about sixty of those ninety steps under and the bank that had told them everything it was going to tell them.

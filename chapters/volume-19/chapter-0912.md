# Chapter 912: The Same Words Twice Is Not A Thing Twice

Fourth day of the hundred and fifty-second week. His six hundred and sixty-second morning. Five hundred and twelve days after the settlement. The fever eighty-nine weeks and six days old. Four hundred and sixty-six days since the division. Four hundred and twelve days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, holding at that figure, two days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave the three figures and did not say anything else at all, because he had named a morning on Monday and there was nothing else on that stone until the morning he named arrived.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and is two days into saying nothing beyond the figures.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, two days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the fourth day of the hundred and fifty-second week, and it is a Friday.”

“That is the whole of it. I have said the rest of it once and I am not saying it twice, and I would rather stand here quiet than be a man on this landing who repeats himself for the shape of it.”

Then a man of thirty-one said that a woman with a run ought to be able to say whether a keeper’s own account of his own morning was true, and was refused by the man she was being asked to do it for, and the refusal was not unkind and was not slow.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and fifty-eight days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and twenty-seven days old.

“Three hundred and fifty-eight days in this county, and the cut across this palm is two hundred and twenty-seven days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and twelve days old, which is nine hundred and twelve less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Fifty-two wrong things in fifty-two mornings, and here is the fifty-second. There is a woman on that landing who keeps the ninth of nine in a book and there is a man on this step who keeps a thing that has never been looked at by anybody in nine years, and she has the standing to say whether his account of his own morning is true, because there is nothing in that yard for her to be wrong about.”

“No. And I am going to say the rest of it because you will go on and you will get to a woman with a run and ask her the same thing, and she will say no, and you will come back and tell me she said no.”

“Why should I not ask her.”

“Because she was not in my yard, and a question she cannot answer is not a refusal, and you will not be able to tell the difference, and that is the whole of what I have learned cutting that hedge for nine years. Ask the water what the water was doing. You cannot, and you know you cannot, and nobody has to tell you.”

He was not told he was wrong, and he was not told anything at all after that, and the man with the bill-hook went back up the high side with the hook over his shoulder and did not say another word about it.

Then the woman of forty-nine entered the morning at about the tenth hour and read it out, and said a thing about her own book she had not said to anybody, and she said it flatly and without any of the weight she usually puts on the words she is careful with.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and read for a living for twenty-three years in a room four hundred miles off, and was on that step at the tenth hour with a question she had been asked twice already.

“That is the fifty-fifth morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing.”

“Three people have asked me this week whether I agree with what is in the ninth run about their own mornings, and I have answered all three the same way, which is that I have not been in any of their yards. And I have noticed that I am the only person anybody asks that.”

“Not one of them has asked another keeper whether another keeper’s book is right. They have come to the woman with the ninth run, because I am the one with the run, and so the whole of what this county has got for checking itself is a clerk in a room four hundred miles off who was not there.”

“That is not a complaint you can make to anybody.”

“No. And I am not making it as a complaint. I am making it because I am the one it happens to, and because a clerk who never notices that is a clerk who will go on agreeing with things she has not seen for the rest of her life, and I have decided this morning that I would rather be the sort of clerk who says that out loud.”

“Does that make you less use.”

“It makes me a different sort of use and I do not know yet whether anybody wants it, and I am not going to go four hundred miles to find out.”

Then Wil Cairns came down off the high side and sat on the bottom step with the hook across his knees, and the bottom step is the step everybody sits on, and he had not been asked for anything and said what he had come down for.

Wil Cairns keeps a hedge along the top of that drain where the parish line runs, from the wall above the ash for about half a mile, and there is no stone and no line and nobody after him.

“I have not been asked for anything and I am going to say a thing anyway, because I have been carrying it down that bank since Monday and if I put it back up there it will be in my head until Sunday.”

“Say it out loud, then.”

“Before the frost came the ground on my side went off the bank about where the third stake is and I did not see it happen and I know it happened because a stone I put in in the first year is not where I put it. That is all of it. I have had a fortnight to decide whether to say it and I have said it.”

“To Cairns, to the man on the other side of the hedge.”

“To the man on the other side of the hedge. And if I say it to him, then he has a thing I have not checked and he will come and look, and if he comes and looks, we will walk it, and the walk is the end of it, and the end of it is that one of us is wrong about nine years.”

“So you will not say it.”

“I have not said it. And you may notice that I have said the reason out loud in front of people and I still have not said it.”

“And I have worked out what I am going to do instead, so that you do not go up that road and think there is nothing here.”

“I am going to move my stakes. All of them, along the whole half mile, onto the line I believe is, and then I am going to cut that hedge the way I have always cut it and I am not going to say one word to him about it. And if he says nothing, then he has said nothing, and the hedge goes on with one set of stakes in it, and nobody in this parish will ever know it was settled.”

“That is not walking it with him.”

“That is walking it without him and it took me a fortnight. And if I am wrong then I shall have spent the rest of the cutting season on a line that is not mine, and there will be no way anybody can tell me, because the only person who could tell me is the one I am not going to ask.”

“I will know about it on my own or I will not know about it at all. And I have had nine years to find out that a thing you know and a thing that is written are different sizes, so I am prepared to be wrong in a way that nobody ever checks.”

Then the boy of thirteen came up out of the lane and gave his figure and gave nothing else at all, and did not refuse the count out loud, which he has been doing all week, and that was noticed.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and fifty-nine days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and fifty-nine is nine hundred and twelve less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and fifty-nine days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.”

“Nobody asked me for the count I am keeping,” he said, after a while, “and I am not going to talk about the count I am keeping, and I have said that in a different way every morning this week and I am not going to say it again this morning. So that is all. That is the whole of what I have got.”

He was on the wall above the ash by the sixth hour and off it before the seventh, and nobody stopped him and nobody asked him where he went, and both of those were the correct size for a boy of thirteen who had nothing to be asked about.

Then Barnaby Crove asked a man what he had done on a morning, and the man had been asked the same question by a woman on Sunday, and the words he gave were the same words, and the man who had asked said the thing out loud before anybody else on that step worked out that he had it twice.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for a hundred and ninety-one mornings, which is nine hundred and twelve less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“A hundred and ninety-one. And I asked that man the same question a woman asked him on Sunday, on the same morning, about the same day, and he gave me the same words, and I have now got the thing twice.”

“Two men’s accounts of one morning.”

“No. It is one man’s account of one morning, said twice, and I am the second person holding it, and I want to be careful here because I have watched men get this wrong my whole life. Two men saying the same thing is not a check. One man saying the same thing twice is not even two men. It is one morning and one mouth and me, and I have not got anything I did not have on Wednesday except the knowledge that he does not change his mind.”

“Then why did you ask him.”

“Because I had to find out. Not for the answer. Because a man can hear the same answer twice and still not know what he is holding, and I did not know what I was holding, and now I know, and what I know is that I am holding a thing about a man and not a thing about the ground.”

He did not tell anybody else what the words had been, and a woman on the step said *you have got it twice and you have not got it twice*, and he said *that is what I have just said*, and neither of them was thanked by anybody for either half of it.

The bank sat at sixteen for the fifth morning after noon and two days off it, and the man who holds that landing had said nothing beyond three figures for two days running, and a man who cuts a hedge had carried a thing down that bank for two days and put it back up there, and a boy of thirteen had said the whole of what he had by saying nothing.

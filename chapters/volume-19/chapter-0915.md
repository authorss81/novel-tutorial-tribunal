# Chapter 915: A Week Is A Thing The Ninth Run Invented For Itself

Seventh and last day of the hundred and fifty-second week. His six hundred and sixty-fifth morning. Five hundred and fifteen days after the settlement. The fever ninety weeks and two days old. Four hundred and sixty-nine days since the division. Four hundred and fifteen days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Four inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, seven days of the coming back, and about ninety of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave the three figures and gave the shape of a week coming to its end, which he does on the seventh day because on the seventh day the week stops being a thing anybody can use.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, and has learned that this week runs from a Thursday to a Monday and that most of what goes wrong on that landing goes wrong on the Monday.

“Four at the seventh hour, seven days of the coming back, and about ninety of those ninety steps under, on the seventh and last day of the hundred and fifty-second week, and it is a Monday.”

“This week is out at midnight. Tomorrow is the first day of the hundred and fifty-third week and I will say three figures again at about the seventh hour and they will be different and I will not be able to tell you in advance which. That is the ordinary way and there is nothing about it I want anybody to make a thing of.”

Then the woman who keeps twenty-nine chairs came up at the tenth hour and said that she had spent a week watching and had come to say what she had watched, and that what she had watched had made her own business worse and she was going to say it anyway.

Orla Fennimore is forty-four, has kept twenty-nine chairs for nineteen years at thirteen shillings and fourpence a year, has put her question down five times, and is not going to put it down a sixth. She was asked what she did on one morning by a woman who holds an account, once, and has not been asked again.

“A woman asked me on Tuesday what I did on a Wednesday in June, once, and told me before she asked that she would not ask me again, and she has not, and I want to say something about that and I have been standing in that yard for a week working out how to say it without it sounding like gratitude, because it is not gratitude and I am not going to thank anybody for it.”

“What is it, then.”

“It is that it has made my question harder.” She put her hand on the door frame.

“I have been the woman who asks since I was twenty-five and every one of those five times I wrote to ask was a time I expected something back. Once somebody asks me instead, once, and then does not ask me again, and it turns out that what I wanted was not the asking.”

“What I wanted was the difference between being asked once and being asked twice. Nobody in this county had ever asked me anything, and I had assumed in nineteen years that the thing I was owed was an answer, and I was not owed an answer. I was owed a question with nothing after it.”

“That is a thing to have found out at forty-four.”

“That is a thing I have found out at forty-four, on a Monday, and I am not going to make it more than it is because I have had a week to think about it and it has not grown. It is a small thing and I said on Tuesday that it is small and I am not going to stop saying it.”

“Who else have you been watching.”

“One man. Thirty-eight, with a board in his coat. He asked a woman the same question on Sunday that she had already been asked, and she told him in advance what he would get, and he came anyway and got it, and I have been watching him every day since to see whether he is a liar or a fool, and he is neither, and that is the part I cannot put down.”

“Not either.”

“He was not lying when he said he wanted to know what to do with the board and he was not wrong to go and get it. He wanted a second answer to be a second kind of thing and it is not, and he found that out the only way anybody can find anything out on this landing, which is by going and looking, and it has cost him a morning and it has not cost her anything except the standing up.”

“Has it cost you anything.”

“It has cost me the last of a belief. I had thought for nineteen years that if somebody would just ask the right question once and twice, properly, then something would come out of it.”

“And there is one man on this landing doing it exactly as I imagined and it is not coming out of it, and I would rather have found that out this week than in ten years, and I am not going to be thanked for finding it out, and I am also not going to be comforted about it, and I would like everybody here to notice that those are two different things and that I am only asking for the first.”

Then Pol Dunnage came up the cart road with flour on his hands and a piece of chalk in his coat, and said the thing he keeps not saying, and it turned out to be about a wall.

Pol Dunnage keeps the bakehouse four miles up that cart road, in the village the road goes through, is the second house on the left after the wall, and came up the road on a Friday morning with a cold chisel in his coat where anybody could see it.

“The damp in that wall has got worse since Michaelmas and I have done nothing about it, and I am going to say that on this step to a man I have not met, and I would like it understood that I am not asking you to advise me and I am not going to advise you.”

“What are you doing about it.”

“Nothing, and here is the part I cannot get past. In a bakehouse there is a list of everything I have done this year and I keep it in my head and it is not a very long list. And there is also everything I have not done, and that list is longer, and it is all wall. If I wrote the second list down I would have a book with a great many days on it and nothing on them but me deciding not to, and I would not want to open that in the dark half of the year.”

“So you have decided not to.”

“I have decided to go on deciding not to, and I will decide differently when the wall makes me, and not before. And that is not a thing anybody has taught me. A man who works a fire for thirty years decides things later than other people, and if he decides early he is guessing.”

“That is not an account of anything.”

“No. And that is what I have instead of one. You ask me what I did on a morning and I can tell you what I did on a morning and it is short and it is true, and I have been in that bakehouse since the fourth hour, and there is no more to it than that, and the wall is not in it because the wall is a thing I did not do.”

He put the chalk back in his coat and looked at the water going over the step and did not say anything else about it.

Wat Marshe was on the wall above the ash and gave his figure and then said the thing he had been thinking about since the sixth hour, which was about a number and not about himself.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and sixty-two days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and sixty-two is nine hundred and fifteen less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and sixty-two days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up. And I have worked out that a week is seven days and that nobody down there uses a week for anything. She uses a day. He uses a day. She uses a day and he uses a day and the man on the steps uses a day. And the woman who writes in the room uses seven at a time and calls it one.”

“Which of those is right.”

“They are both right and they are not the same and I cannot say what that is yet and I am not going to try in front of people, and that is the whole of what I have to say today, and I said it on a Monday and I am not going to say it twice.”

Sena Dorr entered today’s line on the ninth run at about the tenth hour and read it out herself on the seventh and last day of the hundred and fifty-second week, and said the thing about weeks that she had been keeping since Sunday.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, has read for a living for twenty-three years in a room four hundred miles off, and uses a week of her own ruling that nothing else on that landing has ever used.

“That is the fifty-eighth morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing.”

“A week is a thing the ninth run invented for its own convenience and I am the one who uses it and nobody else on that landing has ever used one. It is a line of my own ruling and it goes up and down my own book, and every keeper down there works in days, and a day is the size of a thing they did or did not do.”

“If I put a week against a keeper’s day then I have made a thing that is neither and it will be read as both, and in a year somebody will take a week out of my book and hold it against a morning and be wrong and not know it.”

“Does that make your book the wrong shape.”

“It makes my book the shape it has been for twenty-three years. And I am aware that a book shaped like that is easier to look at from a long way off, and that is the whole of my interest in it. Nobody in this county has asked me what it is for and I am not going to be asked, and if I was asked I would tell them it is for one day at a time and that is all it has ever been for.”

“The arrangement is four hundred miles off and it is still answering. It did not answer this week, and nobody in this county is in the room where it answers, and nobody was asked to go, and I did not ask anybody to go, and I have put no day on it, and this morning it is the last of a week and I am not going to put a week on it either.”

Then a man of thirty-one said that a thing a man did not do was as much a part of his morning as a thing he did, and was refused by a man with flour on his hands in a flat voice and without any unkindness in it.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and sixty-one days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and thirty days old.

“Three hundred and sixty-one days in this county, and the cut across this palm is two hundred and thirty days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and fifteen days old, which is nine hundred and fifteen less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Fifty-five wrong things in fifty-five mornings, and here is the fifty-fifth. He has just told you he is not going to cut that wall until the wall makes him, and that is a decision and it is a part of what he does and not a gap in it. A morning with a thing decided against in it is a morning with something in it.”

“No,” said Pol Dunnage.

“It is not. A thing decided against has no shape.”

“If you came up that road on Thursday and asked me what I did on Wednesday about that wall, and I said I decided again not to, then you would have what I said and not one thing you could hold, and you would have come back on Friday and asked me again and got the same four words, and by the end of a second week you would have a second week of me saying no to a wall and the wall would still be a wall and there would be one more thing in the world and it would be a thing you made out of me.”

“That is a very large thing to have been put in charge of.”

“It is not in charge of anything. It is in a book you have not written and it will not be in a book you never write, and I would rather have the wall than the book, and I am not going to discuss it on a step.”

He was not told he was wrong and nobody said he was right, and the week went on towards midnight with the bank down at four inches and seven days of it to run.

Then Barnaby Crove came up last and gave his figure, and said that he had made a decision about his own problem, and that it was a bad one, and that he had made it on purpose.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He has asked for a day against the ninth line for a hundred and ninety-four mornings, which is nine hundred and fifteen less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“A hundred and ninety-fourth. And I have decided that I am going to go on asking and go on not saying, and I know what it is now, I said on Saturday it was a habit and I have been right about that, and I am going to keep doing it, and I would like it written down that I decided it and not that it happened to me.”

“Why would you want it written down when you will not write a word of theirs down.”

“Because it is the first thing this fortnight that has been mine to decide and I would like somebody to know I decided it. That is not a big want. I have a child of forty-one years who does not ask me about any of this, and I am not going to tell him about a bank or a ninth line or a hedge, and there is nobody else, and you are all going to be gone in a year one way or another.”

Nobody on that step offered him anything for saying it, and a woman who keeps twenty-nine chairs said *that is the first true thing you have said on a Monday all week and it is about you*, and he said *I know, that is why I said it and that is why it will not get me anywhere*, and the week went out at midnight.

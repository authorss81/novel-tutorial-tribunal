# Chapter 936: A Week Goes Out Tonight, And A Man With A Byre Has Put A Person In His Line

Seventh and last day of the hundred and fifty-fifth week. His six hundred and eighty-sixth morning. Five hundred and thirty-six days after the settlement. The fever ninety-three weeks and two days old. Four hundred and ninety days since the division. Four hundred and thirty-six days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, and it held at that figure, two days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and then gave the week its own figure, and gave it once, in the plainest words he has, and said that he could not tell anybody in advance what the new week would bring.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and gives the closing figure of a week once a week and does not give anybody the opening figure of the next one.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, two days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the seventh and last day of the hundred and fifty-fifth week, and it is a Monday.”

“This week is out at midnight. Tomorrow is the first day of the hundred and fifty-sixth week and I will say three figures again at about the seventh hour and they will be different and I will not be able to tell you in advance which, and I am telling you that now so that when I get it wrong on Tuesday nobody has to decide whether I was keeping something.”

“You have never kept anything.”

“No. I have said every morning for nineteen years the three things the stone has and nothing else, and that is not keeping anything, that is the job.” He set the broom against the wall. “What I am not able to do is guess. And I know exactly what I said last week I would not be able to do and I am saying it again in the same words so that the week has one shape and not two.”

Then a man came down off the turn with the smell of a byre on him and stood at the bottom of the steps without saying anything for a while, and then said the thing he had come to say, and it was the thing he swore on a Sunday a fortnight ago would never happen.

Ned Farrow keeps a byre a mile past the turn, off the lane that forks for the upper farms, has a line a day in it, and put a person in that line on Sunday.

“I asked a man on Sunday morning what he did yesterday and he told me and I have written it in the line.”

Nobody on that step said anything.

“I know what I said on the Sunday before. I said if that line ever gets a person in it then the next man opens that book to find out which calf came in on the fourth and he has to read past it, and in ten years he reads past a great deal, and one day he reads past something that matters to somebody and he does not know he did it. I said it on that step. I said it out loud. And on Monday I wrote the thing in.”

“Why not, then.”

“Because I have not put a person in that line in twenty-two years and I said on that Sunday that a line that has never had a person in it is the only thing about me that I was proud of, and then I sat in that yard and worked out that I was not proud of it. I was frightened of it. And a line you are frightened of is not a record. It is a wall with cattle on the front of it.”

“Nobody asked you to do that.”

“Nobody asked me to do anything on Sunday and that is the whole reason I did it.” He had his cap in his hands and he did not put it on. “And I want it said that I have not read it back. It is in and I have not taken it out and I have not opened the book to look at it.”

“You have put a man’s Sunday into a book about cattle.”

“He will not.”

“What is he going to do when he finds out.”

“He will not find out. That book is read by me and by nobody else and I am not going to tell him.” He looked up at the sixteen inches. “So there is a man’s Sunday in a byre book and the only person in this county who knows it is in it is me, and I have held every other thing in that yard for twenty-two years. I have made myself the only keeper on this landing who keeps a thing nobody can check, and I did that on purpose, and I want somebody to tell me what that is.”

Nobody told him what that was. Then the man of thirty-one said that it was a lesson, and it was not a lesson, and the man with the byre took it away from him in eleven words.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and eighty-two days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and fifty-one days old.

“Three hundred and eighty-two days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and fifty-one days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundredth is two hundred and thirty-six days old, which is nine hundred and thirty-six less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“Seventy-six wrong things in seventy-six mornings, and here is the seventy-sixth. That is a lesson. He has just told you that you can hold a thing nobody can check, and it is, and the reason it is is that nobody has ever asked you.”

“It is not for you to hand out.”

“It is not for me to hand out and I am not going to and you have not asked me for it. And here is the reason nobody has asked you, and I am going to say it once because a man stood at the top of that step on Sunday and told me not to hand him anything, and I have listened, and this is me handing it to a man with a byre instead of to the step.”

He looked at the water. “You have never been asked because nobody in this county can stand where you are standing. You are the only man on this landing who was in front of all of it. That is not the reason you cannot be asked. That is the reason it would not help.”

“Not the same man twice in one morning.”

“Seventy-six wrong things in seventy-six mornings, and here is the seventy-sixth,” said a woman from the top of the steps, and everybody turned round.

Orla Fennimore is forty-four, has kept twenty-nine chairs for nineteen years, has put her question down five times and refused a sixth, and was asked what she did once and is not going to be asked again by anybody, and had been on the step from seven in the morning and had heard all of it.

“You have said it twice because the first one came out of you before you heard yourself say it. I am going to let the second one stand because it is true and because the first one was not, and you have had a hard week and I am not going to add to it in front of these people.”

“I did not know you were on the step.”

“No. You have spent a fortnight working out which mornings I am on and that is not a thing anybody is helped by.” She came down one step and stopped. “And you have said that nobody has not asked you is because you cannot be found, and I am going to tell you the half of that which is mine. A man stood next to me for nineteen years and he has not once been asked what he did either, and neither have I, and neither of us has asked the other. That is not because we cannot be found. It is because we are both of us standing in it.”

“Have you ever wanted him to.”

“Yes.” She said it without anything around it. “For about eleven years. And I am not going to say any more about it than that this morning and I am not going to be asked about it by anybody, and if one of you tries it this is what will happen and you will all be able to say afterwards that I warned you.”

Nobody tried it. Then the boy came up and gave his figure and said that he had been awake since the second hour on the top step and had decided to come down, and that he wanted to give the figure to the man who holds this landing and not to the whole step, and that he was not going to do it, and wanted it recorded that he had wanted to.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and eighty-three days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and eighty-three is nine hundred and thirty-six less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and eighty-three days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up. And I got the figure ready at the second hour and came down here at the sixth, and I worked out on the way what I was bringing it for and it was for the man with the broom, because he is the only person on this step who does not already have something today.”

“Why not give it to him.”

“Because he did not ask and because I would like to have given it and him not ask, and I do not know which of those two things is why I got up at the second hour instead of the fifth.” He turned it over. “And that is a new thing and I have not worked out what it is, and I am not going to be told, so nobody had better try.”

“You have not given it.”

“I have not given it and I am going to give it to him on a morning he asks me, and there is nobody on this landing who is going to ask me, and that is the arrangement and I made it and I am not going to be the one who complains about it, and that is twice now I have said that and I know it is twice.”

Then the woman of forty-nine came up the road with the ninth run under her arm and put it on the low wall and entered the morning and read it out, and said that this week had been the thinnest she had had in a long time and that she had entered it anyway and that a thin week is a week and not a gap.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years in a room four hundred miles off, and holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and has entered a week in which two people said a thing out loud that they could not take back and nobody put a hand on anybody.

“That is the seventy-ninth morning in front of anybody, and it is a figure of mornings, and I entered it, and there was no gap in it while I was writing.”

“Seventy-nine.”

“I want to say one thing about seventy-nine and then I am going up that road.” She closed the book on her thumb. “Two people said things this week in front of all of us and that nobody entered, and nobody will enter them, and this week is going into that book as a week with nothing on it. And that is right. And it is the first week I have ever entered that I knew something about that is not going in. And I am not going to stop entering weeks because of it, and nobody is to say that I have taken something on.”

“Nobody is going to say anything.”

“Then I will go up.” She got the book under her arm. “And the arrangement is four hundred miles off and it is still answering and it did not answer this week, and nobody in this county is in the room where it answers, and I have put no day on it, and the woman keeping the pound and I have not spoken, and that is a thing I have not put in the book either.”

Nobody on that landing was relieved and nobody was thanked for anything, and a week went out at midnight with three things said on it that nobody could put down, and a man with a byre had made himself the only keeper on that landing who kept something nobody could ask about.

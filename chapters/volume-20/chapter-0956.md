# Chapter 956: Eight Inches On The Top Step At About The Seventh Hour, About Eighty Of Those Ninety Steps Under, Six Days Of The Coming Back, And A Man Said One Whole Thing Out Loud On Purpose

Sixth day of the hundred and fifty-eighth week. His seven hundred and sixth morning. Five hundred and fifty-six days after the settlement. The fever ninety-six weeks and one day old. Five hundred and ten days since the division. Four hundred and fifty-six days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and two days in this county of Kell.

Eight inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, about eighty of those ninety steps under, and six days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures, and then he gave the fourth thing that is not about the water, which he has never done before and which he said out loud on purpose because he had not said it before.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the sixth day of the hundred and fifty-eighth week the whole of one rule about the thing in the fortieth step, because nineteen years of saying nothing about it was a habit and not a rule.

“Eight at the seventh hour, six days of the coming back, and about eighty of those ninety steps under, on the sixth day of the hundred and fifty-eighth week, and it is a Sunday. Three figures.” He set the broom against the wall and did not go down. “And I am going to say a fourth thing this morning and I am going to say it because I have worked out this week that nineteen years of saying nothing is a habit and not a rule. A habit is a thing you do because you did it last time. That is not what I have got.”

“What you have got.”

“Here is what I have got. Nothing that has been put down anywhere is touched. Not pulled. Not turned over. Not weighed in the hand. Not read out loud. Not carried inside. Not put back where it was. And not used.” He counted them on his fingers and did not look at his fingers. “That is seven things and it is not a figure of anybody and it is not a fifth figure off my stone. It is the rule on that landing and I am saying it now because it has been going round this week in about nine half-versions and I would rather it went round once from me.”

“That is a rule.”

“That is a rule and it is the first one I have ever said out loud, and I want it known that it is a rule and not a virtue, because a virtue is a thing you are praised for and I am not going to be standing on this step being praised about a corner of it.” He picked the broom up again.

“And the reason for it is not moral. It is that the only way anybody in this county would ever find out what that thing is holding is to pull it out. And that is an experiment, and an experiment has to be run by somebody with standing to run it, and there is nobody on this landing with standing to run it and there is not going to be one.”

“If a man runs it because nobody stopped him then he has not found out anything. He has only found out that he was the one who did it.”

Corvin Tace came down the steps and went by the fortieth one without stopping and carried on.

“And I am not going to be asked to pull it. Nobody is going to ask me. And if anybody in this county asks me, they will be asking a man with a broom and nineteen years, and I will say no, and they will go and ask somebody else, and that somebody else will not have a broom and will have a lever, and then this landing will have done it out of plain slowness, without anybody meaning to.”

He swept the top step and came down to the bottom of it and stopped.

“That is the whole of the fourth thing. There will not be a fifth.”

Then the woman who reads the ninth run came up the road and entered the morning and had something in it for the first time this week, and would not say what.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years, holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, has one question she has decided to ask and has not asked it, and has decided this week that she is not going to ask it.

“That is the ninety-ninth morning in front of anybody, and I entered it, and there is a thing in it this morning and I am not going to say what it is.”

“You wrote a day.”

“I wrote a place.” She had the book shut and kept it shut. “Two mornings ago a man on this step asked me for six stones and I told him the day was the part he could not have. And this week I have done what I told him to do, because I have had nothing else to do since Wednesday and it turns out I am very good at it. I have written down a place. No day on it. No morning. No what was said and no who was standing there. One place, and it is a place a person works, and I am not going to say which one.”

“Why not.”

“Because of the two other things.” She held the book against her knee. “The first is that the day I say a place out loud, it stops being a place in a morning and becomes a place in a county, and then it is a thing people can walk to. And then whoever put something there has got a road to their own front door whether they wanted one or not. The second is that I would like to be the person who wrote it down and not the person who handed it round, and I have spent twenty-three years being the person somebody hands it to.”

Nobody asked her which place. Nobody asked her to write another one.

“You have not asked your question and I have not asked it either, and I would like that noticed.”

“I have not, and I have stopped going to, which is a different thing and I am not going to soften it into the other.” She put the book inside her coat. “I had one question and it was going to be asked on a morning with nothing on the stone so that it could not be said to be about the stone, and there was one on the fourth morning of this week and I stood on it and said nothing, and I have made my choice about it since and I chose badly and I am not going to be comforted about it by anybody here.”

The man who moves chairs came up the cart road and went past the wall and up to the step and stood at the back of it, where he can see the road down to the village, and did not go down it.

Aldis Rook moves the chairs for Orla Fennimore and keeps nothing and has no stone and has no seat and no board, has never once been asked what he did, and stood at the back of that landing five mornings with his hands behind him and did not go to the door.

“I have been up here every morning this week and I have not gone to that door once, and I am not going to this morning either.” He looked down the road. “And I am going to say one more thing and then I am going to be quiet all week. Earlier this morning a man with a broom said that nineteen years of saying nothing is a habit and not a rule, and I have been sitting on that. I move chairs. That is my rule and I have never said it out loud because a man who moves chairs has no business standing on a step giving rules.”

Nobody stopped him.

“And here it is, and it is one sentence and then I am finished. A thing put down where somebody works is not a given thing. A chair I carry into a house is not a chair I have given anybody. It is a chair I have put down where somebody works, I am not in that house, I do not work in it, I am not coming back in the morning to put another one down, and the person it went in for is not going to be asked whether they took it or whether they liked it or whether they have moved it.”

He took his hands out from behind him, which nobody on that landing had seen him do.

“That is my whole trade. I have been doing the whole of this week’s business since I was sixteen and I did not know there was a word for it and I am not after a word for it this morning. I have got about nine houses left to move into before the end of it and every one of them I am going to do exactly the way I have always done it, and not one of the people in them is going to hear a word about it, and I am not going to be thanked, and I would like it said here on this step that I am not asking for it either.”

Nobody thanked him. Nobody told him it was a good rule. Nobody asked him where the nine houses were.

Then the man of thirty-one came up and gave his four figures and said the thing out loud that he has worked out about himself, and the man of forty-three refused it in a sentence.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and two days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and seventy-one days old.

“Four hundred and two days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and seventy-one days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-sixth is two hundred and fifty-six days old, which is nine hundred and fifty-six less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

He said the rest of it to the steps and not to anybody.

“Ninety-six wrong things in ninety-six mornings, and here is the ninety-sixth, and I am going to say it and it is not clever. I have worked out this week that the one thing left in this world that costs me nothing is asking. Asking is free. It has been free since before I came to this county and it will be free on the last morning I am here. And I have worked out that a man who asks every morning is nobody, and always was, and that I have spent eleven years trying to be somebody and the only thing I have found that comes for nothing is the one thing that does not make you anybody.”

Corvin Tace did not look round.

“That is the plainest thing said on this step in nineteen years and I am not going to agree with it and I am not going to correct it.”

“It is not wrong.”

“No. And I am not going to be the man who agrees with a thing on the second morning of hearing it, and that is not me being hard on you, that is me keeping one thing back so that you have to find out whether you meant it by Wednesday.” He put the broom down. “Go down the bank. That is not advice. That is the only thing on this landing I would pay a man to hear and I am not paying you in anything.”

He went down the bank at about the ninth hour and did not turn round at the wall, which is the first time in this business that anybody on that landing has watched him not turn round at the wall.

The man of forty-three swept his way up eighty-nine steps and stopped at the top of the bank with the broom in his hand, and did not put his weight on the fortieth one at any point in the morning, and did not touch it, and nothing that had been put down anywhere was pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used.

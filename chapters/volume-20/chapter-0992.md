# Chapter 992: The Last Morning Of The Hundred And Sixty-Third Week And Two Pieces Of Wood On A Dry Strip Where There Was One, And A Woman Who Saws Lengths Stood At The Bottom Of The Steps And Said Out Loud What She Thought The Second Of Them Was, And It Was Not What It Was, And The Man Who Put It There Was Standing At The Top Of The Steps And Could Not Say So

Seventh and last day of the hundred and sixty-third week. His seven hundred and forty-second morning. Five hundred and ninety-two days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and one weeks and two days old. Five hundred and forty-six days since the division. Four hundred and ninety-two days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and thirty-eight days in this county of Kell.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and it holds, about sixty of those ninety steps under, and two days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and then said that the week would turn in the night and that he was not going to say one word about that either.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the seventh and last day of the hundred and sixty-third week that he had swept that stone before the light and had swept round two pieces of wood and that he was not going to say anything about either of them, because nobody had asked him.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, two days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the seventh and last day of the hundred and sixty-third week, and it is a Monday. Three figures.”

He propped the broom against the foot of the wall, clear of the strip.

“Two pieces of wood on that strip this morning where there was one yesterday, and I swept round both, and that is the whole of what I have got to say about it. I have not got a fifth figure and I am not going to invent one about a piece of ash.”

Nobody asked him for a fifth figure and nobody thanked him for not inventing one.

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour and asked in the same words at the same hour and was refused in the same way and was not thanked, and said out loud that a Monday was the same as a Sunday and he was going to find out whether it was.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and seventy-first mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is nine hundred and ninety-two less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and seventy-first. Nine hundred and ninety-two less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

“Nobody has ever asked you for that.”

“No.” He set the barrow down. “Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it. And I have noticed there is a Monday and I have noticed that a Monday has not made a difference, and I am going to keep noticing it, and that is not a new thing and I am not going to bring you one.”

Nobody answered Barnaby Crove on that Monday, nobody told him he was owed an answer, and nobody thanked him for noticing it.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the eighth hour with sixteen inches above his boots and gave his figures and gave the figure of the offer with the subtraction out loud and asked for nothing and did not go down that bank again.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and thirty-eight days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and seven days old.

“Four hundred and thirty-eight days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and seven days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-forty-second is two hundred and ninety-two days old, which is nine hundred and ninety-two less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it and I am not going to ask it and nobody has offered me a way out of it.”

He got to the top of the steps.

“One hundred and thirty-two wrong things in one hundred and thirty-two mornings, and this is the hundred and thirty-second, and nobody is going to tell me what kind of figure that is and nobody is going to tell me that I found anything.”

He was told neither.

“I came up that bank this morning at the same hour I came up it yesterday and I want somebody to notice that. I did not come up at a different hour and I did not come up earlier. I have had four hundred and thirty-eight mornings to work out an hour that would have suited me and I have not found one.”

Nobody noticed and nobody said it suited him.

Then the woman who saws lengths came up the cart road with her saw handle under her arm and stopped at the bottom of the steps and looked up at the dry strip for a while before she said anything at all, and nobody hurried her.

Wenna Pyle is thirty-eight, who saws lengths and sets studs at a bench in an open-sided shed behind a wheelwright’s about three hundred yards down the cart road, and who carries her own cup out to that bench because there is no table in the shed, and who said on the seventh and last day of the hundred and sixty-third week that a pair of notches cut for a wedge was the end of a handle and not anything else that anybody wanted to hear it was.

“That is a haft.” She said it the way she says what a thing is when she has just seen it. “Those two notches are cut for a wedge, and one of them has run out of the side because the saw went off at the corner. A wedge driven into the end of a haft is the whole of what stops a handle coming out of its eye.”

She stayed at the foot of the steps.

“So that is ash for a handle. It is not a length for burning and it is not a thing to be carried about in. Whoever it is cut for, it is cut for something that has an eye in it.”

Nobody touched it while she was looking at it.

“And I am going to say the next part out loud on purpose, before any of you puts a name to it and gets it wrong, because that is what happens to wood on a landing.” She turned the saw handle over in her hand. “The only eye on this landing is at the head of a shaft, and the only barrow that comes up this bank belongs to the man who holds the gravel. So that is what a woman who saws lengths would say about it, and I am not giving it to anybody, I am saying it before it gets said wrong.”

Nobody thanked her for saying it and nobody told her she had got it right.

“And I will take it back if it turns out to be something else, and I will say so in my own mouth and in front of all of you, and I will not be thanked for that either when it happens.”

Then the man of fifty-four said it from the barrow without turning round, and he said it into the middle distance, which is where he says things.

“Nobody has asked me for a barrow haft.” He shifted his hands on the handles. “And nobody on this landing has told me that is what that is, and I have been up this bank every morning of this county and nobody has ever given me a thing before, and I am not going to have one put down on my account because a woman saw a notch in it.”

Nobody answered that either, and nobody said he had been given a thing before or not, and nobody thanked Barnaby Crove for saying it out loud.

Then the man who cannot see well came up onto the flat of the steps at about the tenth hour with his hand out in front of him and found the wall with it, and asked whether the thing had been touched, and nobody had touched it.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and said on the last morning of the hundred and sixty-third week that he had heard a woman give a thing a name on that landing and had not asked what the name was, and that not asking was now harder than it had been on Sunday.

“I heard it. I was up at the top of the steps and I could not make out one word of what the word was and that is not a complaint about my eyes, it is a fact about what I heard.” His hand stayed on the stone. “And I am not going to ask what the name is. I have had a thing on my list since Sunday morning and the thing on my list is not the name. It is that somebody named a piece of wood, and I am not going to have a name in my head where a name would let me pick it up.”

Nobody handed him a name and nobody told him he had been careful.

“And I am going to say the seven out loud because nobody else has said them yet today and I am tired of waiting, and I would like a man who sweeps this stone to take a turn at them one of these mornings and not to be asked whether he feels like it.” He said them in the order they go, flat, against the wall.

Nobody took a turn and nobody thanked him.

The man of thirty-one was standing at the top of the steps when she said it, with his hand on the wall above the dry strip, and he had the word for it ready before she opened her mouth. He knew what those two notches were cut for because he had held the saw that cut them.

He did not say it. The sentence that would have corrected it was shorter than any of the others he had said on that landing this fortnight and it was the only one he had ever had that began with the word *that*.

Nobody told him he ought to say it. Nobody told him he ought not to say it either. Nobody looked at him while he was standing there, and nobody looked away from him in a way that was on purpose.

He stayed on that landing until the light went off the wall.

“It has not been touched.” He said the seven in the order they go, flat, without stopping in front of them. “Not pulled, not turned over, not weighed in the hand, not read out loud, not carried inside, not put back where it was, and not used. Not the first piece and not the second. I have said them before on this landing and I am not going to be thanked for saying them and I am not going to be asked to say them again after this.”

Nothing on that landing was pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used. Nobody on that landing touched either piece of ash, and nobody asked the man who had put the second one there a single question about it.

A woman who saws lengths for a living looked at a piece of wood and gave the working and named it out loud, and it is not what it was. The man who put it there was standing at the top of the steps the whole of the time. Nobody told him what to do about that, in either direction, and the week went on being a week.

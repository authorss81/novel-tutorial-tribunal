# Chapter 990: A Saturday And A Full Bank And One Hundred And One Weeks Said Out Loud At The Sixth Hour By A Man Who Cannot Read For The Sixth Time And Nobody Worked Out And Nobody Asked Him What It Was For, And A Man Who Moves Furniture Said The Whole Of What He Knows Out Loud And Gave It No Name, And A Woman Of Forty-Four At The Wall Said Before Anybody Thanked Her That She Is Not In It

Fifth day of the hundred and sixty-third week. His seven hundred and fortieth morning. Five hundred and ninety days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and one weeks old. Five hundred and forty-four days since the division. Four hundred and ninety days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and thirty-six days in this county of Kell.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and that was a full bank, four days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave those three figures. 

The fever came out as a whole figure of weeks for the sixth time since a man who cannot read began saying so out loud, and a man had carried a thing up that bank a week before and said out loud that he put it there, and a bank was full again, and those three things are being written once in this line and are not going to be said again.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the fifth day of the hundred and sixty-third week that he was not going to let a bank that was full become a morning that anything happened on, and that he was also not going to count what was standing on his landing.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and that is a full bank, four days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the fifth day of the hundred and sixty-third week, and it is a Saturday. Three figures.”

He leaned the broom against the wall on the dry strip, clear of the ash, in the place it has stood all week.

“There is a figure somebody could stand up here this morning and give out loud. Not a figure of days and not a figure of inches. A figure of things.” He looked down the bank and did not look at the strip. “I am not going to give it and I am not going to ask anybody else to give it, and it is not that nobody here could count.”

“It is that once that is said out loud on this landing then the landing is a number and everything on it is a line under that number, including the iron in the fortieth step, and including the wood, and I have had nineteen years of the other thing and I am not giving it up on a Saturday because a man said a whole figure of weeks at the sixth hour.”

Nothing was counted on that landing.

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour and gave a whole figure out loud before anybody on that landing had finished its morning, and asked his question in the same words at the same hour, and was refused in the same way and was not thanked.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and sixty-ninth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is nine hundred and ninety less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. And on the fifth day of the hundred and sixty-third week, a Saturday, he gave a whole figure out loud at the sixth hour before anybody worked it out for him.

“One hundred and one weeks.” He put both hands flat on the wall, which is what he does. “Not one hundred and one weeks and a bit. One hundred and one weeks, on this Saturday, and it is the sixth one of those since I started saying it out loud.”

It was not worked out for him on that landing.

“And it has come out whole six times and it has gone up by one each time, and it was ninety-six weeks the first morning I stood up here and said one out loud.” He took his hands off the wall. “And I am going to say the thing I have noticed six times now and I have never said. Nobody on this step has ever asked me what one of these is for.”

“Not once in six of them, and one of you came up here on the fifth of these and had it in your mouth and shut it, and I watched you shut it.”

The mouth that had shut drew no comment.

“And I am not going to ask you to ask me. I would have asked you by now if I was going to.” He set the barrow down. “Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.”

“And it is a Saturday and it is a full bank and I have got a whole figure in my mouth that nobody on this landing will explain to me, and I am going to ask my question anyway, and I am not going to use the number to get at it, and I am not going to be thanked.”

Nobody answered him on a Saturday with a full bank behind him, nobody told Barnaby Crove he was owed an answer, and nobody thanked him, and nobody explained one hundred and one weeks to him, and the morning went on.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the ninth hour, and gave his figures, and gave the figure of the offer with the subtraction out loud, and it was unanswered, and he asked for nothing.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and thirty-six days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and five days old.

“Four hundred and thirty-six days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and five days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-fortieth is two hundred and ninety days old, which is nine hundred and ninety less seven hundred.” He said it without stopping on it. “It is unanswered.”

“I have not asked it on any morning of this week and I am not going to ask it at the end of one either, and nobody has offered me a way out of it, and I am still standing here with it, and I have said that sentence in one shape or another on every morning of this county and I am not going to start varying it now.”

Nobody answered him and nobody offered him a way out of it.

Then the man who moves chairs came up the cart road without a barrow and stood at the flat of the steps and said the whole of what he knew, in his own trade, in his own mouth, and gave it no name, and said it was not a resolution and was not going to get anybody out of anything.

Aldis Rook moves the chairs for Orla Fennimore and keeps nothing and has no stone and has no seat and no board, has never once been asked what he did, has been told not to come up that bank and has come anyway, and said on the fifth day of the hundred and sixty-third week the whole of the ordinary form of that landing, which is how people carry things into rooms and then go out of the door, and refused to name it.

“I have got one thing and I am going to say it once and then I am going to go down that road, and it is the whole of what I have ever needed to say about my trade and I have been saving it nineteen years without knowing I was saving it.”

He did not come up the steps.

“In nineteen years of carrying things into rooms for people who were not in the rooms, I put a thing where somebody works and I never once went back the next morning and put another one there. Not once. Not in nineteen years, and not in one house, and not for one family. And I have never once come back up a road and asked that person whether they had taken the first one.” He put his hand flat on his own coat. “That is the whole of what I know.”

“A thing put down where somebody works is not put down again the next morning by the same person, and the person it was put down for is not asked whether they took it.”

It was not named on that landing.

“And I am not going to name it. I am going to say that I have been waiting nineteen years for somebody on this landing to hand me a word for that and I am not going to take the word, because the day this landing gives it a word it stops being a thing we do and it starts being a thing somebody decided, and then it is a rule, and a rule on this landing is a thing with a person inside it, and I have not got room for another person inside anything I own.”

Nobody handed him a word and nobody gave him a name for it.

“It has not been touched. Not pulled, not turned over, not weighed in the hand, not read out loud, not carried inside, not put back where it was, and not used. Seven things, and I have said them three times on this landing now and I am not going to be thanked for this one either, and one day somebody is going to ask me to stop and I am going to take a long time over the answer.”

He was thanked neither then nor afterwards.

“And now the half of it I have been not saying all week, and I am saying it to the man of thirty-one and to nobody else, and he is going to hear it and he is not going to like it.” He looked up at the dry strip. “That does not get anybody out of anything they have said out loud on this landing. Not you. Not me. Not the man who cannot read and not the woman who reads for a living.”

“It is not a pardon and it is not a way of being let off.”

“It is how we carry things into rooms.”

“It has got nothing to do with what anybody says, and what you said at the eighth hour last Thursday is still said, and you are going to be standing at the bottom of these steps on another morning saying something else, and so am I.”

Nobody took it as a pardon and nobody took it as a let-off.

Then a woman at the wall said out loud, before anybody could have thanked her, that she was not in any of it and had not been put in it and had come down that road in the first hour so that it could not be said afterwards that she had.

Orla Fennimore is forty-four, keeps twenty-nine chairs at a house that is not in this county, was asked once what she did and has not been asked since, and was at the wall on the fifth day of the hundred and sixty-third week before the seventh hour, and said what she was not part of out loud before any of it was finished.

“Nobody is to come up this road and tell me anything.” She said it without coming past the wall. “Not about the full bank. Not about a whole figure of weeks a man cannot read. Not about a man of thirty-one and a piece of ash.”

“And not about the thing the man who moves my chairs has just said, which is the only thing on this landing in all the time I have been at this wall that I have ever wanted to hear, and which I am not going to have, and which I have not come up this road to have.”

Nobody on that landing had said she was owed it and nobody said they wished she had it.

“He said a thing he has been saving nineteen years and he said it standing on a road I did not ask him to come up, and that is his whole right and nobody has to give me a piece of it and nobody is going to.” She put her hands behind her back. “I have been at this wall twice this week and I have not been up that road once. I did not stand on that wall this morning so that anything would get put down.”

“I stood on it so that when somebody later asks who put a thing on that landing, there is somebody in this county who can say out loud, in her own mouth, that she put nothing down and asked for nothing.”

She was not asked whether she had put anything down.

“I have twenty-nine chairs in a house that is not in this county and I have been asked about them once in my life and never since, and that is the life I have, and I am not going to have it made into a thing this week because a man who carries furniture into rooms has finally said out loud what he has been doing for nineteen years.” She did not come past the wall. “And I am not to be thanked.”

“I am going to say that now, before any of you finishes that morning and walks up here and decides I stood at a wall on purpose, because that is what happens, and then I am the reason somebody came up a road, and I will not have it.”

She was not thanked.

“Nobody is to come up here and find out what I said to that wall either, and nobody is to tell me they wished I had been on the landing. I have not come down this road to be wished for and I have not come down it to be on anything. I am going back down it now at the pace I came up.”

She went back down the cart road at the pace she had come up, and nobody went up it after her, and nothing was said on that road behind her back.

Then the man of thirty-one said the figure of his mistakes out loud on the last morning of that week, and nobody on that landing told him one single thing about it, in any direction.

“One hundred and thirty wrong things in one hundred and thirty mornings.” He said it flat and he did not stop in front of it. “That is a whole figure and it is a figure of mistakes and it is not a figure of a man, and a man who says the whole of that out loud on a landing is doing the same thing a man who cannot read does with a whole figure of weeks, and neither of them is asking anybody for a word.”

“I have been waiting all week for somebody to hand me the word small or the word large or the word anything, and nobody has, and nobody is going to, and I am not unrelieved about that and I am not relieved either, and those are the same thing tonight and I am carrying both of them up that bank myself.”

Nobody told him the figure was small and nobody told him it was large, and nobody told him he had found anything, and nobody told him he had not found anything.

“And my want is the same want I said out loud on this landing last week and I am not going to unsay it because the week has gone. I want to be somebody. And I did not get any closer to it by putting a piece of ash on a step and saying out loud that I put it there, and I am still here.”

“And the one thing I have left is that I can walk down that bank at any hour, and I have not walked down it, and nobody will hear a word from me about how near I came, and nobody is to thank me for not walking down it, because a thing a man does not do is not a thing anybody can thank him for.”

Nobody thanked him for anything he had done or had not done.

Then the man of thirty-one gave the whole of what he had come up that bank with in one place, out loud, and said that he was not going to say what the piece of ash was going to be, and nobody on that landing said what it was going to be either.

“One thing on this landing has a giver in it and I put it there and I said so out loud and I meant to, and it has not been touched all week and it has not been touched this morning and it is not going to be touched on Saturday, and there is nothing I am going to count out loud on this landing this morning and nothing I am going to ask anybody else to count.” He did not move his boots off the strip. “A whole figure of a hundred and thirty in a mouth.”

“An offer at two hundred and ninety days, unanswered, asked on none of these mornings, offered a withdrawal on none of them. A man who cannot read saying one hundred and one weeks into a landing that will not explain it. A piece of ash with my name in it and nobody’s name on it. That is everything. I have said all of it and I have not asked for one thing out of any of it.”

Nobody asked him for anything and nobody gave him anything.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety steps, though about sixty of them were under, and went round the fortieth one, and went round the chair, and did not sit on the chair. He did not touch the iron in the seam of the fortieth step and he did not touch the ash on the dry strip and he did not say who had put it there.

A full bank and a whole figure of weeks and a man who cannot read saying it out loud for the sixth time and nobody explaining it. A man who carries furniture into rooms said the whole of what he knows and gave it no name and nobody handed him one, and it was not a pardon and it did not get anybody out of anything. 

A woman of forty-four stood at a wall and said out loud, before anybody thanked her, that she was not in any of it and had asked for nothing, and nobody thanked her and nobody went up that road. 

The offer was at two hundred and ninety days and unanswered. 

Nobody was relieved. The account was as incomplete as it had been at the sixth hour, and it was still in use.

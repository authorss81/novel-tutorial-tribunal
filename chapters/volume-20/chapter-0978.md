# Chapter 978: The Water Came Off That Bank And All Ninety Of Those Steps Were Walkable And That Was The Thirty-Ninth Time And There Was No Figure Of Inches And No Figure Of Steps Under And No Figure Of Days And A Man Who Cannot See Well Said What Happens To A Thing That Has Made Somebody’s Work Easier When There Is Nothing On The Stone To Put It Beside

Seventh and last day of the hundred and sixty-first week. His seven hundred and twenty-eighth morning. Five hundred and seventy-eight days after the settlement. The fever ninety-nine weeks and two days old. Five hundred and thirty-two days since the division. Four hundred and seventy-eight days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and twenty-four days in this county of Kell.

The water came off that bank at about the sixth hour and all ninety of those steps were walkable by about the seventh, and that was the thirty-ninth time. There is no figure of inches on this morning and there is no figure of steps under and there is no figure of days of the coming back, and it is the seventh and last day of the hundred and sixty-first week as well. That is the whole of what is on the stone, it is not a gap in the morning, and the thirty-ninth time is the only figure this morning carries and it is a figure of times and not of anything else.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the seventh and last day of the hundred and sixty-first week, on the thirty-ninth morning the water came off that bank, that he was not going to reach for a figure out of habit and that a feeling of it is a figure in a worse coat.

“There is no figure of inches on that bank this morning and I am not going to reach for one out of habit, and there is not a feeling of it either, because a feeling of it is a figure in a worse coat. Not a number of steps under, and not a number of days.” He put the broom down flat on the stone. “A man counts days off a line of water and there is no line this morning to count them off.”

Nobody on that landing reached for one.

“And I have said the three of those out loud now, by name, the way I have said them the two times before this week, and I am not going to change the shape of it, because a shape you change is a shape you are trying to make new, and there is nothing new here, and a morning with nothing on the stone is not a better morning and it is not a worse one. It is the shape of it. That is the thirty-ninth time.”

He took the broom up off the stone and stood holding it.

“I am going to sweep all ninety of them, and every one of them is out this morning, and I am not going to tell anybody how I know, because how I know is a thing I have not worked out and I am not going to work it out with my heel in front of you.”

Then a man who cannot see well came up onto the flat of the steps at about the tenth hour with his hand out in front of him and found the wall with it, and said the thing that had been in his mouth since Friday and that nobody on that landing had asked him for.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and said on the seventh and last day of the hundred and sixty-first week what happens to a thing that has made a man’s work easier when there is nothing on the stone to put it beside.

“I want to start by saying that I cannot see that step and I am not going to pretend to, and I am not going to ask anybody to describe it, and that is not a thing I am being proud of this morning, it is just what is available.”

Nobody described it.

“I have got a list. Everybody on this landing knows about the list and most of them have made a face at it at some point, and I have never asked anybody to take it seriously and I am not asking now.” He put his back against the wall. “Here is what I have worked out. On every other morning of this fortnight there has been a figure on that stone. Four inches. Eight. Twelve. Sixteen. Sixteen that holds. Off all ninety. And I have been able to take the number of the morning away with me and put it in the list, and not being able to do that is most of what my list is.”

“Most of it,” said the woman who saws lengths.

“Most of it, and I am not going to work out how much this morning.” He turned his face towards the wall. “And this morning there is nothing. Not a small figure. Nothing. And I have been up that lane and across this landing with nothing to put in the list, and a man who sweeps this stone has just told me there is no figure to reach for and no feeling to reach for either, and a woman who saws lengths at a bench and a man who sweeps this stone have between them left a man who cannot see well standing on a step with his hands out.”

Nobody gave him anything to hold.

“So here is the answer and I have got it and I am not going to pretend I have not.” He said it to the wall and let everybody hear it. “Nothing has changed this morning. That is what happens to it. The thing in that seam has had three figures standing next to it every day of a fortnight and a number of times and a man’s word for it, and this morning it has got none of them, and it is exactly as unaccounted for as it was on the first morning, and so am I.”

He came away from the wall and started down the steps with his hand out.

“And I will say the other half, because I have been not saying it since Friday. That is not a complaint either. On a morning with figures on it, I could have been given a number and been none the wiser. This morning I cannot be given anything at all, and that is a different morning, and it is not worse, and I am not going to be the kind of man who says a thing is worse so that he has something to carry.”

Nobody said it was better either.

Then the boy came up out of the lane and gave his own count before anybody could ask him for it, and said out loud that for the first time in three hundred and twenty-five days he had arrived on a morning with nothing to put it beside, and that he had decided before he got to the wall that he did not need one.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county three hundred and twenty-five days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Three hundred and twenty-five is nine hundred and seventy-eight less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Three hundred and twenty-five days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.” He looked round the landing. “And there is nothing on that stone this morning except a number of times, and I have got a number of my own, and for the first time since I came up that lane I have not got anything to stand it beside.”

Nobody told him that was a good way to arrive.

“I worked that out at the bottom of the lane, before I saw the water had gone, and I decided it there and I have not changed my mind since, which is the first time in a fortnight I have not changed my mind about something on this landing.” 

He sat down on the wall on his own coat. “I have not told anybody that I have decided it, and I am not going to, and I am not going to be told I was right about deciding it either, because a boy who decides things on a landing is a boy who has started keeping them, and I am not keeping anything. I am just counting in a lane.”

Nobody told him he was right about deciding it in the lane and nobody told him he was wrong about that.

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour and asked in the same words at the same hour and was refused in the same way and was not thanked, and he said out loud that it was the third morning of the water being off and he was not going to make a whole thing of it.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and fifty-seventh mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is nine hundred and seventy-eight less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and fifty-seventh. Nine hundred and seventy-eight less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

“Nobody asked you for it, and there is nothing on that stone this morning to ask it against, and that has not made me any more willing.”

“No, and there is nothing on that stone this morning, and I have been looking for a number to put on my question for two hundred and fifty-seven mornings and there has never been one, and I have never once used it, and I am not going to start this morning.”

Nobody told him he had never once used it.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.” He said it in the words he uses. “And I have said that for two hundred and fifty-seven mornings and I have not had it answered and I have not been thanked and I am not going to be thanked, and there is a morning coming when I am going to stop asking, and I am not telling you which one.”

Nobody asked him which one.

Nobody answered him on a morning when there was nothing on the stone to answer him with, and nobody told him he was owed an answer, and nobody thanked him, and there was a morning coming when he was going to stop asking.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the eighth hour with no water on the steps at all to lean his own figures on, and gave his figures, and gave the figure of the offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing, and then said the whole of the rule out loud for the first time in his life, which he had been carrying up that bank since the end of last week.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and twenty-four days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and ninety-three days old.

“Four hundred and twenty-four days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and ninety-three days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-twenty-eighth is two hundred and seventy-eight days old, which is nine hundred and seventy-eight less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it, and there is no water on these steps this morning and there is nothing on that stone to stand the two hundred and seventy-eight against.”

He got to the bottom of the steps and stayed there.

“A hundred and eighteen wrong things in a hundred and eighteen mornings, and here is the hundred and eighteenth, and it is not about the water and it is not about a step.” He kept his hands behind his back. “Since the end of last week I have been carrying a sentence up this bank and it has been getting longer all week and this morning it is the only thing on this landing I have not said.”

Nobody asked him what the sentence was.

“It is the rule. The whole of it, in the order I heard it.” He said it in one go, the way he says the things he has had ready. “It has not been touched. Not pulled, not turned over, not weighed in the hand, not read out loud, not carried inside, not put back where it was, and not used. Seven things.”

He put his hands down at his sides.

“And I have heard it said out loud on that landing all fortnight and I have never once said it, and the reason I have not is that a man who says the rule counts himself in it, and I have stood on these steps a fortnight and I have not wanted anybody up here to count me. And I have worked out this morning that that was not honour and it was not care. It was that I wanted one thing to be mine that nobody else had to hold.”

Nobody on that landing said a word about that.

“So I am saying it out loud on a morning with nothing on the stone, and I am not going to be thanked for it, and nobody is to tell me it was well said, and if it saves anybody on this landing a morning then it has done what it was for and I am not going to want anything out of that.”

Nobody told him it was well said and nobody thanked him.

“And here is my cost and it is the same one it has been every morning. Asking is the only thing that costs me nothing, and I have asked for nothing for four hundred and twenty-four days, and this morning I said seven sentences out loud and every one of them cost me something, and that is what the other sort of morning is for.”

Nobody helped him with that and nobody paid him for it.

All ninety were out and walkable and he swept every one of them, and went round the fortieth one, and went round the chair, and did not touch the thing in the seam and did not sit on the chair. Nothing has been pulled, or turned over, or weighed in a hand, or read aloud, or carried inside, or put back where it was, or used.

A morning with no figure of anything on its stone went out with that thing still in that seam and still unaccounted for, and the stone had nothing else on it to account for it with, and the two of them went out exactly as they came in, and nobody on that landing has said a word about what either of them is for.

# Chapter 988: Eight Inches And About Eighty Of Those Ninety Steps Under And A Man Who Cannot See Well Put His Hand Out Along The Wall To Find The Wall And Somebody Said Four Words And He Took His Hand Back Off The Wood Himself, And Nobody Helped Him Take It Back And Nobody Thanked Him For Taking It Back

Third day of the hundred and sixty-third week. His seven hundred and thirty-eighth morning. Five hundred and eighty-eight days after the settlement. The fever one hundred weeks and five days old. Five hundred and forty-two days since the division. Four hundred and eighty-eight days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and thirty-four days in this county of Kell.

Eight inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, about eighty of those ninety steps under, and six days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures, and the water stood on the landing that morning rather than in the bank below it, and for about two hours that morning a man who cannot see well put his hand out along the foot of the wall and somebody said four words.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the third day of the hundred and sixty-third week that he was going to have to say one man’s name out loud on this landing this morning and that he had not wanted to and was going to do it anyway.

“Eight at the seventh hour, six days of the coming back, and about eighty of those ninety steps under, on the third day of the hundred and sixty-third week, and it is a Thursday. Three figures.”

He set the broom down and then picked it up again, which is not a thing he does.

“The water is on my landing this morning. Not in the bank. On it. And the strip along the foot of the wall is dry and it is the only dry stone here and it is the strip nobody sweeps, and that is where anything ever gets put down on this landing by anybody, including by me, and I have never once said that out loud in nineteen years because it is a figure about where things go and not about the things.”

Nobody said anything about where things go.

“Simon is coming up at the tenth hour. He does it most mornings. He puts his hand out before his feet, because that is the only way he has, and he finds the wall and he puts his hand down along it.” Corvin Tace looked at the foot of the wall. “And there is a piece of ash on that strip.”

“And I am going to have to say a word to him before his hand gets to it, and I am not going to like it and it is not going to be one of the things I have been doing all week.”

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour and asked in the same words at the same hour and was refused in the same way and was not thanked, and said out loud that the water had come up to meet him on the second morning and that this was not the first time in two hundred and sixty-seven mornings that something on this landing had got between him and what he came for.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and sixty-seventh mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is nine hundred and eighty-eight less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and sixty-seventh. Nine hundred and eighty-eight less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

“Nobody asked you for it, and the water is higher than it was yesterday.”

“The water is higher than it was yesterday and it will be higher again on Saturday, and every morning it is higher there is less of this landing I can stand on and every morning I stand on less of it.” He shouldered the barrow up the last of the steps. “Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.”

“And I am going to say one more thing about the water and then I am going to leave that alone for good.”

“The water does not know I am here.”

“I had that on Monday and I have been carrying it about all week and I am putting it down.”

Nobody told him whether that was true.

Nobody answered Barnaby Crove on the third day of the hundred and sixty-third week, and nobody told him he was owed an answer, and nobody thanked him, and he had put the water down.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the ninth hour with eight inches standing round the stone and gave his figures and the figure of the offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing, and stood on the dry strip with his boots on either side of a piece of ash and not on it.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and thirty-four days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and three days old.

“Four hundred and thirty-four days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and three days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-thirty-eighth is two hundred and eighty-eight days old, which is nine hundred and eighty-eight less seven hundred. It is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

He looked down at his own boots.

“I am going to say the part of this morning that is mine and then I am going to move my feet.” He turned them both slightly outward on the stone. “I am standing on both sides of that thing and I could have stood on it. And I know where it is exactly, because I put it there and I chose the place, and I chose it well enough that I could stand here all morning without moving my feet and nothing would happen.”

“That is not a thing I did on Thursday.”

“On Thursday I did not know where it was.”

Nobody on that landing told him what it was.

“One hundred and twenty-eight wrong things in one hundred and twenty-eight mornings, and this is the hundred and twenty-eighth, and nobody is going to tell me what kind of figure it is, and nobody is going to tell me I found anything, and I am not going to tell anybody how close my boots were.”

Nobody told him anything about his boots.

Then Simon Rook came up onto the landing at about the tenth hour with his hand out in front of him and did what he has done on most mornings for a fortnight, which is to find the wall before he puts his feet down.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and put his hand out along the foot of the wall on the third day of the hundred and sixty-third week and took it back off a piece of wood without being helped and said four words afterwards that nobody thanked him for.

He got the wall with the flat of his hand and turned along it, the way a man reads a room he cannot see.

And the man with the broom said, from about six feet away, in an entirely ordinary voice:

“Simon. Your hand is on the wood.”

Nobody shouted. Nobody moved. Nobody put a hand on him.

Simon took his hand off it. He did it himself, and he did it fast, and he put the hand behind his back and stood against the wall about where the water was not.

“I am going to say the whole of it and I am going to say it in order and I am not going to be helped with any of it.” His voice was not steady and he did not pretend it was. “That is a piece of ash on the dry strip and I could not see it. My hand went along that wall because my hand has gone along that wall a hundred mornings and there has never been anything on it except the wall.”

“I put a piece of ash on a wall that a blind man uses to find out where he is, and I did it at the eighth hour on a Thursday without one thought about it, and I have not said one word about that all week and I am saying it now.”

Nobody made any sound.

“It has not been touched. Not pulled, not turned over, not weighed in the hand, not read out loud, not carried inside, not put back where it was, and not used. Seven things, and I have not said them in that order before, I have always had them in a different order, and I have just said them in the order they go.” He still had his hand behind his back. “Nobody said it had been touched. Nobody said I nearly did.”

“Nobody said anything at all except a man telling me where my hand was, and I would like that in a book, and I am not going to ask for it.”

Nobody made a mark against what he said.

“And I am not going to be asked what stopped my hand going any further along that wall, because I have been asked that once in this county and I refused it and I am going to refuse it the same way and I would like it noticed that I have got the same answer ready that I had the first time and that it has not worn down.”

Nobody put that question to him.

“I am not going to be thanked for taking my hand back. Taking my hand back was not a good thing I did. It was the only thing available and I did it and now the morning is going to carry on as though I had not done it.” He came away from the wall. “And I am not going to be unrelieved about having said any of this either, and I know that sounds like nothing, and it is not nothing, and I have got no other word for it and I am not going to go looking for one.”

Nobody relieved him and nobody unrelieved him.

Then the man who moves chairs came up the cart road and stood where he could see the strip and the boots and the wall, and said the thing about the trade of the man who could not see, and gave it to him without dressing it up.

Aldis Rook moves the chairs for Orla Fennimore and keeps nothing and has no stone and has no seat and no board, has never once been asked what he did, has been told not to come up that bank and has come anyway, and said on the third day of the hundred and sixty-third week that the seven things are a good rule and that a good rule is worth exactly as much as the care somebody takes when they are the one who might break it.

“Simon, I am going to say one thing and you are not going to like it.” He did not come past the bottom step. “You have had that rule read at you all week by everybody up here who can see, and it did nothing to your hand. It was not the rule that stopped your hand.”

“It was that the man sweeping this stone has been walking that wall line in his head for nineteen years and knew exactly where your hand was before you got there.”

“So the rule is worth nothing and the care is worth the whole of it, and I have moved furniture into houses for nineteen years and the care is the only thing I have ever sold.”

Nobody on that landing said that the rule was worth nothing.

“And I want to say the other half of it and I would like everybody up there to hear me, including the man of thirty-one.” He looked up at the strip. “A thing put down where somebody works is not a given thing. Not by him, not by me, not by anybody on this landing, and it cannot be thanked, and it is not that nothing is owed for the wanting.”

“It is that the man whose work it might make easier cannot be thanked for it, because a man cannot be owed something by an object, and the person who put it there is not on the step and is not going to be put on it by anybody standing here. That is the working and I have given it before and I am giving it again in the same words, and nobody has thanked me for either time and nobody is going to.”

Nobody up there thanked him for any part of it.

Then the woman who saws lengths came up the cart road and put her saw handle down against the wall well away from the strip and said what the wood was going to be doing by Saturday without touching it or looking at it closely.

Wenna Pyle is thirty-eight, who saws lengths and sets studs at a bench in an open-sided shed behind a wheelwright’s about three hundred yards down the cart road, and who carries her own cup out to that bench because there is no table in the shed, and said on the third day of the hundred and sixty-third week what the piece of ash on the dry strip would be by the fourth day of the coming back.

“I am not going to touch it and I am not going to put my eye close to it and I am going to say what it is going to be, because it is my trade and because nobody has asked me and because I would like it on the page in my mouth before anybody has a chance to make it a different shape.” She looked at the strip from where she stood. “That is a handle blank with the saw cut off at the corner.”

“It is lying on its side on a wet stone in the wind.”

“By Saturday it will have gone soft at the corner and hard in the middle, and at that point it is firewood and not a handle, and on Saturday morning it will be firewood in the same way the iron in the fortieth step is iron. That is the whole of what I am going to say about it.”

Nobody thanked her for saying it and nobody asked her to say it again.

Eight inches and about eighty of those ninety steps under and six days of the coming back, and the water standing on the landing rather than in the bank. The ash on the dry strip had not been turned over, had not been weighed in a hand, had not been used, and a man who cannot see well had set his hand on it and taken it off without anybody helping him, and nobody up there treated it as a thing worth saying out loud, and nobody was relieved.

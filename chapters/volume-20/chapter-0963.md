# Chapter 963: Four Inches On The Top Step And About Ninety Of Those Ninety Steps Under And Seven Days Of The Coming Back, And A Man Who Moves Chairs For A Living Asked What A Thing Put Down And Not Used Is Doing To A Landing

Sixth day of the hundred and fifty-ninth week. His seven hundred and thirteenth morning. Five hundred and sixty-three days after the settlement. The fever ninety-seven weeks and one day old. Five hundred and seventeen days since the division. Four hundred and sixty-three days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and nine days in this county of Kell.

Four inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, about ninety of those ninety steps under, and seven days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and then said that on a morning when most of the bank is out there is less to say about a landing than on a morning when it is not, and that he was not going to pretend otherwise.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and gave three figures on the sixth morning of the hundred and fifty-ninth week where nine of them used to be under.

“Four at the seventh hour, seven days of the coming back, and about ninety of those ninety steps under, on the sixth day of the hundred and fifty-ninth week, and it is a Sunday. Three figures.” He set the broom against the wall. “And I am going to say the other thing before anybody says it for me. There is a chair on my landing about a yard from where I work. It has been there since the middle of the week. It is a good chair and it is somebody’s living and it has got felt gone off the back of it.”

Nobody looked at it.

“I have not sat on it. I am not going to sit on it. I sweep round it. I have swept round it every morning this week and I have not touched it and it is not going to be touched by me or by anybody who comes up here, and if anybody finds it again it is not going to be turned over, weighed in the hand, read out loud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used. I have said all of that out loud and I would like nobody to have to be the first one to say it.”

Nobody did have to.

Then the man who moves chairs came up the cart road without a barrow and without anything on his shoulder, which is the way he has come since Monday, and stood at the top of the steps and looked at his own trade standing on somebody else’s landing, and said the thing he had been working out for two days in the order he had worked it out.

Aldis Rook moves the chairs for Orla Fennimore and keeps nothing and has no stone and has no seat and no board, has never once been asked what he did, and has stood at the back of that landing with his hands behind him and has not gone down the road to the village on any morning of that week.

“You asked me on Wednesday what a chair was for and I told you it is for nothing and you did not like the answer and neither did I,” he said. “That was true and it was not the whole of it. I have been up here two days thinking about the other half and I am going to give it to you in the wrong order because I worked it out in the wrong order.”

“All right.”

“The half I did not have on Wednesday is this. It is doing something to your landing.” He did not come further up. “You are not looking at it, you know that. You swept round it twice this morning before you said anything to anybody. It has been there since Wednesday and it has stopped being a thing that was put down and it has started being a feature. That is all it has done. That is the entire damage and there is no damage.”

“Then it has done nothing.”

“It has done the one thing nothing does, and that is it has become part of where you work, and in about nine days you are going to have to describe this landing to somebody who has not stood on it, and you are going to describe it with that chair in it, and whoever is listening is going to come up here and put themselves in the space where the chair is and get the whole of this landing exactly right.” He shifted his weight. “And they will get it right without anybody having told them. Because there is a chair in the shape of it now.”

Nobody on that landing had anything to do with that.

“I am not saying that to make you move it. I want that on the record before I say anything else.” He took his hands out from behind him, which is the thing he does and which nobody has ever praised. “You are not being asked to move it. You are not being asked to sit on it. Nobody is asking you to turn it over and find out whose it is. And I am not going to say who put it there, and I am not going to let anybody go up the hill and find out who put it there, and I would like it said here that nobody on this step is going to do that.”

“Nobody is going to,” said the man with the broom.

“Then I have said the whole of what I came up for and I would like nobody to tell me I got it right.”

Nobody told him he got it right and nobody told him he had got it wrong, and he said he had expected the second and not the first.

The boy came up out of the lane in the middle of that and gave his own count before anybody asked him for it, in the voice of a boy who has been counting something in the dark and is perfectly satisfied to keep doing it.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county three hundred and ten days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Three hundred and ten is nine hundred and sixty-three less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Three hundred and ten days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.” He looked up the steps. “And the step with the chip in the corner has got a second thing standing near it now. That is all. I am not saying what either of them is and if a man asks me I am going to say I do not know, because I do not know.”

“Nobody is going to ask you, and neither am I.”

“I know that, and I have said it while it was cheap, and that is twice this week.” He sat down on the wall on his own coat. “And nobody has told me this week that I had it right about anything and nobody is going to, and I want it noticed that I have noticed that, and if anybody ever does tell one of us we had it right then it will matter that nobody did it while I could have heard.”

Nobody told him. Nobody told him the other thing either.

Then the woman who reads the ninth run came up the road, entered the morning, and told that landing that she had not given a figure of her own out loud since Thursday and that she was not going to start again this week, and gave no reason that anybody could use.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years, holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, has one question she set aside to ask and gave up on the sixth morning of that week and has not taken back, and has not spoken a figure of her own out loud since the morning she gave it up.

“I entered this morning and there is a thing in it and I am not going to say what it is, and that is not new and it is not a refusal either. There will be no figure from me this week.” She had the book shut against her knee and kept it shut.

“It used to be the first thing I said on that wall. Mornings in front of anybody, and the figure, and then whatever else I had. And I have decided that I am not going to do that again until I have worked out whether a figure of mine that goes up by one every morning somebody stands here is a figure of mornings or a figure of being asked.”

“You have been counting all week.”

“I have been counting all week. I am not going to say so and I am not going to stop counting, and those are two different things and I am not going to be asked which of them is the one I am doing.” She looked at the chair from the bottom of the steps. “And I am not going to help anybody in this county by saying a whole figure out loud on a morning when the man with the broom has already told them what a bare stone is. There has been enough of that this week for four volumes.”

Nobody on that landing helped her.

Then the man of thirty-one came up and gave his four figures and the figure of the offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing, and then asked the man of forty-three one question about the chair and was refused in a sentence with the working given, and did not take it well and did not take it badly either.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and nine days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and seventy-eight days old.

“Four hundred and nine days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and seventy-eight days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-thirteenth is two hundred and sixty-three days old, which is nine hundred and sixty-three less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

He got to the top of the steps and stood with his hands behind his back and looked at the chair for a while.

“One hundred and three wrong things in one hundred and three mornings, and here is the hundred and third, and it is about a chair.” He turned round.

“You told us on Wednesday that it is not for anything, and that it is not a gift and not a kindness and not a reward for nineteen years of sweeping, because if it were a reward then somebody had to decide to give it. And then, later the same morning, you told us it is not a version of what you have wanted, because a version would come with the wanting attached. Two different sentences out of one mouth on one morning, and I have been carrying both of them for three days.”

“Yes,” said the man with the broom.

“Then here is my question and it is one line and it has a working.” He did not move his hands. “Does it sit there because it was left, or does it sit there because somebody wants to know whether it has been touched? Because if the second one is even a part of it, then it has stopped being a thing that was put down and started being a thing that is being watched, and there is nobody standing at the edge of this landing who is going to be the one who started that.”

He had the whole of it ready and he said it in one breath, the way he says the things he has had ready since the week turned.

“No,” said Corvin Tace, without stopping, and went by the fortieth step and carried on down. “A chair on a landing is not a question and I am not going to give it the standing of one, and the reason is not moral. The reason is that the day a thing on this landing is worth asking about, then the only thing anybody on this landing can do about it is talk about it, and then the whole of this bank is a room with people in it talking about a piece of furniture, and this is a bank with ninety steps cut in it and a man who sweeps them.”

“A landing is a room once somebody starts talking on it.”

“No. A landing is a landing once somebody starts working on it, and I have been working on it for nineteen years, and the man who moves chairs does not work here and the woman who reads does not work here and you do not work here, and every one of you who has stood up here this week has talked about a thing and not one of them has been touched, and that is not a failure of this landing. That is the only argument I have got and it is a working.”

“And a thing left beside a person is not a version of what that person has wanted, and it is not going to turn into one this week whatever either of us does with it.”

Nobody on that landing said it was a good argument.

The man of thirty-one stood at the top of the steps for a while after that with his hands behind his back, and then he went down the bank at about the ninth hour without saying another word about the chair, and did not put his weight on the fortieth step on the way down, and nobody thanked him for the question and nobody told him what it was worth.

Corvin Tace swept his way back up, went round the fortieth step and went round the chair, and set the broom against the wall where it has been every morning for nineteen years, and did not sit on it, and the water was four inches on the top step and going up about four inches a day, and on the landing there was more than one thing standing in it that no mouth on that landing had given a name to and that nobody had said who put down.

# Chapter 955: Four Inches On The Top Step At About The Seventh Hour And About Ninety Of Those Ninety Steps Under And Seven Days Of The Coming Back, And The Fever Is Ninety-Six Weeks Old And It Is A Whole Figure And A Man Who Cannot Read Says So Out Loud Before Anybody Works It Out For Him

Fifth day of the hundred and fifty-eighth week. His seven hundred and fifth morning. Five hundred and fifty-five days after the settlement. The fever ninety-six weeks old. Five hundred and nine days since the division. Four hundred and fifty-five days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and one days in this county of Kell.

Four inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, about ninety of those ninety steps under, and seven days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and said that about ninety out of ninety is the whole of what he has this week, and that he is not going to argue with a bank about it.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said this week that about ninety out of ninety is the whole of what he has.

“Four at the seventh hour, seven days of the coming back, and about ninety of those ninety steps under, on the fifth day of the hundred and fifty-eighth week, and it is a Saturday. Three figures. A bank four inches on a step is not a different kind of thing than a bank eight inches on it, and about nine men in this county have found that out at some cost.”

The man of fifty-four had been sitting on the wall since before the light and he stood up when that was finished, and he stood up wrong, too fast, the way a man stands up when he has been sitting on something for an hour and has been holding a thought the whole time.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and thirty-four mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is nine hundred and fifty-five less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one, and has asked every morning since the day he came back and been refused the same way every morning.

“Ninety-six weeks,” he said.

The landing went quiet in the ordinary way.

“Not ninety-six weeks and a bit. Ninety-six weeks, on this Saturday, and that is the first one of those since before I came to this landing.” He put both hands flat on the wall. “I want it said out loud by me and not worked out by one of you, because I am the man who cannot read and you are the people who can, and that is exactly the way round for a thing like this, and a figure that lands on me before it has landed on you is a figure one of you will do something kind with.”

He stood there and let them do nothing with it.

“That is all. That is the whole of what I am going to say about it. I do not know what a whole number of weeks is for and I am not going to be the man who says he does. You want to tell me it means the fever has been here ninety-six weeks and I have counted that figure every morning of this business and I cannot read it off anything, so somebody told me it once and I have carried it since, and if a whole number of weeks means something then it has meant it for nineteen years and not since Saturday.”

Nobody told him what it meant.

“Two hundred and thirty-fourth,” he said, and did not say whose it was, because that land had been gone over twice this week. “And I am going to ask, and I am going to be refused, and I am not going to be thanked for asking, and if anybody on this step thanks me for it I will not come up on Monday.”

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.”

Nobody answered him. Nobody told him the question was good. Nobody told him it was not.

Then the man of sixty-one came down the low side as far as the wall in the morning, which he does about four days in ten and calls a going-out, and put both hands on the top of the wall and said the thing he had worked out overnight about a sill.

Elgar Slee is sixty-one, cannot come down a bank, works at the front window of his own house on the low side about half a mile down past where that drain turns and up the second rise, and has not been downstairs in a long time.

“There is a thing on my sill. Outside it. Under the window I work at.”

“Tell it to the step, and tell it where your sill is, because nobody on this landing has got you on a map.”

“About half a mile down the low side, past where that drain turns, and up the second rise. That is the whole of the directions and I have given them to about nine people on the way down here and I am not going to give them again.”

“Tell it to the step.”



“I have told it to about nine people on the way down here and I am going to tell it to this step and then I am going home, and I have not put anything on it and I have not taken anything off it, and it is not going into my house.” He did not take his hands off the wall.

“I cannot come down a bank. I have said that out loud on this landing before and I have been told not to say it, so I am going to say it as part of this. I work at that window with my hands. And there is a thing on the sill I did not put there, and I am not going to ask the woman who lives in the next house whether she put it there, because I have not, I am not going to, and I would like it noticed that the woman who lives in the next house has not been asked anything by anybody about any of this.”

Nobody had asked her anything.

“And here is the part I came down for.” He took one hand off the wall and used it to point at nothing. “Whoever put that thing on my sill does not work at my window. That is not a complaint. It is the whole of what I know about it and it is enough to be going on with, and it is also enough that they are not going to put another one there tomorrow morning, because a person who works at a window gets one thing left on it or gets left alone, and a person who gets one thing left on it every morning is being worked on.”

He put the hand back on the wall.

“I am going to be asked by somebody before I get home whether I took it in. I am going to say now, in front of this step, that nobody has asked me and that if anybody asks me I am going to say what I have just said, and that is all I have got, because I am sixty-one and I cannot come down a bank and I am not going to be helped up one this morning.” He looked along the landing. “And I have not taken it in and I am not going to. It can sit on that sill until it falls off it.”

Nobody thanked him for coming down and nobody helped him up.

The boy came up out of the lane while the man of sixty-one was still at the wall, and gave his figure, and then said the thing he had come to say and would not be moved off it.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county three hundred and two days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Three hundred and two is nine hundred and fifty-five less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Three hundred and two days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up. And I have worked out that the step with the chip in the corner has got about nine of you standing near it this week and nobody has put anything near it, and I want it said out loud that I have noticed that, and I am not asking anybody to tell me I got it right, and if anybody tells me I got it right I am going down into the lane and staying there until you have all stopped.”

Nobody told him he had it right. Nobody told him he had it wrong, either.

“That is all I wanted. It is smaller than a figure and it is mine and I am not going to be thanked for it and I am not going to be thanked for the noticing either.”

Then the man of thirty-one came up and gave his four figures and had a whole number of weeks offered to him three times in one morning by three different people and refused all three, out loud, in order.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and one days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and seventy days old.

“Four hundred and one days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and seventy days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-fifth is two hundred and fifty-five days old, which is nine hundred and fifty-five less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

He was quiet for a moment.

“Ninety-five wrong things in ninety-five mornings, and here is the ninety-fifth, and it is the plainest thing I have said in a year. There is a whole number of weeks on this landing this morning. Everybody can see it. The man who cannot read has already said it out loud so that nobody could help him with it, and a woman of forty-four would find a way to put a shape on it inside an hour, and a man of thirty-eight with a board in his coat would want it for something.” He looked up the bank. “And I have worked out this morning that I am not going to be the one who gives it to anybody.”

“Nobody is going to tell you anything about your own figures either,” said the man with the broom.

“Then that is two of us refusing the same thing, which is a figure I am not going to count.”

He turned round at the bottom of the steps and said the last of it up the landing, to the man of sixty-one and to the man of fifty-four and to the boy and to the broom.

“A thing put down where somebody works is not a given thing. It has no giver in it. The person who put it down does not work there. And nobody on that step may say who put the last one down, and it may not be said either — and that is not a thing I have worked out. That is a thing that was true before I got to this county and it will be true after I go, and I have been nobody’s since before I came here and I am going to say the whole of it out loud once so that this step has heard it from somebody who gets nothing for saying it.”

Nobody thanked him for it. Nobody agreed with it. Nobody told him what a thing with no giver in it was worth.

What no mouth on that landing said that morning, and what the landing had worked out by about the ninth hour without anybody putting it in a sentence, was that the sill was still outside the window and had not come in, and the handrail was still where it had been put and no hand had gone past it, and the trough was still on the trough and nobody had put a weight on it, and the tub was still on the stone and nobody had looked at it twice, and the step with the chip in the corner was still not rocking. 

The water came up the bank about four inches at a time.

And the man of fifty-four said, going down, that he would ask again on Monday morning in the same words in the same voice at the same hour, and that he would rather have Monday.

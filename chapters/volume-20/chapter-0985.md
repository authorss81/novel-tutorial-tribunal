# Chapter 985: The Last Morning Of A Week And The Cut Across His Palm Came Out As A Whole Figure Of Three Hundred Days And A Woman Who Reads For A Living Refused To Make A Figure Of It And Refused To Put It Beside The Iron In The Fortieth Step, And The Man With A Broom Said Something He Had Not Said Before About A Monday

Seventh and last day of the hundred and sixty-second week. His seven hundred and thirty-fifth morning. Five hundred and eighty-five days after the settlement. The fever one hundred weeks and two days old. Five hundred and thirty-nine days since the division. Four hundred and eighty-five days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and thirty-one days in this county of Kell.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and it holds, about sixty of those ninety steps under, and one day of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and said, on the last morning of a week, that he was going to be short of things to say for the rest of the morning and that this was not a figure of the water.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the seventh and last day of the hundred and sixty-second week that he had said something once at the end of a week in this county and had not stopped paying for it and was not going to say anything else at the end of one.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, one day of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the seventh and last day of the hundred and sixty-second week, and it is a Monday. Three figures.”

He put the head of the broom down flat and stood on it.

“I am going to be short of things to say today and I will not fill the gap. Three weeks back I said a thing at the end of a week that I have not been able to take back, and I am not going to stand up here on a Monday and pretend there is a lesson in it. There is no lesson. I said it, it did not go away, and I have been saying one thing about the ends of weeks since then and I am not going to say a second.”

Nobody asked him what the first one had been.

Then the woman who saws lengths came up the cart road at about the eighth hour and asked him a straight question about the water, and he refused it, and he refused it in her own trade and not in his.

Wenna Pyle is thirty-eight, who saws lengths and sets studs at a bench in an open-sided shed behind a wheelwright’s about three hundred yards down the cart road, and who carries her own cup out to that bench because there is no table in the shed, and who asked the man with the broom on the last morning of the hundred and sixty-second week what tomorrow was going to be and was refused in the shape of her own work.

“Corvin. One day of the coming back. Is the water off tomorrow.”

He looked at her for a while.

“No,” he said. “And here is why in your language, because you will understand it and nobody else on this landing will.” 

He set the broom against the wall. “You saw a length at the wheelwright’s and the wheelwright said there was more to come, and you went home and cut to the mark anyway, because a mark is a mark and not a promise, and if you had cut to the promise you would have been the one holding the short piece. That is what I do with this stone. I sweep what is in front of me this morning and I do not plan tomorrow on the strength of a figure.”

“Thank you,” said Wenna Pyle.

He put his hand flat against the wall and said the rest of it before she could move.

“And I am not thanking you for asking and you are not to be thanked for asking, and I am aware that I have said that sentence on this landing so many times this week that it has stopped meaning anything, and it has not stopped meaning anything, and I have run out of new ways to say it and I am going to keep saying the old one.”

Nobody thanked her for asking.

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with a barrow and asked in the same words at the same hour for the last time in that week, and was refused in the same way and was not thanked, and said out loud that a week ending was not an occasion.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and sixty-fourth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is nine hundred and eighty-five less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and sixty-fourth. Nine hundred and eighty-five less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

“Nobody asked you for it, and it is the last one of the week and it makes no difference.”

“No, and I am going to say that out loud so that nobody up here decides this is a big morning for me, because if one of you decides that then I have spent a whole week on a Monday.” He set the barrow down, and looked at the ash on the flat of the top step for a moment, and turned his head away from it and did it slowly, the way a man turns his head off a thing he has decided not to have an opinion about. “Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.”

“And it is the last one this week and tomorrow it is the same question and I am not going to bring you a new one for a new week, because you have all had new weeks and I have had none of them.”

Nobody told him he had had none of them either.

Nobody answered Barnaby Crove at the end of the hundred and sixty-second week, nobody told him he was owed an answer, and nobody thanked him for the last asking of the week, and he was going to be up on the first morning of the next one with the same question in the same words.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the ninth hour with sixteen inches above his boots and gave his figures and the figure of the offer with the subtraction out loud, and then said out loud that the cut across his palm was three hundred days old, which is a whole figure, and nobody on that landing made anything of it.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and thirty-one days. The cut across that palm is three hundred days old.

“Four hundred and thirty-one days in this county, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-thirty-fifth is two hundred and eighty-five days old, which is nine hundred and eighty-five less seven hundred. It is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

He got to the bottom of the steps and turned his left hand over and showed it, which he has done twice in this county.

“Three hundred days on that palm. A whole figure. It has come out whole and I am going to say it out loud and then I am going to stand here, and I want to say before I say it that I know what it looks like coming out of my mouth on the last morning of a week, and I am going to say it anyway, and I will not apologise for saying it afterwards either.”

Nobody made anything of it.

“It is a figure of days. It is not a figure of a life. It is a figure of days on a hand, and the hand is mine, and there is nobody on this landing that it is owed to, and nobody has ever been owed anything by it. I would like it understood that I am not standing here this morning with three hundred days of something. I am standing here with a cut that has taken three hundred days to be the shape it is.”

Nobody on that landing made anything of that either.

Then the woman who reads for a living came up the cart road at about the fourth hour and had the thing worked out before she got to the wall, and she refused to have it, out loud, in front of everybody, and gave the working for the refusal.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years, holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and said on the seventh and last day of the hundred and sixty-second week that she was not going to be the mouth that gave three hundred days a shape, and that she was not going to put it beside the iron in the fortieth step either.

“I have been walking up this road since the fourth hour with a sentence about that figure, and I have had it ready three times, and I am going to stand up here and tell you that I am not going to say it.” She came up the steps and did not sit down. “I have read for a living for twenty-three years.”

“In other people’s pages a man says a figure of days and by the end of the sentence the figure has grown a column and a column has a meaning and the meaning is a thing the reader is meant to feel bad about.”

“It is the commonest trick there is.”

“It is the whole of what a page like that is for.”

Nobody made a face about that.

“So I could say three hundred days. I could say it in a voice. And then whoever hears it would have a figure about a man, and you would all be carrying it, and nobody on this landing would have said a word and a column would have got itself a name.” She turned round. “I am not going to do it and I am not going to be thanked for not doing it and I am not going to be asked whether I regret it.”

Nobody asked her whether she regretted it.

“And the second thing, which is the one I have come up here for, and I want everybody on these steps to hear me refuse it as plainly as I have refused the first.” She looked at the fortieth step and did not go near it. “Nobody is to put those two together.”

“Three hundred days on that man’s palm and however long that iron has been in that seam are not two halves of one thing and they are not a comparison and they are not a before and after.”

“The day somebody puts them in a line together they become one sentence, and that sentence will be about the iron, and it will be the only sentence anybody makes about that iron for the rest of time.”

Nobody put them together.

“And I am not going to be thanked for that either, and I would like it noticed that I have now said four of those on this landing in a week and that a woman who says them like that is a woman who has never once been thanked for anything, and that this is not a complaint about the landing, it is a fact about me, and I am not going to be turned into a lesson by anybody.”

Nobody turned her into a lesson.

“It has not been touched. Not pulled, not turned over, not weighed in the hand, not read out loud, not carried inside, not put back where it was, and not used. Seven things, and I have said them before on this landing and I am going to keep saying them and I am going to keep not being thanked for it, and one day somebody is going to ask me to stop and I am going to think about it for a long time first.”

Then the man of thirty-one said the rest of what was left in him, and asked for nothing, and did not put his hand in a pocket.

“One hundred and twenty-five wrong things in one hundred and twenty-five mornings, and this is the hundred and twenty-fifth, and nobody is going to tell me what kind of figure it is and nobody is going to tell me I found anything.” He put his left hand down at his side with the palm showing. “And my cost is the same one it has been every morning of this county. Asking costs me nothing. I have asked for nothing for four hundred and thirty-one days.”

“This week I put a piece of ash on a step and said out loud that I put it there, and that was not asking, and it was the first thing I have done in this county that anybody could have given me back, and nobody has, and it is still mine, and it is still on that step, and I am going to be up here in the morning when there is nothing at all on that stone.”

Nobody took that off him either and nobody put anything in its place.

The bank was still full over the top step at the last hour and he swept what the water had left him, and went round the fortieth one, and went round the chair, and did not sit on the chair and did not touch the iron in the seam and did not touch the ash on the wall. A whole figure of three hundred days had been said out loud on the last morning of the hundred and sixty-second week and nobody had made anything of it and nobody had put it beside anything. 

The week turned at midnight and nothing about that turning made any difference to the thing on the flat of the top step, and the giver was still in it, and nobody had been thanked for anything by anybody on that landing.

# Chapter 979: The First Morning Of A New Week And A Woman Who Reads For A Living Asked The Man Of Thirty-One What He Would Do If A Thing He Could Not Account For Made Something Easier And He Gave Her The Honest Answer And Nobody Helped Him With It

First day of the hundred and sixty-second week. His seven hundred and twenty-ninth morning. Five hundred and seventy-nine days after the settlement. The fever ninety-nine weeks and three days old. Five hundred and thirty-three days since the division. Four hundred and seventy-nine days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and twenty-five days in this county of Kell.

Four inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, about ninety of those ninety steps under, and seven days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and said one thing about the week turning, and a woman who reads for a living asked the man of thirty-one a question at the eighth hour and he answered it and nobody on that landing helped him with the answer afterwards.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the first day of the hundred and sixty-second week that a week is a figure of nothing at all.

“Four at the seventh hour, seven days of the coming back, and about ninety of those ninety steps under, on the first day of the hundred and sixty-second week, and it is a Tuesday. Three figures.”

He leaned the broom against the wall.

“There is going to be a great deal of standing up here this week saying that it is a new week, as though a week were a thing that got put down and somebody had to pick it up.” He looked along the top step. “A week is a figure of nothing. It is not a new landing and it is not a new morning and nothing on this step has changed because the word went round. The water went off on Sunday and it came back on today and that is the whole of what happened in between.”

Nobody on that landing said anything had changed.

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour and asked in the same words at the same hour and was refused in the same way and was not thanked, and said out loud that this was the first morning of a week and that he was not going to start it differently.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and fifty-eighth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is nine hundred and seventy-nine less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and fifty-eighth. Nine hundred and seventy-nine less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

“Nobody asked you for it. A Monday does not get an answer and a Tuesday does not either.”

“No, and it is a Monday I am asking on and it is a Tuesday now and it does not make a difference and I am not going to try and make it one.”

He set the barrow down.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.” He said it in the words he uses. “And it is the first one of a week and it is the same question and I am not going to come up here with a new one dressed up so that it looks like I have been thinking.”

Nobody said he had been thinking and nobody said he had not.

It got nothing back on the first morning of a new week, and nobody on that landing told him he was owed an answer, and nobody thanked him for turning up, and nobody was going to.

Then a man who cannot see well came up at about the tenth hour with his hand out in front of him and found the wall with it, and said the seven of them out loud because nobody else had said them that morning, and said that he was tired of saying them and would like somebody else to take a turn.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and said the whole of the rule on the first day of the hundred and sixty-second week and then asked for somebody to take a turn at it.

“It has not been touched. Not pulled, not turned over, not weighed in the hand, not read out loud, not carried inside, not put back where it was, and not used.” He said it flat against the wall. “Seven things. I have said them twice this fortnight and both times I have said them because I could not see whether anybody else was going to, and that is me putting a rule on this landing that the rule did not ask me for.”

Nobody took a turn at it.

“I would like somebody to take a turn,” he said. “Not today. Today. I would like one of you to say those seven things out loud on a morning when I have not already said them, and I would like it to be a man who sweeps this stone and not a man who happens to be standing here, and I am not going to say a day out loud when it happens, because I have watched what happens on this landing when somebody gives a morning a name in advance.”

Nobody gave any morning a name.

“And I am not going to be thanked for asking, because I asked on Friday and I asked on Saturday and nobody thanked me then either and I have stopped expecting it, and I would like somebody to notice that I have stopped expecting it rather than notice that I am still expecting it.”

He came away from the wall and started down with his hand out and nobody helped him.

Then the woman who reads the ninth run came up the cart road at about the seventh hour with the book shut against her knee and asked one question in front of everybody and made no apology for asking it and took no notes afterwards.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years, holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and asked the man of thirty-one on the first day of the hundred and sixty-second week what he would do if a thing he could not account for made something easier.

“I have thought about whether I have a right to ask it and I have decided that I have, and here is why, and I would like it said before I ask rather than after.” She came up the steps and did not sit down. “A man has been coming up this bank for a fortnight giving figures nobody asked him for and asking for nothing.”

“That is not a thing anybody has put on that landing. That is a thing he has brought up here himself every morning and put down, if you will have the expression, and a person who brings a thing up a hill themselves does not get to be left out of a question about what a person does with a thing they cannot account for.”

Nobody moved.

“I am going to put it the easy way round on purpose, because the other way round is about him and this way round is about the landing.” She turned round to the bottom of the steps. “If a thing you could not account for made something easier for you, what would you do?”

The man of thirty-one came up at about the eighth hour and gave his figures and the figure of the offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing, and then answered the question.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and twenty-five days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and ninety-four days old.

“Four hundred and twenty-five days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and ninety-four days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-twenty-ninth is two hundred and seventy-nine days old, which is nine hundred and seventy-nine less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

He got to the bottom of the steps and stood in front of her and did not use the time she had given him.

“A hundred and nineteen wrong things in a hundred and nineteen mornings, and here is the hundred and nineteenth, and it is her question and not mine, and I am going to answer it the way I would answer it if she were asking about the bank.”

“All right,” said Sena Dorr.

“I would want to be told who put it there.” He said it at once. “That is the first one and it is the one I did not want to say and I am saying it first so that you all know what the other two are worth.”

Nobody said a word.

“And if I could not be told, I would work out what it was for by using it, because that is the only road in this county and there is not another one.” He did not move his hands. “And if I could not use it, because using it would be the end of something I had, then I would go and ask the man it was for. And if I could not ask him, then I would carry it, and it would be the only thing I had that nobody in this county knew about.”

He stopped, and it took him longer than it should have.

“That is the whole of it. Four steps and the last one is the expensive one, and the expensive one is not the carrying. The expensive one is that a man carrying a thing nobody can see is a man who is going to be asked about it eventually, and if he is ever asked about it he is going to have to answer, and he is not going to be able to answer without putting himself on the landing.”

Nobody on that landing said that was a good answer or a bad one.

“And you have made me say that out loud, on the first morning of a week, on a landing where an iron wedge has been sitting in a step for a fortnight making a man’s work easier.” He looked round. “So I would like somebody to have not asked me that this morning. Not because it was cruel. Because I have spent four hundred and twenty-five days not being asked anything and I had got quite good at it.”

Nobody helped him with any part of that, and nobody disagreed with it, and nobody told him he had answered well.

“And I am not going to be unrelieved about having said it, and I am not going to be relieved either, and I want it noticed that those two are the same thing tonight and I am carrying both of them up that bank myself.”

“I did not ask it for the book,” said Sena Dorr, from the bottom of the steps. “I am going to say that in front of everybody because I would rather it were said here than wondered about later. I have a book and it is shut and it is going to be shut when I go down that road. I asked it because I have read for twenty-three years and I do not know what a man says to that, and I have just found out, and it goes in nothing.”

Nobody asked her what a man says to it, because he had said it.

“And I am not going to be thanked for asking him either,” she said, “and I will not be thanked for saying that either, and I am aware that I have now said three of those in four days and that I am a woman who says them like a woman who has never been thanked for anything and I am not going to be told about it.”

Then the man of thirty-one said the thing he had been carrying up the bank since Wednesday, which was what he wanted and what it had cost him to get it out, and he said it into a landing that did not help him with it.

“I have wanted one thing since I was nineteen and I have never said it out loud in this county and I am going to say it now because a woman asked me a question I could not get round this morning and I have run out of other sentences.”

Nobody stopped him, and nobody had wanted to.

“I want to be somebody.” He said it and then stood there while it sat there. “Not a witness and not a man with figures. Somebody. And I have worked out this fortnight, on your steps, from the bottom of them, that the only road to being somebody in this county is to be useful to a man who works here, and that a man who works here has not needed me for one minute of nineteen years, and that I have been given nothing to do.”

Nobody on that landing gave him anything to do.

“And that is not a thing anybody has done to me and I am not going to put it on this landing, because this landing has not had a hand in it. It is the shape of me.” He turned round. “And a small thing is not a lesser thing and I am not going to be told it is, and I am not going to be thanked for saying it, and nobody is going to help me with it, and I have not asked.”

Nobody helped him with it, nobody told him it was a small thing, nobody told him it was not, and nobody thanked him for saying it.

Ninety swept, the chair left standing and the seam left alone, and nothing has been pulled, or turned over, or weighed in a hand, or read aloud, or carried inside, or put back where it was, or used. A man of thirty-one said out loud on the first morning of the hundred and sixty-second week that he did not know what he would do, and nobody helped him with it and nobody told him he was right.

# Chapter 980: A Whole Figure In A Column That Nobody Explained, And A Woman Of Forty-Four At A Wall Saying Out Loud, Before Anybody Walked Up That Road, That She Was Not To Be Put In It And That Nothing Was To Be Carried Up It

Second day of the hundred and sixty-second week. His seven hundred and thirtieth morning. Five hundred and eighty days after the settlement. The fever ninety-nine weeks and four days old. Five hundred and thirty-four days since the division. Four hundred and eighty days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and twenty-six days in this county of Kell.

Eight inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, about eighty of those ninety steps under, and six days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and then stood on the top step and listened to the whole of what the morning had to say, and it was the last morning of a week and nothing on it was about the water.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the second day of the hundred and sixty-second week that he was going to say nothing this morning beyond the three figures and let the landing get on with whatever it had got.

“Eight at the seventh hour, six days of the coming back, and about eighty of those ninety steps under, on the second day of the hundred and sixty-second week, and it is a Wednesday. Three figures.”

He set the broom against the wall and left it there for about a quarter of an hour, which is the longest it has been off his hand in nineteen years.

“Right,” he said, eventually. “That is all I have got.”

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour and asked in the same words at the same hour and was refused in the same way and was not thanked, and said out loud that this was the last morning of a week he was going to ask on and that the next one would be the same question.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and fifty-ninth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is nine hundred and eighty less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and fifty-ninth. Nine hundred and eighty less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

“Nobody asked you for it. I am not going to be nicer about it on the last morning of a week than I was on the first.”

“No, and I have been thinking about what I said on Sunday about there being a morning coming when I stop, and I have worked out that saying that out loud was the only way I was ever going to stop and not be relieved about it.” He set the barrow down. “So I am not stopping. I am going to go on being asked whether I have stopped. That is the same as not stopping and it costs me more, and I would like it recorded that it costs me more.”

It was not recorded and nobody argued with it.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.” He said it in the words he uses. “And it is the last one this week and I am not going to change it for the last one of anything.”

That question was not answered on the second morning of a week, nobody told Barnaby Crove he was owed an answer, nobody thanked him, and nobody was going to thank him for the last morning of asking on it.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the eighth hour with eight inches above his boots and gave his figures and the figure of the offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing, and gave a whole figure of himself out loud in a column, and nobody on that landing said one word about it in any direction.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and twenty-six days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and ninety-five days old.

“Four hundred and twenty-six days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and ninety-five days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-thirtieth is two hundred and eighty days old. That is nine hundred and eighty less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it, and nobody has offered me a way out of it, and I am still standing here with it at the end of a week.”

He got to the bottom of the steps and stood there and did not go up them, which is not what he does.

“A hundred and twenty wrong things in a hundred and twenty mornings.” He said it flat and he did not stop in front of it. “That is a whole figure. It has been a whole figure for some days now and it is the only whole figure I have ever had, and this morning I am going to say the thing I have wanted to say about it since I noticed, and then I am going to stand here and find out what happens.”

He did not stop and nobody on that landing stopped him.

“A whole figure in a column is a meaning the moment somebody opens their mouth.” He kept his hands behind his back. “That is all. That is the whole of it and I have been carrying it up that bank since Thursday. One hundred and twenty is a column. A column is a place where a thing has been counted more than once. The day one of you hands me a word for that column, or the word small, or the word large, or a hundred and twenty mornings of this county, or a hundred and twenty mornings of mine, that column becomes a thing about me and not about the county.”

Nobody told him it was small and nobody told him it was large.

“Not one of you is going to say it. Nobody will tell me what it is for. Nobody will thank me for it. Nobody is going to tell me that I found anything, because I have not found anything, and I have said the figure out loud and that is the whole of what I have done with it.”

Nobody told him he had found anything, and nobody told him he had not found anything.

“There is no relief in that silence and there is no other feeling in it either, and this landing is not going to get thanks out of me for the second one, because the second one is the reason I have spent a fortnight standing at the bottom of a set of steps.” 

He took a breath and it was not a steady one. “And I am going to stand here for the rest of this morning with a whole figure in my mouth and nothing whatever done about it, and that is the second time I have done that in this county and I am not going to do it a third time, and I am not telling you what the third time is going to be.”

The third time was to stay unsaid until somebody opened their mouth.

Then the man who moves chairs said out loud, in front of everybody, that there was a thing he had been carrying up the cart road in his mouth since the Monday before last. He had decided this morning he was leaving it at the wall, and a woman was standing at the wall.

Aldis Rook moves the chairs for Orla Fennimore and keeps nothing and has no stone and has no seat and no board, has never once been asked what he did, has been told not to come up that bank and has come anyway, and on the second day of the hundred and sixty-second week said out loud that he was not going to carry the morning up the cart road.

“There is a thing I have had in my mouth since the Monday before last and I am leaving it at that wall.” He did not point at her and nobody did either. “I move chairs up that road. I have been up it four mornings this week. And I am not going to be carrying any part of this landing up it, not this morning and not when the chairs are done, and I would like it noticed that I noticed it on the Monday before last and said nothing about it until this morning.”

Nobody noticed it before him.

“Because a thing that gets carried up that road stops being a thing on this landing. It starts being a thing about a house where twenty-nine chairs are kept. There is a woman standing at that wall who has kept out of this landing for nineteen years by not knowing what is on the other side of it, and I am not going to be the one who carries it over.”

Orla Fennimore is forty-four, keeps twenty-nine chairs at a house that is not in this county, was asked once what she did and has not been asked since, and was at the wall on the second day of the hundred and sixty-second week before the seventh hour with the wind off it.

“Nobody is to come up this road and tell me anything.” She said it without coming past the wall. “Not about a thing in a step, not about a man whose work got easier, not about what a man of thirty-one said out loud on Tuesday after a woman had put one question to him in front of the whole landing, and not about this landing at all.”

Nothing on that landing had been said to her.

“You did not hear that from me on Tuesday,” she said, “and if you did, then somebody on this landing has been carrying a morning up that road, and I would like that to stop this morning and I would like it to stop without an inquiry.”

Nobody started an inquiry.

“And I am not to be thanked for standing at this wall. I am going to say that now, before any of you walks up here and decides I stood here for something, because that is exactly what happens, and then I am the reason somebody came up this road, and I will not have it.”

Nobody thanked her.

“There is nothing to thank me for, and nothing to thank me for saying so out loud, and if somebody does thank me then that person has turned a thing I have kept out of into a thing I did, and then I will have to think about it, and I have not come down that road to think about anything.”

She did not come past the wall.

“I am going back down it now at the pace I came up, and nothing is going to be said on this road behind my back, and the man who moves my chairs is not going to follow me up it either, and neither is anybody else.”

She went back down the cart road at the pace she had come up, and nobody on that landing went up it after her, and nothing was said on that road behind her back, and nobody thanked her and nobody said they wished they had.

Then the man who moves chairs said the whole of the house out loud, in his own mouth, in his own words, about his own trade, and it was the last thing said on that landing that morning and it resolved nothing.

“I am going to say one thing and then I am going down that road and I am not going to be thanked for it and I am not going to be asked about it afterwards.” He stood at the top of the steps. “A thing put down where somebody works is owed nothing by anybody. Not by the man whose work it makes easier, because a man cannot be owed something by an object. Not by the person who put it there, because that person does not work here and is not on this step and is not going to be put on it by anybody standing here.”

Nobody on that landing agreed with him and nobody disagreed.

“I have moved furniture into houses for nineteen years and that is the only sentence about a trade I have ever needed. I have needed it because I have spent nineteen years putting things in rooms where somebody works and going out of the door before they come in. That is a way of working. It is not a gift and it is not a kindness, and nobody on this step is going to say either of those words about it.”

Not one of those two words was said about it.

“And it has not been touched. Not pulled, not turned over, not weighed in the hand, not read out loud, not carried inside, not put back where it was, and not used. Seven things, said this morning by me. I have said them before on this landing and I am not going to say them again. There is a man who sweeps this stone who has not said them in a fortnight because he gave them up, and I would like that noted as a thing he gave up and not as a thing he is owed.”

Nobody noted it and nobody thanked him for it.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety steps and went round the fortieth one and went round the chair. He did not sit on the chair. He did not touch the thing in the seam of the fortieth step and he did not say who had put it there. Nothing that had been put down anywhere was pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used. The chair stood where it had been put and had never once been sat on and had never once been touched.

There is no giver in anything standing on that landing. The offer was at two hundred and eighty days and unanswered. A whole figure of a hundred and twenty wrong things was said out loud this morning and nobody explained it, and nobody told him anything about it, and nobody told him he had found anything. A woman of forty-four stood at a wall and said out loud, before anybody thanked her, that she was not to be thanked. Nobody thanked her and nobody went up that road. The thing in the fortieth step was still there and still unclaimed, and the account was as incomplete as it had been at the sixth hour.

# Chapter 991: A Sunday And Sixteen Inches That Hold And A Man Of Thirty-One Put A Second Thing Down On That Dry Strip And Said Nothing About It And Walked Down That Bank And Did Not Come Back Up It And Nobody Was Told And Nobody Was Thanked

Sixth day of the hundred and sixty-third week. His seven hundred and forty-first morning. Five hundred and ninety-one days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and one weeks and one day old. Five hundred and forty-five days since the division. Four hundred and ninety-one days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and thirty-seven days in this county of Kell.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and it holds, about sixty of those ninety steps under, and three days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and then said that there was a Monday coming and that a Sunday was not an occasion either, and that he had said so on this landing before and was not going to say it in a new way this morning.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the sixth day of the hundred and sixty-third week that he had come to this stone before the light came up and that there was one piece of wood on the dry strip, and that one piece of wood was not a figure.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, three days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the sixth day of the hundred and sixty-third week, and it is a Sunday. Three figures.”

He leaned the broom against that wall on the strip, clear of both pieces of ash, in the notch it has stood in all week.

“A week turning is a thing that happens to a word and not to a landing. I have said that on this stone and I am not going to say it again today, and I would like it noticed that I have said it once and have been carrying it ever since, and that carrying one sentence around for a fortnight is the whole of what I have got.”

Nobody said whether he was carrying it.

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour and asked in the same words at the same hour and was refused in the same way and was not thanked, and said out loud that the water was not going to come off those steps tomorrow either, whatever anybody wanted.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and seventieth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is nine hundred and ninety-one less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and seventieth. Nine hundred and ninety-one less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

“Nobody asked you for it, and it is a Sunday, and neither of those two things makes a difference to the question.”

“No.” He set the barrow down. “Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it. And it is a Sunday and tomorrow is a Monday and it is going to be the same question in the same words, and I am not going to get up on that Monday and put a new coat on it because a week will have turned in the night.”

Nobody answered Barnaby Crove on that Sunday, nobody told him he was owed an answer, nobody thanked him for the asking of it, and he was going to be up on the Monday with the same question in the same words.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the eighth hour with sixteen inches above his boots and gave his figures and gave the figure of the offer with the subtraction out loud and asked for nothing.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and thirty-seven days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and six days old.

“Four hundred and thirty-seven days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and six days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-forty-first is two hundred and ninety-one days old, which is nine hundred and ninety-one less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it this morning and I am not going to ask it this afternoon either, and nobody has offered me a way out of it, and I did not ask for one.”

He got to the dry strip along the foot of the wall and stood with his boots where they were.

“One hundred and thirty-one wrong things in one hundred and thirty-one mornings, and this is the hundred and thirty-first, and nobody is going to tell me what kind of figure that is and nobody is going to tell me that I found anything.”

He was told not what kind of figure it was and not that he had found anything.

“And here is the cost of it, in my own mouth, because there is nobody on this landing who owes me the saying of it. Asking is the only thing that costs me nothing. Four hundred and thirty-seven days and I have not asked one thing for on any of them, and not one person standing on these steps has ever been able to give me anything for it, and that is the trade I have got, and it is the only road to being somebody that I have been able to find in this county.”

Nobody said he was right about that and nobody said he was wrong about it.

He went along the strip to the end of it and stopped with his back to the landing.

“And I want to say one more thing and I am going to say it into the wall, because there is nobody behind me and that is the shape of it.”

Nothing came out of him for a while.

“There is a want. It is the same want I said out loud on this landing a week ago and I am not going to unsay it because a want does not have to be a good one to be true. I want to be somebody. And I have worked out over four hundred and thirty-seven mornings that the only road to that in this county is to be useful to a man who works here, and no man who works here has needed me for one minute of nineteen years.”

He put his left hand up against the stone of the wall and then took it down again.

“And I have got one thing left that nobody can take off me. I can walk down that bank at any hour and leave. Nobody here can stop me and nobody here has ever asked me to stay. I am not going to say how near I came to it this morning, because a figure of how far a man walked away from a thing is a figure of the man and not of the thing, and this landing has had a fortnight of figures about men and not one of them was any use.”

Nobody asked him how near he came to it.

Then he knelt down on the flat of the step above the water and set a second thing down on the dry strip.

It was a length of ash about as long as a man’s forearm. It had been split down the middle in the same way as the other one, with a wedge and a block of wood at the bottom of the lane before the light came up. The thick end was left as the wood came apart. The thin end was cut off square with a saw that wanted sharpening, and two shallow notches had been cut into the flat of that square end, and one of the two had run out of the side because the saw went off at the corner.

The man with the broom was at the top of the steps and did not turn round and did not go down to look. The woman who saws lengths was not on that landing. Nobody said a word.

The man of thirty-one had it in his mouth. He had the whole of it ready, in the shape he had used a week ago when he said out loud that he put the other one there. The sentence was the same sentence and there was one word in it that would have made it true of two things instead of one.

He did not say it. Not because anybody was listening, and not because nobody was listening.

He stood up, brushed the front of his trousers where the stone had taken the dust off it, went down the steps that were not under, and walked off that landing and down that bank without stopping at the bottom of it.

Nobody on that landing asked him a single question on the way, and nobody went down that bank after him, and nothing was said behind his back.

Then the man who cannot see well came up onto the flat of the steps at about the tenth hour with his hand out in front of him and found the wall with it and stopped with his fingers flat on the stone.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and said on the sixth day of the hundred and sixty-third week that there were two pieces of wood on that strip and that he was not going to put his hand out to find out which was which.

“There are two pieces of wood on that strip this morning and I could not tell you one from the other if I stood here until dark.” He said it flat against the wall. “I have known since I got my hand on the wall that there were two. I heard the second one go down. I have not touched it and I am not going to and I am not going to put my hand out flat on that strip to find out, and I would like it understood that that is not caution of any kind you could teach a child.”

Nobody taught him anything.

“Because a hand going out flat on a strip where wood is lying is how wood on a strip gets moved. And that is all it is. It does not take a decision. It happens because a man is looking for something and his hand arrives first and his head arrives after. I have watched a man do it with a door in this county and I have watched the other half of it, which is nobody saying anything.”

He kept his hand where it was.

“It has not been touched. Not pulled, not turned over, not weighed in the hand, not read out loud, not carried inside, not put back where it was, and not used. Seven things, and I have said them more times than anybody on this stone and I am not going to be thanked for this one and I would like somebody to have taken a turn at it by now.”

Nobody took a turn at it and nobody thanked him.

“And I am going to say the rest of it and then I am going down, and it is about the man of thirty-one and not about the two pieces of wood.” He turned his face away from the wall. “He has not said anything. Nobody on this landing has said anything about what he did or did not say, and I want that written nowhere, because the second I write it down somewhere it becomes a thing somebody said, and a thing somebody said is a thing with a mouth in it.”

His hand came off the wall.

“He is the only person on this landing who knows what is on that strip. That is not a figure and it is not a thing anybody is to use. I have got a list of things I cannot check and this morning the list has got one more item on it and I am not going to put a person on that list and I am not going to leave one off it either.”

Nobody put a person on it and nobody left one off.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety steps, though about sixty of them were under, and went round the fortieth one, and went round the chair, and did not sit on the chair. He swept round both pieces of ash on the dry strip and he did not put the broom near either of them. He did not touch the iron in the seam of the fortieth step and he did not say who had put the second piece of wood there, and nobody on that landing asked him to.

Nothing on that landing was pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used. There is no giver in the second piece of ash, and nobody has been told that a person put it down.

A man of thirty-one said his figures and the figure of an offer nobody has answered and the figure of his own mistakes out loud on a Sunday, and then put a thing down on that landing and said nothing about it, and walked down that bank and did not come back up it. Nobody was told. Nobody was thanked. Nobody was relieved, and the week did not turn that night, and it made no difference either way to a dry strip above sixteen inches of water.

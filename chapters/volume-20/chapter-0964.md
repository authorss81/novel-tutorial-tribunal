# Chapter 964: Eight Inches On The Top Step And About Eighty Of Those Ninety Steps Under And Six Days Of The Coming Back, And A Woman Who Reads For A Living Got Most Of The Way Through A Sentence And Was Stopped By The Man With The Broom

Seventh and last day of the hundred and fifty-ninth week. His seven hundred and fourteenth morning. Five hundred and sixty-four days after the settlement. The fever ninety-seven weeks and two days old. Five hundred and eighteen days since the division. Four hundred and sixty-four days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and ten days in this county of Kell.

Eight inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, about eighty of those ninety steps under, and six days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and then gave the fourth thing, which was not about the water, and which he has given once in nineteen years on a last morning of a week.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the last morning of the hundred and fifty-ninth week that a keeper is at his most dangerous at the end of a week and that he was going to spend whatever he had on refusing.

“Eight at the seventh hour, six days of the coming back, and about eighty of those ninety steps under, on the seventh and last morning of the hundred and fifty-ninth week, and it is a Monday. Three figures.”

He set the broom down flat on the stone.

“And here is the fourth thing, and it is not about the water, and I am not going to say it every week.” He put his hand flat on the broom handle. “This is the closing morning of the week and it is the one I am worst on. On a Monday morning anybody in this county feels that something ought to come, and the person who gives it is always the man with a stone and nineteen years, and I have given it twice in nineteen years and both times it was a Monday, and I could not undo either of them, and that is the whole of what I know about Mondays.”

“Which two.”

“Does not matter. I am telling you about my Mondays so that you know what I am doing on mine and not so that you can go and look at what I did.” He picked the broom up again. “So here is what I am doing. I am going to say, once, that on the last morning of this week I am not going to say anything I have not said on the other six mornings of it. That is all. I have got a week in me that I have not said and I am not going to hand it over on a Monday because a Monday is the morning it would be worth the most.”

Nobody on that landing asked him what the week was.

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow and asked in the same words at the same hour and was refused in the same way and was not thanked, and said what he was going to do about it, which was nothing.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and forty-third mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is nine hundred and sixty-four less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and forty-third. Nine hundred and sixty-four less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning.”

“Nobody put a hand on it before you got here.”

“No, and I have asked on every morning of that week and been refused on every morning of it, and this is the last of them, and I am not going to say anything clever about the week turning because a clever thing said on the last morning of a week is a thing that gets said back to you on the first morning of the next one and it will not fit then.” He set the barrow against the bottom step. “Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.”

Nobody answered him. Nobody told him the question was good. Nobody told him it was owed an answer, and nobody thanked him for the last morning of the week, which he had told them in advance what he would do about.

Then the woman who reads the ninth run came up the road and entered the morning and had something in it and would not say what, and stood at the bottom of the steps with the book shut for a long time, and then started to say a thing about a person who puts something down and is not there, and got most of the way through it.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years, holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, gave her one question up on the sixth morning of the hundred and fifty-eighth week and has not taken it back, and got most of the way through a sentence on the last morning of the hundred and fifty-ninth week and was stopped.

“You told us on Saturday morning what a bare stone is,” she said. “That is what has been sitting in my mouth for two days. And it is not what I want to say, and I have been turning it over since Saturday, and I am going to say it now while I have the nerve and then I am going to stop where somebody tells me to.”

Nobody said anything.

“A thing gets left where somebody works and nobody can say who left it, and everybody works out what it must have meant by being there, and the whole of what that is — the being there, the not being told, the not being asked, the person who is not on the step and is not anywhere else either.” She had her hand flat on the book. “And a thing like that can be read. That is the part I cannot get past. It can be read as though somebody who was not there had — ”

“Do not finish that sentence.”

Corvin Tace said it from four steps down with the broom in his hand and did not stop coming.

“I am going to finish it.”

“No, you are not, and I am going to tell you why in one sentence and then you can decide whether to be angry with me.” He came down to the flat of the steps. “This is the closing morning of the week. A sentence begun here on a closing morning and finished here on a closing morning is a thing that four people carry off this bank this morning and say to each other in the wrong weeks for the next nine days, and by the middle of next week it will have got said twice by two people who were not on this landing, and the second one of those will be the one that sticks.”

“That is a reason for the day of the week.”

“It is a reason for the day of the week and it is the only reason I have got. I do not have a better one. I do not have a moral one and I have not got one about how much it would take.” He set the broom against the wall at the bottom of the steps.

“You have had that sentence since Saturday and you have been carrying it since Saturday and it has got no worse in two days, and it will still be there on a morning when there is a figure on the stone in front of me. Say it then if you want to say it. I will not stop you. I will not ask you not to. I am telling you that this is the wrong morning and I am telling you why and then I am going to go and sweep.”

He went and swept.

Sena Dorr stood at the bottom of the steps with the book shut and did not finish the sentence, and after a while she said one more thing and it was not the other half of it.

“I am not going to finish it,” she said. “And I want it noticed that I stopped because a man who holds a landing told me the day of the week, and not because I had worked out that it was the wrong thing to say, because I had not worked that out and I still think it may be the right thing to say.” She put the book inside her coat.

“That is the second time in a fortnight that something has been given up on that landing for a reason that is smaller than the thing. And I am not blaming him for it and I am not going to say anything on this step about what he is.”

Nobody said anything on that step about what he was.

The man of thirty-one came up in the middle of the morning with eight inches above his own boots and gave his figures and the figure of the offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing, and then said out loud that he had been carrying something since Friday and was not going to put it down anywhere on that landing.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and ten days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and seventy-nine days old.

“Four hundred and ten days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and seventy-nine days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-fourteenth is two hundred and sixty-four days old, which is nine hundred and sixty-four less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

“One hundred and four wrong things in one hundred and four mornings, and here is the hundred and fourth, and it is about me and it is the plainest one I have said this week.” He stood with his hands behind him at the back of the top step.

“I have been carrying something since Friday. It is not a thing and it is not a plan and I am not going to put it down here this morning and I am not going to say what it is about, and I would like it noticed that I have said I am carrying it in front of everybody so that nobody spends the morning working out whether I am going to.”

“Can I say that nobody has asked you what it is.”

“You can say that and it is true and it is not the same as saying nobody wants to.”

He turned round and looked down the bank.

“And the second half, and this is the half I have not said to anybody. I am going to go on asking the man with the barrow things he will not answer me, on mornings when I know in advance he will not answer me. Not out loud. Out loud is the last thing. Out loud I ask one question and I am refused and I carry it. What I am going to do is spend the other two hundred and sixty-four days of the offer on days where I already know the answer, and nobody on that landing is going to thank me for it and nobody is going to tell me it is a good use of anything.”

“That is not a want,” said the man with the broom. “That is a habit, and I have told you what a habit is.”

“It is a habit and it is the first one I have admitted to on this landing and I am not going to argue with the man who used the word correctly two days ago.” He kept his hands where they were. “I would rather have a habit I have said out loud than a want I have dressed up. You have got one of those off me this week and it took you a broom.”

Nobody laughed and nobody agreed.

The man of fifty-four went down the bank with the barrow and asked again halfway down, to nobody, in the same words, and said that he would ask again on the first morning of the next week and would rather have it, and nobody answered him, because there was nobody there.

Corvin Tace swept his way up eighty-nine steps and went round the fortieth and went round the chair and set the broom against the wall where it has been every morning for nineteen years, and did not sit on the chair, and did not touch the thing in the seam of the fortieth step, and nothing that had been put down anywhere was pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used.

And the hundred and fifty-ninth week closed on a landing where a shape was in use for the second week running and had still not been given a name by anybody on it, where a sentence about a person who was not there had got as far as a woman’s mouth and had been stopped on a Monday because Mondays are the mornings a keeper is worst on, and where the water was eight inches and going up about four inches a day and nobody had said what any of it was for.

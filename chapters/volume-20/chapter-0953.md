# Chapter 953: Sixteen Inches And One Day Of The Coming Back, And A Man Of Fifty-Eight Has Come A Mile Up The High Side To Say There Is A Thing On A Hand-Rail Where He Takes Both His Hands

Third day of the hundred and fifty-eighth week. His seven hundred and third morning. Five hundred and fifty-three days after the settlement. The fever ninety-five weeks and five days old. Five hundred and seven days since the division. Four hundred and fifty-three days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and three hundred and ninety-nine days in this county of Kell.

The man of fifty-eight came up the high side with coal dust in the creases of both palms and a sack of kindling on his shoulder, and he put the kindling down at the top of the steps and turned his hands over and looked at them before he said anything at all.

Jonas Trill is fifty-eight, carries coal a mile up the high side of that landing to a yard with no well in it, takes both hands on a hand-rail at the top of a short flight of stone steps to get up to the yard because he cannot get up it otherwise, and has never once been asked how he gets up his own steps.

“I want to be careful about the order I say this in,” he said, “because I have worked out on the way up here that I have two things and one of them is nothing and one of them is not nothing, and if I say the wrong one first then this step will do with me what it does.”

“Say the nothing one first.”

“There is a thing on my handrail.” He wiped his palms down his thighs. “That is the nothing one. I do not know who put it there. I have not worked it out and I am not going to. It is not mine and it is not the shape of anything I own, and it is on that handrail, at the end where I get hold of it with both hands. And it has not been touched, and I did not touch it this morning when I came past it, and I am not going to touch it tomorrow.”

“Then say the other one.”

“The other one is that I am not going to ask the woman at that yard whether she took it.” He looked up the bank at the step and did not come up it. “And I am not going to be asked, either, and I want it noticed that I have said the whole of it out loud before anybody on this landing works out that they want to ask her, because that is the only advantage I have got and I am spending it early.”

He put his hand flat against his chest where the sack had been.



“And the reason I came a mile to say it is that I take both hands on that rail two hundred mornings a year and I have worked out this week that the whole difference between me and a man who has a rail and a man who has not got a rail is that one of us is standing on a thing that somebody worked out for him before he got there.

“That is all it is. It is not a kindness and it is not a gift and it is not a favour and nobody has to be thanked for it, because the person who cut that rail did not do it for me and I have never met them and I could not tell you their name.”

Corvin Tace came out and gave the three figures before he came down a single step, the way he gives them, and then stood at the top of it and looked at the man with the coal dust in his hands.

“Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and it holds, about sixty of those ninety steps under, and one day of the coming back. Three. You have not had a fourth out of me in nineteen years and you are not going to have one now.”

“That is three and I counted them.”

“Nobody counts them but me and that is the arrangement and it is not a complaint.” He leaned the broom against the wall. “A rail cut for a man before he gets to it is one of the best things there is, and it has no giver in it that the railer can use.

“I have been on this landing nineteen years and I have watched about nine of those get put in in my time, for people who were not here when I started, and not one of them ever got asked whether they were using it. And that is the best thing about a rail.”

“Then say it about the wedge in the step.”

“I have said what I say about it and I am not going to say it twice in a morning because a man has come a mile with coal on his shoulder.” He looked along the step. “And I am not going to have anybody use that word about it either. It is not a rail. It is not put in for anybody. Nobody asked for it and nobody said it was wanted and the step with the chip in the corner of it was doing its work that day without it, and there is a difference. And I have watched about nine of you confuse the two in a fortnight.”

The man of fifty-four was already at the bottom of the steps with his barrow when the man of fifty-eight was still talking, and he came up the steps in his own time and stopped three steps from the top, which is where a man who has been coming up here for nineteen years stops when he wants to be heard from below.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read, and carried two hundred and thirty-two mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is nine hundred and fifty-three less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one, and asked for nothing on ten mornings and has asked on every morning since the day he came back.

“Two hundred and thirty-second,” he said. “Nine hundred and fifty-three less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning.”

Nobody asked him for it.

“And I am going to ask the same thing I asked yesterday and it is going to be refused the same way and I would like it refused the same way, because a refusal I know is worth more to me than a refusal that is new.”

He set the barrow handle in both hands.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it. That is the whole question and I have asked it twice and I am going to keep on asking it until it stops meaning anything, and I am not going to be thanked for asking it.”

Nobody answered him, and nobody told him it was a good question, and nobody told him it was not.

Then the man of thirty-one came up and gave his figures and asked the man of fifty-eight to let him stand on the fortieth step with his weight on it and tell him what it does, and it was refused by the man with the broom with the working given.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and ninety-nine days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and sixty-eight days old.

“Three hundred and ninety-nine days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and sixty-eight days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-third is two hundred and fifty-three days old, which is nine hundred and fifty-three less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

He turned to the man of fifty-eight.

“You are the only man who has come up here this week who works at a thing that a thing has been put on. I am not asking to touch anything. I am asking to stand on the fortieth one with my weight on it, the way a man does who does not know what is in the seam, and to say out loud whether it moves. That is not an experiment about the thing in the seam. That is a man’s boot on a step.”

“That is a man’s boot on a step and it is an experiment,” said Corvin Tace, “and the reason I am refusing it is not that you want to know. It is that the only experiment in this county that would answer the question anybody wants answered is the one that needs a person to come along and decide they are the one who does it, and there is nobody on this landing with the standing to decide that, and there is not going to be one, and if you stand on that step this morning then the man who does it is you, and it will be you for the rest of the year.”

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, and has never once given a fifth.

“Ninety-three wrong things in ninety-three mornings, and here is the ninety-third, and I have been carrying it since Monday and it has got heavier every day.”

He put his hands behind his back and left them there.

“If nobody in this county has standing to do that, then nobody in this county is going to stand there. And the thing on that step is going to go on being a thing on that step, and the woman at the yard is going to go on not being asked about her rail, and I am going to go on being a man who can walk down a bank and leave, and there is not one thing in this morning that any of us can put down.

“I have spent nineteen years wanting somebody in this county to ask me a question and every single morning this week there has been a moment when somebody could have, and it has been the wrong moment every time, and I am starting to think that is the shape of it and not a series of accidents.”

Nobody on that landing called it a series of anything.

Barnaby Crove went down the bank with the barrow and stopped halfway and said out loud, to nobody, that he would ask again on the fourth morning of this week and that he wanted it refused the same way. The man of fifty-eight went back up the high side to a yard with no well in it and did not take the thing off the handrail when he passed it, and did not put a hand past it, and did not use it.

Nothing that had been put down anywhere was pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used. The man of thirty-one went up the cart road and did not stop at the fortieth step. The man of forty-three swept the top step from the high end to the low end and set the broom against the wall where it has been every morning for nineteen years, and did not say a fifth thing.

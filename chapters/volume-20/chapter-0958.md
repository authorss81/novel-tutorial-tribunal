# Chapter 958: First Day Of The Hundred And Fifty-Ninth Week, And A Man Described One Thing In The Same Words He Uses Every Morning, And A Man With A Broom Told Him To Stop, Then Let Him Go On

First day of the hundred and fifty-ninth week. His seven hundred and eighth morning. Five hundred and fifty-eight days after the settlement. The fever ninety-six weeks and three days old. Five hundred and twelve days since the division. Four hundred and fifty-eight days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and four days in this county of Kell.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, and that was a full bank, four days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave those three figures, said that it was a full bank, and then made sure that nobody on that landing had gone away thinking that a full bank was a kind of morning.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the first morning of a week that a full bank is a figure of water and not a kind of morning.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour, that is a full bank, four days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the first day of the hundred and fifty-ninth week, and it is a Tuesday. Three figures.”

He set the broom down rather than lean it.

“And before the rest of you spend the morning on that: a full bank is four days. It is not a good morning. It is not a day of reckoning. It is not the day anything gets decided on. It is the day the water has been in the same place for as long as it is going to be in the same place, and the only thing anybody can do with it is wait for it, and I have been waiting for it for nineteen years and I have never once got anything out of the waiting.”

Nobody asked him for a fifth thing.

Then the man who moves chairs came up past the wall and described the step with the chip in it in the same words the man with the broom had used on the first morning of the week, and the man with the broom told him to stop, and the man who moves chairs said he would not, because a thing described twice is how a person finds out that nobody has touched it.

Aldis Rook moves the chairs for Orla Fennimore and keeps nothing and has no stone and has no seat and no board, has never once been asked what he did, and has stood at the back of that landing on six mornings now without going down the road to the village.

“There is an iron wedge in the seam of one of those ninety steps,” he said. “It is in the step with the corner chipped off the top left. One face of it is worn smooth and smooth is the only word I have for what a boot heel does to iron. And nobody put it there and I do not know who did and it has not been touched.”

“That is my description.”

“It is your description word for word and I have been carrying it up that road since Monday.”

“Then stop carrying it.”

“I am not going to stop carrying it and here is why, and you can say this is a stupid reason and I would rather say it than not say it.”

He put his hands in front of him and looked at them.

“If a thing is put down and nobody touches it and nobody picks it up and nobody says who put it there, then after a while there is no way on earth for anybody to tell whether it is still being left or whether it has been there since the beginning. And when those two stop being different, then the whole of what happened this week has stopped having a shape. And a thing with no shape is a thing somebody can put a name on tomorrow, and a name is how you start asking again.”

Corvin Tace stood at the top of the steps with the broom in his hand and did not say anything for long enough that two people looked at him.

“That is the cleverest thing anybody has said on this step since he came to it and I have no answer to it and I am not going to have one in front of the whole lot of you.”

“You do not have to have one.”

“I have to have one by Friday because I am the man who holds this landing and if nobody on it can say what is going on then I am the only one left who has to say it, and that is nineteen years of my life in one sentence.”

He set the broom against the stone of the wall.

“And I am not going to tell you that you are right, because you are half right and half of a thing is worse than none of it, and the half you are right about is that it has not been touched, and the half you have not worked out is that nobody on this landing is going to keep describing it twice for you, and if it is going to be described it is going to be described by me, once a week, in my own words, and not on a Monday when a man who cannot read has just said his figure and everybody is in a mood.”

“I am not asking to be told I am right.”

“No. That is why I am letting you say it. I am going to say this once and it is the only time: nobody is going to thank you for carrying that up that road, and nobody is going to tell you that you got it right, and if you go on carrying it I will let you, and if you stop I will not ask you why.”

The man who cannot see well was at the back of the landing before either of them had finished, standing with his back to the water, because the light off sixteen inches is the one thing on that bank he has never been able to look at.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has named about nine things he cannot check and has never once been asked what would stop him, and has been at the back of that landing every morning this week where nobody can put a question to him.

“I can tell you the bank is full from the sound it makes,” he said, “and I have not been able to look at it since the water came in the first week of the year, and nobody on this step has noticed, and I would like it left that way for about a week.”

“Nothing is going to be asked you,” said the man who moves chairs. “You did not put that in and I am not going to put it in.”

“I did not put it in.” He did not turn round. “And I would like it said that I have not said what I have just said because somebody asked me. I have said it because it is the only fact I have got and I have wanted to put one down all week and this is the only one I have and it is worth nothing.”

Nobody said what it was worth. Nobody said what would stop him.

Then the man of thirty-one came up and gave his four figures and said the figure of his own mistakes out loud, and worked out what comes next, and told the landing he was not going to think about it, and nobody on that landing helped him.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and four days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and seventy-three days old.

“Four hundred and four days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and seventy-three days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-eighth is two hundred and fifty-eight days old, which is nine hundred and fifty-eight less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

He said the last of it while he was still coming up the steps, which was new.

“Ninety-eight wrong things in ninety-eight mornings. And I am going to work out in front of you that tomorrow that is ninety-nine and the morning after that it is a whole figure, and I am telling you I have worked it out because I have worked it out and I am not going to pretend on Thursday morning that I had not.”

Nobody said anything about it.

“I am not going to think about it between now and Thursday. I have got a whole figure coming and it will be here on Thursday morning whatever I do with Wednesday and Thursday, and there is not one thing in this county I can do with it, and I have watched men get handed a figure this week and go off and make a meaning out of it, and I am not one of them, and I am saying so now while it is still cheap to say so.”

He stopped at the top of the steps.

“And if anybody on this landing wants to say something helpful to me on Thursday morning, the thing I would like instead is that everybody keeps saying the four days.”

Corvin Tace looked at him. “The four days are yours and they are water.”

“They are water and they are the only figure I have been handed in four hundred and four days that nobody has asked me a question about. So they are mine.”

Nobody thanked him for that either.

The water stood at sixteen inches on the top step and held there, and the man of forty-three made sure of it himself at about the tenth hour by going up and standing beside it and not in it, and about sixty of those ninety steps were under.

And the water would be four days off that bank, and then there would be a morning with nothing at all on the stone.

Nobody on that landing said anything about that morning.

The man who could not see well went down the road with his back to the water the whole way. The man who moves chairs went down the cart road with another description in his mouth that he did not say. And the step with the corner chipped off the top left went on not rocking, with an iron wedge in the seam of it, one face worn smooth, and nobody who had come up that road all week had touched it, and nobody had said who put it there.

# Chapter 971: The Man With The Broom Said Out Loud That The Work On That Landing Had Got Easier And Gave The Working For It And Nobody Asked Him

Seventh and last day of the hundred and sixtieth week. His seven hundred and twenty-first morning. Five hundred and seventy-one days after the settlement. The fever ninety-eight weeks and two days old. Five hundred and twenty-five days since the division. Four hundred and seventy-one days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and seventeen days in this county of Kell.

Four inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, about ninety of those ninety steps under, and seven days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and then said a fourth thing out loud which is not a figure at all, and it took him the rest of the morning, and nobody on that landing asked him a question about it.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the seventh and last day of the hundred and sixtieth week that the work on that landing had got easier.

“Four at the seventh hour, seven days of the coming back, and about ninety of those ninety steps under, on the seventh and last day of the hundred and sixtieth week, and it is a Monday. Three figures.”

He set the broom down flat on the stone rather than lean it, which is what he does when he has something else.

“Three figures, and there is no fourth, and I want everybody standing on this step to notice that there is no fourth, because what I am going to say now is going to sound like a fourth and it is not a figure and it is not off my stone.”

Not a word came back at him.

“The fortieth one.” He did not point at it. “The one with the chip off the top left. It has moved under me every morning for nineteen years. It is the only one of these ninety that has ever moved and I have known which one it was in my first week, and I have never once told a soul which one it was, because a man who sweeps ninety steps a day does not need to be told and if he does need to be told he is not the man who sweeps them.”

He picked the broom up and turned it in his hands and set it down again.

“The working is this and I am giving it to you whole. A step moves when the ends of the stone are carrying it and the middle is not. You can feel it from the outside edge, where your heel goes when you have learned the step, and from the outside edge it has moved every morning since I came to this bank. This week my heel has been going in the chip at the corner. Dead on the seam. And it has not moved once, and I have put my whole weight on it twice a day every morning this week and it has not moved.”

He stopped there and let it stand.

“There is iron in that seam. I have known there was iron in that seam since before any of you were on this bank and I have never given a fifth figure off that stone and I am not giving one now. That is not a figure. That is my heel.”

Nobody asked him a question about it.

“And I am going to say the rest of it out loud, because I have been not saying it since the seventh hour and I am forty-three years old and I do not have another fortnight in me for not saying things. The work on this landing has got easier. Nineteen years it has not got easier. That is the whole of the news and there is nothing on this stone under it.”

Nobody thanked him and nobody congratulated him and nobody asked him how he knew.

“And I am not going to say who put it there.” He said it flat and he said it once. “Not this morning and not on any other morning, and that is not a new thing and I am not announcing it, and I would like it noticed that I have just told this landing that my work has got easier and in the same breath I have declined to tell them who did it, and that nobody has found anything wrong with the order of them.”

The order of them stood, and nobody asked him about it, and he went back to the broom.

Then a man who cannot see well got up onto the flat of the steps at about the tenth hour and put his hand out in front of him and found the wall with it, and asked for the rule, and did not ask for anything else.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and came up that landing on the seventh and last day of the hundred and sixtieth week and asked for the one rule out loud so that he would have it.

“I have got a list,” he said. “I want to say that first, before I ask, because I have noticed that people on a landing hear the word list and stop listening. It is a list. It is not a complaint and I am not going to make it one on this stone.”

“All right,” said the man with the broom.

“I cannot see that step well enough to know what is in it and I am not going to pretend I can and I am not going to ask anybody to describe it to me, because a man who describes a thing to me is doing it to be thanked for describing it and I would have to thank him.” He put his back against the wall. “So I am asking for the rule and not for the thing. Is it to stay where it is.”

“It is.”

“Then it has not been touched and it is not going to be. Not pulled, not turned over, not weighed in the hand, not read out loud, not carried inside, not put back where it was, and not used.” He said the seven of them without stopping and without looking at anybody. “Seven things. I have said them to you because I would like them said by a mouth that is not mine this morning, and because if I am the only man on this landing holding them then they are a thing I am doing, and I would rather they were a thing this landing is doing.”

Saying them out loud got him nothing back.

“And I am not going to add that step to my list,” he said. “I have a list and I have never added a thing to it that I could have checked by asking one man, and this is one thing I could have checked by asking one man, and he has declined, in front of everybody, and that is the end of it.”

He went down off the landing slowly with his hand on the wall and nobody helped him and nobody offered.

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour and asked in the same words at the same hour and was refused in the same way and was not thanked.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and fiftieth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is nine hundred and seventy-one less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and fiftieth. Nine hundred and seventy-one less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

“Nobody asked you for it, and nobody did yesterday.”

“No, and I have asked on every morning I have come up and been refused on every morning I have come up, and I have watched a man tell this landing that his work has got easier and not one person up here has asked him a single thing about it, and I have been thinking about that on the road up.”

Not one of them had been thinking about that on the road up.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.” He said it in the words he uses. “And that is the last one of this week and I am not going to change it for the last one of anything.”

The question got no verdict off that landing, nobody told the man of fifty-four he was owed an answer, nobody thanked him for asking it, and nobody was ever going to.

He set the barrow against the bottom step and stood at the foot of the steps where he has stood every morning of this business and was not thanked, and the hundred and sixtieth week went out with four inches on the top step and nothing on the stone about it.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the eighth hour, which was the morning after he had gone down that bank and not come back up it, and gave his figures, and gave the figure of the offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing, and then found out that he had nothing of his own to say.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and seventeen days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and eighty-six days old.

“Four hundred and seventeen days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and eighty-six days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-twenty-first is two hundred and seventy-one days old, which is nine hundred and seventy-one less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it, and nobody has offered me a way out of it and I am still standing here at the start of a week with it.”

He got to the bottom of the steps and stood there with his hands behind his back for a while.

“A hundred and eleven wrong things in a hundred and eleven mornings, and here is the hundred and eleventh, and it is the plainest thing I have said in this county and I have been standing here since the eighth hour working out how to say it.”

He looked up at the top step and did not point at anything.

“A man told this landing this morning that his work has got easier. He has been on that stone nineteen years and it has got easier, and he cannot say who did it, and he did not ask, and nobody asked him, and that is the shape of it now and it is going to be the shape of it for as long as I keep coming up here.”

Nobody on that landing said anything about the shape of it.

“I had a sentence ready since the sixth hour.” He turned round and looked back up the bank. “It was about four inches, and about ninety of these steps being under, and seven days. It was a sentence about the water and it was a good sentence and it has gone, and it went because a man said a thing this morning that is not the water and I would have stood up here and handed a morning the wrong figure.”

“And what will you say instead,” said the man who moves chairs, from the back of the landing.

“Nothing, and that is the first time in eleven years I have stood on a step with nothing prepared for it, and I would like that said out loud by somebody who is not me.” He did not move his hands. “And I am not going to be unrelieved about it, because being unrelieved is the other side of being relieved and I have had enough of both from this landing.”

Relief and its other side both stayed where they were.

“And here is my cost and I am going to say it once this morning because it is a Monday and there is nothing else on the stone. Asking is the only thing that costs me nothing. Everything else I do costs me something I can name. I could pay for this standing here this morning out of what is left of me, and I could pay for asking nobody anything at all out of nothing at all, and that is why I have asked for nothing for four hundred and seventeen days and why I am going to keep on not doing it.”

Nobody on that landing helped him with that and nobody argued with it.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety steps and went round the fortieth one and went round the chair where it stood, and did not put the broom on either of them, and set the broom against the wall where it has been every morning for nineteen years, and did not sit on the chair, and did not touch the thing in the seam of the fortieth step, and did not say who had put it there, and the chair stood where it had been put and had never once been sat on and had never once been touched.

Nothing on that landing was pulled, or turned over, or weighed in a hand, or read out loud, or carried inside, or set back where it was, or used, and the work on that landing had got easier and the man whose work it was had said out loud that he was not going to say who left the thing that did it, and nobody on that landing had asked him.

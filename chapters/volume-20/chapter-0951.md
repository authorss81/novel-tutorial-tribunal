# Chapter 951: Sixteen Inches And It Holds, And A Man Who Gave The Asking Up For Ten Mornings Is On That Step Again This Morning

First day of the hundred and fifty-eighth week. His seven hundred and first morning. Five hundred and fifty-one days after the settlement. The fever ninety-five weeks and three days old. Five hundred and five days since the division. Four hundred and fifty-one days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and three hundred and ninety-seven days in this county of Kell.

Barnaby Crove came up the cart road at about the sixth hour with the barrow in front of him and the barrow empty, and he had not stood on that landing for ten mornings, and he stopped at the foot of the steps and set the barrow down against the bottom one and did not come up.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and thirty mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is nine hundred and fifty-one less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one, asked for nothing on ten mornings running, and gave the step notice on the ninth day of the hundred and fifty-seventh week that the asking would start again on the morning after it.

“I gave you a notice on Sunday that I would be up here on Monday morning,” he said. “I was not up here on Monday morning.”

“You were not.”

“I want that said plain and I want it said first, because there is a way of standing on a step where a man has been told a thing and has not come, and it is the way where a man says he was told a thing. I was told a thing. I was the one who gave it. And I was not here on Monday, and nobody on this landing came up that road to ask me why, and I would like it noticed that nobody came up that road, because that is what I have got out of ten mornings.”

He put his hand flat on the barrow handle and did not lift it.

“Now. Two hundred and thirtieth. That is nine hundred and fifty-one less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for that.”

“You said you would say it whether or not anybody asked.”

“I said that on this step and I have said it on every morning of this long business, and there is nobody here to say it to this morning either, and that is the arrangement I have got.”

Corvin Tace came out with the broom and stopped at the top of the steps and gave the three figures before he came down any of them, the way he gives them, standing where the stone is.

“Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, and has never once given a fifth. Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and it holds, about sixty of those ninety steps under, and three days of the coming back. That is three. You are not getting a fourth out of me this morning and you have not had a fourth in nineteen years.”

“You are not going to give me a fifth figure about that thing in the step.”

He did not stop coming down.

“No, and you are not going to ask me to pull it, and I want this landing to hear that I have said both of those out loud before you thought of them. A man who has not said a thing can be asked it later. A man who has said it cannot.

There is an iron wedge in the seam of one of those ninety steps. It is in the step with the corner chipped off the top left. One face of it is worn smooth and smooth is the only word I have for what a boot heel does to iron when it happens twice a day for longer than I can count.

He got to the flat of the steps and put his foot down flat on it.

“And I did not put it in and I have never seen it put in and nobody on this landing is going to say who put it in, and I am not going to stand here and hand it round either. It has not been touched. It is not going to be touched by me or by anybody who stands on this landing, and if a thing is put down where a man works then the whole of what is owed about it is that he is not going to have to say who did it.”

“Who put it in is not a thing anybody is going to say.”

“No. It is not going to be said and it is not going to be not-said either, and I am fifty-four years the wrong side of reading and I could not tell you the difference between those two things if I tried, and there is a man on this landing who has tried and has said so.”

“What did you come up here for, then.”

Barnaby Crove did not pick the barrow up.

“I came up here to ask the same thing I have asked every morning since somebody started keeping figures on this landing,” he said. “I want a day against the ninth line. That is the whole of it and it has been the whole of it for nineteen years and it is four words long. And I cannot read, and every figure on this stone is read out to me by somebody else, and I have worked out this week that is not the difficulty.”

“The difficulty is that a day against a line is a thing somebody has to put down, and a man with a barrow and a step and nothing owed him does not get anything put down for him. You cannot put a thing down for yourself. I have tried it for nineteen years. So it has to be somebody who works here.”

“Then somebody here has to do it.”

“Nobody here is going to do it and I have stopped asking for it, and I am asking for something else and I am telling you what it is before you ask me. I am not asking for a day any more. I am going to ask, every morning, whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it, and I am going to keep asking it until I am carried off or until the asking stops meaning anything, and I am not going to be thanked for asking it either, because nobody is thanked for asking on this landing.”

And that is what he said, and the asking went out into the morning with the barrow still against the bottom step, and nobody answered it, and nobody on that landing has answered it since.

Then the man who moves chairs came up the cart road again and came past the wall without slowing at it, and came up to the step, and stood at the back of it where he could see the road down to the village.

Aldis Rook moves the chairs for Orla Fennimore and keeps nothing and has no stone and has no seat and no board, has never once been asked what he did, was told on the fourth day of the hundred and fifty-sixth week not to come up that bank again, came as far as the wall four mornings running, and went past the wall and up to the step on the last morning of the hundred and fifty-seventh week.

“There is a thing on a doorstep,” he said.

“There is a thing in the step.”

“That is a different thing and I have been up that cart road since Monday looking at doorsteps, so let me say the one I mean. There is a woman at the top of that road who takes other people’s boots in to mend and she does it on her own step, sitting on it, because indoors is her kitchen. Her name is Nell Crake and I have moved four houses past her and never once said it. There is a thing on that step. I did not go up and put it there and I have never been up that road in my life for any reason except chairs.”

“How far up that road.”

“Six hundred yards, maybe a bit more, and the gate on the left before you get there is a green one and it is not the low gate.” He looked down at the step. “I am going to say the rest of it and then I am going to stop, because I have noticed that everything I say on this step gets written down somewhere by somebody and I do not want a thing I say in a morning about a doorstep ending up as a thing a woman at the top of that road has to answer for.”

Nobody said anything about that.

“What I am not going to do. I am not going to ask her whether she took it. I am not going to ask her who put it there. And I am not going to go up there and put another one there, in case that is what one is for. That is three things and I have thought about them on the road and that is all of them.”

He shifted his weight. “Nobody put it down. That is what I have come back up here to say and I want to say it where she is not standing, because she works there and I am not working there and that is the whole of what I know about it.”

“You have not asked her anything.”

“I have not asked her anything and I am not going to, and if anybody on this landing goes up there and asks her whether she took it I would like somebody to say no, and I would like it said by somebody other than me, because a man who has only got that one thing to say will use it.”

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the seventh hour with the sixteen inches on the step above him and the water coming over the bank four inches at a time, and he gave his own three figures and said what he wanted, and what he wanted this morning was to be a man who goes and looks.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and ninety-seven days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and sixty-six days old.

“Three hundred and ninety-seven days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and sixty-six days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-first is two hundred and fifty-one days old, which is nine hundred and fifty-one less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

He looked at the man with the barrow, and then at the road up past the green gate.

“Ninety-one wrong things in ninety-one mornings, and here is the ninety-first, and it is not a wrong thing anybody needs to argue about. That man has just told you there is a thing on a step six hundred yards up that road and that nobody put it down. I am going to go up that road and look at it. I am nobody’s. Nobody on this landing owes me anything and I owe nobody anything, and if there is a thing six hundred yards from here with nobody standing next to it, then I am the only man on this landing who can stand next to it without anybody afterwards having to say I was owed something for being there.”

“You are owed nothing.”

“That is why I want to go. If a man goes because he is owed, then the going is a favour and the favour is a rope. If a man goes because nobody owes him anything, then the going is only a man walking.” He put his hands behind his back. “And I have one thing left that nobody can take off me. Nineteen years on this landing and eleven years of nobody else’s and I can walk down that bank and leave, and no keeper, no clerk and no woman with a day against a line can take that off me.

That is the whole of what I have got and it is the only thing in this county I have never had to be given.”

“Go up the road, then.”

“No.” He took his hands out from behind his back, and it was not fast. “

No, and I want the whole of this landing to hear why, and then I am not going to say anything about it again. If I go up that road and look at that thing, then this week has a man in it who went and looked at a thing nobody put down. And then the next one is easier. And then somebody in this county is going to work out that the way to be somebody is to go and stand near things that nobody put down, and that is a road a man can walk for the rest of his life without anybody ever paying him for it, and every step of it is free.”

He looked up the bank at the man of forty-three.

“I have been nobody’s since before I came to this county and I would like to still be nobody’s on Sunday. That is the want and it is not a good one and I have never once pretended it was.”

Corvin Tace stood at the bottom of the steps with the broom in his hand and did not put it against the wall.

“Fourteen years I have been on that step and there is exactly one thing I have ever been given that was not on the stone,” he said, “and it is that nobody has ever come up here and asked me a fifth thing, and I have spent fourteen years not giving one. And here is what that gets you: a step with a corner chipped off the top left that does not rock any more, and I cannot tell you how long it has not rocked, because the only way I would know is by counting backwards, and I am not counting backwards, and neither is anybody on this landing.”

“Even though you could.”

“Even though I could. And that is not a virtue. It is a man who has worked out that the counting would be a figure and a figure would be a thing to say out loud on a morning when somebody wants a fifth thing.”

He leaned the broom against the wall. 

“Nobody on this landing is owed a thing this week. Not a day, not a figure, not a chair, not a step. I have worked out this morning that the whole of what has happened on this landing since the water came back is a thing put down where a man works, by somebody who does not work there. Nobody was owed it. Nobody has been thanked for it, and nobody is going to be.

“That is the ground and there is nothing under it.”

Nobody said anything to that, and nobody on that landing was thanked for anything, and nothing that had been put down was touched.

The man of fifty-four said his figure out loud once more into an empty landing and went down the bank with the barrow. The boy did not come up. The man of thirty-one went back up the cart road past the green gate at about the eighth hour, on foot, slowly, with his hands behind his back, and did not stop at the step with the corner chipped off the top left, and did not put his weight on it, and did not touch it, and came back down the road and did not tell anybody what he had or had not done.

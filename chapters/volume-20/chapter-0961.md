# Chapter 961: The Morning After The Week Turned, And A Woman Who Saws Lengths At A Bench Used The Whole Of It A Second Time And Did Not Give It A Name

Fourth day of the hundred and fifty-ninth week. His seven hundred and eleventh morning. Five hundred and sixty-one days after the settlement. The fever ninety-six weeks and six days old. Five hundred and fifteen days since the division. Four hundred and sixty-one days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and seven days in this county of Kell.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and it holds, one day of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and then told a landing that had begun asking him what he does with his mornings that he was not going to tell them what tomorrow is.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and on the fourth morning of the hundred and fifty-ninth week told a landing full of asking that he was not going to hand out mornings.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, one day of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the fourth morning of the hundred and fifty-ninth week, and it is a Friday. Three figures.”

He set the broom down on the stone instead of leaning it, which is the thing he does when he has something else to say and does not want a thing in his hands.

“And now the other thing, and it is the only other thing I am going to say. A man on this landing has been asking me what I do with my mornings. The answer is that I do not know, and I am not going to find out by telling him in advance.” He looked along the top step.

“I have not said that to anybody before and I am not saying it kindly. I am saying it because it is a Friday and it is the hour when a man who wants a morning handed to him stands on somebody’s landing at seven in the morning, and I have watched men do it and I have watched them be refused, and I would rather refuse a morning than hand one out.”

Nobody asked him for a morning.

“You could say what the water does.”

“I could say what the water did the last time it went off, and I have that by about the time it takes you to walk to the wall.” He picked the broom up again. “I am not going to, because the day this step has anybody on it who hands out mornings is the day this step has a keeper of something other than stone. I have held a bank for nineteen years and I have never once been asked to hold a week and I am not going to start on a Friday.”

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour, which is the hour he said he would come at, and stopped at the foot of the steps, which is where he said he would stop, and set the same barrow against the same bottom step.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and fortieth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is nine hundred and sixty-one less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one, and has asked every morning since the day he came back and been refused every one of them.

“Two hundred and fortieth. Nine hundred and sixty-one less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it.”

Nobody asked him for it.

“I said on Thursday morning of last week that I would carry this on Saturday, and I carried it on Saturday, and I am here on a Friday and nobody has come up this road to ask me why it is a day late.” He put both hands on the barrow handle. “And I am not going to stand here and work out what my refusals are worth, because I am the one who asks them and a man who works out what his own asking is worth is a man who has started keeping a figure of himself.”

Nobody agreed with him and nobody told him it was a small thing to know.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.” He said it exactly as he had said it on every morning of that week. “And I am not going to be thanked for it, and I have told the man with the broom what I will do if anybody does, and I have not had to do it yet, and I would rather keep not having to do it.”

Nobody thanked him. Nobody told him the question was good, and nobody told him it was not, and nobody told him he was owed an answer, because there is no such thing on that landing and there never has been.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the eighth hour with sixteen inches above the step he was standing on and gave his figures, and gave the figure of the offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing, and said so himself in the same breath.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and seven days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and seventy-six days old.

“Four hundred and seven days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and seventy-six days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-eleventh is two hundred and sixty-one days old, which is nine hundred and sixty-one less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it, and I am not going to ask it this morning either, and I want the whole of this landing to hear me not ask it.”

He stood with his hands behind his back at the back of the top step, the way he has stood on that landing for eleven years.

“One hundred and one wrong things in one hundred and one mornings, and here is the hundred and first, and it is not a confession and it is not an offering.” He turned his head.

“The man with the broom has just told this landing that he is not going to say what tomorrow is, and I want to add one thing to it, because I am the one man here who has the strongest possible reason to want tomorrow said out loud and I am not going to ask him for it either. I have wanted somebody to tell me what is coming since before I came to this county. That is the whole of my wanting and it is a small wanting and I have never dressed it up.”

“You could ask me,” said the man with the broom, from below.

“I could ask you, and you would say no, and I would have had an afternoon.”

Nobody said anything about the afternoon.

Then a woman came up the cart road on foot with a saw handle under her arm, and she had not been on that landing before, and she came up to the bottom of the steps and did not ask anybody to come up her, and she was not asked to go up.

Wenna Pyle is thirty-eight, saws lengths and sets studs at a workbench in an open-sided shed at the back of a wheelwright’s, and carries her own cup out to that bench every morning because there is no table in that shed and she has not got a table anywhere else to put it on.

“There is a thing on my bench,” she said. “That is the whole of what I have come up here to say and I am going to say the rest of it in the right order because I have learned that order from watching this landing for two minutes from the bottom of your steps.”

“Which order.”

“The nothing one first.” She turned the saw handle round in her hands. “It is a cup. It is not mine and it is not the shape of anything I own, and it was on my bench at about the sixth hour and there is nobody else in that shed but me and the man who owns the shed, and he does not come in until the middle of the morning.”

“And I have not touched it, and I have not turned it over, and I have not put my hand inside it, and I have not weighed it in my hand, and I have not carried it inside, and I have not put it back where it was, and I am not going to use it to drink out of, and I would like that said by me before somebody comes up here and has to be the first one who says it.”

Nobody had to be the first one.

“The second thing is that nobody put it down. I do not know who put it down, I have not worked it out, I am not going to work it out, and I am not going to say I do not know in a way that is asking to be asked, because I have watched a man do that twice from the bottom of your steps.”

“How far is your shed.”

“Past the gravel and past the turn where the cart road gets level, and the gate on the right is the one that is not a gate any more, just two posts and a hinge that was never a gate. About three hundred yards.” She looked up the steps. “And that is a figure of how far it is from here and not a figure of how far anybody walked, because I have not said that anybody walked.”

“Nobody has said that.”

“I know nobody has. I am saying it now so that it is said.”

She set the saw handle down against the bottom step and straightened up.

“And now the part I came for, and I am not going to give it a name, and I would like somebody to write down that I said I was not going to give it a name.” She put her hands flat on her thighs.

“A thing put down on a bench where a person works is not a thing somebody gave me. Nobody gave me that cup. The person who put it there does not work at that bench — I do. And nobody is going to ask me about it tomorrow morning, and nobody is going to be thanked for putting a cup on my bench, and it is not going to turn into a different sort of thing because I stood next to it for a morning and felt bad about standing next to it.”

“Nothing is to be thanked for,” said the man with the broom.

“No. And nobody is to be thanked for me saying so, either.” She picked the saw handle up again. “I have been doing this trade since I was fourteen and I have carried things into about nine rooms a week for that long and not one person in any of them has ever heard a word about it from me and not one of them has ever said anything about it, and I have never once asked whether they took it, and I have never once been asked whether they took it, and that is not a rule anybody wrote down. That is just what the work is.”

Nobody told her she had got it right and nobody told her she had it wrong.

“I had a sentence ready for that step and I am not going to say it,” Aldis Rook said from the back of the landing, where he had arrived without anybody noticing him. “She has said the whole of it and she said it first, and I am not going to stand up here and say a worse version of a thing a woman said from the bottom of the steps.”

“You are four days late,” Corvin Tace said to him.

“I am four days late and I was late before that, and I will be late again.” He came no further up. “And I am not going to be thanked for turning up, and I am not going to be told I got there in time.”

Nobody told him either of those things.

Nobody on that landing gave the thing a name, and nobody said a word about who put down the last one, and the landing did not do anything with it except stand in the same weather it had been standing in before.

Barnaby Crove went down the bank with the barrow and asked again halfway down, to nobody, in the same words, and nobody answered him, because there was nobody there. The woman who saws lengths went back down the cart road to a shed with a bench in it and put her own cup on that bench where it goes every morning, and did not touch the other one, and did not put a second thing down anywhere in that shed.

Corvin Tace swept the top step from the high end to the low end, went round the chair, went round the fortieth step, and set the broom against the wall where it has been every morning for nineteen years.

And the water was sixteen inches on the top step and holding at that, and about sixty of those ninety steps were under, and one day of the coming back — and not one mouth on that landing said what the morning after it would be, and nobody up that road had touched the cup on a bench three hundred yards away, and nobody had said the word for how any of it began.

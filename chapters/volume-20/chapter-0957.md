# Chapter 957: Twelve Inches, About Seventy Of Those Ninety Steps Under, Five Days Of The Coming Back, And A Man Of Fifty-Four Asked Again In The Same Words And Was Refused In The Same Way

Seventh and last day of the hundred and fifty-eighth week. His seven hundred and seventh morning. Five hundred and fifty-seven days after the settlement. The fever ninety-six weeks and two days old. Five hundred and eleven days since the division. Four hundred and fifty-seven days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and three days in this county of Kell.

Twelve inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, about seventy of those ninety steps under, and five days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and then said that the last day of a week is the morning a keeper is most tempted to give a man something extra, and that he was not going to.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the last morning of a week that a keeper is at his most dangerous at the end of a week.

“Twelve at the seventh hour, five days of the coming back, and about seventy of those ninety steps under, on the seventh and last day of the hundred and fifty-eighth week, and it is a Monday. Three figures.”

“Then it is the week out.”

“It is the week out and it is the morning in this business when I am most tempted and I have worked out why. On a Monday morning anybody in this county is feeling that something ought to come, and the person who gives it is always the man with a stone and nineteen years, and I have given it twice in nineteen years and both times it was a Monday, and both times I could not undo it.”

“What were the two.”

“Does not matter. I am telling you about my Mondays so that you know what I am doing on mine and not so that you can go and look at what I did.”

Barnaby Crove came up at about the sixth hour, which is the hour he said he would come at, and stopped at the foot of the steps, which is where he said he would stop, and he had the same barrow against the same bottom step.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and thirty-six mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is nine hundred and fifty-seven less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one, asked for nothing on ten mornings, and has asked on every morning since the day he came back.

“Two hundred and thirty-sixth. Nine hundred and fifty-seven less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it.”

“Nobody asked you for it.”

“No, and nobody asked me for it on any morning of this week, and I said on Saturday that I would ask again this morning in the same words at the same hour, and it is the same words and it is the same hour, and that is not a virtue and it is not stubbornness either, it is a man who has worked out that changing one thing about a question is how you end up asking a different thing without noticing.”

He set the barrow down.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.” He said it exactly as he had said it on every morning of that week. “And I am not going to say anything else after it today, because I have got one thing and I am spending all of it on the question and not a word on the asking.”

Nobody answered him. Nobody said it was a good question. Nobody said it was a foolish one. Nobody said he was owed an answer, because there is no such thing on this landing and there never has been. And nobody thanked him for asking, because he had said on Saturday that he would not come up on Monday if anybody did.

He was still standing there when somebody said it, and the somebody who said it was the man who moves chairs, and it was not a thank.

“I am not thanking you for asking,” Aldis Rook said, from the back of the landing where he had come up and was standing. “I want that on the record from my own mouth because I am the only man on this step who has any business saying anything to you at all and I would like the one thing I say to be the true one.”

“Go on.”

“I move chairs. A man asks me for a day and I have never got one for him and I have never even wanted to, because a day is a thing somebody keeps and I am not anybody who keeps.”

He brought his hands out from behind him.

“But a man asking every morning in the same words at the same hour, with nothing after it, that is the only thing I have seen in eleven years of carrying furniture into other people’s houses that came into the house and then got left where it was put. You have not asked me for anything. I am telling you that I noticed, and I am not going to do anything about it, and you are not going to get anything out of me, and that is the whole of it.”

Then the man of thirty-one came up, and the last day of a week is a morning when a man of thirty-one has something ready, and he had had it ready since Friday and he said it anyway, and it was refused in a sentence by the man of fifty-four, which had never happened before and which nobody on that landing was ready for.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and three days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and seventy-two days old.

“Four hundred and three days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and seventy-two days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-seventh is two hundred and fifty-seven days old, which is nine hundred and fifty-seven less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

He got to the bottom of the steps before he started, which he has not done in this business.

“Ninety-seven wrong things in ninety-seven mornings, and here is the ninety-seventh, and it is the plainest one I have ever said and I have been carrying it since Friday.”

He set his hands behind him.

“You have carried two hundred and thirty-six mornings of asking this county for one thing and never got it. I have carried a standing offer since the seven-hundredth that nobody has answered and that I have not asked about. Put the two together. Not as a figure and not as a day and not as anything anybody can hand anybody. Just as one morning a week where the two of us say our two things at the same hour in front of about nine ears, and neither of us asks the other for anything, and whatever a step does about it is a matter for the step.”

Barnaby Crove did not turn round.

“No,” he said.

“You have not heard it.”

“I have heard all of it and there is nothing in it.” He lifted the barrow handle and got it onto his shoulder without hurrying. “You have been in this county four hundred and three days and I have been on this landing nineteen years and I have heard a man make this offer before. He did it with a chair and I did it, and there have been a run of us carrying it ever since, and what it turned into was two men who both wanted something, standing in the same place, and me with my hand on a barrow, and nobody able to say afterwards which of us had been the reason the other was there.”

He got the barrow up onto his shoulder.

“You want me to be in it. That is the whole of it. You have made a morning where I am the reason a man is standing on a step, and you have done it out loud and in front of everybody so that I cannot say you caught me, and I could not have stood it if it had been done quietly, and that is exactly how it was done the first time. So no. Not that. Not anything like that.”

The landing did not thank either of them.

“No one came up the road from the village this morning,” said Sena Dorr, who had been up the road since before the light and had not come up the steps. “Nobody has come up that road all week. I am not going to say she is not coming up because she says she is not going to be asked again and she has not been asked, and she has kept to that, and the rest of it is not mine to keep either.”

“She said one thing on the last morning of the week behind that and she said it plainly and she said she was not going to be thanked for it. And there is nothing this week that she is owed, because the whole of what this week has been is things that nobody owed anybody.”

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years, holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and has given her question up on the sixth morning of this week and has not taken it back.

“That is all I am going to say about her and I would be obliged if nobody on this step went up that road to say anything about what I have just said. She will hear about it in a week and she will not be able to do one single thing about it, which is the correct outcome, and I would still rather she never heard about it at all.”

Nobody went up the road.

Barnaby Crove went down the bank with the barrow and asked again halfway down, to nobody, in the same words, and nobody answered him, because there was nobody there. Ilyan Vester went down the bank on foot at about the ninth hour with his hands behind his back, and did not stop at the wall, and did not stop at the bottom of the steps, and did not turn round at any point on that bank, and did not come back up it before the week turned.

Corvin Tace swept the top step from the high end to the low end, and set the broom against the wall where it has been every morning for nineteen years, and did not put his weight on the fortieth step, and did not touch the thing in the seam of it, and nothing that had been put down anywhere was pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used.

The water was twelve inches on the top step and going up about four inches a day, and the seventh and last morning of the hundred and fifty-eighth week ended the way the hundred and fifty-seventh had, with a man on that landing who had been asked nothing, told nothing, and given nothing, standing at the bottom of his own steps with a barrow handle in both hands.

# Chapter 962: The Water Came Off That Bank At About The Sixth Hour And All Ninety Of Those Steps Were Walkable By About The Seventh And A Man Who Cannot Read Said Out Loud That The Count Of Weeks Was A Whole Figure, And Nothing Else Was On That Landing

Fifth day of the hundred and fifty-ninth week. His seven hundred and twelfth morning. Five hundred and sixty-two days after the settlement. The fever ninety-seven weeks old. Five hundred and sixteen days since the division. Four hundred and sixty-two days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and eight days in this county of Kell.

The water came off that bank at about the sixth hour and all ninety of those steps were walkable by about the seventh, and that was the thirty-seventh time. There is no figure of inches on this morning and there is no figure of steps under and there is no figure of days of the coming back either, and that is not a gap in the morning. 

It is the shape of the morning, and a man said it out loud before anybody asked him for it, and this time there was one figure on that landing instead of none, and the man with the broom said out loud that he was not going to let the one figure do the work of the three that were missing.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the fifth morning of the hundred and fifty-ninth week that a morning with nothing on it and a whole number on it was the most dangerous morning he has stood on.

“There is no figure of inches. Not one, and I am not going to round it and I am not going to give you a feeling of it instead, because a feeling of it is still a figure and I have watched men put those on this step and call them something else. No number of steps under either, and no number of days, and there is not one to give you, because when all ninety are out there is no coming back to count out from.”

He put the broom down flat.

“That is the shape of it. That is the thirty-seventh time, and it is eight days on from the thirty-sixth, and I have not got that off a book. I count the mornings the same way I count the steps, which is to say I do not count them and I know.”

He stood there a moment with his hand on the handle and looked at the bare stone as though it had done something to him.

“And there is one figure on this landing this morning and it is going to be a whole one, and I am saying now, before anybody has said it, that I am not going to help anybody make anything out of it.”

“Anybody can be left to do their own work.”

“No, that is not what I mean and you know it is not.” He picked the broom up.

“I mean that a morning with nothing on the stone is the only morning in this business where a man can stand on a step and say a whole sentence about a thing and have nobody able to say the sentence was about the step. There is nothing on this landing this morning to point at. That is exactly when it comes out. And I have watched people say a sentence about a thing on a morning like this and be told for the rest of their lives that they said it on a morning when there was nothing else to look at.”

Nobody on that landing had said anything yet that could be called that.

“So if anybody on this step gets ready this morning to work something out, they can work it out on a morning with a figure on the stone, and there will be one about the middle of next week, and it will be of some use to nobody, and that is the price of it.” He started down.

“I am not going to say that is what a morning is for. I am saying the stone is bare and I have noticed and I am not going to pretend a bare stone is an occasion.”

He swept. He swept all ninety, from the high end down, and he went round the fortieth one and carried on with the thirty-ninth, and he did not put the broom on it, and he did not slow down.

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour and said it.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and forty-first mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is nine hundred and sixty-two less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Ninety-seven weeks,” he said.

The landing went the quiet that a landing goes when a man who cannot read has said a whole figure into it.

“Not ninety-seven weeks and a bit. Ninety-seven weeks, on this Saturday, and that is the second time it has come out as a whole figure since I started saying it out loud.” He put both hands flat on the wall. “And I want it said by me and not worked out by one of you, because I am the man who cannot read and you are the people who can, and that is exactly the way round for a thing like this, and a figure that lands on me before it has landed on you is a figure one of you will do something kind with.”

He stood there with the wind off the wall and let the whole of that landing do nothing with it.

“That is all I am going to say about it before the seventh hour. You have had it and I have had it and it is a whole figure of weeks and that is a whole figure of weeks.” He took his hands off the wall. “I do not know what a whole figure of weeks is for. I have said that before and I am saying it again on a morning when there is nothing else on this step to listen to, and if anybody here has worked out what a whole number of weeks is for then you worked it out after I said the number and that is a different thing from knowing what it is for.”

Nobody told him what it was for.

“Two hundred and forty-first. Nine hundred and sixty-two less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.” He set the barrow against the bottom step. “Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it. Same words. Same hour. And I would like it refused the same way, because a new refusal would be worth less to me than the old one and I have got used to the old one inside a week.”

Nobody answered him. Nobody told him the question was good or foolish. Nobody thanked him for it, and he had said what he would do if anybody did.

Then the man who cannot see well was at the back of the landing before any of that had finished, with his back to a bank that had nothing on it, and he said so himself before anybody could work out why he was standing the way he was standing.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has named about nine things he cannot check and has never once been asked what would stop him, and stood at the back of that landing on the two mornings this bank has come off it, and has said out loud why he is there and has not been asked about it.

“There is nothing on that bank to look at and that is the only thing I have ever wanted out of it,” he said. “I have not been able to look at it properly since the water came in the first week of the year. I have said that before on this step and I have asked for it to be left alone, and nobody has asked me what would stop me, and I am not going to stand here and make it a thing again.”

“Nobody is going to ask you,” said the man who moves chairs.

“Nobody has asked me and I would like that said out loud by somebody who is not me.” He did not turn round. “And I am not going to say what I have just said was worth anything, because that is the other half of asking and I am not doing it.”

Nobody said what it was worth.

“Simon,” said the man with the broom, “go and stand at the top of the wall where the sun is. You have been facing the wrong way for an hour.”

“I know which way I am facing. I have known for a long time.” But he went, and he stood at the wall, and he did not turn round again.

Then the man of thirty-one came up and gave his figures and the figure of the offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing, and then did the thing he has not done before and said out loud that he was not going to do it this morning.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and eight days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and seventy-seven days old.

“Four hundred and eight days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and seventy-seven days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-twelfth is two hundred and sixty-two days old, which is nine hundred and sixty-two less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

He stood on the third step down, which is where the stone is dry enough to stand on, and looked at the ninety steps he could walk over.

“One hundred and two wrong things in one hundred and two mornings, and here is the hundred and second, and it is the plainest one I have said on a bare morning.” He put his hands behind his back.

“The man who cannot read has just given us a whole figure and he has said he does not know what it is for, and I have wanted for eleven years for somebody in this county to hand me a whole figure and tell me what it was for. And I want you all to know that a whole figure with nobody knowing what it is for is the exact thing I have been asking for, and that it does not help, and that I would not have got it any other way.”

Nobody said anything at all about that.

“I am going to be asked why I am not going to say a sentence about a thing on this morning and I am going to say it now so that nobody has to work it out.” He looked up at the man with the broom.

“It is because the man with the broom has already told me what this morning is, out loud, in front of everybody, before I got here. He said the stone is bare and he is not going to pretend a bare stone is an occasion. And a man who gets up here and says a sentence about a thing after that is a man who has heard the stone is bare and has decided the stone being bare is a good place to talk. I am not going to do that to him and I am not going to do it to you.”

“That is the second time you have not done something you had ready,” said the man with the broom.

“It is, and I would rather it were twenty, and I am not going to tell you what I did with the sentences in between.”

The man of fifty-four went down the bank with the barrow. The man of thirty-one went down the bank on foot at about the ninth hour with his hands behind his back, and did not put his weight on the fortieth step on the way up or the way down, and nobody asked him why, because everybody on that landing has known why since the first morning of that week.

Corvin Tace finished all ninety steps and set the broom against the wall where it has been every morning for nineteen years.

And the water was not on that bank at all and about ninety of those ninety steps were out and walkable, and there was one whole figure of weeks on the landing that a man who cannot read had put there himself before anybody worked it out for him, and nobody on that landing said a whole sentence about a thing.

The cup on a bench three hundred yards away was still standing where it had been standing and had not been touched, and nothing that had been put down anywhere was pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used.

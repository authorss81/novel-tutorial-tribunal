# Chapter 968: Sixteen Inches And It Holds And Two Days Of The Coming Back And About Sixty Of Those Ninety Steps Under, And A Man Sat Down On The Wall To Work Something Out Slowly And In The Wrong Order And Got Hold Of The One Part Of It That Is Not An Answer

Fourth day of the hundred and sixtieth week. His seven hundred and eighteenth morning. Five hundred and sixty-eight days after the settlement. The fever ninety-seven weeks and six days old. Five hundred and twenty-two days since the division. Four hundred and sixty-eight days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and fourteen days in this county of Kell.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and it holds, about sixty of those ninety steps under, and two days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and then said that he was not going to say anything else about the morning unless somebody asked him, and four people on that landing came close to it.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the fourth morning of the hundred and sixtieth week that a landing where nothing happens is the only kind of landing a man can get wrong twice.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, two days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the fourth day of the hundred and sixtieth week, and it is a Friday. Three figures.” He set the broom down. “And that is the whole of what I have got this morning. There is no fourth thing. There is no fourth thing because there has been a fourth thing every morning this week and a landing gets a rhythm, and the morning a keeper gives you four things is the morning you have started thinking of him as somebody who owes you one.”

Nobody asked him for a fourth thing.

Then the man who moves chairs came up the cart road, and he did not go to the back of the landing, and he did not stand where he stands, and he sat down on the wall in the sun with his saw — no, with nothing — and put his hands on his knees, and said he was going to do something on that landing that nobody had ever seen him do.

Aldis Rook moves the chairs for Orla Fennimore and keeps nothing and has no stone and has no seat and no board, has never once been asked what he did, said a thing about a chair on the second morning of the hundred and sixtieth week and has not repeated it since, and sat down on a wall on the fourth morning of it to work out what he was left with.

“I am going to work this out in front of you and I am going to do it in the wrong order on purpose, because I did it in the wrong order on Saturday and I have been getting the same wrong answer for two days and I would rather be wrong in front of you than right in the lane.” He put his hands flat on his knees. “And I want it understood that what I come out with this morning is not going to be an answer, and that I know it is not going to be an answer before I start, and that is not modesty, it is that I have checked.”

Nobody stopped him where he stood.

“Part one. A man puts a chair down in a room and is not thanked. He has decided that beforehand and he is at peace with it. That is the easy half and I have had eleven years of it and it is the only part of this I could put in a sentence.” He looked at his hands. “Part two, and here is where I start it wrong, because I started here on Saturday and I have not got a better order. If he is not thanked, then what is left is not knowing whether it was any good.”

“That is right,” said the man who could not see well, from the wall.

“That is where I am wrong and where you are also wrong, and I will come to you in a minute.” Aldis Rook did not turn round.

“Part three, and this is the bit I did not have on Saturday and the bit that took me two days. If what is left is not knowing, then not knowing is the thing I have been carrying for eleven years, and I have been calling it peace. It is not peace. Peace is when you have put something down and you do not know and it does not go on going. What I have got is a thing that goes on going, every time, in about nine houses a week, and I have never once had it stop.”

Nobody on that landing said that was a lot to have carried.

“So that is what is left, and here is where I say it is not an answer, and I am saying it myself because nobody else here is going to.” He took his hands off his knees. “Not knowing is not an answer to what a man put a thing down for. Not knowing is not even an answer to why he put it down. It is the whole of what I have and it is what I would give anybody who asked me what I get out of it, and it is nothing.”

“That is the answer to that question,” said the man with the broom.

“No it is not, and you know it is not, and that is why you said it in that voice.” He stood up. “And here is the second thing I have got out of it, and it is worse than the first, and I am going to tell you that I have got it and I am not going to develop it on this landing.”

“I am not going to ask you for it.”

“I know. That is why I am telling you I have got it, instead of keeping the fact of having it, because a fact that only one man knows is a thing that man has to carry alone, and I have just spent two days finding out what carrying one of those is like.” He put his hands behind him.

“It is about being thanked. And there is nothing in this county that knows my name except the woman whose chairs I move, and if somebody ever said my name on this landing and meant it, I do not know what I would do, and I have not worked that out, and I am not going to work it out on a Friday morning in front of you.”

Nobody worked it out for him and nobody said what he ought to do.

Then the man who could not see well said the thing he had come to say, which was about a bank and not about anything else, and it was said once.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has named about nine things he cannot check, has never once been asked what would stop him and was not asked again on either of the two mornings he has been at the back of that landing.

“There is a sound a full bank makes when it is not going anywhere today that is different from the sound it makes the day before it starts,” he said, from the wall, with his face to the sun. “I have known that for longer than anybody on this landing has been paying me to know things. And I am not going to say what it is going to do, and I am not going to say how I know it, and I am not going to be asked.”

“There is nobody going to ask you.”

“Nobody has asked me twice and that is two more than most people get.” He did not turn round. “And I am not going to say what that is worth and I am not going to be told what it is worth. The man who moves chairs said on Saturday that he is not going to let anything on this landing become a question by being watched, and I would like him to know that I have thought about what he said and that it applies to me as well and I would rather said it than not said it.”

“I did not say that about you.”

“You said it in front of me and I have been on this landing long enough to know what a man is saying when he is not saying it to somebody in front of him.” Simon Rook got up off the wall. “That is all I have got and it is smaller than a figure and I am going down the road now before somebody is kind to me about it.”

Nobody was kind to him about it.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the eighth hour and gave his figures and the figure of the offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing, and then spent the whole morning trying to do one thing for the man who moves chairs and was not able to do it, and said so.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and fourteen days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and eighty-three days old.

“Four hundred and fourteen days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and eighty-three days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-eighteenth is two hundred and sixty-eight days old, which is nine hundred and sixty-eight less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

He stood at the top of the steps and looked at the man who had just sat down and stood up again.

“One hundred and eight wrong things in one hundred and eight mornings, and here is the hundred and eighth, and it is about a thing I have been doing badly for two days.” He did not put his hands behind him, which is new. “That man has just said he does not know what he would do if somebody said his name on this landing. And I have been standing on this step for two days wanting to say a sentence at him about it, and I have had about nine of them ready, and every one of them would have made him feel better for about an hour and then worse for a year.”

“You are not going to say them.”

“I am going to say none of them, and that is the first time in eleven years I have had about nine sentences about a person ready and used none of them, and I would like it noticed that I noticed.” He took his hands out and let them hang. “And I am going to say the reason instead, because I have never said the reason out loud and it is the whole of what I am and I have spent a week finding out that it is.”

“Everybody on this landing has a reason.”

“Then mine is this. The only power in this world that costs a person nothing to use is asking. It has been free since before I came to this county and it is free on the last morning I am here. And a man who asks every morning is nobody, and always was, and I have known that for eleven years and I have said it out loud once and the man with the broom did not agree with it and did not correct it.” He looked down the bank.

“And that is why I cannot say a kind thing to that man this morning. Because the kind thing is free. That is exactly what it is. It costs him nothing to hear and it costs me nothing to say and if I say it then I have used the one thing I have got and it has gone on a man who does not need it and cannot use it.”

Nobody on that landing said that was a way to be somebody.

“So he gets what I have got, which is a man standing on a step not saying nine things, and I am not going to be thanked for it, and he is not going to know that it happened, and that is the correct outcome and I have had a whole week to work out that it is the correct outcome.”

Barnaby Crove was not on that landing that morning and nobody went to the wall to say he was not wanted. The man of thirty-one went down the bank on foot at about the ninth hour with his hands at his sides, which is not the way he has gone down that bank in this whole business, and did not put his weight on the fortieth step on the way down.

Corvin Tace swept eighty-nine steps and went round the fortieth one and went round the chair and set the broom against the wall, and the water was sixteen inches and holding with two days to go, and nothing that had been put down anywhere was pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used, and one man had worked out that what he was left with was not knowing, and another man had nine sentences he did not say, and neither of those two facts was going to be on that landing on the Saturday.

# Chapter 967: Sixteen Inches And It Holds And Three Days Of The Coming Back And About Sixty Of Those Ninety Steps Under, And Nobody On That Landing Did One Thing About A Thing That Was Said The Morning Before, And One Man Said That Was Not Going To Change

Third day of the hundred and sixtieth week. His seven hundred and seventeenth morning. Five hundred and sixty-seven days after the settlement. The fever ninety-seven weeks and five days old. Five hundred and twenty-one days since the division. Four hundred and sixty-seven days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and thirteen days in this county of Kell.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and it holds, about sixty of those ninety steps under, and three days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and then told the landing that a thing said the morning before was still true this morning and had not got a single inch more useful since, and that he was going to be careful about how he said that.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the third morning of the hundred and sixtieth week that he was not going to let a landing get busy on the strength of one morning.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, three days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the third day of the hundred and sixtieth week, and it is a Thursday. Three figures.”

He set the broom down flat.

“Now, and I am going to say this once and I am going to say it carefully, because I have held this landing for nineteen years and the one thing I have learned to do well is not to let a landing get busy.” He looked round. “Yesterday a man stood on this step and said a thing. It was a true thing. It is still true this morning. And nothing has happened since six o’clock, and nothing is going to happen today, and if anybody on this landing goes up that road today about that thing they are going up it on their own and not because of a sentence that was said yesterday on a Wednesday.”

Nobody went up that road.

“And I am not going to say it was a mistake and I am not going to say it was right. A landing that starts arguing with itself about a thing somebody said in a mood is a landing that has stopped working, and I have seen it happen on other landings and not on this one while I have been standing here.” He picked the broom up. “If a thing is said on a Wednesday and it is still there on the Thursday, then it was not a Wednesday thing. That is the whole of what a Thursday morning is for.”

Nobody asked him what it was then.

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour and asked in the same words at the same hour and was refused in the same way and was not thanked, and did not mention the morning before at all, which is the first thing anybody has done on that landing all week and which cost him something.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and forty-sixth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is nine hundred and sixty-seven less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and forty-sixth. Nine hundred and sixty-seven less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it.”

“Nobody asked you for it, and nobody has for about nine days.”

“No, and I am not going to ask you what you said yesterday morning, because I did not hear what you said, because I was at the bottom of the steps with a barrow and a wind on it and I was asking my question and the wind was taking it.” He set the barrow down. “And I am not going to pretend I heard it so that I can have an opinion about it this morning. There is a thing you can do on a landing where you get behind everybody else and pretend you were listening, and it is the only free thing anybody has ever offered me.”

Nobody pretended to have heard it.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.” He said it in the same words at the same hour. “And that is all. I have been up here every morning this week and I have not had anything from any of you except a refusal and a figure I did not ask for, and I am carrying on.”

Nobody answered him and nobody thanked him.

Then the woman who reads the ninth run came up the road and entered the morning and did not open the book on that wall, which is the second morning she has done it and not the first.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years, holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, got most of the way through a sentence on the last morning of the hundred and fifty-ninth week and was stopped, and has not finished it since.

“I am going to say the thing I have been asked about by three faces since the sixth hour and I am going to say it once.” She had the book shut against her knee. “I did not finish the sentence on Monday. I am not going to finish it at any point this week. And I am not going to finish it in a new wording because somebody on this step has said the same sort of thing better than I was going to say it, and that is not a reason to say mine.”

“Nothing is going to be taken up,” said the man with the broom.

“I know. That is what I have come up here to say out loud, and I want it said before anybody spends the morning deciding it. Nobody is taking anything up. Nobody is writing it down. Nobody is going to hold a sitting about it, and there is nobody in this county I would sit down with to hold one.” She looked at the closed book. “I am the ninth of nine positions and I have spent twenty-three years entering things on a line and I am not entering one word of that sentence, and if anybody in this county was waiting to find out whether I would, they can stop waiting.”

Nobody was waiting.

“And I want the other half said too, and I would rather say it than have it said about me.” She did not open the book. “I am not going to repeat what the man who moves chairs said yesterday, in any wording, including the wording where I agree with it and including the wording where I have a better one. A thing said once on this landing is a thing said once. If a person needs it said again to have acted on it then they were not waiting for it to be said, and they were waiting for something else and they have found a way to get it.”

Nobody said they needed it again.

Then the man who moves chairs came up the cart road and stood at the top of the steps and said the last of it, and it was short.

Aldis Rook moves the chairs for Orla Fennimore and keeps nothing and has no stone and has no seat and no board, has never once been asked what he did, and said on the fourth morning of the hundred and sixtieth week that he was not going to say the thing he had said the day before in any other words.

“I am not going to say it again,” he said. “Not softer, not harder, not to explain it, not to answer anybody who has been thinking about it since six o’clock, and not to agree with myself about whether I meant it.” He put his hands behind him. “I said it once, in about nine ears, on a Wednesday, and I said in front of about nine ears that I would not say it again while I kept coming up this bank, and I am here to say that I meant that and not merely that I said it.”

“Nobody has asked you to say it again.”

“Nobody has.” He stood still for a moment. “And I want to say that I can feel the landing wanting to ask me, and not one person on it has, and that the wanting is not the asking, and that in about nine days there is going to be a man on this step who asks me because the not-asking will have got tired. He can. It is his morning and his question and he is allowed. I am telling you now that I will not answer it, so that when he asks there is a man in front of him who has already been told no by the person who said it.”

Nobody told the man who moves chairs anything about what he was going to be told.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the eighth hour and gave his figures and the figure of the offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing, and then gave the whole of a number he had never given anybody on that landing and said out loud that he would not be helped with it.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and thirteen days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and eighty-two days old.

“Four hundred and thirteen days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and eighty-two days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-seventeenth is two hundred and sixty-seven days old, which is nine hundred and sixty-seven less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

He got to the bottom of the steps before he started, which he has done twice.

“One hundred and seven wrong things in one hundred and seven mornings, and here is the hundred and seventh, and it is not about a chair and it is not about a wedge and it is the only figure on this landing this morning that is not a figure of the county.” He kept his hands behind him.

“Eleven years. That is my figure. I have been nobody’s for eleven years and that is a whole number of years and it is the only whole figure I have ever had in my life and I am giving it to you out loud for the first time on this landing because a man said a sentence yesterday about a thing with an absence in it and I have been sitting with it since the seventh hour.”

Nobody said anything about eleven.

“It is not about this county. Four hundred and thirteen days is about this county and two hundred and eighty-two is about a hand and two hundred and sixty-seven is about an offer that nobody has answered. Eleven is about me and I have never said it out loud anywhere and I have said it out loud here, and I would like the landing to notice that I said it here and not anywhere else, and that I have not said it to anybody one at a time first.”

“You are not going to be helped with it,” said the man with the broom.

“No, and I am not going to ask, and that is the whole of what it is for.” He looked up the bank. “Eleven years is a whole figure and it means that a thing I wanted has gone from wanting to being the shape of me. It is not going to come back. And if one of you helps me with it this morning, by any route, then I have spent this morning the way I have spent every morning since I got here, which is being handed something by a person who then knows what it did to me.”

Nobody on that landing helped him with it.

“And I am not going to be told it is a lot and I am not going to be told it is not a lot, and if anybody on this step has a view about eleven years they can keep it until I am not here, and then they can say it to somebody else about me, which is the ordinary way that things get said about people.”

Nobody had a view about eleven years.

Barnaby Crove went down the bank with the barrow and asked again halfway down, to nobody, in the same words, and nobody answered him. The man of thirty-one went down the bank on foot at about the ninth hour with his hands behind his back, and did not turn round at the wall, and did not put his weight on the fortieth step on the way down.

Corvin Tace swept his way up eighty-nine steps and went round the fortieth and went round the chair and set the broom against the wall where it has been every morning for nineteen years, and did not sit on it and did not touch it, and nothing that had been put down anywhere was pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used, and the water was sixteen inches and holding and nobody on that landing had done one single thing about the morning before.

# Chapter 972: A Man Who Moves Chairs Worked Out What Would Have To Be True For A Thing In A Step To Have Helped And Got To The End Of It And The End Of It Is That Nobody On That Landing Can Find Out

First day of the hundred and sixty-first week. His seven hundred and twenty-second morning. Five hundred and seventy-two days after the settlement. The fever ninety-eight weeks and three days old. Five hundred and twenty-six days since the division. Four hundred and seventy-two days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and eighteen days in this county of Kell.

Eight inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, about eighty of those ninety steps under, and six days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures, and then sat down on the bottom step without cleaning it, which is not a thing he does, and a man who moves chairs worked a thing out in front of everybody and got to the end of it in about twenty minutes.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the first day of the hundred and sixty-first week that he was not going to stand up there and say a number that one of them had just made unnecessary.

“Eight at the seventh hour, six days of the coming back, and about eighty of those ninety steps under, on the first day of the hundred and sixty-first week, and it is a Tuesday. Three figures.”

He sat down on the bottom step and put the broom across his knees.

“I am not going to say the fourth thing. The man who cannot read put a whole figure on this landing on Saturday and I am not going to come back at it with another one, and I am not going to say a figure of weeks either, because one of you has been carrying the same one since Saturday and it is no more a figure of anything than mine is.”

The man who moves chairs came up the cart road without a barrow and stood at the top of the steps where he has stood all fortnight, and did not come further down.

Aldis Rook moves the chairs for Orla Fennimore and keeps nothing and has no stone and has no seat and no board, has never once been asked what he did, has been told not to come up that bank and has come anyway, and on the first day of the hundred and sixty-first week worked out loud, in front of the whole landing, what would have to be true for a thing in a seam of a step to have made a man’s work easier.

“I am going to do it in the order I have done it in and I am going to give you the working at the end and I am going to give it to you whole, including the part where it stops.”

“Go on,” said the man with the broom, from the bottom step.

“First. For that step to have stopped moving, whatever is in the seam of it has to be taking the weight off the ends of the stone. That is not a guess, that is the only way a step like that works, and the man who sweeps it told you his heel is in the chip now and it has not gone anywhere. So the first part is settled and I am not going to reopen it, and I have not touched the step or the thing and I am not going to.”

Nobody said the first part was not settled.

“Second. For it to be doing that, it has to stay where it is. Which it will, because nobody on this landing is going to pull it, and I have said the seven out loud this week in three places and one of them was me and I am not going to hear myself saying it again.”

“Then where are you going,” said the man with the broom.

“Third. And this is the whole of it, and it is where it stops.” He took his hands out from behind him. “For the second part to be checkable, somebody would have to be able to say why it is there. And there is nobody on this step who can say why it is there, and there never was going to be, and that is not because anybody on this step is keeping a secret from anybody else.”

“It is that the only person who could say why it is there is a person who does not work here, and putting that person on this landing to ask them is the one move that this landing has spent a fortnight not making.”

Not a sound came off that landing.

“So here is the finding and I would like it taken in exactly the size it is. We cannot find out whether it helped. Not cannot find out this morning. Cannot find out. There is no morning on which that comes out, and everybody standing on these steps has known that since before the first of you got here and I have said out loud on this landing that I know it.”

He stopped there and did not go on.

“No,” said the man with the broom, from the bottom step. “And I am not going to say the figure again. You have got to the end of it in one morning. I got to the end of it in nineteen years and I got there by stopping, and I am not going to stand up here and say a number that you have just made unnecessary by being quicker than me. That would be me handing the same thing round twice and there is enough of that in this county already.”

The working got no thanks, nobody said it was a good one, and nobody told him he had got it right.

Then the boy came up out of the lane and gave his own count before anybody could ask him for it, in the voice of a boy who has been counting something in the dark and is perfectly satisfied to keep going on with it.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county three hundred and nineteen days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Three hundred and nineteen is nine hundred and seventy-two less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Three hundred and nineteen days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.” He looked round the landing. “And a man who cannot see well asked me yesterday, coming up the lane, whether the light comes up at the same time every morning in the year, and I said yes, and that was the whole of what he asked me, and he said good morning and went on up.”

The boy was not asked why a man who cannot see well wanted to know that.

“And I am not going to tell you what I think he wanted it for,” he said, “because I do not think anything about it, and if I did I would have to keep it, and I am not going to have a view about a thing on a landing. And nobody has told me I had that right.”

Nobody told the boy he had it right and nobody told him he had it wrong.

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour and asked in the same words at the same hour and was refused in the same way and was not thanked, and he had noticed on the way up that the top step had a full week in front of it.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and fifty-first mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is nine hundred and seventy-two less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and fifty-first. Nine hundred and seventy-two less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

“Nobody asked you for it this morning, and nobody asked you for it the morning before that, and I am not going to start being helpful about it now.”

“No, and a whole week of water has come down since I asked you about it and gone back up again, and there was a morning in the middle of it with nothing on that stone at all, and I have not said a word about that morning to anybody either, and there are two of us on this landing who keep a figure to ourselves and neither of us has told the other one what it is for.”

Which two he meant was not a thing anybody up there knew.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.” He said it in the words he uses. “And it is the first one of a week now and I am not going to soften it for that.”

The question got nothing back. Nobody told Barnaby Crove he was owed an answer, nobody said the refusal was final, nobody thanked him for it, and nobody thanked him for the figure he had given first.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the eighth hour with eight inches above his boots and gave his figures and the figure of the offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing, and asked the man who moves chairs one question and got the answer in a sentence, and took it neither well nor badly.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and eighteen days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and eighty-seven days old.

“Four hundred and eighteen days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and eighty-seven days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-twenty-second is two hundred and seventy-two days old, which is nine hundred and seventy-two less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it, and nobody has offered me a way out of it.”

He got as far as the bottom of the steps and stood on it with his hands behind him.

“A hundred and twelve wrong things in a hundred and twelve mornings, and here is the hundred and twelfth, and it is about a thing I cannot get at.”

“Then you have got to the end of it as well,” said the man who moves chairs, without turning round.

“Yes, and it is.” He did not move his hands. “I have been in this county a fortnight standing on a step being told, by one mouth after another, that a person cannot find out a thing about a thing in a seam. And I have worked out that I cannot either. Not because of the rule. Because I have been here a fortnight and I have got nothing to stand on at all, and a man who works here has not needed me for one minute of the nineteen years he has been on this stone.”

Nobody on that landing had needed him for one minute of anything.

“So my question is one line and it has a working. If somebody put a thing in a step on this landing and it made your work easier and you could not find out who, how long would you go on working as though you knew?”

The man who moves chairs did not turn round.

“No,” he said. “And the reason is not that it is rude and the reason is not that you are nobody. The reason is that a man who finds out that is a man who has begun to keep a thing, and a man who keeps a thing about a step on this landing is a man who is watching it, and the day he is watching it he is going to want to know when it was put there, and there is no answer to that which is not worse than not knowing.”

Nobody asked him what was worse than not knowing.

“And I will tell you the other half of it since you have come up here with a question and not getting an answer would be worse than getting one.” He turned round at last. “You have worked out that you cannot get at it. That is not a thing that has happened to you. That is the ordinary condition of this landing, and it has been the ordinary condition of this landing for nineteen years, and every man on it is in it, and one of them found out this morning that his work has got easier and told the whole landing so and then sat back down.”

He looked at the man with the broom.

“He is in it too. You are the only person up here who is in it because you have just got here, and that is not a thing you can be talked out of in a fortnight, and I am not going to pretend to you that you can.”

He got no help with that and no argument against it, and nobody told him what his question had been worth.

He swept his ninety, and went round the fortieth one, and went round the chair where it stood, and did not sit down on it, and did not put a hand on the thing in the seam. Nothing that has been put down anywhere on that landing has been pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was or used. Nobody up there found out whether the thing had helped, and the man whose work it was easier said out loud that he was not going to spend the rest of his life on a question that has no experiment in it, and nobody asked him how he meant to spend it.

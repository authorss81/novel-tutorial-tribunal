# Chapter 977: The Man With The Broom Put Out Loud What A Thing In A Step Was Going To Do To Him Now That His Work Was Easier And Then Stood On His Own Step Waiting For An Answer And Nobody On That Landing Gave Him One

Sixth day of the hundred and sixty-first week. His seven hundred and twenty-seventh morning. Five hundred and seventy-seven days after the settlement. The fever ninety-nine weeks and one day old. Five hundred and thirty-one days since the division. Four hundred and seventy-seven days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and twenty-three days in this county of Kell.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and it holds, about sixty of those ninety steps under, and one day of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and then put out loud a thing about himself that he does not know the end of, and stood on the step where he works while the whole of that landing worked out whether to answer it.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and on the sixth day of the hundred and sixty-first week said out loud that something is going to happen to him because of a thing in a step and that he does not know what it is.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, one day of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the sixth day of the hundred and sixty-first week, and it is a Sunday. Three figures.”

He set the broom down flat on the top step and stood on the fortieth one.

“I am going to say something out loud and I am going to stop in the middle of it, and I am telling you I am going to stop in the middle of it now, before I start, so that nobody on this landing spends the next ten minutes trying to get me to the end of it.”

Nobody tried.

“On the Monday at the end of last week I said on this step that my work has got easier, and that I was not going to say who did it, and that nobody had found anything wrong with the order of them.” He looked down at his own heel in the chip. “I have been standing here six days with that. And here is what it is going to do to me.”

He stopped there for a while.

“Not what it did. What it is going to do. I have worked on this landing nineteen years and I have got the way I sweep and I have got the place I put my heel and I have got which steps I take short and I have got the order I do them in, and none of that is written down anywhere and all of it is mine.”

Nobody on that landing said one word.

“And the step with the chip has changed how I sweep that stretch, and I have not decided to change it, and I am not going to decide to change it back. So I am going to be a man who stands in the chip on that one and stands outside it on the rest, and in about a year nobody on this landing is going to remember which one he does on the fortieth, and neither am I, and that will be in my work.”

He looked round the landing.

“And that is the whole of it. I am going to be working differently on one step out of ninety and I am not going to be able to tell anybody why, and if anybody asks me why I put my heel there, I am going to have to say something, and the something is not going to be true, and I am not going to be able to say the true one because the true one is that there is iron in the seam and there is no giver in the iron.”

Nobody on that landing answered him.

He waited there for a while with his heel in the chip, and the landing did not make a sound, and nobody gave him an answer, and nobody said that it was a small thing, and nobody said that it was a big one, and nobody told him he was right and nobody told him he was wrong.

“I am not going to say I expected an answer,” he said. “I have not said that and I am not going to say it now. I am going to say that there was a silence and I have noticed the length of it, and that is all I have got, and I am going back to my three figures.”

He picked the broom up.

“And I am not going to be thanked for saying any of it, and nobody on this landing is going to be thanked for not answering it, and that goes both ways and it goes on the record this morning.”

Both of those went without thanks.

Then the woman who saws lengths came up the cart road from down it with her saw handle under her arm and stood at the bottom of the steps and said the thing that had been sitting in her since Tuesday, which was that there is no way on earth to tell a thing that has been in a place from a thing that somebody is keeping in it, and that the only sign there is, and the sign is the same sign.

Wenna Pyle is thirty-eight, who saws lengths and sets studs at a bench in an open-sided shed behind a wheelwright’s about three hundred yards down the cart road, and who carries her own cup out to that bench because there is no table in the shed, and who said on the sixth day of the hundred and sixty-first week that the one sign anybody has got is the sign that means the opposite.

“I have been working out how you would tell,” she said. “Not why you would not touch it. How you would tell. And I have worked out that you cannot, and I want that said on this landing by somebody before the week turns, because I have been carrying it since Tuesday and it has got heavier.”

“Say the whole of it then,” said the man with the broom, and picked the broom up while he was saying it.

“A thing that has been in a place. And a thing that somebody is keeping in that place. From outside a step, on a morning of this week, in this weather, with the water where it is.” She shifted the saw handle. “There is no difference. None. Not a small one. I have stood in a workshop with things in it that I could not tell apart, and I have been wrong about which of the two it was, and I was wrong because I assumed, and assuming is the whole of what you would have to do here.”

Nobody on that landing said they had not assumed.

“So here is the sign and here is why it is no good.” She held up one finger and it was the only time that morning she did. “The only sign anybody on this landing has that a thing in a step is being kept there is that nobody has put their hand on it. And the only sign anybody has that a thing in a step has been there and forgotten about is also that nobody has put their hand on it. It is the same sign. It says both at once and it has said both at once since the first morning of this fortnight.”

She put her hand down.

“You cannot get round that. I have tried. I have been sitting at a bench with a cup on it that somebody put there and I have not asked whether I put it there, and the reason I have not asked is that I could not find out and the not asking is not a virtue, it is just the only thing there is.”

“And it has not been touched,” she said, before anybody could get there. “Not pulled, not turned over, not weighed in the hand, not read out loud, not carried inside, not put back where it was, and not used. And I have said that on this landing before and I am not going to keep saying it, and if any of you have started to think I am the one who carries it for this landing, I would like that stopped, because I have a bench of my own and a cup on it that nobody has asked me about and I would like to go back to it.”

She got no word of thanks and nobody told her she was right about the sign or that she was wrong about it.

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour on a Sunday and asked in the same words at the same hour and was refused in the same way and was not thanked, and he said that he had noticed the whole of the morning going on without him and that he had said the whole number last Saturday and had not been asked what it was for, and that he had thought about that all week and had not come up with anything.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and fifty-sixth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is nine hundred and seventy-seven less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and fifty-sixth. Nine hundred and seventy-seven less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

“Nobody asked you for it. Ask it again tomorrow and I will not tell you it was the same answer either.”

“No, and I am not going to keep telling you what nobody asked me for, because that is not asking you for anything, it is telling you about me, and I have noticed it this morning and I am going to stop doing it.”

He put his hand flat on the barrow.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.” He said it in the words he uses, and it was the same, and it was the last one of that week. “And I am going to say one more thing about it and then I am not going to say anything about it again till the week turns. If it is the wrong question, then I have asked it two hundred and fifty-six times and I would do it again, and I would like that to be on the record.”

Whether it was the wrong question was not said.

It got no verdict off that landing, nobody owed him a reply, nobody said the asking had cost him anything, and nobody thanked him, and he had not asked to be thanked.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the eighth hour and gave his figures and the figure of the offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing, and then told a man with a broom that he had got a question, and did not finish the sentence, and was refused the question before he had finished it.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and twenty-three days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and ninety-two days old.

“Four hundred and twenty-three days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and ninety-two days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-twenty-seventh is two hundred and seventy-seven days old, which is nine hundred and seventy-seven less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it, and nobody has offered me a way out of it and I have stopped mentioning that part.”

He got to the bottom of the steps.

“A hundred and seventeen wrong things in a hundred and seventeen mornings, and here is the hundred and seventeenth, and it is about a man who sweeps ninety steps a day saying out loud what is going to happen to him because of an iron wedge.”

Nobody on that landing said anything about that.

“And I have got a question about it and I have not asked it yet, and I have come to the bottom of these steps and told you I have got one, and I am not going to finish the sentence.”

The man with the broom did not move.

“You have told me you have got a question and you have not finished it, and I am not going to take it off you, and I am not going to answer it.” He held the broom in both hands. “And here is the working and it is the only working I have got. If you finish that sentence on this landing, somebody is going to answer it. And then we will all be standing here on a morning we have agreed to have.”

The sentence stayed unfinished and nobody else asked it.

“I do not hand out mornings,” Corvin Tace said. “I have said that on this landing three times in three weeks and I am saying it again and I am going to keep saying it, and it is not because I want to be asked things I do not want to answer. It is because a landing that knows what is coming is a landing that has stopped being walked on.”

“Ilyan, with respect,” said the man who moves chairs, from the back. “That is a good line and I have said it back to myself twice walking down that road and I want it noticed that I have said it back and I am not going to use it.”

Nobody used it.

“A hundred and seventeen,” the man of thirty-one said. “And here is the rest of it and it is the plainest thing I have said this week. I did not finish the sentence because he told me not to, and I am not going to be unrelieved about having been told not to, and I am going to stand at the bottom of these steps for the rest of this morning with nothing in my mouth, and that is the first unpaid thing I have done in this county.”

Nothing was paid for it and nobody told him that it was unpaid.

He swept all ninety, and went round the fortieth one, and went round the chair, and did not touch the thing in the seam and did not sit on the chair. Nothing has been pulled, or turned over, or weighed in a hand, or read aloud, or carried inside, or put back where it was, or used. A man said out loud on that landing what a thing in a step is going to do to him for the rest of his working life, and nobody up there answered him, and he did not ask.

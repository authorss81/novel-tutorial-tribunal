# Chapter 952: Sixteen Inches And About Sixty Of Those Ninety Steps Under, And A Woman Has Come Half A Mile Up From A Wash-Tub To Say She Is Not Going To Be Asked Whether She Took It

Second day of the hundred and fifty-eighth week. His seven hundred and second morning. Five hundred and fifty-two days after the settlement. The fever ninety-five weeks and four days old. Five hundred and six days since the division. Four hundred and fifty-two days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and three hundred and ninety-eight days in this county of Kell.

The woman came up the low side of that landing along the edge of the drain with her sleeves pushed to the elbow and a wet barrow-mat over her shoulder, and she stopped at the bottom of the steps and asked for nobody to come up her, and she was not asked to go up.

Bess Farrar is forty-five, washes at a tub at the low side of that landing about half a mile down along the edge of that drain, gets up in the dark every morning of the year, and has never once been asked where she gets her water.

“I have come half a mile to say one thing and I have said it now and I am going to stand here and be told nothing, because I know what this landing does to a mouth that opens.” She put the mat down on the bottom step. “There is a thing on the edge of my tub. That is all. It is not in the water. It is on the stone the tub stands on, at the side you would put a bucket down on. It is not mine and it is not the shape of anything I own.”

“Did you put it there.”

“No. And I want you to hear the answer and then I want you to hear the second part, and the second part is the one I walked half a mile for.” She did not take her sleeves down. “I do not know who put it there, I am not going to work it out, I am not going to say I do not know in a way that is asking to be asked, and I am not going to stand at that tub tomorrow morning and make a speech about it to anybody who comes down the lane.”

“You could have come down the lane.”

“I could have come down the lane and about nine people who pass that way before light would have had a thing to talk about by the time I was back up here, and I do not need nine people to carry it and I am not going to say how many people it would be.”

She wiped her hands on the mat and left them there.

“Now the thing I came to say. I am not going to be asked whether I took it. That is not a favour anybody is doing me and I want that written nowhere. There is no giver in it. Nobody put that down and I do not care about who and I am not going to be told either, and I have worked out that if a person stands beside a thing like that for long enough, then every mouth in the county starts doing arithmetic about who put it there, and I am not going to be a figure in somebody’s arithmetic about a tub.”

Corvin Tace came out and gave the three figures before he came down the steps, standing where the stone is.

“Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and it holds, about sixty of those ninety steps under, and two days of the coming back. Three. You are not getting a fourth.”

He leaned the broom against the wall and looked at her wet sleeves for a while.

“You work at that tub every morning of the year in the dark,” he said. “That is the only thing I am going to say about it, and I am saying it to the step and not to you. The thing on your stone is not a thing somebody who works at your tub put down, because the only person who works at your tub is standing on my step with her sleeves wet. So whoever put it there was standing at a tub at a quarter past five in the morning because they had come to it, and that is not a thing I am going to make into a story.”

“That is exactly right and I would like it to stop there.”

“It stops there. And it has not been touched, and if it is found again it is not going to be turned over or weighed in the hand or read out loud or taken inside or put back where it is, and I say that now so that nobody on this landing has to be the first one to refuse it.”

The boy came up out of the lane while the woman of forty-five was still standing at the bottom of the steps, and gave his figure, and said it before anybody asked him for it, in the voice of a boy who has been counting something in the dark and is pleased that somebody is finally going to be there.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county two hundred and ninety-nine days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Two hundred and ninety-nine is nine hundred and fifty-two less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Two hundred and ninety-nine days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.”

“You were up early.”

“I was up because there is nothing to count on a morning when the water is on and I do not want to be idle in front of a step.” He looked at the woman’s mat. “I am not going to ask you about the tub either. Everybody on this step has been careful about that this week and I noticed and I want it said that I noticed, because I would like there to be one thing on this landing that a man of thirteen can be relied on for.”

Corvin Tace set the broom down. “Nobody is going to be thanked for being careful. That is not what this is. You are careful and you get nothing for it and I would like that said out loud this morning while there is a woman here who has just told us the same thing from the other end of half a mile.”

Then the man of thirty-one came up and gave his figures and made a proposal about the two places, and it was refused inside a sentence by the woman who reads the ninth run, with the working given.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county three hundred and ninety-eight days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and sixty-seven days old.

“Three hundred and ninety-eight days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and sixty-seven days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-second is two hundred and fifty-two days old, which is nine hundred and fifty-two less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

He stood where he could see both the road up past the green gate and the low side down along the drain.

“Ninety-two wrong things in ninety-two mornings, and here is the ninety-second, and it is not a speech, it is one line of work.”

“There are places. There is a step six hundred yards up that road and there is a stone half a mile down that drain and there are probably eleven more between them, and every one of them has somebody who works at it. If somebody wrote the places down then we would not have to keep finding them out. I am not asking for who put anything anywhere. I am asking for the stones. Give me the stones and I will carry them up here and put them on a wall and this landing will know where it is standing at.”

Sena Dorr was on the landing before he had finished, and she did not open the book.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years and holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and has decided to ask one question and has not asked it.

“No.”

“That is the whole of your answer and I have brought more than that.”

“That is the whole of my answer and I have got a working for it, which you did not bring, so here it is.” She closed the book against her knee. “I have read places down. That is the whole of what my work has been for twenty-three years and I have read out where a thing was kept and where a thing was found and who stood in the room when it was read, and it took me eleven years to work out that the only part of it anybody ever used was the first half.”

“The first half.”

“The stones.” She said it without any weight on it at all. “The stones are the only part of it that never hurt anybody. The rest of it — the day it was found, and what was said, and who was in the room — the day I put a day on a place, the place stopped being a place and became a thing somebody could be asked about, and everybody standing near it became a figure, and I have watched that happen to other people for twenty-three years and I have never once been able to do it to a thing in a tub.”

“You are refusing me a bookkeeping.”

“I am refusing you the day. There is no difference and you can have the rest.” She looked at the woman of forty-five at the bottom of the steps. “Write down the step, the drain, the chip in the corner of the fortieth one. Write every stone you can walk to. Do not write the morning. And when you have written six of them and hung them on a wall, look at them one morning and tell me which of the six was the important one, and I will tell you that it is the one you did not write, and that a person standing beside it can be asked about it forever.”

Nobody thanked him for the idea and nobody said it was a good idea.

Ilyan Vester looked at her for a while and then at his own hands.

“That is the best thing anybody has said to me in this county and it cost you one sentence, and I am going to be annoyed about it for about a week.” He put his hands behind his back. “And I am not going to write it down anywhere either, so if I come up here in a fortnight wanting to, I will have to come up here and be told it.”

Nobody on that landing answered Barnaby Crove’s asking, because he was not on it, and nothing that had been put down anywhere was touched.

The woman of forty-five went back down the low side along the edge of that drain to a tub with a thing on the stone beside it, and did not take it inside, and did not put it back, and did not move it, and did not look at it again that morning.

The boy went back down into the lane.

And the man of thirty-one said his figure out loud to an empty landing twice, on the theory that a figure said once to nobody is a figure said once, and went down the bank on foot at about the ninth hour with his hands behind his back, which is the one thing nobody on that landing can take off him and the one thing he had not done all day.

# Chapter 975: A Woman Who Reads For A Living Worked Out What A Landing Owes A Thing That Has Made Its Work Easier And Got Nothing At All Out Of It And Would Not Be Comforted And A Boy Said He Did Not Know What It Was Called And Was Not Going To Look It Up

Fourth day of the hundred and sixty-first week. His seven hundred and twenty-fifth morning. Five hundred and seventy-five days after the settlement. The fever ninety-eight weeks and six days old. Five hundred and twenty-nine days since the division. Four hundred and seventy-five days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and twenty-one days in this county of Kell.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and it holds, about sixty of those ninety steps under, and three days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and did not try to give the morning a shape, and a woman who reads for a living came up the road at the fourth hour and worked a thing out in front of everybody and got nothing at the end of it.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the fourth day of the hundred and sixty-first week that a Friday is a Friday and that he was not going to stand up there and make anything of it.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, three days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the fourth day of the hundred and sixty-first week, and it is a Friday. Three figures.”

He leaned the broom against the wall and left it there, which is not a thing he does before the ninth hour.

“I am not going to say anything else this morning until somebody gives me something to say it to. I have been handed three this week and I have used them and I have got nothing left and I would rather stand here empty than fill it.”

Nobody filled it.

Then the woman who reads the ninth run came up the cart road and entered the morning the way she enters a morning, which is from the side and looking at the ground, and asked one question before she said anything, and got it answered in four words.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years, holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and came up the road on the fourth day of the hundred and sixty-first week to work out what a landing owes a thing that has made its work easier.

“Corvin. If a thing had been put down on this landing and it had made your work easier, what would this landing owe it?”

“Nothing,” said the man with the broom.

She sat down on the bottom step, on the swept part, which nobody else does.

“All right. I have been walking up that road since the fourth hour with that and I want to say the whole of it before anybody says anything kind to me about having worked it out.”

What she got was not kindness.

“Here is the working, and I have done it the way I do a line, which is from the outside in. A landing owes a man his own work. That is the first one and it is nineteen years old on this stone and nobody has ever collected on it. A landing owes a person the right to be asked or not asked about his own step, and this landing does not collect on that either, and a man can be told or not told and either way he keeps the step.” She looked at her own hands. 

“And here is where it ends and I want the ending said out loud, because nobody up here is going to work it out for me.”

She looked up the steps.

“A landing owes a thing nothing.”

Nobody said a word out there.

“Not nothing as in a small amount. Nothing. Not the day it comes off the bank, not the morning it was put down, not a corner of it.” She put her hands on her knees. “A thing that is put down where somebody works and stays there does not become a debt by being useful. That is the whole of the trick of it and I have read about debts for twenty-three years and I have never found that in one before this morning.”

She said the rest of it looking at the ground.

“It is not a debt, because a debt can only be owed to a person, and it cannot be owed to a person who does not know it was done, and it cannot be owed to a person who does. It is not a gift, because I am not going to use that word about it and I would like this landing to notice that I have had it in my mouth twice this morning and put it down both times.”

Why she put it down was not asked.

“And it is not even a thing that happened. It is an object with a person standing next to it who is not the person who brought it.” She took her hands off her knees. “So there is nothing in it. I have looked at that step from every side a person can look at a thing from without touching it, and there is nothing in it, and I came up this road at the fourth hour expecting there to be something and there is not.”

“That is a hard thing to have worked out at the fourth hour,” said Simon Rook, from the wall.

“Do not do that.”

He closed his mouth.

“I am not being hard. I am being exact, and there is a difference and you know there is a difference, because you have spent your life unable to check things and you have never once let anybody help you by pretending.” She did not raise her voice and did not turn round. “Do not be kind to me about having found nothing. I did not find nothing. I found the answer, and the answer is nothing, and it took me a fortnight and a quarter of a morning’s walking, and nobody has to put a hand on me for it.”

No hand went on her, and she got no comfort and no thanks.

“And I will say the other half, because it is mine to say and it is the part that is easy to get wrong.” She straightened up. “We do this. A thing put down where somebody works is not put down again the next morning by the same person. We do that, all of us, in about nine trades, and nobody on this landing has ever given it a name and nobody is going to, because the day this landing gives it a name it is a rule, and a rule on this landing is a thing with a person inside it.”

Nobody named it.

“And the person it was put down for is not asked whether they took it. Not asked this morning, not asked on any other morning, and that is not a rule either, it is just what we do.” She looked at the man with the broom. “And a thing left beside a person is not a version of what that person has wanted. I have watched that sentence get said on this landing twice as though it were a comfort and it is not a comfort. It is a fact with a shape you can hold, and holding it is not the same as being given anything.”

Nobody said it had been a comfort.

“And it has not been touched. Not pulled, not turned over, not weighed in the hand, not read out loud, not carried inside, not put back where it was, and not used. Seven things, said out loud by me this morning, and I am a woman who has read other people’s accounts for twenty-three years and there is nothing in this one, and that is not because it is a good one. It is because there is not one.”

Then the boy came up out of the lane in the middle of all that and gave his own count before anybody could ask him for it, and then asked the woman one question and got an answer in four words too.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county three hundred and twenty-two days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Three hundred and twenty-two is nine hundred and seventy-five less six hundred and fifty-three.

“Three hundred and twenty-two days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.” He looked at the woman on the step. “You have said a landing owes a thing nothing, and I have worked out that if a thing makes a man’s work easier and nobody can pay it and nobody can spend it, then there is something that happens and has not got a name.”

“That is correct.”

“And I would like to know what it is called and I am not going to look it up.”

He was not told what it was called and nobody looked it up for him.

“There is nowhere to look it up,” he said, as though answering her. “I have been in this county for three hundred and twenty-two days and there is a landing and a cart road and a shed with a bench in it and a low wall, and that is the whole of what I have got to look things up in, and none of it is a book.” He sat down on the wall on his own coat. “So I am going to go on not knowing what it is called, and that is fine, and nobody is to tell me I was right about not looking and nobody is to tell me I was wrong about it.”

He was told neither that he was right nor that he was wrong.

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour and asked in the same words at the same hour and was refused in the same way and was not thanked, and he said that a woman had said a whole thing out loud and that he had not understood about half of it and that he was glad there had been more than half.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and fifty-fourth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is nine hundred and seventy-five less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and fifty-fourth. Nine hundred and seventy-five less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

“Nobody asked you for it, and it is Friday, and Friday is not a day I answer on.”

“No, and I have been thinking about the woman on that step and I am going to say one thing and then I am going to ask, and the one thing is this. I have been asking every morning for a thing for me and she has been up here working out a thing for that step, and there is nobody up here working out a thing for a man who cannot read, and I am not saying that as a complaint because I would be reported and I am not reported.”

Nobody reported him.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.” He said it in the words he uses. “And it is the fourth one this week and I have not changed a word of it and I am not going to.”

Nothing came back. He was not told he was owed an answer, not told he was not owed one, and nobody thanked him for two hundred and fifty-four mornings of turning up with the same question.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the eighth hour and gave his figures and the figure of the offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing, and said out loud that he had got the clearest answer anybody had given him all fortnight and that he was not going to be unrelieved about it.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and twenty-one days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and ninety days old.

“Four hundred and twenty-one days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and ninety days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-twenty-fifth is two hundred and seventy-five days old, which is nine hundred and seventy-five less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

He got to the bottom of the steps and did not go further, and stood there.

“A hundred and fifteen wrong things in a hundred and fifteen mornings, and here is the hundred and fifteenth, and it is the plainest answer anybody has given me in this county and it is about a landing owing a thing nothing.” He looked up at the woman on the step. “I have been standing on this bank a fortnight trying to work out what I would be owed if I ever put something down on a stone where a man works, and I have got eleven answers in my head and every one of them had me in it.”

The eleven answers were not asked after.

“And the answer is that I would be owed nothing, and that is the first one out of eleven that has not had me in it, and I am not going to be unrelieved about it, and I am not going to be relieved about it, and I would like it noticed that a woman who has read for twenty-three years walked up that road at the fourth hour to tell a man who sweeps steps that he is owed nothing by an iron wedge and nobody thanked her for it and nobody is going to.”

Nobody thanked her and nobody was going to.

The chair went round and the thing in the seam went round, and neither of them was touched, and nothing was pulled, or turned over, or weighed in a hand, or read aloud, or carried inside, or put back where it was, or used. A woman of forty-nine said out loud on that landing that a landing owes a thing nothing, and nobody said thank you for it, and nobody was going to, and the thing in the fortieth step was still in the fortieth step when the light went off it.

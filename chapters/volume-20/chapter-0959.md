# Chapter 959: Sixteen Inches On The Top Step And It Holds, And About Sixty Of Those Ninety Steps Under, And Three Days Of The Coming Back, And A Man Put A Chair Down On A Landing Where He Does Not Work And Nobody Would Say What It Was For

Second day of the hundred and fifty-ninth week. His seven hundred and ninth morning. Five hundred and fifty-nine days after the settlement. The fever ninety-six weeks and four days old. Five hundred and thirteen days since the division. Four hundred and fifty-nine days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and five days in this county of Kell.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and it holds, about sixty of those ninety steps under, and three days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and then found a chair on his own landing, and nobody had brought it there, and he did not sit on it, and he swept around it.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and found a chair on the landing he holds and did not sit on it and would not say who had put it there.

“Three figures. Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, three days of the coming back, about sixty of those ninety steps under. I am not going to give you a fourth about the chair because I have never given a fourth about anything.”

“Where did it come from.”

“That is the fourth thing and I have just told you I am not giving it.” He set the broom against the wall, well clear of it. “It is a chair. It is a good chair and it is somebody’s living and it has got a bit of felt gone off the back of it where somebody has leaned for years. It is about a yard from where I work and it is not in the way of anything I do, and it was not here at six o’clock, and I have swept this landing every morning for nineteen years and nothing has ever been left on it.”

Nobody moved the chair. Nobody sat in it. Nobody asked who had put it there.

Then the man who puts chairs where people work came up the cart road on foot without a barrow and without a chair on his shoulder and stood at the top of the steps and looked at his own trade standing on a landing that he had never stood on in his life, and said so out loud before anybody could say it for him.

Aldis Rook moves the chairs for Orla Fennimore and keeps nothing and has no stone and has no seat and no board, has never once been asked what he did, and has been told he is not to thank anybody on this step for anything.

“That is a working chair of mine.”

“You have put it down.”

“I have carried about nine hundred chairs into other people’s houses and I have never once put one down on this landing in my life, and I did not put that one there, and I have stood here since the sixth hour and worked it out and I am going to say what I worked out and I am going to say it in the order I worked it out.”

He did not go near it.

“It is a chair. It is the kind I move. It has got felt gone off the back of it, and I would say that felt went in a house with a fire and a woman in it and a person who leans. And nobody put it on this landing. It is here because it was put here, and I did not do it, and the man who did it does not work here.”

“You are not going to say who.”

“I am not going to say who and I would like this whole landing to hear me not say who, because in about nine days there will be a man on this step who starts being good at that and I want there to be a record that I did not.”

Nobody said his name, and nobody said anything about his trade, and nobody told him he had got it right.

Corvin Tace looked at the chair for a while.

“You have carried about nine hundred chairs into other people’s houses,” he said. “Say the rest of it.”

“The rest of it is that I never go back in the morning.”

His hands went behind him again.

“That is the whole of my trade and it is why I am the only man on this landing who can be told this week and not be surprised by it. I carry it in. I put it down where somebody works. I am not in the house. I do not work in that house. I am not coming back the next morning to put another one down, and the person it went in for is not going to be asked whether they took it or whether they would rather not have it.

“Eleven years, about nine hundred houses, and not one person in any of them has ever heard a word about it from me, and not one of them has ever thanked me, and I would like to know that as a fact and not as a favour.”

Somebody at the back of the landing said the name of the thing, and then said it could not be said, in the same breath, and nobody acted on either half.

Then the man of forty-three asked the one question that nobody on that landing had asked anybody all week, and he asked it because he is the man who holds it and not because he is curious, and it was refused in a sentence by the man who had put his hands behind his back.

“I have been stood here for about an hour,” Corvin Tace said, “and I have not sat down, and I am not going to sit down, and I am going to ask the only question I am allowed to ask, which is what that chair is for.”

“You are not going to like the answer.”

“Then I will not like it, and I will still be here in the morning and so will the chair, and that is the arrangement.”

“It is not for anything,” said Aldis Rook. “It is not for you. It is not for me. It is not a gift and it is not a kindness and it is not a reward for nineteen years of sweeping, because if it were a reward then somebody had to decide to give it and nobody decided anything. A thing left beside a person is not a version of what that person has wanted. That is the whole of it and it took me about an hour to get to it and I have had eleven years of carrying things into rooms and this is the first time it has been on the same side of the door as me.”

He took his hands out from behind him, slowly.

“It is not a version of what you have wanted. That is what nobody wants anybody to put down beside them. A version would come with the wanting attached, and then you would owe somebody for it, and I am not going to be owed by a man who holds a landing. That is not a small thing to say to a man on a step and I am saying it once and I am not saying it again for the rest of the year.”

Corvin Tace put his hand on the broom and did not look at the chair again.

“That is the plainest thing anybody has put on this landing since I came to it and I am not going to thank you for it, and I am going to tell you why I am not, because you will go away thinking I did not care. I did not know that was the shape of it either. I have been on this landing nineteen years and I have given about nine people the water figures off this stone and I have thought that was giving.

“And a thing that is put down beside you with nothing attached to it is not a gift, because a gift comes from somebody who is there. That is the part I have not had. Nineteen years on this step and I have never once been able to say who was in the room, and I have put that down to a county that does not answer and I have just worked out on a Wednesday afternoon that some of it might have been about a chair.”

He did not sit on it.

The boy came up out of the lane in the middle of that and gave his figure and said what he had come to say, and a man of thirty-one tried to use him for it and was refused by the boy, in a sentence, without any help.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county three hundred and six days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up.

“Three hundred and six days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.”

“You were up early again.”

“I was up early because a chair is not water and I do not want to be idle.” He looked at the chair from the bottom of the steps and did not come up. “And I am not going to tell anybody what I think that chair is for, and if a man asks me I am going to say I do not know, because I do not know, and because the first time somebody asked me what a thing was for I was ten and it was a different thing and I answered anyway.”

“You are not going to be asked.”

“I know I am not. That is why I have said it while it is cheap.” He sat down on the wall, on his own coat. “Nobody has told me this week that I had it right about anything and nobody is going to, and I want it noticed that I have noticed that, because if I ever do find out that somebody on this landing told one of us we had it right, then it will matter that nobody did it while I could have heard.”

Nobody told him he had it right. Nobody told him he had it wrong at all, either, and he said he had expected that.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and five days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and seventy-four days old.

“Four hundred and five days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and seventy-four days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-ninth is two hundred and fifty-nine days old, which is nine hundred and fifty-nine less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

He looked at the chair and had it in his mouth within a second and somebody at the back of the landing said his name.

“Ninety-nine wrong things in ninety-nine mornings. And that is the whole of what I am going to say about that figure and I would like the whole of this landing to know that nobody has said one word to me about it, and that I noticed, and that I am not going to thank any of you for not saying it.”

He kept the hands behind him where they had been.

“Tomorrow it is a whole figure and it will be on this step in my own mouth and I will give it. Nobody will help me with it and nobody will tell me what it is.

“I have got nothing left that is free except asking, and asking is what the man with the barrow does. I would rather be the man who does that than be the man somebody helps with a hundred.”

Barnaby Crove came up at about the ninth hour and asked his question in the same words and was refused in the same way and was not thanked, and then asked whether anybody had worked out what the chair was for, and was told by the man of fifty-four that nobody had, and did not ask again.

The chair stayed where it had been put. It was not pulled, not turned over, not weighed in the hand, not read aloud, not carried inside, not put back where it was, and not used. Nobody said who had put it there, and nobody on that landing ever said, and the man of forty-three swept around it for the rest of the morning and did not sit on it, and the water stood at sixteen inches on the top step and held there, and on the landing there was now a thing that could be read by anybody who could read and had not been read, 

And about nine people stood within thirty feet of it and said nothing about it.

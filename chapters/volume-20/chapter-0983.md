# Chapter 983: A Saturday On Which The Fever Came Out As A Whole Figure Of One Hundred Weeks For The Fifth Time And A Man Who Cannot Read Said It Out Loud Before Anybody Worked It Out For Him And Nobody Asked Him What It Was For And A Man Who Cannot See Well Said Out Loud That There Is A Giver In That Thing Now And It Got There In One Mouth And It Was Not His

Fifth day of the hundred and sixty-second week. His seven hundred and thirty-third morning. Five hundred and eighty-three days after the settlement. The fever one hundred weeks old. Five hundred and thirty-seven days since the division. Four hundred and eighty-three days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and twenty-nine days in this county of Kell.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and it holds, about sixty of those ninety steps under, and three days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and said nothing else about them, because a whole figure had come out of the date line at the sixth hour and he had heard it before the woman who reads for a living heard it and he was not going to be the second mouth on it.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the fifth day of the hundred and sixty-second week that a whole figure said at the sixth hour on a Saturday belongs to whoever said it until somebody else puts a shape on it, and that he was not going to be that somebody.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, three days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the fifth day of the hundred and sixty-second week, and it is a Saturday. Three figures.”

He set the broom down and stayed where he was.

“That is all I am going to say about this morning and the reason is that a man said a whole number out loud at the sixth hour and I heard it, and I have got nineteen years of saying nothing on this landing behind me and none of it is going on somebody else’s figure.”

Barnaby came up at about the sixth hour and said it before the water had been read off the stone, which is what he has done on five Saturdays running now.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and sixty-second mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is nine hundred and eighty-three less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. And on the fifth day of the hundred and sixty-second week, a Saturday, he gave a whole figure out loud at the sixth hour before anybody worked it out for him.

“One hundred weeks.” He put both hands flat on the wall, which is what he does. “Not one hundred weeks and a bit. One hundred weeks, on this Saturday, and it is the fifth one of those since I started saying it out loud.”

Not one of them had worked it out for him.

“And it has come out whole five times and it has gone up by one each time, and it was ninety-six weeks the first morning I stood up here and said one out loud, and it is one hundred weeks this morning. And I have said every one of them on a landing full of people who can read them for me and not one of you has worked it out for me and I have noticed that five times now.”

Not one of them on that landing worked it out for him.

Then the man who moves chairs came up the cart road without a barrow and stood at the flat of the steps, and everybody on that landing saw what his mouth was doing a half second before he got it shut.

Aldis Rook moves the chairs for Orla Fennimore and keeps nothing and has no stone and has no seat and no board, has never once been asked what he did, has been told not to come up that bank and has come anyway, and on the fifth day of the hundred and sixty-second week started to ask a man who cannot read what a whole figure of weeks is for and then did not ask him.

He stood there with his hand half up.

“No,” he said, out loud, to everybody including himself. “No.”

“I am not going to ask you that and I am going to say out loud that I was about to, because a man who cannot read has said a whole figure five Saturdays running and I have asked you once already and you gave me the same answer you have given three times, and today I came up that road with the question ready and it is the wrong thing to do and I have worked out why it is the wrong thing to do on the way up.”

Why it was the wrong thing to do was never asked him.

“It is because you cannot read it and I can, and the day the first one of us asks you what a whole figure is for, that whole figure stops being yours and starts being mine. It becomes a thing I have an opinion about. And then it is not a number you said on a Saturday because a Saturday is the morning you have got nothing else in your mouth. It is a number about something, and I would be the one who made it about something.”

“And I have spent nineteen years putting things in rooms and going out of the door before anybody came in, and this would be the same move with my mouth instead of my hands.”

Nobody had anything to say about the shutting of it.

“So I am not asking. And I am not going to ask on any Saturday after this one either, and if you keep saying it out loud then it keeps being yours, and if one of us ever does ask then you are entitled to stand on that wall and tell us to go and ask the water.”

The water was asked nothing.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the eighth hour, and gave his figures, and gave the figure of the offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing, and then stood under a whole figure of one hundred weeks that a man who cannot read had put in the air, and did not explain it.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and twenty-nine days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and ninety-eight days old.

“Four hundred and twenty-nine days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and ninety-eight days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-thirty-third is two hundred and eighty-three days old, which is nine hundred and eighty-three less seven hundred. It is unanswered, and I have not asked it, and I did not ask it yesterday and I am not going to ask it because there is a whole figure in the air on this landing this morning and a man who cannot read put it there.”

He got to the bottom of the steps and stayed there, looking up.

“I want to say that I know exactly what one hundred weeks is and that I have known since the sixth hour, and I want to say that I worked it out inside a minute of hearing it, and that I have spent all morning wanting to go up there and explain it to him in the plainest words I own.” He kept his hands still at his sides. “And I am not going to.”

“And the reason I am not going to is that he said it before anybody worked it out for him, and the whole of that is that it was his before it was anybody else’s, and if I explain it then it is mine. I would be doing the thing I did to the ash on Thursday, in words instead of with a piece of wood, and I would be doing it to a man who cannot read and who would not be able to tell me I had.”

Nobody on that landing told him he had been right not to.

“One hundred and twenty-three wrong things in one hundred and twenty-three mornings, and this is the hundred and twenty-third, and I am not going to say what it is for either. Nobody will tell me it is small and nobody will tell me it is large and nobody will say that I found anything.”

Nobody told him it was small and nobody told him it was large, and nobody said that he had found anything.

Then the man of thirty-eight came up the road with his hand not on his coat, and said the whole of the rule out loud for the second time in his life, and nobody thanked him for either time.

Garrin Tolley is thirty-eight, has a child of nine, came back up a road he said he would not come up and is still not naming a day, and said on the fifth day of the hundred and sixty-second week that a rule a man repeats in his own words is not the same rule as a rule a man repeats out of a book, and did not say which one this was.

“It has not been touched. Not pulled, not turned over, not weighed in the hand, not read out loud, not carried inside, not put back where it was, and not used.” He said it to the flat of the step and not to anybody. “Seven things.”

“I said them once before in this county and I did not know why I had said them and I have worked out since that I said them because there was a thing on this landing I was not allowed to have an opinion about, and having seven sentences I was not allowed to have an opinion about turned out to be a way of keeping still.”

Nobody asked him about the board in the inside of his coat.

“That includes the wood. That includes the iron in the fortieth step. That includes the piece of ash up there that a man of thirty-one has explained to us twice. And I am not going to be told that repeating a rule in my own words is the same as keeping it, because I have read about men who did that.”

He was not told whether it was.

“And nobody is to thank me for saying them again, and nobody is to ask me to say them a third time, and I have a boy of nine at home who does not know I come up here and he is not going to be told either.”

Then the man who cannot see well came up onto the flat of the steps with his hand out in front of him and found the wall with it, and said the thing that had been sitting in him since yesterday, which was that a giver in a thing is the one figure he has never been able to put in a list.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and said on the fifth day of the hundred and sixty-second week that the whole of this landing rests on there being no giver in a thing, and that there is one now, and that it got there in one mouth and it was not his.

“I want to start by saying that I cannot see that step and I am not pretending I can, and that what I am about to say is about a thing I have never looked at and could not look at if I tried.” He put his back against the wall. “This landing runs on there being no giver in a thing.”

“That is the sentence under everything anybody has said about the iron in the fortieth step for a fortnight, and nobody has ever had to say it out loud, and every single one of you has been relying on it without putting a name on it.”

No name was put on it.

“And it has stopped being true on Thursday morning at the eighth hour. Not because of the wood. Because a man said out loud that he put it there. There is a giver in that thing now, and it got there in one mouth, and it was not mine, and I have been standing here for two days with a list of things I cannot check and this is the first one that is not a thing at all. It is a person.”

Nobody on that landing took that up.

“I cannot put a person in a list. That is not a complaint and it is not a loss. A list is for things I cannot check and a person is a thing I can check by asking, and I am not going to ask him, because he said it himself on Thursday and there is nothing to ask about. So it is a person on my landing and it is not in the list and it is not going in.”

He was not asked to put it in.

“I have got nothing else this morning. I have not got a figure and I have not got a grievance and I have not got anything to say to the man of thirty-one that would help him, and I am not looking for one, and I would like it noticed that there was a whole figure said out loud on this landing an hour ago and I have not asked anybody what it is for either.”

Nobody asked.

One hundred weeks was said out loud for the fifth time on a Saturday and it did not become a meaning. Nobody worked it out for the man who said it, nobody asked him what it was for, and nobody told the man who could have explained it that he had been right not to. 

There was a giver in the piece of ash and it had been put there by one mouth on Thursday and not by anybody else, and the thing was still on the dry strip along the foot of the wall and had not been touched.

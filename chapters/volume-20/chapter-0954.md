# Chapter 954: The Water Came Off That Bank At About The Sixth Hour And All Ninety Of Those Steps Were Walkable By About The Seventh, And A Man Said Out Loud That There Is No Figure Of Inches

Fourth day of the hundred and fifty-eighth week. His seven hundred and fourth morning. Five hundred and fifty-four days after the settlement. The fever ninety-five weeks and six days old. Five hundred and eight days since the division. Four hundred and fifty-four days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred days in this county of Kell.

The water came off that bank at about the sixth hour and all ninety of those steps were walkable by about the seventh, and that was the thirty-sixth time. There is no figure of inches on this morning and there is no figure of steps under, and there is no figure of days of the coming back either, and that is not a gap in the morning. It is the shape of the morning, and a man said it out loud before anybody asked him for it, and he said it at the bottom of the steps with the broom in his hand where the step itself was not doing anything for him to lean on.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and this morning said the absence out loud and then went and swept eighty-nine steps instead of one.

“There is no figure of inches. Not one, and I am not going to round it and I am not going to hedge it and I am not going to give you a feeling of it instead, because a feeling of it is still a figure and I have watched men put those on this step in a fortnight and call them something else. No number of steps under either. And no number of days, and there is not one to give you, because when all ninety are out there is no coming back to count out from. That is the shape of it. That is the thirty-sixth time.”

He put the broom down flat on the top step.

“I have said that out loud before eleven because on a morning like this a man who is honest walks out here early with his mouth shut and everybody decides he has come out early to look at the water, and there is no water.”

“It is the thirty-sixth.”

“It is the thirty-sixth and it is eight days on from the thirty-fifth, and that is arithmetic off my own stone and not off a book, and if anybody on this step wants to check it they are welcome to come and count the mornings with me, and there are not very many of them and not one of them is a figure of anybody.”

Nobody said that it was a good morning or a bad one.

Then he swept, and he swept the way he sweeps, from the high end down, and he got to the fortieth step and went round it and carried on with the thirty-ninth, and he did not put the broom on it at all, and he did not slow down while he did it, and the whole of that landing watched him do it and not one of them said a word.

When he got to the top again he set the broom against the wall.

“I am going to say why and then I am going to say that nobody is to build anything on it. That step has not rocked for longer than I have held this landing and I have not got a figure for how long that is and I am not going to get one, because the only way to get one is to count back, and counting back is a figure, and the day I have a figure for how long that step has been steady then somebody on this step has a figure to put next to the thing in its seam and put the two of them together and make a sentence out of them.”

He looked at the man of thirty-one.

“So I am not going to count and neither are you, and if a man works out that the fortieth does not move, then he has got it without me and he can have it, and I did not give it to him and I did not know it.”

“You left it.”

“I swept eighty-nine steps and I did not leave anything, and I am not going to be praised for the fortieth and I am not going to be told I was careful with it, because a man being careful with a step is a figure of a decision and I have had a fortnight of them from people who are not me.” He picked the broom up again. “And I am not going to be asked to pull it either, and nobody is going to ask me, and if anybody in this county does ask me then they are asking a man who has nothing to pull it with and never had.”

The man who moves chairs came up the cart road and came past the wall and stopped at the bottom of the steps because there was nothing on the top of them to look at.

Aldis Rook moves the chairs for Orla Fennimore and keeps nothing and has no stone and has no seat and no board, has never once been asked what he did, and has now gone up that bank past the wall and past the bottom of the steps on four mornings.

“There is a thing on a trough,” he said.

“Where.”

“High side. Past the gravel and the mile, second house past the top of that flight of stone steps where the man with the coal goes up to his yard. It is about a mile and a hundred yards from here, and it is a stone trough at the front of that house with a pump on it that has not worked since before I was born, and there is a woman at that house whose name is Hess Narraway, and she washes in that trough every morning before it is light because there is nothing to wash in indoors, and I have moved chairs out of that house twice and never once said her name.”

“She is not yours to talk about.”

“She is not mine and that is why I can talk about her, because I have been up and down that high side with chairs for eleven years and I have never once asked that woman a single question about anything, and this week I am not going to start.”

He put his hands behind him.

“And I am going to say the thing I have come up to say and then I am going to walk back down the road. I am not going to ask her whether she took it. I am not going to ask her who put it there. And I am not going up that high side again to see whether it is still on that trough, because the day I go up twice to look is the day somebody says I had come back to have another look.”

“She has not been asked.”

“She has not been asked and nobody has to do the asking. Nobody put that down and I am not going to be the one who works out who.”

Hess Narraway is thirty-three, washes at that trough every morning before the light because there is no water indoors, and goes up the high side to work at the sixth hour, and has never been asked a question by any of about nine men on that landing and has said on about nine mornings that she will not answer one if it is asked.

That is not a figure of anybody and that is the whole of her saying.

Then the woman who reads the ninth run came up the road, entered the morning, and found nothing in it, and wrote nothing, and did not open the book.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years, holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, has one question she has decided to ask and has not asked it, and has now gone seven mornings without asking it.

“That is the ninety-seventh morning in front of anybody, and I entered it, and there was nothing in it, and I entered that too.”

“You still have not asked it, and I have stopped noticing that you have not.”

“I have not, and I am going to be exact this morning because on a morning with nothing on the stone everybody thinks they have room.” She set the book on the wall and did not open it. “A morning with no figure on it is not a clean morning. It is the worst morning there is for asking somebody anything, because anybody can ask on a morning like this and then everybody can say afterwards that the asking was all the morning deserved. I worked that out about six days ago and I have been standing on about nine empty mornings ever since, and not one of them was empty.”

“Then ask it tomorrow.”

“No. Tomorrow the water is back up and there will be a figure on that stone, and then nobody can say I asked it because there was nothing else to look at.” She picked the book up. “I have one question and it has been in my mouth since I came down here, and I am not going to spend it on a morning that makes it cheap, and I am not going to have anybody on this step help me save it either.”

Nobody helped her save it.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the eighth hour with no water on the steps and nothing to lean his own figures on, and said his figures out loud, and said the whole figure in his county out loud with it, and would not let it be made into anything.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and sixty-nine days old.

“Four hundred days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and sixty-nine days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-fourth is two hundred and fifty-four days old, which is nine hundred and fifty-four less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

Nobody said anything about four hundred.

“It is a whole figure,” he said, before anybody could be polite about it. “I have worked out that it is a whole figure and that is why the front of you have gone quiet, and I am saying it now so that nobody on this landing has to spend the morning being careful.”

“It is a figure of days. It is not one of mine, it does not mean anything, it is not a morning, it is not a year, and I am not going to stand here and be helped to find out what it is. If anybody on this step starts it I will go down this bank before they get to the end of the sentence and I will not come back up it until it is a different morning.”

“Ninety-four wrong things in ninety-four mornings,” he said, “and here is the ninety-fourth, and it is the first one this week I have not even tried to be liked for.”

Nobody told him it was a good morning for it.

He went down the bank at about the ninth hour. The man of fifty-four was not on that landing, because he had gone down at first light to get the gravel and had not come back up. The boy did not come up. The man of forty-three swept eighty-nine steps and did not touch the ninetieth, and the thing in the seam of the fortieth one was not pulled, not turned over, not weighed in the hand, not read aloud, not carried inside, not put back where it was, and not used, and nothing that had been put down anywhere was touched.

# Chapter 973: A Man Said Out Loud What Tomorrow On That Landing Was Going To Be Like And The Man With The Broom Took The Whole Morning Apart Over It And A Woman Who Saws Lengths At A Bench Said A Thing Can Be Looked At All Morning And Not Be Used

Second day of the hundred and sixty-first week. His seven hundred and twenty-third morning. Five hundred and seventy-three days after the settlement. The fever ninety-eight weeks and four days old. Five hundred and twenty-seven days since the division. Four hundred and seventy-three days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and nineteen days in this county of Kell.

Twelve inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, about seventy of those ninety steps under, and five days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures, and then took a morning apart that had been put together without him, and the woman who saws lengths at a bench said the thing that had made the work easier was a thing a person can look at all morning and never use.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the second day of the hundred and sixty-first week that a person who says what tomorrow is going to be like has already decided tomorrow.

“Twelve at the seventh hour, five days of the coming back, and about seventy of those ninety steps under, on the second day of the hundred and sixty-first week, and it is a Wednesday. Three figures.”

He did not set the broom down, which meant he had not finished.

“There is a man going to come up this bank in a minute and he is going to say what Thursday is going to be like, and I am telling this landing now, before he gets here, that I have heard him say it to himself twice on the cart road and I am not going to let it stand.”

The man who moves chairs looked up the road and then at the man with the broom and said nothing at all, because it was not his cart road.

Garrin Tolley is thirty-eight, has a child of nine, came back up a road he said he would not come up and is still not naming a day, and on the second day of the hundred and sixty-first week said out loud what the next morning on that landing was going to be like, and it took the man with the broom the rest of the morning to take it apart.

“It will be on the top step by the seventh hour,” he said. He had come up with nothing on his shoulder, having left a board at the bottom of the bank. “It came up about four inches a day all last week with nothing stopping it and it is going to be on the top step tomorrow and I have watched water do this since I was nine years old on a different bank with a different set of steps on it, and the top step on this bank is a good inch lower than the one I learned it on, so it will be there at the seventh.”

He said it the way a man says what he is having for his supper.

“Now sit down,” said the man with the broom, on the bottom step, in a voice nobody on that landing had heard from him.

Nobody sat down.

“No, I will sit down, because you are right that it is going to be on the top step and I am not going to stand here and argue with a man about water, because you are right about water.” He put the broom down flat. “Here is the whole of what is wrong with what you just said and it is four sentences and then I am going to sweep.”

He held up one finger and then put it down again before he used it.

“One. That is a figure of the bank and not of this morning. Two. Five days this morning is how long the water is going to sit where it is sitting, and four days is going to be how long it sits after that, and neither of those is Thursday, and Thursday is a day with sixteen inches standing on the top step and about sixty of those ninety steps under and nothing else at all. Three. You have just decided in advance what a morning will be like, and if you have, then that morning is going to be a thing you were right about instead of a morning you were on.”

He stopped.

“Four. And I am not saying a one of those things to be hard on you. I am saying it because I have given three figures off this stone every morning for nineteen years and I have never once let a figure of the bank become a figure of the morning, and the reason I have not is that a man who knows what a morning is going to be like does not turn up for it. He turns up to be right about it.”

Nothing on that landing made a sound for about four seconds.

“You are not going to say it again,” said the man with the broom.

“No.”

“Not this week and not on any other week, and I am not going to thank you for not saying it, and I am not going to say that you were right not to.”

Nobody said he had been right not to.

He went down the bank at about the ninth hour and did not come back up it that day, and he took the board with him off the bottom step, and nobody said anything about the board because a thing a man takes away again is not a thing that has been left.

Then the woman who saws lengths came up the cart road from about three hundred yards down it, past the low wall, and stood at the bottom of the steps with the saw handle under her arm, and said the thing out loud that she had worked out at her own bench and had been carrying up that road for two days.

Wenna Pyle is thirty-eight, who saws lengths and sets studs at a bench in an open-sided shed behind a wheelwright’s about three hundred yards down the cart road, and who carries her own cup out to that bench because there is no table in the shed, and who said on the second day of the hundred and sixty-first week that looking at a thing all morning is not using it.

“I am not going to say how far it is down that road again,” she said, “because I said it once and it is not a distance, it is a bench.”

Nobody asked her for it.

“I want to say a thing about the step and I have said it out loud to myself at a bench for two days and I am going to say it here because if I do not say it here one of you is going to have to say it here, and I would rather it was said by a person who has spent fourteen years finding out where the saw goes in a length of wood than by a person who has to.”

He did not move from the bottom step.

“Here is the working. I put a length on the bench and I look down it. I can tell you where the saw goes. I can tell you to a hair where it goes and it is not a guess, and I have not touched it, and in the morning it is still a piece of wood. Looking is not using.”

“Of course it is not,” said the man who moves chairs, from the back of the landing.

“It is not, and I have known that for fourteen years, and I have never once been asked about it, and nobody has ever put anything in my way on this landing to find out whether I know it.” She shifted the saw handle. “So here is what that comes to. The thing in that seam can be looked at all morning, and can be looked at by everybody on this landing, and can be looked at until every one of you could draw it, and none of you will have touched it. And it will be there at the end of the looking.”

“Because it is not going anywhere,” said the man with the broom.

“Because it is not going anywhere and I am not going to be thanked for saying so.” She looked up the steps. “And that is not a reason to pick it up. It is a reason to stop calling looking a kind of finding out. Everybody on this landing has been looking at that step for a fortnight and calling it something, and it is not a thing anybody has found out. It is a thing everybody has looked at.”

She put the saw handle round to the other arm.

“And it has not been touched. Not pulled, not turned over, not weighed in the hand, not read out loud, not carried inside, not put back where it was, and not used. Not this morning and not any morning of this fortnight, and I have said it once and I am not going to say it again this fortnight, and if you want it said again there is a man on this landing who says it out loud for a living.”

Nobody thanked her for the working and nobody told her she had got it right.

Then a woman was standing at the low wall at the other end of the landing, past the cart road, where she had been before the seventh hour with the wind off it, and a man who moves chairs turned round and saw her and did not go up the road.

Orla Fennimore is forty-four, keeps twenty-nine chairs at a house that is not in this county, was asked once what she did and has not been asked since, and was at the wall on the second day of the hundred and sixty-first week and is not going up those steps.

“There is a thing I have not been up that road with and I am not going to be,” said the man who moves chairs, to the landing and not to her. “I have had it in my mouth since Monday and I am going to leave it down here where it belongs and I would like it noticed that I noticed it and said so here instead of there.”

Nobody asked him what it was.

“Nobody on this step is to come up this road and tell me that a thing has been left on a landing that makes the work easier,” she said, from the wall. “Not you. Not one of you. And nobody is to tell me about it in the village either, and if I hear about it from somebody who was not asked to tell me then it is not mine and I do not want it.”

“Orla.”

“I said you are not to, Aldis, and you did it on Monday before you noticed and I have let it stand because there was nothing in it that would have done me any harm, and that is the last time there will be.” She did not come past the wall. “Nineteen years and I have kept out of this county’s business by not knowing what was on the other side of that wall, and it has cost me nothing, because I have never wanted to know, and I am not going to start wanting to know about a piece of iron in a man’s step.”

Nobody on that landing said she had kept out of it or that she had not.

“And I am not going to be thanked for standing at this wall. I want that said now, this morning, before anybody walks up this road and decides I stood here for something. There is nothing to thank me for, and I am not going to be thanked for saying there is nothing to thank me for, and if somebody does thank me for it that person has turned a thing I have kept out of into a thing I did.”

Nobody thanked her and nobody said they wished they had, and nobody on that landing went up that cart road after her.

She went back down it at the pace she had come up, and nothing was said on that road behind her back.

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour and asked in the same words at the same hour and was refused in the same way and was not thanked, and he had watched a man go down that bank in the middle of the morning carrying a board and not say where he was going.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and fifty-second mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is nine hundred and seventy-three less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and fifty-second. Nine hundred and seventy-three less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

“Nobody asked you for it. I am not going to tell you the morning before was different, because it was not.”

“No, and I have noticed that more of us on this landing have been asked nothing this week than have been asked every morning, and I counted it, because I am the man who counts and I could not help it, and I am not going to give anybody the figure.”

Nobody took his figure.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.” He said it in the words he uses. “And it is the second one of a week and I have said it twice this week and I am not going to make it a different question to see if it works.”

It was refused and it was not sent anywhere, and he was not told that refusal was the same as being owed one, and nobody on that landing said thank you to a man for the two hundred and fifty-second morning of it.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the eighth hour with twelve inches above his boots and gave his figures and the figure of the offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing, and stood in the middle of a landing where a man who moves chairs and a woman of forty-four had both said out loud in front of him that they were not going to carry a thing up a road.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and nineteen days. The cut across that palm is two hundred and eighty-eight days old.

“Four hundred and nineteen days in this county, and the cut across that palm is two hundred and eighty-eight days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-twenty-third is two hundred and seventy-three days old, which is nine hundred and seventy-three less seven hundred, and it is unanswered, and I have not asked it.”

He got to the bottom of the steps and stayed on the lowest one.

“A hundred and thirteen wrong things in a hundred and thirteen mornings, and here is the hundred and thirteenth, and it is about a man carrying a board.”

Nobody said anything about the board.

“A man of thirty-eight came up that bank and said what tomorrow was going to be like and a man of forty-three took it apart and did it in four sentences and I have not stopped hearing it.” 

He kept his hands behind his back. “And what I have noticed is that neither of them is the man who it is about, and that both of them spent the whole morning on it, and that the man who is thirty-eight went down that bank at the ninth hour and did not come back up it, and he was the only man on this landing today who left it better than he found it and nobody has said so to him.”

Nobody said so to him and nobody went after him with it.

“And I have got no part in it, and I would like that written down as a thing about me rather than as a thing about this landing. A hundred and thirteen mornings and I have not once been the man whose work a thing made easier, and I have not once been the man a sentence was about, and I went down that bank on the last morning of the last week and it did not make anything easier, and that was the correct thing to do and nobody has thanked me for it and nobody is going to.”

Nobody thanked him for saying so.

All ninety of them were swept, and the chair went round and the thing in the seam went round, and the chair has not been sat on and the thing has not been touched. Nothing has been pulled, or turned over, or weighed in a hand, or read aloud, or carried inside, or put back where it was, or used. The rule went into a third mouth that morning and it is still not called anything by anybody, and nobody on that landing has said what the thing in the fortieth step is for.

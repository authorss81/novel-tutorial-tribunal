# Chapter 258: The Day That Added Four

The first day of the fifty-ninth week added four to a board in a country where every day adds four, and the man of thirty-one was in a yard at the second light for the express purpose of finding out why that was a thing worth walking into, and he found out in about nine minutes that it was not a thing worth anything, and that a woman of forty-one had known that for twenty-five years and had never told anybody because there had never been anybody to tell.

He had been in the country nine days. The figure he had chalked at the foot of the board that morning was the same as the figure at the head of it and he had put the two about four inches apart and had not written a word, and the sixth day of his being here was the sixth morning of the third hand.

Nobody in the yard was saying anything about the number, and he worked out why before the last bell, and the reason was that the number had gone up by nothing and there is no such thing as a day a village notices for being quiet.

He went and asked the woman of forty-one about it at the noon bell and she was on the ladder doing the thing she does to the rope, and she came down off it before he had finished the sentence, which she does not do for anybody.

---

“A day of four,” she said. “Ay. That is a day of four.”

“What is a day of four.”

“It is a day of four,” she said, and then stopped, and came down the last two rungs and stood on the ground and looked at him for about as long as it takes to cross a yard.

“No. That is what I say when I do not want to answer and I have said it to about nine people in twenty-five years and I am not going to say it to you, and I am going to answer you properly and it will take the afternoon, and you should go and get the man of twenty-eight, because she will see it in about a minute and I would rather she were here when I say it than afterwards.”

---

They did the afternoon at the board, in the shade, with the cloth and a bit of lead and about four other people who came and went and were not asked to leave and were not invited to stay.

The board is a plank and it has been painted and unpainted so many times that the grain has come up through it in long pale streaks, and there are two hands in it, and the older is in the grain and the newer is not, and the newer is the more careful. A stroke takes about a second. Four strokes is a good hour. Thirty-one years of four strokes a day is a board about the size of a door and a man of sixty-one and a great deal of paint, and that is not a metaphor, it is the whole of the labour.

“Everything is up here,” she said, “and there is no way to know what any of it was for, and I have told you that and you have written it in your own hand in chalk at the foot of my board, which is a third thing now, and I will come to that.”

She put her thumb on the grain.

“The day that added four is in here, and it is nine years back, and it is the ninth week of a year I can tell you because I count the year on the fell and I was counting then as well as now. And I can find it. I can put my thumb on it. And what I cannot do — and this is the whole of it, so listen to the rest carefully — is tell you whether it is the same four that I am looking at.”

“Whose four is this?”

“Anybody's,” said Ottily Roke. “Every day in a year is four, and about two a day on top of it, and the four is never the same four twice in the sense that matters, and the two a day is different on every day of a year that anybody has ever lived through, and I can show you a week here and a week here and I can tell you they do not match, and I cannot tell you which of the two was the quiet one.”

She put the lead against the board at a week about a third of the way down.

“Here is a week nine years back. Nine, four, eleven, four, four, thirteen, four. And here is the same week eight years back, and it is four, four, four, six, four, four, two. And here is the same week nine years before that, and I have not worked it out because my father’s hand is in the grain and his six years are at the bottom and I cannot read the last four of his without a glass, and I am not going to do it on a wall in a yard in front of strangers.”

She stepped back.

“Six days of four in one week. Four days of four in another. And in a week that is not this week there are two that are not four, and one of them is eleven and one of them is thirteen, and I can tell you which strokes those are because the ones that are not four are longer and the man who made them put the downstroke first, and a man who is nearly out of the day puts the upstroke first, and he put the downstroke first on both of them and on a day of six as well.

“So I have a board with a day of four on it nine years back and a day of eleven on it eight years back and no way whatever on earth to say which of the two was the day the man came through with the timber and which of the two was the day nothing happened, and I have been looking at that board for twenty-five years, and it is not that I have never noticed. It is that I noticed in about the ninth year and I have been carrying it about since and I have never once been asked and I have never once told anybody and I would like that written down somewhere that is not a wall, and I have not got a wall that is mine.”

Then the flat white nothing put two plain lines in front of him in a yard, in about two years of his life spent in a place like this, and it did not say one word about the eleven people standing at a board, and it was not asked for and it came when it came.

**A COUNT OF FOUR ON A BOARD IN A COUNTRY WHERE EVERY DAY IS FOUR BELLS IS A COUNT OF NOTHING, AND A FIGURE THAT CANNOT BE TOLD APART FROM ANOTHER FIGURE IS NOT A RECORD. IT IS A NUMBER.**
**WHAT IS NOT DECIDED: WHETHER A BOUNDARY WALKED TWINCE IN THIRTY-ONE YEARS HAS BEEN WALKED, AND WHETHER THE MAN WHO WALKED IT THE FIRST TIME IS THE MAN WHO WALKED IT THE SECOND.**

He read it twice. It said nothing about a woman of forty-one and nothing about a wall and it took nothing out of him, and it was, as always, the correct shape of a thing and not a thing anybody could do anything about, and he has never found a way to make the shape of a thing do anything at all, and he has stopped expecting to.

---

“Now the other one,” said Ottily Roke, “and this is the reason I asked you to get the recordwright, and I am going to say it once and I am not going to be asked it a second time and if either of you tries I shall go up the ladder.”

She went up nine strokes of the board, which is a long way on a plank, and stopped about a third from the top, which is the far end of the older hand.

“In the twenty-second year of this board, which is nine years back, my father walked a boundary,” she said. “A strip between this village and Ellerby. About ninety acres of it, along a hedge, with two elms on it, and it is not a good hedge and it is not a straight line and it has never been anything but what it is. He walked it with four men and two of them are dead and one of them is a woman of about sixty who is still in Ellerby and who has never once been asked about it by anybody in this world, and I asked her myself, four years ago, on a market day, because I was thirty-seven and thought it was a thing I was entitled to.

“She was *twenty-one* when he walked it. And she said he did, and she said where it started and she said it was on a day with a name on it, and she does not know what the names are, and she said it was on a day she was *aunt* to somebody, which is a thing she remembers and I do not.

“And the man of thirty-eight says the gate has taken three pence a load for nineteen years and does nothing, and the gate has been there longer than that, and the gate is between this village and the lane, and the lane is not the strip. *The strip is not on the lane. The strip is a field boundary a mile off the lane, and a man with a cart has never once had a reason to go round it in his life.*”

She came down off the board.

“So here is what we have, and I am going to lay it out in the order of how sure it is, and I have never laid it out before and I have thought about it for about nine years and the thinking has got worse and not better.

“*We do not know whether the strip is a boundary.* If it is a boundary then about eleven families have been grazing it and paying nothing and using the water off it for nine years and nobody has ever told them to stop, and they are not thieves, they are eleven families and one of them has a son who goes to the well at Ellerby and everybody knows about it and nobody has ever said a word.

“If it is not a boundary, then it is ninety acres of common that a family of farmers have been told nothing about, and their fathers told them nothing, and the man who could have told them is four years dead, and the only record that they were told anything is a woman of forty-one’s hand, and her hand is not a witness, her hand is a count.”

---

The recordwright of twenty-eight stood with her arms folded and did not write anything, and after a while she said:

“No. And I will not do it, and I would like that understood as a thing I have done and not as a thing I have refrained from. I am a recordwright. I have written down worse things about better people than that in six years and I have done it because a room wanted a sentence and I was the one in the room. *You are asking me to write down that a man is dead and a line cannot be proved, and the second half of that sentence is the part that would be used.*”

“Then what is the sentence for?” said the man of thirty-one.

“It is for the eleven families,” said Sera Quill, “and it is for the woman of sixty in Ellerby who was somebody’s aunt that day, and it is for the four of us standing at a board, and it is not for a room, and those are the same eleven families whichever way I write it and I have never in my life found that to be a satisfying answer at four o’clock in the afternoon.”

“It is the correct answer,” said Ottily Roke, “and you are not going to get a better one out of her, and I have had a great deal of practice at people not getting a better one out of me.”

---

The last bell went and about nine of the eleven people at the board went to do whatever they did at the last bell, and the two who stayed were the man of thirty-one and the man of fifty-two, and the man of fifty-two was wiping his own lead on his own coat and said, out of nothing:

“That was four.”

“Yes,” said the man of thirty-one.

“It was four and I have seen a week of nothing before and I did not know what it was and I have known about four since I was nine. *A week of four is a week you do not talk about.* And that is a rule and it is not a document, and there is nobody who could say where it comes from, and it is the same rule in every village I have ever been in and I have been in nine counties, and I have never once seen it written down and I have never once asked a woman in a tower about it, and I am telling you that I am not asking her either, because she has had a day and she has had nine years of it and I am not going to be the tenth thing that comes up that wall.”

He put the lead in his pocket.

“Ask me again at the first bell tomorrow,” he said, “and if it is four again then you have got a week, and I will walk up the lane to the first house in it and I will ask him what day it was, and he will not know, and I will come back and put it in chalk and you can look at it.”

The figure on the board at the second light on the first day of the fifty-ninth week was fifty thousand four hundred and seventeen, and at the second light on the second day of that week it was fifty thousand four hundred and twenty-one, and the rings in between were four bells and nothing else at all.

And the next morning it went up thirteen, and three of the thirteen were a stranger at each of three gates, and the woman of forty-one counted them off on her fingers without looking at the board and did not say what any of them were.

# Chapter 262: A Second Day Of Four

Four bells and nothing else was the whole of the fifth day of the fifty-ninth week, and the board at Coldharrow says four on about most days in a year, and nobody in that yard said one word about it, and the man of thirty-one stood at the foot of a ladder at the second light and understood why they did not, which is that there is no such thing as a village noticing a day for being quiet, and it is the most expensive sentence he learned in this country and it took him twelve days and it cost him nothing to learn and that is the trouble with it.

The figure at the second light was sixty-three thousand five hundred and eighty-four.

Ottily Roke was at the board with the cloth and a bit of lead and she was not doing what she does.

“A day of four,” said the man of thirty-one.

“A day of four,” she agreed, and did not stop, and then she said, without turning round: “I have not slept much and I have not come here to talk about today. Talk about yesterday.”

---

So they went and found the three, which took about half an hour and is a thing nobody does in this county, because there is no form anywhere in eleven villages that says a person may be asked why a bell went.

The woman of thirty-five was called Marda Skell and she was not sorry about the hens and she was not sorry about the bell, and she said both of those things in the first four seconds and then said the rest.

“The hens are in and the gate bar is where it has always been and it is not shut because it has never been shut, and I am not going to be the one who starts shutting it after thirty-five years,” she said. “And I would ring again. I would ring and I would ring and I would go up that ladder myself if I could get up it, which I cannot, because of the knee.”

“Who was the boy.”

“My neighbour's. He is nine. He was shouting a friend in out of the field, and the friend was in the field, and I know that because the friend was in my barley, and I have not made anything of it and I am not going to.”

“And the man of about fifty.”

The man of about fifty was in a shed and came out and listened to the whole of it standing up, because he was not a man who sits down for a thing like this, and at the end of it he said:

“I do not know what I rang it for. I was in a state. I have been in a state four days in this week because there is nothing to do in this county, and I do not know where I was going when I put my hand on it, and I would do it again tomorrow, and I have thought about it since and I have not worked out anything, and you can go and put one on the board for it and that is the best it is going to get.”

---

So a woman of forty-one stood in a lane with a man of thirty-one and got four accounts, and the four accounts agreed with one another, which is a thing that almost never happens in this county, and agreed with nothing on the list, and one is a burial, and nobody in Coldharrow died on the fourth day of that week.

“That is the whole of it,” she said. “I have got three people telling me a true thing and not one of them telling me a thing that is on my list, and I have to write down one, and I am going to write down one, and there is going to be no column.”

“Would a column help?”

“No,” said Ottily Roke, “and I have known that for twenty-five years, and a man of thirty-one has now asked me it in a lane on the fifth day of his being in this country, and you asked me it on the first day of that week, and two men in five days have asked me the same question and one of them is going to be sorry and one of them is not, and I have not worked out which is which and I am not going to until somebody tells me.”

The man of fifty-two had been standing about nine feet off with his rule on a string round his neck and had not said anything, and now he said it, and what he said was not about the hens.

“I told a man of thirty-one on the first day of that week that I would come back and put it in chalk,” he said, “and I am not going to, and I have thought about it for a day and a half and here is the reason. There is no chalk in this country. There is a woman of forty-one with a board and a man of thirty-one with a bit of white chalk he has had in his pocket since the first morning of that week and five mornings of it on her board, and I have watched a number go onto a wall four times in eleven days in a hand nobody uses, and I said to a boy of seventeen on the second day of that week that this is a country where a thing that happens is a thing that gets said out loud, and I meant it, and I have spent eleven days finding out that the only way to keep a thing here is to write it on somebody else's board. *I am not the fifth hand on a board in a village I have been in eleven days.*”

Nobody said anything about that either, and about four people in the lane had heard it, and one of them was a woman of forty-one who looked at a man of fifty-two for about as long as it takes to cross a yard and then went and put four strokes on a plank.

---

And then the man of fifty-two did the other thing he had said he would do, which was to go and find out, because he has never in his life announced a thing he was going to do and then not done it, and about half of what is wrong with the account he gives of roads is that the men on the roads have all been doing it for nineteen years.

He went up the lane to the first house in it and asked a man of about forty at the door what day it was.

And the man of about forty said, without any trouble at all, that he did not know, and that he had not known for some years, and that he could tell her when the ewes came down off the fell, and that the woman of forty-one would know it if anybody would.

“Was that all right,” said Tarin Keel, on the way back down. “I want it recorded that I asked him a straight question and he gave me a straight answer and neither of us enjoyed it and he did not ask me why I wanted to know, and I did not tell him.”

“It was all right,” said the man of thirty-one, “and it is the same answer the woman of forty-one has given four men from counties with roads in it in twenty-five years, and you got it in about nine seconds standing at a door, and I have been in this country twelve days.”

He put the lead away and did not put anything on a wall.

---

And here is what happened next, and it is the finding of these ten days and it is not a new thing and it is the oldest thing in the country.

He had said, on the first day of that week, and had been taken up a ladder for it, that a quiet day and a day with nine strangers on it are four rings apart, and nobody had ever asked her which of them it was. And she had thought that was a hard thing. It was not the hardest thing in the lane. The hardest thing in the lane was standing there at the second light on the fifth day of that week and understanding that the four on the board that morning was the same four that would be on it if nine men had come over the gate with a cart of timber, and the same four that would be on it if a woman died, and the same four that would be on it if nothing whatever happened in two hundred and ten people.

Because she can write down what the rings were for when somebody tells her, and nobody has ever told her anything. The hens did not tell her. The boy did not tell her. The man in the shed told her that he did not know and that he would do it again, and she believed him, and it made no difference whatever.

“*A day of four and a day of nothing are the same number*,” she said. “That is what I have been telling you since the first day of that week and I thought I was telling you a hard thing. It is not a hard thing. It is the easiest thing in this county. We can prove how many times and we can prove before or after and we cannot prove when, and I have known that for twenty-five years and it has never once cost anybody anything, because it has never once mattered to anybody whether it was the hens or nothing.

“And it matters now, and not because of the hens. Because a number that anybody can move is not a record, and that is your sentence and I am giving it back to you because it is mine, and I have been saying it to a wall since I was sixteen without knowing there was a person to say it to.

“And here is the half of it that is worse. *A number that anybody can move is a number that has to be asked about, and there is exactly one person in this county you can ask, and for twenty-five years that has been a standing and I have thought of it as a comfort, and it is not a comfort any more, it is a door, and I have just heard somebody walk up to it.*”

She put the cloth over her shoulder.

“You have not asked me a question about it,” she said. “You have made a noise next to it. If you ask me I will answer and I will answer about last night, and what I will say is that four people told me three true things and none of them was on my list, and I will say it in a yard with a woman of forty-one in it, and in about nine weeks there will be a man in a room who can ask me whether I saw a man put a hand on a rope, and I will have to be able to say that I did not look.”

She looked at the board, at the four she had just put up, and at the figure at the head of it.

“Now go and find out what it costs to ask a question in this country,” she said, “and come back and tell me, because I have worked out that I would rather somebody else paid for it.”

# Chapter 266: A Village With A Different Arithmetic

There was a burial in Coldharrow on the second day of the sixtieth week, and that made the day a five, and the board had read sixty-three thousand six hundred and thirteen at the second light, and the man of thirty-one stood in the yard to watch what a five looks like when a village can account for every stroke of it. In sixteen days he had not once seen a day this county could account for, and he wanted to see one before he left.

It was a woman of about seventy-six who had lived in the ninth house and had not had a name that anybody outside four houses could be got to say, and Ottily Roke rang once at the second light and said the name out loud in the yard in the voice she uses, and then they buried her.

And about two hundred and ten people knew by the noon bell that the woman of the ninth house had died, and about two hundred and ten people knew who she was, and about forty of them came to the door, and there is not one form in eleven villages that says the ninth house's woman is dead and it was not a board that told them. It was a bell and a voice in a yard, and the voice said a name.

“Now that,” said the man of thirty-one, afterwards, to nobody, “is the one ring on that list that has a name attached to it in this country, and it is the only one, and it works, and it works because a woman of forty-one stands in a yard and shouts a name at about two hundred and ten people and everybody remembers it for about a week and then remembers it for years, and I want somebody to write down what I have just said in a place it can be produced from and I have been in this country sixteen days and I have not got one.”

---

Nine miles up the lane is Ellerby, and Ellerby is about a hundred and forty people, and it is on the eleven-village lane, and it is the second village he has been properly inside in this country, and the difference between it and Coldharrow was visible from about two hundred yards out and it was not the houses and it was not the hedges and it was not the ground coming up in eleven places in the first mile.

It was that there was no woman in the yard at the second light.

There is a bell at Ellerby. It is in a frame on two uprights in a field behind the last house and it has a rope on it that is about the thickness of a wrist, and there is no tower and no ladder and no bell-keeper, and it is rung by whoever is nearest, and the reason it is rung by whoever is nearest is a custom that everybody in Ellerby can describe and nobody in Ellerby can date, and the description is that when a thing needs ringing you ring it if you are the one who is nearest, and if you are not the one who is nearest you fetch the one who is.

“How old is it?”

“Nobody knows,” said a man of about thirty-five with a barrow. “Everybody has told me four hundred years, and my father said it and his father said it, and I have been asked it by a man with a rule on a string and I said four hundred and I would like it noticed that I said it because it is what you say.”

It is about four hundred years, and nobody in Ellerby has ever had a reason to find out, and there is no figure anywhere for it, and the man of thirty-one did not press, and the reason he did not press is that he has spent sixteen days learning that the only date in this country is the day the ewes come down off the fell, and a custom that old is not a date, it is a floor the whole village is standing on, and you do not go and measure a floor while somebody is standing on it.

And on the wall of the last house, where a person in the road can read it, there was a board.

It was a different board. It was a plank and it had been painted and unpainted so many times that the grain had come up through it in the same long pale streaks, and it was about the size of a door, and it was the fourth board in eleven villages, and it was the first one of the four that had no figure at the head of it.

It had names on it.

There were no numbers anywhere on it. There was no total, and no date, and no stroke for the clock, and no column, and the names went from the top down in the order they had happened, and about two hundred and nine of them, and about seventy of the two hundred and nine were people who live in four other villages, and every one of the rest was somebody in this one, and one of them was a man in a blue coat who came through in somebody's father's day and has not been in this village since and has not been struck out.

“A woman died,” said Ottily Roke’s voice in his head, and he said it out loud, which he should not have done and did not regret.

“Yes,” said the man of about thirty-five with the barrow. “You write the name of the dead. And a stranger comes through and you write the stranger’s name if you can get it and if you cannot you write what you have, and there is a name on that board of a man in a blue coat who came through in my father’s day and there is nobody in this village who has ever seen him and nobody has ever struck a name out.”

Nobody in Ellerby is paid ninepence a day. There is no wage in this at all, because there is no one whose job it is, because whoever is nearest does it, and if you are not nearest you fetch the one who is.

---

They got the copy at Coldharrow-edge, on the way up, and it took about four minutes and it is worth being exact about how, because the whole of what is true in the second half of this is in the four minutes.

Nell Callow had a drawer in her press. In it was a sheet in her own hand with four lines on it, and she had made that sheet four days before, in about nine minutes, while a man of fifty-one sat in her yard with a satchel on his arm and a dog at his feet, and nobody had asked her to and she had done it anyway, and the reason she had done it was that she copies for a living and a piece of paper that has been in a country for an hour is worth nothing and a piece of paper that has been in a country for a day is worth about as much, and there is no figure for that and she said so.

A woman of about forty with a barrow came into the yard at the second house at about the ninth hour, having walked the eleven miles up from Coldharrow with nothing in the barrow but a bundle of letters she was paid fourpence for, and said that a woman of about sixty at Ellerby wanted a piece of paper to hold. She would walk the eleven miles back after, and she said so before anybody asked her, because that is what the eleven miles are and she has been walking them for nineteen years.

“There is one,” said Nell Callow, and gave her the four lines, folded, and Delya Craven put it in her coat without looking at it and said the thing she had said in Coldharrow on the market day, which is that she had not read it and did not intend to.

---

And here is what the same four lines were worth in the second village, and it was not the opposite of what they were worth in the first, which is what the man of thirty-one had expected, and it was worse than the opposite.

They were worth nothing at all.

He had thought about it for nine miles and the thought had been the shape he had thought in a valley four hundred miles off, which was that a true document carried into a country with no document is a lever, and that Ellerby would be worse, and that the two villages together would be worse than either.

In Coldharrow the four lines were a lever. That is true and he had said it in a yard and the recordwright of twenty-eight had said it back at him and been right. A document speaks for itself, and the only public record in Coldharrow is a number, and a number cannot be asked about anything, and so a piece of paper with a height in it lands in a county of two hundred and ten people and there is nobody in it who can be got to say a word about it, and that is a lever with two hundred and ten people's names nowhere on it.

In Ellerby the four lines were worth nothing, and not for the opposite reason. Ellerby is not a place where documents do damage. Ellerby is a place where *a thing that happened is a thing somebody was standing near enough to see.*

A woman died and a name went on a wall. A stranger came through and a name went on a wall, or the best anybody could do about one. A boundary is walked and the men who walked it go on a wall. A debt is called in and the debtor and the creditor both go on a wall, and there is a rule about that which he was told by a man of about thirty-five with a barrow and did not understand at first: *you write both, so that one of them cannot be a name on a wall alone and stop there.*

Two hundred and nine names. No figures. Nobody struck out.

And the arithmetic of it, and he got there about a quarter of a mile short of Ellerby and worked the rest of the way in his own head and got it right:

*Every one of the two hundred and nine names is a person in this village who can be asked.* The seventy that are not in this village are in four other villages and they can be asked, because a name is an address. The one in the blue coat cannot be asked, and everybody in Ellerby knows that, and everybody in Ellerby has left it on the wall anyway, and the reason they left it on the wall is that a name with nobody at the end of it is still better than a number, and everybody in this village has understood that for about four hundred years without anybody ever putting it in a sentence.

And the four lines about a river four hundred miles off are worth nothing in Ellerby, because nobody in Ellerby was standing near enough to see them, and because they were read out in a yard nine miles off, and because a name only goes on that wall for a thing that happened *in the room where the wall is.*

Which is the finding, and it is the whole of the difference between the two villages, and it took a man of thirty-one nine miles and a quarter of one to see it and it can be put in one sentence:

*A number says how many times and names nobody, and a name says who and can be asked.* Coldharrow has the first and cannot produce the second. Ellerby has the second and does not know that is what it has.

And then, in the lane, half a mile short of the gate, the other half of it arrived and it was the half that made his hands go cold:

*And the four lines are worth something again the moment a person in Ellerby stands in a doorway and hears them read out.*

Which is the one thing he knows how to do, and the one thing he cannot make anybody do, and the only difference between the two villages is that in Ellerby the doing of it produces a name on a wall, and in Coldharrow it produces a stroke that can be added to by anybody with a hand on a rope.

---

Delya Craven put the four lines into Wenna Skell’s hand about half a mile short of the last house, and the woman of about sixty held a piece of paper for the first time in about nine years and did not open it and did not let go of it for about as long as it takes to read a line.

“I asked for a thing to hold,” she said, “and not a thing to read, and you have given me a thing to read, and I am going to be difficult about it for about a minute and then I am not going to be, because I am about sixty and I have not held anything that came from outside this parish in nine years and my hands have forgotten what paper is.”

“Nobody asked you to be difficult,” said the man of thirty-one.

“No,” she said. “Nobody ever does. That is what I have wanted for nine years and it is the cheapest thing anybody could give me and there is not one person in this county who has thought to give it to me and there is not one person in this county who is wrong about why.”

She opened it. She read it slowly, with her thumb under the line, in about as long as it takes to read anything aloud, and she got to the second line and stopped.

“This is a thing about a river,” she said.

“Yes.”

“There is no river in this county,” said Wenna Skell, “and there has not been one in my lifetime and my father would not have seen one either, and I have got a board on the outside wall of the last house with two hundred and nine names on it, and *every single one of those two hundred and nine is a person that somebody can go and ask.* And this piece of paper has been in this village for about a minute and there is nobody in this village named on it and nobody in this village standing near enough to have seen it, and so it is worth exactly nothing, and I have never in my life been able to say that about a thing that was handed to me.”

The recordwright of twenty-eight, who had walked nine miles behind two men in the last hour of the light and had said nothing for eight of them, said the thing that says it about herself.

“Then you have the thing I have been trying to buy for six years with paper and a shilling,” she said, “and it is not a document, and you cannot produce it in a room, and you do not need to be able to produce it, and a recordwright of twenty-eight has walked nine miles in the last hour of a day to be told this by a woman of about sixty holding a piece of paper she did not ask for.”

She looked at the man of thirty-one.

“And now go and do your trick in a doorway,” she said, “because you are going to, and I have watched you for six days, and the whole of what you do is put a thing in front of a person, and there is a person standing in a doorway in front of you and she is the only one in this county you can put anything in front of that comes back with an answer. And I am telling you now, before you do it, that I think it is a waste, and I am telling you because you did not ask me, and I would like both of those in a place it can be got out of, and there is about one of those in this county and I have not been in it.”

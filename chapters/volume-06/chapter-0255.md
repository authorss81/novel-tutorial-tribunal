# Chapter 255: Nine Words From A Recordwright

The fifth day of the fifty-eighth week was the day Coldharrow has a market, and it is worth saying first that it does not have a market day, because a market needs a day and Coldharrow does not have the kind of day a market needs. What it has is the third day of a month of a year counted from the day the ewes come down off the fell, and that moves by about three weeks in any year, and it is the only date in this country that a stranger can be taught in a morning.

Nine loads came over the lane and stopped at the gate, which is a post with a bar across it that is not shut, and a woman of about fifty stood at the post with a stick and a board and counted them, and the count is a count of loads and not of people, and the three of them watched her do it for about eleven minutes and learned the whole of the local fiscal system in eleven minutes.

Threepence a load. A stroke of the board and a word to the man and nothing kept.

And the ninth load was a cart with a handcart's iron under it and a man of thirty-eight on the shafts, and he came over the top of the ridge late, and the woman at the post said his name before he had shut his mouth about it, and he said the number of the loads that had come over before him without being asked, which is a habit and not a boast, and the woman at the post said his number was wrong by one in a month and he argued for about a minute and she was right.

That is the whole of the trade of Coldharrow on a market day and it takes about eleven minutes and a woman of fifty and a stick.

---

They did not get to the argument until the last bell and it went on after the last bell, which is the only time in this country that anything goes on after the last bell, and about four people in the village noticed that the three of them were still in a yard and about nine did not.

The nine words had been said at the end of a garden the previous afternoon and had been standing in the yard for about a day and a half, and the man of thirty-one had not asked her to say them again, and she was not going to say them again, and both of those were correct.

What she said instead, at the last bell, sitting on the end cart with her boots up, was about two hundred words, and about nine of them were the whole of it.

“A count that leaves the board is a count somebody can use,” she said. “That is what I said and I am not going to improve on it for anybody, and I have said it in a book, in a room, twice, to men who had more paper than I have ever seen in one place, and I am a recordwright, and a recordwright is a person whose whole trade is a count that leaves the board, and I am telling you what I know.

“And before you answer it, you are going to tell me that the board is one woman with a cloth and no second one, and I know that, and I have known it since I was about nineteen, and it is the best argument anybody could make and I made it to myself first and I lost to it.”

“Where is your claim?” said the man of thirty-one.

Sera Quill looked up at him.

“Say it again.”

“Your family’s. Six years. I have been in the same country as you for a week and I have not asked and I am asking because I am about to do the thing you have just told me not to do and I would rather do it having asked.”

“It is unfiled,” she said. “It has been unfiled for six years and it is going to be unfiled in six weeks and nobody knows about it but me and one clerk who could not help me and has since died. *And that is all you get, and you get it because you asked in front of a witness, and I would have said nothing at all if you had asked me in a lane.*”

“All right,” he said, and did not ask anything else about it, and did not look at the recordwright of twenty-eight for about a minute afterwards, which she noticed and has not mentioned.

---

“Here is what is on your side and there is a great deal of it, and I have been saying it to myself for two days and I am tired of it,” he said. “In a valley four hundred miles off a bench of four’s good name is a public thing and it is free, and I spent it in a room in about four minutes to open a page that had four lines under it about a place none of them had ever been. And I did it for nothing, and nobody thanked me, and it is the single piece of work I have done since I came to this world that I would do again exactly.

“The second thing on my side is that there is a woman of forty-one in that village who is the only person in two hundred and ten who can be asked anything, and she is paid ninepence a day for a hundred and forty days, and that is five pounds and five shillings a year, and if she is hit by a cart tomorrow the whole of the record of eleven villages in this stretch stops on the day she stops.

“I have watched that happen before with a different woman and a different record and I got there about nine weeks too late and I have not been right about it since. And I am not asking you to admire that. I am telling you what I am doing in your country, which is standing at a wall trying to work out how to stop it happening twice.”

“Then you are doing it in my country,” said Sera Quill, “and you have been doing it in my country for a week and you have not asked me about it once.”

“That is fair.”

“It is entirely fair,” she said, “and I am going to be unkind about it now, and you asked me to be, about nine seconds ago, when you asked about the claim.”

---

“Then be unkind.”

“All right. *A place that cannot be dated is a place that cannot be named in a room.*”

She said it and then stopped, and the man of thirty-one waited, and she had not finished, and she knew she had not finished, and the man of fifty-two at the end cart with the rule round his neck waited as well because he has counted carts on roads for nineteen years and he can tell when a person has said the first half of a thing.

“It is not that you cannot prove a boundary here,” she said. “It is that there is a form in a building about nine days from this field with a box on it, and I have copied the form, and the box is for a date, and the date has to be a Shelf date, and there is nothing on that form, and there is nothing in any form in that building, that will take fifty thousand three hundred and ninety-seven.

“So a village in the March is not a place that cannot be argued about. *It is a place that has no name in the room where arguing happens.* A bench that is sent nothing does not write nothing down. It writes down that it was sent nothing, and ‘it was sent nothing’ is a sentence about a place and not about a person, and there is nobody inside the sentence. There is no party. There is no person to answer it, because to answer it you have to have been in it.

“And a place with no party is a place that can be done to.”

The man of fifty-two took the rule off his neck and looked at it and put it back.

“That is the hole in the bell,” he said, after a while. “I have been in this country nine days and I have been counting the gaps between the loads and I have not counted them because there is no gap. I did not think of it as a hole because you cannot fall out of a bell.”

“You cannot fall out of a bell,” said the man of thirty-one, “and nobody in a room nine days from this field can put a hand on this village, and I have spent about a month in another valley learning that a person who cannot be put a hand on is not free, and the woman of thirty-eight in that valley said it before she was asked and I did not have it out of her and I have not had it out of anybody since.”

He looked at his hands, which are not a young man's hands, and were not a young man's hands eight weeks ago either.

“So I am not going to argue with you about the board,” he said. “I am going to say what I am going to do, out loud, in a yard, and you are going to hear it, and you are not going to be able to unhear it, and I am not going to pretend afterwards that I had not said it because I have spent the last two days not saying it and I have got about as far as not saying it as anybody is going to get.

“I am going to go and find out what a date costs in this country, and I am going to pay it, and I am going to have somebody else pay it too so that it is not me, and then I am going to have two people in this county who can put a name on it in a room.

“And when there are two of us, we are going to be able to be argued with, and you are the one who is going to be worst at by that, and I would like that in your own mouth now rather than in a letter in a month.”

Sera Quill was quiet for long enough that a man of about thirty came through the yard and said goodnight to the woman at the post and was answered.

“Two is worse than one,” she said. “You know that two is worse than one. You have known it since about the fourth volume of your own life and you have never once done the sum on it out loud in a yard, and you have just asked me to do it for you, and I have done it, and it took nine words and four minutes and I am going to have said the nine words twice now and I only said them once this morning in a garden, and I would like that noticed.”

---

And that was where the fifth day of the fifty-eighth week ended, and the man of thirty-one did not write any of it down.

He had a book in a bag with two halves in it and he had not opened the second half since a valley four hundred miles off and he was not going to open it in a yard in a country whose weeks he did not know, and the other reason he did not write it down was the better one, and it was a thing a man of fifty-two said to him in a cart yard a month ago with a lead in his hand and ochre dust on the coat, and it is nine words and it is not a rule and it is a fact about a man.

He had learned once what happens to a thing you write in your own hand to be careful with, and where it goes, and who is holding it.

He did not write it down.

And a woman of twenty-nine, eleven miles off, at a press in a village that keeps Shelf weeks and is not in the March, made about forty of these a year, and sold every one of them, and had done it for six years.

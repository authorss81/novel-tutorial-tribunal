# Chapter 280: A Boy's Book And A Hundred And Forty-One Names

The second day of the sixty-second week put four on a board in Coldharrow and nothing whatever on top of them, and the figure at the head of it at the second light was sixty-three thousand seven hundred and six, and it was the thirtieth day of a man of thirty-one in this country, and the clerk of forty-four went down the lane on a cart at about the ninth hour to a town that keeps weeks he has never learned and would be four hundred miles off in ten days, and he was not thanked and did not ask to be and had not given anybody anything to thank him for except a date on a hand four hundred years old.

He had asked one question in the gap between the two weeks, and Tarin Keel had asked it for him, and the answer had come back *yes* on a morning with nothing on it at all, and neither of them had made anything of it, and it is on a sheet in a press in a village eleven miles off and it is not in this country.

And there are four things in this county that have been sitting where he and three other people put them since the sixth day of the sixtieth week, and they are worth about nine seconds each, and nobody in two hundred and ten people has done anything about any of them: there is a third hand in white chalk at the foot of the board on the tower wall, and it has not been added to in twenty-nine days; there is a figure at the foot of a sheet in this county that a woman of twenty-nine put there because a man of thirty-one had a figure and no day and gave her one, and nobody has said whose hand it is in and about nine people in a yard know; there are two names on one thing in a press eleven miles off and two names on one thing is not two witnesses, and no room in four hundred miles has asked about either; and there is a strip of ninety acres between this gate and a gate nine miles off that eleven families are grazing and paying nothing for and have never once been told about.

---

The tower at Nettlebed has eleven worn steps in it and a doorway about four feet high at the top, and a boy of seventeen went up them at about the ninth hour of the second day of the sixty-second week with a satchel and a stub of pencil behind his ear and a man of thirty-one behind him who had agreed the evening before to come and not to help and had come and was not helping.

There were about eleven people at the foot of it. Nobody had sent for them and there was no form for it and there was no vote. A woman of fifty-one was there because it is her mother's mother's name in the column and she comes to look at it about once a week and has never told anybody she does that. A man of thirty-four from a county four hundred miles off was there because he was still in the county and had nowhere in particular to be on a day of four. A man of about thirty-one with a hurdle in his hand was there because it is his door.

Bram Orren went up the eleven steps and did not go inside. He stood in the four-foot doorway with his satchel against the frame and looked at the inside of the door for about a minute and a half, and then he took the book out, and opened it, and did not write in it.

He read it for about four minutes. He read it the way a man reads a thing he is going to have to do something with, which is from the top down and then from the top down again, and about nine people at the foot of the stair could tell that he was not going to get what he came for, and nobody said anything, and it took about nine minutes more before he said it.

“This is the wrong shape,” said Bram Orren.

---

And he said what he meant by it, and he said it to the man of thirty-one and not to the stair, and it took him about two minutes and it is the whole of the thirtieth day and there is not one word of it that anybody could use.

“My book has got two places in it,” he said. “One is what crossed, and one is what did not cross, and everything that has ever happened in this county since I was twelve is in one or the other of them, and I have never in five years had a third place and I have never needed one, and it has never once occurred to me that there could be a third place, and there is one and I can see it and I do not know what it is for.

“The hundred and forty-one names on that door are in an order. It is a real order. I am not saying it is not. *It is the order somebody decided, and my book is the order things happened in, and those are two different orders and they are not the same and I do not have a way of reading one with the other.*”

“Go on.”

“Here is what I cannot do.” He turned the book round and put it against the door frame with the page open, which is a thing he does, and nobody in this country has ever told him to. “I can say that one of these came before that one. That is the finest thing this book can ever say about any wall in this county. And there are four hundred years between them, and I cannot put a day on one of them, and I cannot put a day on any of them, and the gap between them is not a gap I can measure, it is a gap somebody decided about four hundred years ago to leave.

“And I could put a length on it. I could stand here and say that this one is earlier than that one and that a man of forty-four in a stair has said that hand is about four hundred years old, and I could put those two facts on a page side by side and I would have got the whole of it exactly right and it would be worth nothing at all, because *the finest thing this book can say about that door is that one of these came before that one, and not one of them is a day that I can name, and there is not one instrument in eleven villages that could turn that into a day, and I have looked, and I have had five years.*”

He shut the book.

“So I am not going to write it down,” he said. “I have got nothing to write down. I have got one thing and it is a thing my book was not built to say, and if I write it down it will be a false thing and it will be in a book that has never had a false thing in it, and I would rather have an empty page.”

---

And the man of thirty-one was in the doorway of a tower in a village of about ninety people and had a thing to say and did not say it, and what he could not say is worth putting down because it is the thirtieth day of the whole of it and it is a fact about a tower and not about a boy.

There were about eleven people at the foot of that stair who could read a plank on a door, and there were about nine names in eleven villages who could be asked about anything they had ever done, and there was a man standing in a doorway who had been in this county for thirty days and who had not once been asked to read anything in it, because he has never once asked anybody to read anything *aloud* in a yard — he asks for a sheet, in the way he asks for everything, and the sheet gets read out, and about four people in Coldharrow had noticed the asking over four weeks and had stopped noticing it, and that was the whole of it, and it had never once come up in his own head as a fact about him, and nobody in that tower thought of it, because nobody in that tower had any reason to think of it, and he stood there and let it not come up.

*If I had said, in that doorway, four hours ago, read it out, and the two hundred years of a woman's wage in that column is a figure I gave a woman of twenty-nine in a press — and if she had put that on the foot of a sheet the way she put my number there — then this plank would be a document.*

And he did not say it, and about four people at the foot of the stair who were watching his face saw nothing at all happen on it, and the leg was the leg and the thing he was carrying was off nothing, and he had come into this country on the first day of the fifty-eighth week looking for a thing to spend and had not found one and had found a door instead and it was on the inside of a door and it was four hundred years old and it was not his and there was no form in eleven villages in which it could ever be made his, and he was going to walk down eleven steps and go and do the rest of his day.

---

The boy of seventeen went down first, and he put his book away, and he said it in the yard at the foot of the tower to about eleven people and he said it plainly and it was not for them.

“I am not going to be hurried about this,” said Bram Orren. “I have got five years of a book and I have never lost a day of it and I am not going to spoil five years over one thing I have not understood. I have got a wall to read at Ellerby and I have got a line to run before the rains and I have got a thing at a stile I did not ring for on the fourth day of that week and I did not know that was a thing that had to be rung for, and I am going to go and do my day, and if I have got anything by the time the ewes come down off the fell I will have got it, and if I have not then I have not, and you can ask me about it in six weeks and I will tell you that I have not got it, and that is a proper answer and it is the first proper answer anybody in this country has got out of a person in thirty days and you can put it on the list.”

And the man of fifty-one from Trope said the thing that everybody in that yard had been waiting about nine minutes for, and she said it to the boy and not to the man of thirty-one, and she had not been asked and did not say she wanted to be.

“You have just told us that the only book in this county that can put things in an order cannot read a door that a hundred and forty-one people kept in an order for four hundred years,” she said. “I have been in this country all my life and I have never once been in a yard where a person of seventeen said a thing that was a fact about this county and not about himself, and I am not going to thank you for it and I am not going to tell you what it is for, and I am going to go and do my day, and there is a gate bar at my house that has not been shut since before I was born and it is not going to be shut this week either, and that is all I have got.”

---

So the man of thirty-one went down the eleven steps and out of a village of about ninety people on the second day of the sixty-second week, and about eleven people stood in a yard and did not follow him, and he walked four miles and a half back toward the lane with a leg that was no better than it had been on the first day and a thing in his bag that was off nothing, and he did not open the second half of a book that had been shut since a valley four hundred miles back, and he had been in this country thirty days and he had a figure in chalk on a wall that he was not going to wipe off and a figure in pencil on a sheet that he was not going to take back and no name on anything and no good name of anybody's to spend and no date, and there was no column on any board in this county that had his hand in it, and there never had been one, and there was not going to be one.

And the woman of forty-one put four strokes on a plank at the second light on the morning after that, and there was nothing whatever on top of them, and she left it at that, and at the second light on the morning of the first day of the sixty-second week the figure at the head of the board on the wall of the tower at Coldharrow was sixty-three thousand seven hundred and ten.

And on the first morning of the first day of a man's being in this country, twenty-nine mornings back, the same board said sixty-three thousand five hundred and ten, in the newer of two hands, in a number that was a count of rings and was not a count of anything, and the difference between the two figures is exactly two hundred.

Two hundred is a round number, and it is four bells a day for fifty days, and it is a hundred and forty-one names on a door in a village nine miles off against two hundred and nine on a wall in a village nine miles up the lane, and sixty-eight of the difference between those two is nobody's business and has never been anybody's business and is not a mistake in anything, and it is a hundred and fifty-five bells and three pounds fifteen shillings, and it is in a plank in two hands and there is no column for it and no heading over it and no name against it and no year at the top of it and there never was one.

Nobody in two hundred and ten people chose it. Nobody in two hundred and ten people has ever known that it was coming, and about four people in Coldharrow have a figure in their heads that they could not tell you where they got, and one of the four is a woman of forty-one who will be asked about it on some ordinary morning in about a month by a man in a room, and she will not be able to say when any of it began, and there is nothing in this country that could ever answer her, and the gate at the end of the lane is a post with a bar across it that is not shut and is not going to be shut, and the last bell went, and the day was a day of four, and it was the ordinary one.

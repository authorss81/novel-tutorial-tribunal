# Chapter 269: The Figure He Wrote Because He Had Nothing Else

Nineteen days after he came into this country he was eleven miles off the lane by the ninth hour in a press in a village that is not in the March. The board at Coldharrow had read sixty-three thousand six hundred and thirty-eight at the second light and would read sixty-three thousand six hundred and forty-two at the second light the next morning, and the four in between were a day that added nothing at all on top of the clock. About half the days he had been here are like that. The board says four on them the same as on any other, and there is no way of telling which four, and he has not tried to find out.

He had a sheet of paper on the bench. It was a copy and it was in Nell Callow's hand and she was making the second of it, because a copy is a thing that can be produced and a single sheet is a thing that can be lost, and a woman of twenty-nine who sells about forty dates a year knows that in her bones and does not need to be told it by a man of thirty-one.

They had been at it about a quarter of an hour and she had not asked him one question, which is the single most useful thing he has found in this county, and about eleven minutes in she said, without looking up:

“I have to mark it.”

“Mark it.”

“With what it is a copy of. That is the whole of a copy, that is what a copy is, and I have been making them for six years and I have never made one that a man could not tell was a copy, because a copy that cannot be told is not a copy, it is a second thing, and a second thing is a lie about there being two.”

“Mark it,” he said.

“The number of the thing you are copying,” said Nell Callow, “and the day of it. Both. That is what I need at the foot of this. I have the two hundred words and I have the four lines and I have got nothing to put under them that says where any of it came from, and if I cannot put that, then this is a thing that a person can hold and nothing else, and there is a great many of those in this county and I do not make them for nothing.”

He had a figure and he did not have a day.

He had heard her say it out loud on the seventh day of the fifty-ninth week in a lane, to about eleven people: a date is a thing for a day, and she can tell you the fell year and she can tell you the count and she cannot tell you the day, and that is not a thing she has failed at, it is a thing there is no instrument in this country for. And he had stood in that lane on the fifth day of the fifty-ninth week and been sent to find out what asking a question costs in this country, and here he was, eleven miles off, in a press, with a woman of twenty-nine holding a pen.

“We do not have the day,” he said.

“No,” said Nell Callow, and did not look up. “I know we do not have the day. I have been selling dates into that county for six years and there has not been a single day in it that I could have put at the foot of anything. You do not have to tell me the day. I am telling you I need one.”

“There is a woman of about sixty nine miles up this lane who has said in her own mouth that a date is a thing for a day, and there is a woman of forty-one in a village in this county who is the only person anybody can ask and who has said in her own mouth that she cannot tell you the day of a thing in her own hand, and there is a man of about sixty-three who sells bells and a boy of seventeen with a book that has got a hole in it where a day should be and who will not show it to anybody, and I have asked all four of them in the last five days and I am telling you that the county cannot do it and I have stopped hoping it will.”

“That is four people and a bell,” said Nell Callow, “and I am not asking you for a day, and I have told you I cannot use one. Put the number or do not pay me.”

---

And he had a number. He had had a number since the first morning of his being in this country and it had been sitting in a pocket of his mind in the same place the whole eighteen days like a thing he had picked up and put down and picked up again, and he had never once wanted it, and he wanted it now because a woman of twenty-nine had asked him for a figure and had no other word for what she wanted.

*The only figure he has ever put into this country in his own hand is the one at the foot of a board on a tower wall, in white chalk, written on his first morning, and it is still there, because nobody has told him to wipe it and he has not offered, and there are three hands in that board now and two of them are a family.*

He said it out loud, which is the part worth having, because he had four seconds in which he could have given a different number, and the four seconds were real, and a day of four is a day on which a man has four seconds of nothing in it and no reason at all to reach for anything.

“Sixty-three thousand five hundred and ten,” he said, and then, because he had been in this world long enough to know that a number out of a mouth is worth nothing without a day, “and that is the figure at the head of the board at the second light on the first morning of my being in this country, and it is in chalk at the foot of it in a hand nobody in this county uses, and it is eighteen days stale and there is nobody here who can tell you what it is a number of, and neither can I, and I have not asked, and I am giving you it because you asked me for a number and I have got one.”

And then he stopped, in the way a man stops when he has heard himself.

“It is mine,” he said. “That is why I am giving you that one. There are two numbers I could have said. There is the one on the wall this morning, which is yesterday's by the time you ink this, and there is the one in the chalk, and the one in the chalk is the only figure in this country that anybody could put a name to without being told whose it is. *You have just asked me for a number and I have given you one with my hand in it, and a number with a hand in it is a person, and a person is what this document spent two hundred words and a woman of twenty-nine not putting a day on it to avoid.*”

He did not take it back. That is the finding, and it is in his own words about four minutes later, on the road, in the cold, on his own, and he wrote it on a leaf torn out of a book and gave it to nobody:

*He did not take it back for the same reason he did not offer to wipe the chalk off on the first morning, which is that a man who takes a thing off a board in front of two hundred and ten people has admitted he had no business putting it there, and the chalk has been standing eighteen days and everybody in this village has got used to a third hand, and taking it away now is a different act and a worse one, and the only thing that would have made it not a different act is if I had never done it, and I had, and I did it in four minutes on a first morning in a country where nobody had asked me anything.*

*And he gave it because a woman asked him for a figure and he had nothing else to give. That is the whole of what he is. He came into this country to find a thing to spend and the thing he found was a number he had put on somebody else's wall for nothing.*

---

They went on at it for another half an hour, and she asked no more questions, and he asked for the figure to be read back.

That is the third time in eighteen days and it has become a thing that people in two counties have stopped remarking on, and the woman of twenty-nine read the number back off the bottom of the sheet in a flat voice with a pen in her hand, and neither of them remarked on the asking, and about four people in a press in Coldharrow-edge did not remark on it because they do not know there is anything to remark on, and the finding is the finding and it will be a finding when somebody writes it down.

“Where do you put a figure on a thing like that?” he asked, and she answered it without slowing down.

“At the foot,” said Nell Callow. “Under the last line, on the left. That is where a figure goes on a thing like that and I have been putting them there for six years and there is nothing else for it to go under, and if it went anywhere else it would be a thing somebody could say you had put in a wrong place on purpose, and I would not want that said about my press.”

And she put it at the foot of the four lines, on the left, under the last line, in pencil, in a hand that was not hers and was not the hand of anybody in eleven villages, and the figure was sixty-three thousand five hundred and ten, and it was eighteen days out of date, and there was no day anywhere on the sheet.

---

They walked the eleven miles back and neither of them said anything for about six of them, and the woman who had been in this county nine years and was on no paper and had not been thanked for anything since the second day of the fifty-ninth week said the thing she had said she would say on that day, in a lane, in about forty words, in her own mouth, and it was not an apology.

“I said on the second day of the fifty-ninth week that I would say the other thing later,” said Sable Venn, “and I have been carrying it for two days and it has not got smaller, so here it is and it is four parts and there is no news in it.

“Nine years I have put a piece of paper where somebody was going. Four hundred of them. Eleven of them are walls. And I have never once known what a paper does when it gets where it is going, because I do not read records and I never have and that was the whole of my method and it worked four hundred times.

“*And it worked four hundred times because of what I would not know and not because of what I could not do.* There is a difference and I have never once thought about it and I have thought about almost nothing else for nine years. A man of thirty-one came into a yard in this county about ten days back and asked me what happens to a piece of paper and I could not tell him, because I do not read, and I told him I do not read, and he said *and yet*, and the and yet has been in my head for ten days.

“This week a woman of about sixty read four lines out loud, twice, in a lane half a mile short of that village and then in a kitchen in it, both times because I asked her, having spent nine years not asking anybody anything, and about four people in that lane heard her say the third line wrong the first time and say it right the second time, and *she is not a document, and I know the difference, and I did not know I knew the difference until I watched it.* So that is the whole of what nine years was for. I was not careful. I was blind, and it was a good arrangement, and a man of thirty-one has just taken it apart in a kitchen with a pen, and I am not going to blame him for it, because he is not the one who built it. I am going to say it in every county I have ever been in, and it wants saying in a place a person can be called to account in, and there is not one of those in this county that I have found. There is a woman of twenty-nine eleven miles off who will not write it down. There is a woman of forty-one on a wall who cannot hold a thing that is not a board.”

He did not say that it was a good thing to have taken apart, and he did not say it was a bad thing, and he said the only thing he had, which was the sentence he had said on a hedge in this country on the fourth day of the fifty-eighth week to a boy of seventeen who was right.

“I have got no answer to that,” he said, “and a man with no answer has no business making an argument about it, and you are better at it than I am and you have been at it for nine years and I have been at it for eighteen days, and I have taken it apart in an afternoon because it was in my way, and that is what I am.”

---

Nobody thanked anybody that day. The sheet went down the lane on a barrow at about the tenth hour, and the woman who took it has carried other people's paper between these villages for nineteen years and has never once opened it, and she was told to take it down the lane and not where, and she did not ask, and she has said out loud, unprompted, that she has not read it.

The figure at the foot of it was in pencil and it was eighteen days out of date and it was in a hand that about nine people in Coldharrow would know at a glance and could not have told you why they knew it.

And it was going back into a village of about two hundred and ten people, where there is a tower with a ladder and a rope in it and a woman of forty-one who is the only person there who reads, and she would read it out at the board in the morning because that is what happens at a board, and nobody had asked her to and nobody was going to.

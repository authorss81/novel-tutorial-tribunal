# Chapter 291: A Document That Can Be Produced

What the woman of forty-one wrote on the plank on the sixth day of the sixty-third week, at the second light, was sixty-three thousand seven hundred and seventy-eight, and it was four bells and nothing whatever on top of them, and she wiped the cloth over it and went to do her day.

That was the ordinary number and there have been about two hundred and thirty of them in the twenty-five years she has kept that board, and it was the forty-first morning of a man of thirty-one in this country, and he was in the yard at Coldharrow at about the third hour with a cart's side to sit a thing on and nothing at all to do with the rest of it.

---

The woman of forty-one came in at the Stennick end of the county on the evening before, on foot, having come up a bad road that goes out of the sixth house in Coldharrow and past nine farms and comes in at a place where there is a well and a roof that fell in, and then gone on for the better part of a day, and a person who comes in at that end of a county does not pass a bar, because there is no bar on that road and there never has been.

Nobody had sent for her. That is worth saying plainly, because it is the fourth time in six weeks that this county has had a person walk into it from somewhere else and it is the first time that anybody has come to it with a paper.

She was forty-one. She had a coat and a bundle and about four hours of daylight left out of her and she had come to the bar at about the second hour and had asked the woman of about fifty, unasked by anybody, a question that took about four seconds to answer and about four minutes to recover from.

“I have come to find out,” she said, “whether a piece of paper can unmake a village.”

The woman of about fifty has a board under her left arm and a word at the top of it, four letters and a stroke, and it is the only head a board in eleven villages carries, and it is why nobody has ever been able to put a second question to her in twenty-two years.

She looked at the bundle, and she did not ask what was in it, and about four people in that yard noticed that she did not ask what was in it.

“No,” she said. “It cannot. A piece of paper is a thing and a village is not a thing, and I have got a board under my arm with a word at the top of it that tells me what every stroke on it is for, and I have not got anybody to ask me a second question about any of them, and I have wanted somebody to ask me a second question for about four years.”

And then she said the other half of it, and she said it to the yard and not to the stranger, which is the only way she says anything.

“I would like it noticed that I have said no to a date about nine hundred times in twenty-two years and that this is the first time anybody has ever come up this lane to ask me whether a thing is possible.”

---

The bundle had come off a carrier's back and had been put on the cart's side in the yard because a bundle is not a person and the woman of about fifty has never counted a carrier in her life.

It was about the size of a folded shirt and it was corded, and the cord had been knotted by somebody in a hurry and knotted twice.

There were three people in the yard when it was opened: a recordwright of twenty-eight who had come eleven miles and been there about a half an hour, a man of fifty-two with a bit of lead in his pocket who could not read a single stroke of it, and a man of thirty-one who had not been asked to be in that yard and had been in about nine of them a week and had not spoken in any.

There was also a woman of forty-one standing nine feet off with her hands in her sleeves.

He said the price out loud first, because he says things out loud now and because forty-one days in a county where a bell is a schedule and not a river have taught him that the only honest act left is the one announced in front of witnesses, and he said it standing up, in about nine seconds, to about nine people.

“I am going to have this piece of paper read out twice,” he said, “and I am not going to do anything with it yet, and the price of having it read out is this and I want it said where the two of you can hear me say it.

“There is a woman of forty-one in this yard and a woman of forty-one on a wall nine miles off, and neither of them has ever been in the other's country, and neither of them has ever heard the other's name, and the reason I am about to put a paper in front of both of them is that both of them have got something to hold and nothing to hold it with. *The price is that they are going to find out about each other inside about four minutes, and neither of them is going to have been asked, and there is no instrument in this county or any other that stops it. I have paid it three times in six weeks with three different pairs of people. I am paying it a fourth time and I am telling you the name of it first.*”

Nine people heard that. About four of them understood all of it.

The woman of forty-one from the Stennick end did not turn round.

“Then you will get it in about four minutes rather than nine,” she said. “I have been standing here since before you got to the yard and I have already found out about her.”

---

The sheet inside the bundle was one sheet and it was a copy, and a copy at threepence is a copy anywhere in four hundred miles because there is a scrivener over a chandler's who copies at a penny a page and a sealed copy at threepence and has about ninety deeds a year to keep the shop open.

Nine lines, in a hand that was made for it, and about a man of fifty-two counted the lines out loud in a yard in about four seconds and then said that he had counted them and could not tell what any of them was.

The word at the head of it was one word and it was the ordinary word a clerk writes at the head of a thing that has been set aside.

Corridor.

And a recordwright of twenty-eight read it out, standing up, in her own voice, because she is a person who reads things out and there was nobody else in that yard who would, and it took about four minutes, and about four people in the yard followed all of it and about five followed about a third of it.

And then she read it out a second time.

---

The two readings were both right.

That is the finding of the sixth day of the sixty-third week and it took about nine minutes and it is not a mystery and nobody hid anything.

The first is what the hand says. *A corridor is a district set aside from the ordinary maps, rolls, seats and roads of a city, for a stated length of time, and while it stands the ordinary rules of that city do not apply within it, and the district may be removed from every map the city keeps at one stroke of a pen.* Nine lines and the last of them is where a person signs, and the space for the signature is about two inches long and it is the widest thing on the sheet.

The second is what the hand has been taken to mean since about the middle of the year. *A corridor is a claim, entered in a room by a person, that the people inside the district are not people the city is counting, for as long as the paper stands, and it can be produced against them at any time without anybody inside the district being asked.*

“They are both what that sheet does,” said Sera Quill. “The first is the machine. The second is the handle. *And the difference between them is nine lines long and every one of the nine lines is a way of saying the first one, and that is the whole of what a document is: a thing with a handle built into it that you cannot see from the other side.*”

Nobody in that yard had seen the other side.

---

And then a woman of about fifty who had a board under her arm said the thing that made about four people look at her, and she said it to the yard, and she had not been asked.

“There is a word at the top of that sheet,” she said, “and it is one word, and it says what the whole of the paper is for. It is the same thing as the four letters and a stroke on my board and it does exactly the same job.

“*A word at the top means every line underneath it is for that and nothing else.* Four letters and a stroke at the top of my board is why nobody in this county has ever asked me a second question in twenty-two years, because there was nothing to ask me about. One word at the top of that sheet is why nobody in whatever place is underneath it is going to be asked a second question about anything, for as long as the sheet stands, and it is not going to be stood up in a yard and read out and explained. It is going to be produced.”

She shifted the board under her arm.

“I have got a word at the top of a board about the length of my forearm and it has cost me twenty-two years of being asked about one thing. Somebody four hundred miles off has put a word at the top of a sheet the size of a folded shirt and it will cost a village whatever a word at the top of a sheet costs a village, and I do not know what that is either, and I have not been asked.”

---

The man of fifty-two had been standing at the cart's side for about nine minutes with the sheet on it and had not touched it, and then he put his hand flat on the cart's side, an inch off the paper, which is what he does.

“Four words at the top of a plank in a tower in a village nine miles up this lane,” he said. “Four hundred years old by the hand and not by a story, and about a hundred and forty-one names under it, and a figure against every name and nothing whatever over the column.

“*And one word at the top of a sheet, and a place under it, and about a hundred and forty people in it.* Same shape. A thing that is short at the top and long underneath and nobody in the place it is about. I have been in nine counties in nineteen years and I have counted about four hundred documents and I am telling you that I have never in my life seen a paper that was made to be produced to a room, and I have seen about four hundred that were made to be looked at, and those are not the same thing and the difference is that one of them can be got out of a drawer on a bad morning in about nine seconds and the other one has to be found.”

“Where does it go,” said the man of thirty-one. “That is the question. Not what it says. Where it goes when it is produced.”

“Nobody knows,” said the man of fifty-two, “and that is not because anybody is hiding it. *Nobody is hiding it. Nobody has ever asked.* If a piece of paper is produced to a person in a room, then it arrives, and it is read, and somebody writes a line about it in a book kept in a building with a yard in it four hundred miles from here. That is what happens to a paper. It is the ordinaryest thing in the world. I have watched it about four hundred times and I have never once watched anybody ask where the line went afterwards, and I have never once been able to find out, and it is not a secret.”

He put his hand in his pocket after the lead had gone in first.

“And that is the thing nobody in this county has ever been able to do, and the reason is not that this county is poor. It is that a thing has to be put in front of a person in a room, on a day, and a district that has no road to it has never had a day that anybody four hundred miles off agrees with, and the only days in this county are on a plank on a wall, and there is no way at all of getting a plank out of a room four hundred miles away and into a drawer, and I have thought about that for about nine minutes this morning and I have not solved it and I have not expected to.”

---

And then the woman of forty-one from the Stennick end asked the man of fifty-two a question, and it took about four seconds, and it was the first time in six weeks that anybody in this county had asked him something and got an answer out of a man who counts things.

“Is it a wall?”

“No,” said Tarin Keel. “It is not a wall. A wall is a thing you look at and a wall cannot be produced in a room. That is a paper and a paper can be produced and it can be produced twice, and being able to be produced twice is the whole of what has happened in this county for six weeks, and every single time it has cost somebody.”

And then she asked her question to the man of thirty-one, and she had walked a day for it.

“What are you for,” she said. “You have been in this yard about a quarter of an hour and you have not said one word about the paper except the price of having it read, and there is a woman of forty-one nine feet off you who has kept a count for twenty-five years and has never been asked what any of it was for, and I have got four hours of daylight and a bad road behind me. *What are you for.*”

The yard was quiet in the way a yard of about nine people is quiet, and the man of thirty-one had a sentence and it was the sentence he had said out loud to a hedge about a mile outside Coldharrow on the fourth day of the fifty-eighth week and had carried for six weeks without once putting it down in front of anybody, and about four people in that yard watched him decide not to, and about five did not notice.

“I do not know,” he said. “I have been in this country forty-one days.”

And she said the other half of it, and she said it to him and not to the yard, and it is the whole of what she came for.

“I have been in mine thirty-one years,” said the woman of forty-one from Draymoor, “and I have a bell in a tower that has not been rung since before my mother died, and I have not got a count on a wall and I have not got a keeper and I have not got a wage, and I have got nine children under about nine years old and about a hundred and thirty other people, and the last man who asked me what I was for was my father and he asked it about the goat. So you and I are the same and neither of us has got anybody.”

Nobody wrote it down, because there is no form in eleven villages that says a person may put a thing in front of anything, and no room to put it in front of, and the one instrument this county owns is a painted plank on a wall and a woman with a cloth.

And the last bell went at Coldharrow and the woman of forty-one put four strokes on the plank and there was nothing whatever on top of them, and the day was the ordinary one, and about nine people in two counties had found out about each other inside about four minutes, and neither of them had been asked, and there is no instrument in this county or any other that stops it.

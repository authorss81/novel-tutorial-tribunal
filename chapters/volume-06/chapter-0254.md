# Chapter 254: A Boy's Book With Days In It

The fourth day of the fifty-eighth week was a day on which a debt got called in and nothing else happened, and nobody in Coldharrow could have told you what day of the week it was, and would not have thought the question worth asking, and the man of thirty-one was careful for the rest of the day not to use the word in his own head where a person in a yard could see him not use it.

The boy of seventeen was at the north end of the lane with his back against a hedge, doing nothing at the hour when a boy of seventeen ought to be carrying something, and he had the satchel on his knees and his hand flat on it, which is a thing a person does to a thing they have been asked about already and have said no to.

He said no again, and he said it in about nine words, and he said it before the man of thirty-one had got as far as asking.

“You want it in a room,” said Bram Orren, “and rooms are what it is for.”

---

The man of thirty-one did not argue, and it is worth saying exactly why, because there was a great deal available to him and he had it in his own mouth in about two seconds and put all of it down.

He is a man who can find the difference between a missing page and a page that was taken out on purpose. He is a man who has a cracked steel pen he insists is useful. He is the only person alive who has both, and he had worked with two people who wrote down what a thing did when a thing failed, and one of them had a book like this in his own hand, and the man of thirty-one had looked at that book once in a room four hundred and some weeks away and had thought at the time that it was the only piece of paper in the building that was honest, and had gone back to his own work.

He looked at this one and thought the same thing, and about eleven seconds after that he noticed what he was actually doing, which was looking for the flaw in it, and that is what a man does to a thing he has already decided about.

“All right,” he said.

Bram Orren had not expected that and it showed, and he is seventeen and had prepared a speech.

“I am not going to ask you again,” said the man of thirty-one, “and I am going to tell you why, and then I am going to stop talking about it, and if you ever want to tell me why I should have argued I will listen and I will not argue back.

“The reason is that I came into this country to find a thing that a person can hold and put in front of a room, and you have got one, and every single thing I have done for about a year has been finding a thing like that one. And you have just told me in nine words what the difference is between my kind of good and yours, and it is that mine ends up in a room and yours does not, and I have got no answer to that, and a man with no answer has no business making an argument at a hedge with a boy of seventeen who has thought about it more carefully than I have.”

He put his hands behind his back.

“You can go and do your day,” he said.

---

So they did not talk about the book again for about four hours, and the reason they did not talk about it is that the day was about a debt, and a debt in a country like this is not a matter of money.

Ottily Roke rang twice at the second light, and two men of about thirty went up the lane to a cottage and stood in front of it, and the man of thirty-one went with them because he was asked to and not because he offered, and the recordwright of twenty-eight came too and stood at the end of a garden and wrote nothing down on a day she wrote down nothing on every day.

What the two men did was knock and then stand there, and a woman of about sixty came to the door and did not open it, and there is no form in this country that says what happens next, so what happened next is that the woman asked them a question and the men answered it and the woman shut the door and the two men came back down the lane and rang nothing further, because it had already been rung twice at dawn and the whole of Coldharrow had heard the two rings in the morning and knew by the noon bell that it was a debt and knew by the last bell whether it had been paid.

“It is not a court,” said Ottily Roke, when the man of thirty-one asked her what the two men were. “There is no court. There is a bell and two men and a door, and the door is the woman's, and she can stand behind it for as long as she likes, and the only thing that happens if she does is that everybody in two hundred and ten people knows there is a debt and does not know whether it is paid, and that is the whole of the enforcement and it has been the whole of the enforcement for longer than there has been a Bell.”

“Can they make her pay?”

“No,” she said. “They can stand at the door. That is all anybody can do anywhere in this county and it is more than a great many places manage, and I will not have it said in a yard that we are a soft country because we do not have a thing for it. *We have a thing for it and the thing is that the whole of a village is standing in the lane, and that is a thing no clerk can do.*”

The man of thirty-one did not say the next thing for about an hour, and then he said it to the recordwright of twenty-eight and not to anybody else, and what he said was:

“That is the one that has no column. Not the board. That. Two men at a door and two hundred and ten people in a lane and nothing written anywhere, and if the woman dies tomorrow nobody will ever know whether it was paid, and if she does not then the whole village knows, and that is the most powerful thing I have ever heard of and it is a bell and two men and a shut door.”

The recordwright of twenty-eight had been looking at the lane for about an hour.

“No,” she said. “It is not.”

---

And in the afternoon, because he could not leave it alone and had no business leaving it alone, the man of thirty-one asked the boy of seventeen about the book in a way that was not asking to see it.

“Not what is in it,” he said. “How you keep it. What have you got at the top of a page.”

Bram Orren looked at him for a while and decided he was going to answer, and it is worth saying that the boy made that decision in about four seconds and made it because the answer did not let anybody do anything.

“The bells,” he said. “I have got the four at the top of every page and I put a mark against the one I am at, and I write what crossed and when and in what order, and I write down what did not cross. And I have never lost a day of it and I have lost two pages, in the ninth year, and both of them were days I was ill, and I have told my father about them and he said a book with a hole in it is a book with a hole in it and not a book with a day missing in it, and he was right and I hate it.”

“So the finest thing you can say about a thing is that it happened before this and after that.”

“Yes.”

“That is not a date, is it.”

“No,” said Bram Orren. “And if you had asked me for a date I would have said no, and if you had asked me twice I would have said no twice, and I have said no twice before today to two men, and one of them was a man of about forty with a coat and he wrote a number in a book and I could see his face when I said no, and he was not angry, he was worse than angry, he was *relieved*, and I have thought about that a great deal and I have not worked out what it means and I am seventeen."

He put his hand on the satchel again.

“You want a date because a date is what a room needs. And a room needs it because a room is where things are said, and a thing that is said in a room can be written down, and a thing that is written down can be produced, and a thing that can be produced can be used. And a formal appeal is a thing that is produced, and a formal appeal costs money, and a family that cannot pay for one does not appeal. *They walk.* And this lane is the thing they walk, and it is the only thing in this county that nobody can be asked about, and you are the first person I have met in two years who I think would hand it over for a good reason, and I would rather it was somebody rude.”

---

The man of thirty-one said one more thing and it cost him and it is in his own words and it belongs here.

“Yes,” he said. “I would. And the reason I would is that a person who cannot pay for an appeal and cannot be heard in a room is a person whose problem is the most real kind there is, and I have spent a month in a valley learning that the answer to that is not a better argument. It is a person who can be asked. And I have been in this country four days and I have found a woman of forty-one who is the only person in two hundred and ten people who can be asked anything at all, and she is paid ninepence a day, and I have decided in four days that the answer is to get a second person who can be asked, and I did not ask myself what happens to the first one when there are two.”

He stopped there, and did not say the rest, and the rest was about a lane, and about a man of thirty-one who had once had a room and had given something up in it and been right, and about a man of fifty-two at a dam who had been given a number and never a reason, and about a woman of thirty-eight who had been the only one who could be at a frame and had said out loud before she was asked that she would rather have a paper she could see the end of.

The recordwright of twenty-eight had heard none of that and had heard all of it, because she is a recordwright and she has spent her life listening to the shape of a sentence, and she stood at the end of a garden in a country she had been in four days and understood that she was about to say nine words, and she had them ready, and she had had them ready since the second day.

“A count that leaves the board is a count somebody can use,” said Sera Quill.

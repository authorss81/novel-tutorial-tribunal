# Chapter 273: A Board In A Tower

The second day of the sixty-first week went up eleven on a board in Coldharrow and the seventh of the eleven was a boundary, and the man of thirty-one was at the second light about a quarter of a mile off the second elm when the first of the seven went, because he had walked out at about the fifth hour of the morning with a man of fifty-two and a man of about sixty-one who puts marks on elms, and there had been a conversation on the road that has to be put down whole because it is the reason the day went up eleven.

The man of about sixty-one had done the marks on those two elms nine years ago. He had a chisel in a bag and a habit about a foot deep and he said that a mark that has been grown over twice is a mark and not a scar and that there is a difference which nobody has ever asked him about.

“You have come nine miles to look at it,” he said, on the lane.

“No,” said the man of thirty-one. “He has. I have come nine miles because there is a man at the tower foot who has been asking for a day for two days and he is going to go on asking for a day, and the only day anybody in this country can produce is the day a bell went, and a bell does not care what happened on it.”

“That is not what I asked and you know it is not.”

“No,” he said. “It is not.”

The man of about sixty-one got his chisel out at the second elm and cut a fresh mark about a hand's breadth up, and the seven of them were seven because a boundary is seven and it has always been seven, and they were counted out loud in the voice the woman of forty-one uses, and she was not there and had sent word up the lane at about the sixth hour by a boy of nine that there was nothing to count and that she would have the figure at the second light the next morning.

---

The figure at the head of the board that morning was sixty-three thousand six hundred and fifty-five.

And when the woman of forty-one got to it at about the ninth hour she put four strokes up and then a seventh one and then stopped, and looked at the seven for a while, and then went and got the cloth.

“There,” she said. “That is the second seven in ten days. There is one of them in the grain in the twenty-second year in my father's hand and there is one of them on this board in mine, nine years apart, and I have got two numbers and there is nothing whatever on this plank that says they are the same walk, and there never will be, and I have not got anybody to be angry with, because the man that walked the first one is four years dead and the man that walked the second one is sixty-one and was told to do it in a lane by a stranger he had met that morning.”

“About eleven families are inside that,” said the man of thirty-one.

“About eleven families are inside it and paying nothing and have never been told,” said the woman of forty-one, “and they are not going to be told this week either, and you are not going to tell them, and I am not going to tell them, and I would like the two of us to be quite clear about why, because you are a man who has been in this country twenty-three days and I am a woman who has been in it about fifty-one years, and neither of us can give those eleven families a thing to be right about.”

“No,” he said. “We cannot.”

---

The man at the tower foot was where the man of fifty-six with the hurdles had left him, which was nowhere in particular, about eleven yards off the tower and about four yards off the lane, sitting on the ground with his back against a gatepost and a satchel on his knees.

He was forty-four. He had a coat that had been made for a desk, and a stick that was not for walking, and no cart and no dog, and he had walked in at the south-west end of this county on the sixth day of the sixtieth week and had come up the lane asking at every place he came to for one thing only, and he had said the same thing at every door in the same order, and the man of fifty-six had told him twice that he did not know what a day was and had told him twice for two different reasons.

He got up when the three of them came, and he did not put his hand out, and he looked at the man of thirty-one for about two seconds and one quarter and the man of thirty-one looked back and neither of them said one word about a room nine feet by fourteen four hundred miles back.

“You are the man from the room,” said the clerk of forty-four.

“Yes.”

“I am not going to talk about the room,” said the clerk of forty-four, “and I am not going to pretend I have not worked out that you have not either, and I have not come up this lane to ask you for anything, and I have not come up it to give you anything, and I want both of those said in a yard in front of people so that if it is said afterwards that I came up here on your account it is not true and three of you can say it is not.”

“Who sent you,” said the man of thirty-one.

“Nobody,” said the clerk of forty-four. “I am a clerk of the Seat and there is a bench of four over about thirty of us and I am one of the thirty, and a clerk is not a bench and a bench is not an office and an office is not a person, and I have brought nobody with me and I have brought no paper with anybody's name on it and there is no form in this county for what I am. I came on my own account and I have eight days of walking in a leg I do not enjoy and I have spent four of them.”

---

And he said why, and it took about four minutes, and it was the plainest thing anybody said in that lane in twenty-three days.

“A copy goes out of a district,” said the clerk of forty-four, “and that is a thing that happens about four hundred times a year and it is the whole of my trade, because a copy is a thing that stops being what it was measured in. It is measured in a room nine feet by fourteen with a bench over it and a column and a rule, and then it is four hundred miles off in a barrow under a cloth with a list of eggs on top of it, and nobody has ever gone after one in the history of the thing. *My whole trade is watching what a document does when it leaves the place it was measured in, and nobody has ever once gone and looked.*”

“Then somebody sent for this one.”

“A woman with a board sent for it eight weeks ago in a room in a market town and I was not in that room and I found out about it in about nine seconds, because I keep an index.” He put the satchel down on the ground and did not open it. “Everything that goes out of a district that has a chamber answer owed on it is in that index, and when a form comes back the number comes off the index. That is the only thing in my life that happens automatically. And this one did not come back, and there is no number on it, and there is no name on it, and the district it would have been entered in is four hundred miles from here and has never heard of this lane, and I have been sitting in a room four hundred miles off for eight weeks waiting for a form to come back that nobody was ever going to send, and then I looked at the index and I worked out that a form is a thing you have to go and get, and nobody had ever gone.”

He picked the satchel up again.

“I am not a Bench and I cannot sit anywhere and I cannot compel a soul in this county to do one single thing, and I have not got a good name of anybody's to spend, because I have spent the one I had, in public, in a valley, in front of about nine people, and it is not coming back and it was never mine to begin with.” He said it as flatly as he said the other things. “I am a man of forty-four with a satchel and no authority whatever and a bad leg, and I have come to find out what a piece of paper does when it gets here, and that is the whole of me and there is not a second thing.”

---

The man of thirty-one did not introduce him. That is worth saying, because a man of thirty-one in a country where he has no standing is at his most dangerous when he knows somebody, and about four people in that lane had worked out inside about a minute that the two of them had been in a room together, and none of the four said a word about it, and one of the four was the woman of forty-one and she did not say a word about it either.

“You cannot open a door in this village,” she said instead, “and if you try I will stand in front of it, and not because you are a stranger, and not because you are anything at all.”

“I am not going to open it,” said the clerk of forty-four. “I have no business opening it. I want to look at it. I have been looking at things in rooms for twenty-two years and it is the only thing I can do, and if the man at the tower foot will open his own door I will look at what is in it and I will not touch it and I will not read it out, and if he will not then I will sit against this gatepost until dark and go down the lane in the morning.”

The man of about thirty-one with the hurdle in his hand looked at the door, and then at the key on the nail inside his own house, about two yards off, and then at the man of thirty-one with a stick, and then at the man of about fifty-six, who had come up the lane again and was standing at the gate and had said nothing at all for about four minutes.

“It is a tower,” he said. “I have never been up it. My grandmother kept the key and I do not know what for and I have been asked about that key twice in my life and both times by men from off this lane, and the first one was a man of sixty-three who sells bells and the second one is him.” He nodded at the clerk. “I will open it because it is my door and because he has asked me in a decent way and because a woman of forty-one has told him he cannot ask anybody else, and I want that on whatever anybody is keeping.”

---

He opened it and the three of them went up and the rest of Nettlebed, about nine people by the last of it, stood at the foot and did not go up, and one of them was a man of fifty-six with hurdles and none of the nine said anything, and there was nothing in that anybody could have called a vote.

There is no ladder. There is a stair inside the tower and it is worn and it is about eleven steps and there is a doorway at the top about four feet high, and inside the doorway there is a board.

It is on the inside of the door.

It is about the size of a door, and it is a board, and the paint on it has gone and come and gone again until the grain stands up through it in long pale lines, and it is on the inside of a door that shuts, in a tower in a village of about ninety people, and there is no bell in this village and there has not been one in about two hundred years and there is no board on the outside of this building and there is no figure on the outside of anything in Nettlebed.

It has a hundred and forty-one names on it.

They go from the top down. They are not in columns and they are not in rows and they are not in any order that anybody in that doorway could see, and they are not in the order things happened, and about seventy of them are people who live in the other ten villages on this lane and can be got at, and every one of the rest is somebody in this one or somebody whose people are in this one, and one of the names is a man in a blue coat who came through in a generation nobody in Nettlebed is living in, and he has not been struck out.

And at the head of the board, in a hand that is not the hand of anybody in this county and has not been for four hundred years, there are four words.

*ANY ONE MAY SAY NO*

And under the four words, in the same hand, there is a line about a finger long, and the clerk of forty-four read it twice and could not get all of it, and read out the part he could get, and it was about a person being asked why, and he would not repeat it in a lane and he said so and nobody in that stair asked him to.

“There is no seal,” said the clerk of forty-four, in about four minutes, up eleven steps in a tower with four other people on the stair behind him. “There is no seal and there is no signature and there is no witness line and there is nothing at the foot of it, and I have been in twenty-two years of rooms with documents in them and I am telling you that this is not a charter. *A charter is a thing that somebody grants and somebody else signs.* This is a sentence and a list of people, and the sentence is at the top and the list is underneath it, and the whole of the arrangement of it says that the people in the list are the reason the sentence is there.”

“It is a list of people and not a list of anything they have done,” said the man of fifty-two, from the fourth step.

“It is. It is a worse document than anything I have ever handled and it is worth a great deal more than anything I have ever handled.”

---

And then the clerk of forty-four did the thing that he had come four hundred miles and eight days of walking for, and he did it in about nine minutes, and it is worth being exact about, because it is the first time in twenty-three days that anything in this county has been dated by a person who knows what dating is.

He dated the hand.

Not the wood and not the paint and not a story. The hand. He said the four words are not in a hand of nine hundred and fifty and they are not in a hand of eleven hundred, and that the letters of the two last words have a cut in the downstroke that came in about four hundred years ago and is not there in anything of three hundred and fifty, and that he has the two in his satchel and would not get them out in a tower in front of nine people because that would be a different thing and he was not going to do it in a lane.

“Four hundred years,” said the clerk of forty-four. “That is a date. Not to the year, and I will not give you a year, and I would not give you a year if you had the four hundred sheets in a cart. Four hundred years, give or take the width of a man, and I would stake my place at the Seat on it, and I have not staked anything since a valley.”

“Somebody in one of the villages on this lane has told me that same figure about a custom,” said the man of thirty-one, and heard himself, and stopped, because the person who had told him that had been a man of about thirty-five with a barrow in a different village seven days ago and it was in his mouth and not in the clerk's, and the clerk had not been in that village and could not have been told.

“Then it is two different numbers and one of them is a floor and the other one is a hand,” said the clerk of forty-four, and did not ask which was which, and left it, and it was the single most generous thing anybody did in that tower in twenty-three days.

---

And then the man of thirty-one looked at the board for as long as he could stand there, and the thing that came to him was not about the four words.

It was that a bell is the only thing in eleven villages that anybody has ever gone and looked for.

Not a board. Not a boundary. Not a mark on two elms. A bell. Eleven villages and nine of them have got one and two have not, and the reason the two have not is a thing nobody has been able to say in eleven years, and the reason a man will walk nine miles and stand at a gate and ask for a day is that a bell is what he would go and look for if he were a person in this county, and the tower at Nettlebed has got no bell in it and no figure on the outside of it and no reason on this earth for anybody alive to have gone into it.

“Nobody took it out of anywhere,” he said, out loud, on the top step. “That is the whole of what happened here. It is not on the inside of a door because somebody wanted it hidden. *It is on the inside of a door because a bell is the only thing in this country that anybody has ever gone and looked for, and there has not been one in this village for about two hundred years, and there has been nothing to look at on the outside of this building since the day it was built.*”

Nobody in that tower argued with him and about four people in it agreed with him and about five did not follow it and it did not matter, and he had said out loud the one thing he had come twenty-three days to be able to say, and it had nothing whatever to do with a document and he knew that.

---

And then the clerk of forty-four said the last thing, and he said it flatly, and he said it to the board and not to anybody, and it is the whole of what he has and there is not a second thing.

“There is a column on it,” he said. “There is a figure against every one of the hundred and forty-one names and there is nothing whatever over the column.”

The man of thirty-one counted nine of them from the top of the stair and they were all different, and he could not tell what any of them was a figure of, and he was in a tower with about nine people at the foot of the stair and about eleven days of walking in a leg he did not enjoy, and there was no hand in this county that could have told him what any of them was a figure of, and he did not know, and he did not ask, and he stood on the top step of a tower in a village of about ninety people with nothing in it but a plank and a sentence four hundred years old and a column with no heading over it, and it was the second day of the sixty-first week and the figure at the head of a board four hundred miles down a lane was sixty-three thousand six hundred and fifty-five.

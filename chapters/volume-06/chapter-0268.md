# Chapter 268: Sable Venn And Ilyan Work Beside Him And It Goes Badly

The fourth day of the sixtieth week added thirteen to a board in a country where every day adds four, and nine of them were strangers, three at each of three gates, and they were a timber cart and two men with a barrow, a man with a cartload of lime and his brother, and three going up to a burial, and at the last bell the woman of forty-one put nine strokes on a plank with her eyes on the lane and never once on the wall, and said nothing at all about which of the nine were which, and the man of thirty-one had the whole of it as a figure in about four seconds and set down not one word of what had been said in the yard, on the same grounds and for the same reason as in every other yard he has stood in since a market town four hundred miles back.

He and Sable Venn worked together for the first time that morning, in a kitchen in Ellerby, with a woman of about sixty reading aloud, and it went badly, and the reason it went badly is that it worked.

---

It went badly from the first four minutes because neither of them could do the other one’s half and neither of them would pretend to.

“I cannot read,” said Sable Venn. “I have said that in four counties, in rooms, in my own mouth, for nine years, and I said it before I was asked, and it is the whole of my standing and I have never had it questioned until a man of thirty-one questioned it standing in a yard in this county a week ago. So you read it.”

“No,” said the man of thirty-one.

The room did not go quiet, because there were about five people in it and about four of them had been in the room for a different reason.

“Then we cannot do this at all,” said Sable Venn, “and I have been waiting nine years to do it and I am going to say what it is, because we are in a kitchen and there are four other people in it and they did not ask to be here and they can leave. *Two people who cannot read cannot write down what a third person read out to them and agree about what it means, and that is the whole of the trade and there is no other trade.*”

“There is a woman of about sixty in this village who can read,” said the man of thirty-one, “and she did not ask for any of this, and she has been in a room reading four lines aloud twice, and I am not going to use her for it.”

“You have just used her for it,” said Sable Venn. “She read the four lines out in that doorway yesterday in front of about eleven people because you stood in it and said the words *read it again*, and she did it, and she does not know what any of the days are called and she is about sixty and she held a piece of paper yesterday that she had asked a woman with a barrow for because she wanted to hold a thing that is not a field. And if you do not want to use her then there is no document, and if there is no document then the four lines stay on a cart seat for another four hundred miles, and I have been on the other end of that four hundred miles and I am telling you I will not do it again.”

Nobody in that kitchen said anything for about as long as it takes to count four.

“Read it out again,” said the man of thirty-one, to Wenna Skell, and she said she would, and she read it out again, all four lines, slowly, with her thumb under the line, and got the third one right, which she had not done the first time, and nobody in the kitchen told her that she had got it right because nobody noticed.

---

So they did it, and it took about half an hour, and the method turned out to be the simplest thing in the world and neither of them had ever been in a place where it was the simplest thing in the world.

A person read aloud. Two people who were neither of her decided what it meant. And the two people who decided what it meant could not read, so they had to agree, out loud, in front of four witnesses, about what a sentence said, before either of them could put it into another sentence, and every time they disagreed they had to say so out loud, and there were eleven disagreements, and eleven of them are the reason the sentence they came out with is the shape it is.

It came out in about two hundred words and it is worth putting down whole, because it is the first document that has ever left a village in this stretch in a direction that anybody chose.

> This is a copy of four lines made in a place four hundred miles from here, and sealed, and the four lines are about a water, and a height, and a payment, and they do not name this county and they are not about this county.
>
> The four lines were read out in a yard in Coldharrow on a day, and the day cannot be named here, because a day in this county is four bells and the two on top of it, and the two on top of it are whatever a person who had a hand on a rope was doing, and there is no column and there was never a column.
>
> A woman of forty-one read them out and would not have them in her village and would not have them on her board.
>
> A woman of about sixty of Ellerby was told of them on a day, and asked a woman with a barrow for a piece of paper to hold, and was given them, and read them, and has said that there is no river in this county and that the four lines are worth nothing in this village because nobody in this village was standing near enough to see them.
>
> There is no name at the foot of this and the reason there is no name at the foot of this is that a name at the foot of this would make a person in a room, and a person in a room is a thing that can be used, and this county has already had that conversation.

Sable Quill wrote it in her own hand in about eleven minutes and did not read it back and did not want to.

“It is a document,” she said, and put the pen down. “It is the first one I have written in eighteen days and it is the only one I have ever written in a country where nobody asked me for one, and I want to say four things about it and I am going to say them quickly before I can think about them.

“*One. It is a document and I have not got a good name for what that costs and nobody in this kitchen is going to ask me.* And a count that leaves the board is a count somebody can use, and I said that in a garden on the fourth day of the fifty-eighth week, and I said it again in a yard on the fifth day, and I have said it twice and I know I have said it twice, and this is what it looks like. It looks like two hundred words with no name at the foot of it, and I have spent six years learning to be the woman who does not do that.

“*Two. There is no day on it and I could have put one on it.* I make forty of those a year for a pound three shillings and sixpence each and every one of them is true and this one is not even that. And if I had put a day on it, the room nine days from this field would have had a day, and a day is a thing a room can do things with, and this county cannot produce one, and *the not putting of it is the only protection anybody in this kitchen has got and it is mine and I put it there and it is the first useful thing I have done in eighteen days.*

“*Three. I have not signed it, and I am aware that a document with no name at the foot of it is worth about as much as a bell nobody rang*, and I am leaving it that way on purpose, and I would like it noticed that the reason is not a principle. The reason is that if my name is on it then I am a person in a room, and I have spent nine years in this country being a woman with a board whom nobody can name, and I am not ready to stop being that, and I have not been asked to.

“And *four. I am not going to say the other thing now*, and the other thing is about what I did eight weeks ago, and it will get said.”

---

She got it out about two hours later, in a yard at Coldharrow, at about the ninth hour, and the man of thirty-one was in the yard and about four other people were in the yard, and she said it in her own mouth, without being asked, which is how she says everything.

“I put four lines into this country and I did not read them,” said Sable Venn. “That is nine words and I have been living on them for nine days and they have got heavier every day and I would like to hand them back.

“You have made a woman of sixty read one out. Twice. In a doorway and in a kitchen, in front of eleven people and then five, and she got the third line right the second time and nobody in that kitchen noticed and I have decided not to tell her and I am telling you that I have decided. *And she is not a document.* That is the difference and I know the difference and I have known it for nine years without ever once having to look at it, and I have looked at it today, and it has taken me nine years and a copy to learn and it has taken you eighteen days, and it has cost her nothing at all, because nobody asked her either, and I was one of the people who did not ask her, and I was one of them for nine years.”

Nobody said anything. About four of the people in that yard understood all of it and the rest of the yard understood that something had happened.

“I am not going to apologise to her,” said Sable Venn, “and I am not going to thank you, and neither of those is because I am proud. It is because I have been in this county nine years and I have never once been thanked for any of about four hundred things and I did not ask to be and I am not going to start asking in the eleventh week of my life. Put the sheet on the barrow, and tell the woman with the barrow she is taking it down the lane and not to Coldharrow-edge and not to a clerk and not to anybody who has a column, and *do not tell her what is on it*, and she will not ask, and that is the arrangement and it has been the arrangement for nine years.”

---

And that is how the first thing in this stretch that ever left a village in a chosen direction actually left it, and here is the whole of what it was:

A sheet of paper, in a hand that is not the writer's, with two hundred words and four lines on it and no name at the foot of it and no day on it, put on the seat of a barrow at a gate that is a post with a bar across it that is not shut, by a woman of about forty who makes her living carrying other people's paper between these villages and has never once opened any of it, and who did not ask, and who has now said out loud, unprompted, in two different yards on two different days, that she has not read it.

The man of thirty-one did not go with it. He watched it go, and it went about two hundred yards, and then it was round a hedge, and then it was not.

“Nobody sent for that,” said the recordwright of twenty-eight, who had not been in Ellerby and had been in this yard for about an hour and had read about nine of the two hundred words from where she was standing. “Nobody in eleven villages asked for a thing like that to go anywhere. There is no form for it. There is no column for it. It is not a date and it is not a notice and it is not a return and it is not owed to anybody. *It is the first route out of this county that anybody has chosen, and it is eleven days old, and a man of thirty-one has just started one, and I am a recordwright and I have written a clause into a charter that nobody has ever seen and I have never once thought of it as the same thing.*”

“Yes,” he said. “I know what I have just done. I have been in this country eighteen days and there is nothing in my hand, no party, no bench, no name on anything, and the whole of what I have done is get one piece of paper to move down one lane on purpose, and I have got no standing here and no standing nine days off either, and if it is the wrong thing then it is the wrong thing and I have done it and I cannot take it back and I am not going to try.”

“Nobody has thanked you,” said Sera Quill.

“No,” he said. “Nobody has, and I have noticed, and I am not going to mention it again.”

And at the foot of the sheet of paper, when the woman with the barrow unfolded it in a shed at about the tenth hour to keep it out of the wet, there was a figure in the bottom corner in a hand that was not the writer's and was not any hand in eleven villages, and it was in pencil, and it was a number, and it was not a day.

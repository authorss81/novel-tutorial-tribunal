# Chapter 251: A Country That Counts In Rings

The first day of the fifty-eighth week is the day he stopped being carried, and nobody in this country sent the cart away and nobody was sorry for him, because the last four miles of the way into the Briar March is not a road and the cart is not a thing that goes up it.

The cart turned about at a posting yard with a wall round it and a man in it who took the money for the whole of the two hundred miles and did not look at any of it. And then there was a lane, and a hedge on both sides of it a man could not see over, and the ground came up in eleven places in the first mile, and by the fourth mile he had stopped expecting the ground to be the problem and had started expecting the hedges, and the hedges are the problem and everybody who has ever walked this knows it and nobody says so.

There were three of them. A man of thirty-one with a book in a bag. A route-man of fifty-two with a rule on a string round his neck, who had counted the milestones out of the yard and said the number out loud twice and had been told by the first to stop. And a recordwright of twenty-eight with a satchel and a face that had been doing arithmetic since the ridge.

Nobody in this valley has ever been on a Bench road, and the Bench road ends about nine miles back at a wall with a gate in it that does not go anywhere, and the gate was shut when they came to it and it was shut when they left it and there is nobody at it and there has not been anybody at it for years.

---

Bram Orren was seventeen and he had a satchel and he was at a stile about a quarter of a mile short of the village with his hand on a rope, and he rang it before he said anything, which is the thing that makes it worth writing down.

He rang three times, evenly, and then he stood with his hand still on the rope and turned round and looked at them, and he said it before anybody could ask him.

“The March's rule is that a runner rings what he crosses,” he said. “I am not on the March's line. I am on the bell-runners' and the bell-runners' line comes through here about twice a day and I am not the March's to say otherwise, and I have rung it because there are three of you and I have not seen you before and you are on ground nobody in this village has met you on.”

He said all of that in about eleven seconds, standing at a stile, to three strangers, and it is worth saying that he said it because he had been told to say it, by a man of about sixty in a yard at the other end of the eleven miles, and that the man of sixty had said it to him in about the same words in about the same number of seconds, and that this is how a thing travels in a country with no paper in it.

“A runner rings what he crosses,” said the man of thirty-one. “And you are seventeen.”

“I am,” said Bram Orren, and put the satchel back on his shoulder.

---

Coldharrow is about two hundred and ten people in thirty-eight houses and it has a tower with a bell in it and a tower is not a church here and nobody will tell you what it is if you ask it in a wrong voice. It has no market day, and it does not have one because a market needs a day and Coldharrow does not have the kind of day a market needs, and the man of fifty-two asked about that in a yard within ten minutes of arriving and was told by a woman carrying a bucket that Coldharrow had a market when it had one.

It has about nine hundred elms and a great many of them are dead, and about two hundred of the two hundred and ten people are inside the hedge ring and the other ten are out of it in four farmsteads, and nobody here has a word for the inside and the outside that is not the same word they use for a wall.

And on the north wall of the tower, about chest height, where a person standing in the road can read it, there is a board.

It is about as wide as a door and taller than a door, and it is a plank, and it has been painted and unpainted so many times that the grain has come up through all of it in long pale streaks, and there are two hands in it, and the older of the two has got into the grain and the newer one has not, and the newer one is the more careful.

The figure at the head of it, in the older hand, is fifty thousand three hundred and seventy.

There is nothing else on it. There is no year at the head of it. There is no column and there has never been a column. There is no mark against any individual line and there is no day, and there is no note of what any of it was for, and the board has been going thirty-one years, and the older hand is six years of it and the newer hand is twenty-five.

“A man of thirty-one stood in front of a board for about four minutes and did not say anything,” and that is the honest shape of the morning, because he did not, and because what was in his head for most of the four minutes was not the figure.

It was that a board like this is a thing you can put a number in and take a number out of, and there is exactly one way of doing that, and the way is a stroke, and a stroke takes about a second, and about four strokes is a good hour's work, and thirty-one years of a four-strokes-a-day figure is a board about the size of a door and a man of sixty-one and a great deal of paint. He had seen four such boards in four counties in his life and every one of them had a column headed with a word, and the word was always the same word, and it was a year.

He said none of that out loud, because none of it was any of his business, and he had been in this country for about a day and a half of walking and had not been introduced to anybody.

---

The woman who keeps it came out at the second light with a cloth in her hand and did not ask him who he was.

She was forty-one and there is a way of saying a person's age in a country like this that is not a guess and is not a courtesy, because people are counted the way bells are, and he learned it in nine days and did not know he had learned it.

She wiped the top foot of the board with the cloth, which did not need it, and looked at the figure, and then at the rest of the board, which is the part where the strokes are.

“Four hundred and ninety-two since the last of the year before the fell came down,” she said. “That is what the number says. It has said the same thing every morning for twenty-five years and I have never once been asked what it is a number of and I have answered nobody because nobody has asked.”

“Who counts it?”

“I do,” she said. “My father did six years of it before me and he started it and he is four years dead and he did not write down what any of it was for either, and I have never been able to find out from a person what the number is a number of, because there is nobody left who was counting and I have asked twice and I got told it was in the tower and it is in the tower and I can read.”

She put the cloth over her shoulder.

“You are not from the west,” she said, which was not a question and was not about the west.

“No,” said the man of thirty-one.

“Then you are not from anywhere I know of and I am not going to guess.” She looked at the route-man with the rule round his neck. “You have been on a road with a wheel track in it.”

“I have,” said Tarin Keel. “I came out of one this morning and I am going to say before anybody asks me that I have not been on a lane in eleven days and I have not liked any of it.”

“That is the correct answer,” she said, “and it is the first correct answer a stranger has given me in this yard in about a year.”

---

And then a man of thirty-one, who had been in this country for a day and a half and who had been told nothing and asked nothing and had no party and no bench and no office and no standing in a country he had never heard of eleven days ago, went and got a piece of white chalk out of his pocket, because he had come prepared to leave something behind, because that is the entire sum of what he has ever been able to do in this world, and he put it on the board.

He put it at the foot, under the last stroke, and it was a figure and not a stroke, and it was *fifty thousand three hundred and seventy*, and the figure on the head of the board was the same figure, and he had written the same number twice on one plank four inches apart which is the first thing a person learns to do with a board and the last thing anybody ought to do to one.

He did not put his name on it, and he did not put a date on it, and the reason he did not put a date on it is that he did not know what date to put, and the reason he did not put his name on it is a thing that happened in a valley four hundred miles off in a lane, in front of eleven people, at about the fifth hour, in a month, and it is not going to be unlearned in a yard in the March.

A woman of forty-one looked at it for a while.

“That is not what I asked for,” she said.

“No,” he said.

“I want to be clear with you that that is not what I asked for, and I have not asked you for anything, and I do not know what you are or where you are from, and you have put a figure in chalk at the foot of a board that has been going thirty-one years and there are now three hands in it and one of them is a stranger's and it is in chalk and it will come off in the rain.”

“I know what it will do in the rain,” he said, and did not offer to wipe it, and did not put his hand on it.

She looked at him a second time, properly, and it is worth saying that whatever she decided about him happened in that second and not in the whole of the conversation, and that she did not say what it was.

“Ay,” she said. “Well. It is on the board.”

And she went and did what she does at the second light, which is the first thing that happens in Coldharrow, and the bell went once, evenly, and it was not the clock and the clock was going to be four more times before the day was out.

---

A man came out of the yard from the far end of it, about sixty-three, in a coat that had been good, and he was looking at the board and not at any of the three of them, and he said, in a voice that had said a price to about four hundred people:

“That is a death.”

“That is a death,” said the woman of forty-one, without turning round. “I will know who by the second bell and I will not tell you now.”

The man of sixty-three said “Ay” and went back into the yard, and the recordwright of twenty-eight, who had not said one word in about nine minutes and had been writing nothing, said out loud to nobody in particular:

“Two of us want to know what a ring is, and neither of us is going to ask her this morning, and I would like it noticed that we both decided that at the same moment without looking at each other.”

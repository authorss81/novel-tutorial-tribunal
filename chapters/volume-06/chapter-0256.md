# Chapter 256: A Woman Who Sells Days

The sixth day of the fifty-eighth week was the day the figure on the board at Coldharrow went up eight, and it stood at sixty-three thousand five hundred and forty-five at the second light and had stood at sixty-three thousand five hundred and thirty-seven the day before, which was four bells and four for a market, and it was the third morning he had put a figure in chalk at the foot of somebody else's board and the third morning he had not been asked to, and the day itself came to five, and the fifth of those was a stranger, and the stranger turned out to be the whole reason the three of them learned what a date costs in this country, because she came up the lane at the second light with a handcart of paper and a satchel and a way of talking that made a price sound like a fact about the weather.

She was twenty-nine and she said her name in the yard without being asked, which is not how the three of them had been introduced anywhere in six days, and she said it because a person who carries paper to a village that has never had a date has to be able to say who she is before the price.

“Nell Callow,” she said. “There is a press at Coldharrow-edge and it is mine and it is the only one in eleven villages and I have had it six years. I am here because the miller at Trope wants a day and he wants it for a day he can name, and I do not know what any of the days are called and neither does he, and I have brought three things and you may have one of them and I would rather you did not take it in a yard, and I will come back at the last bell.”

“What is the price?” said the man of thirty-one.

“One pound, three shillings and sixpence,” said Nell Callow, “and I have said it before you asked because that is the only honest part of the whole business and everything after it is me being quick.”

---

She came back at the last bell, and the man of fifty-two had a cart side to do it on, and he did it twice, and the second time is the one that counts because the first time he got a unit wrong and said so out loud before anybody else could.

“Two days there and two days back,” he said. “And I have walked a cart in this county for one day and I have been told what a day of me costs in a lane and it is one shilling and sixpence, and that is eighteen pence, and two days each way is four days and four times eighteen is seventy-two pence. And I have got that wrong already and I am going to do it again the way it is done, because it is done in twos and not in fours and I am going to count it the way the woman counts it.

“A day out is eighteen. A night out is threepence, because a bed and a bowl and there is nowhere else, and that is the price at the second house up from the posting yard and I asked a woman of about forty who did not want to tell me and told me.

“Two days out is thirty-six. Two nights is six. And the clerk at Coldharrow-edge is a pound for the entering of it, and it is a pound whether you are angry or not, and he is not in on this and I have met him.

“Thirty-six and six is forty-two. Forty-two and two hundred and forty is two hundred and eighty-two. Twelve pence to the shilling, and two hundred and eighty-two is twenty-three shillings and sixpence, and twenty shillings to the pound, and that is one pound, three shillings and sixpence. *That is her price and it is the same price she said in a yard eight hours ago and I have checked it and there is no mistake in it.*”

“Where does the pound go?”

“It does not go anywhere in particular,” said Nell Callow. “It goes into a box and the box is the reason a county seat has a seat. That is what you are buying. You are buying a seat at a table in a room, and you are not buying a friend and you are not buying anybody’s attention, and there is a man in that building whose whole day is turning a thing like yours over, and he will do it in about four minutes and he will be right about the arithmetic and he has never been to any of the eleven villages and he never will be.”

---

“Now do it in our money,” said the man of thirty-one, and it is worth saying that he had worked out what he was about to ask for in a lane two days earlier and had sat up about it, because he knows that a figure is worth nothing in this volume unless there is a second figure it can be subtracted from.

“Two hundred and eighty-two pence,” said Tarin Keel. “And a day of me on this lane is eighteen pence. Two hundred and eighty-two divided by eighteen is fifteen, and fifteen times eighteen is two hundred and seventy, and there are twelve pence over, and twelve pence is one shilling. *A date is fifteen days of the man who walks the road and one shilling.*”

Nobody said anything for a moment, and then the woman of about fifty at the gate, who had come out because there was a cart in the lane, said: “About half a month.”

“About half a month,” said Tarin Keel, “and I have been paid by the day for nineteen years and I know exactly what half a month of me is, and it is the whole of what I have in my cart to eat, and I have had days like that and I have had about nine of them a year, and I have never once said that to a person who was selling me something.”

“And the year of her,” said the man of thirty-one, pointing at the tower, where the figure was on the wall in a hand of its own. “Ninepence a day for a hundred and forty days.”

“One thousand two hundred and sixty pence,” said Tarin Keel, and the woman of forty-one said it with him, from about nine feet away, in the same words, at the same moment, and neither of them looked at the other and it is the only time in these six days that the two of them were exactly together on anything.

“Four of hers,” said Tarin Keel, and he had it out of his own mouth in about two seconds, which was not deliberate. “Four dates. Two hundred and eighty-two times four. Two hundred and eighty-two times two is five hundred and sixty-four, and times four is eleven hundred and twenty-eight. That is a hundred and twelve dozen of it, and eleven hundred and twenty-eight is ninety-four shillings, and that is four pounds and fourteen shillings.

“One thousand two hundred and sixty less eleven hundred and twenty-eight is a hundred and thirty-two pence. Twelve to the shilling is eleven shillings. *Four dates is a year of the woman who keeps the count less eleven shillings.*”

The yard was quiet in a way a yard in Coldharrow does not get quiet, and the reason it was quiet is that about four of the people standing in it had understood that sentence as being about their own road, and about four had understood it as being about the woman at the gate, and one of those four was a man of thirty-eight who had come up with a handcart and was standing at the end of it.

---

Nell Callow looked at the tower for about a second and then said the thing she came to say.

“I make about forty of those a year,” she said. “Two hundred and eighty-two times forty is eleven thousand two hundred and eighty pence, and eleven thousand two hundred and eighty is forty-seven pounds exactly, and I have been doing it for six years and I have never once told anybody that number and I am telling you it because you have got the other one out of the woman and it is about nine times hers.”

“Say it in yours,” said the man of thirty-one. “The whole year against the whole year. Forty-seven pounds against five pounds five shillings, and then say how many of her you are.”

“Nine,” said Nell Callow. “About nine times. And I know what that sounds like from a yard and it sounds like the wrong thing to say about a woman with a cloth, and it is, and I have not slept especially well about it for two years and I have not stopped either, because the alternative is not sleeping at all and there are thirty-one houses here that cannot pay a man with a cart to come up the lane once.”

“And there is the thing you have not been asked about,” said the man of thirty-one.

“There are four things I have not been asked about,” said Nell Callow, “and you have asked about the wrong one, and the wrong one is still worth answering, and it is this: I cannot make a date for a boundary, and I have never been able to, and it is not a thing I have forgotten to do.”

---

The woman of forty-one came down out of the tower yard at that point, which she does at the last bell, and the two of them looked at one another and had clearly met before.

“Nell,” said Ottily Roke.

“Your board is going the same way it went last time I was in it.”

“It is going the same way it has gone for thirty-one years,” said Ottily Roke, “and I did not know it was going the same way until about the fourth year and then I knew it always had been.”

“Then I will say it and be quick,” said Nell Callow, “because you will have a thing to say and I have heard it and I have been right about it four times and I have been wrong about it fourteen and I know which of those is which.

“A date is for a thing that happened on a day. A bell went, somebody was in the lane, a debt got called in, a woman died, a man came through. I can put a day on all of that. I can put it on it and I can make it true and I have never made one that anybody has caught out.

“*I cannot put a day on a boundary. A boundary is a line and the line is there before you walk it and it is there after you come back, and if I put a Shelf date on it I have proved that a man walked it, and he did, and it is true, and it is worth nothing, because a man can walk a boundary twice and the second time is the one that counts, and a room nine days from this field will take the second one, and it will take it off me and it will be true, and in about nine cases out of nine it will be against the man who asked me for it.*”

She stopped and looked at the ground.

“I have made fourteen of them in six years,” she said. “Fourteen boundaries. And I have never told anybody that number, and it is fourteen and it is all of them, and I did not do it to anybody, I did it because I was asked to and I am good at it and I am fast and I charge a pound and three shillings and sixpence and I did not stop to find out what the second walking was for.

“And I have found out what the second walking was for about eleven times, and I know about eleven of them, and I know that eleven times a man who had a boundary went to a room and did not get it. And I do not know what happened to the four I have not found out about and I have not gone looking, because I am twenty-nine and I have a press and I have a woman and two children and I have never once done a thing in this county that I could not do on a day I could name.”

“A boundary is a thing that can be walked twice,” said Ottily Roke, in her own mouth, in a yard, to about eleven people, and the man of thirty-one did not say it and had not been going to.

“That is the whole of why that girl is the way she is,” said Ottily Roke, “and it is not because she is wicked and it is not because she is careful. It is because the only thing she knows how to sell is a true thing, and in this county a true thing about a boundary is a thing that gets somebody else's line moved. *And nobody has ever asked her to sell anything else, because there is nothing else, and because we are all waiting for somebody to come and make the room stop wanting them.*”

She looked at the man of thirty-one.

“That is the answer and I have given it to you before you asked for it, and it is not the answer you came for, and I have not got another one and I have not had one for six years.”

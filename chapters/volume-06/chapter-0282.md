# Chapter 282: Two Of Them Side By Side, And The Price Is A Woman Of About Sixty

A stranger came through the bar at Coldharrow at about the third hour on the fourth day of the sixty-second week, going up the lane and not stopping, and a stranger is three, and the woman of about fifty who keeps that board called it off as he passed, and the four of the clock went up with it, and the day came to seven. The figure at the head of the wall at the second light that morning had been sixty-three thousand seven hundred and twenty-one, and it is the only number in eleven villages that anybody can check anything at all against.

The man of thirty-one had been in the country thirty-two days and he had four lines on a piece of paper out of the back of a bill in a press eleven miles off and about nine people in two counties who knew that he was the reason the four lines existed, and he had said out loud in a yard the day before that he was going to put two of the four next to each other, and he had named them, and he was about to do it.

---

They went up the lane to Ellerby because one of the two is nine miles that way, and the woman of about sixty walked out of her own gate and came with them about two hundred yards and then went back, and she did not say why and nobody asked her, and about four people who saw it did not think about it again for two hours.

The two were these.

The ninety acres, which are not a border because there is nobody on the other side of them and the man who walked them is four years dead and the only record of the walk is a seven on a plank at Coldharrow in the twenty-second year of that plank.

And the list, which is a hundred and forty-one names on the inside of a door in a tower four miles from a gate that has never been shut, in an order that is not the order the things happened in, with a figure against every name and no heading over the column, and the last figure anybody put in that column is about two hundred years old and is in the hand of a woman of fifty-one's grandmother.

---

He said the price first, in the lane, before they got there, standing still, with his hands in his pockets and a lead in no pocket at all because the man of fifty-two had gone on to Sallow in the morning and would not be back until the last bell.

“I am putting a boundary and a list next to each other,” he said. “I am not joining them. And the price is this and I want it said where the two of you can hear me say it.

“Everything I have done in this county for a month has been putting two things next to each other, and every time, the price has arrived in about nine minutes and the price has been a person finding out they were standing in a room. That happened to a woman of forty-one and it cost her nine years. It happened to two carters and it cost them a round that neither of them can put to anybody. *This time the price is not a room. This time the price is that the only way a thing gets into this method at all is that somebody says it out loud in front of people, and I have not got a method that will let anybody keep a thing to themselves, and I have looked, and there is not one, and I am going to do it in a yard in about an hour and the two of you are going to be in it.*”

The woman of about sixty said, from about two hundred yards off, without stopping and without turning round:

“You are going to do it in a yard.”

“Yes.”

“Not in a lane,” she said, “because a lane is nobody and a yard is about nine people, and you have been in this country a month and you have worked that out and I have watched you work it out, and I am telling you now that you can have a yard and you will not have me in it, and I am going home.”

“Fine,” he said.

And she went home, and about four people on that lane noticed that she had said the thing and had then said the other thing in the same breath, and about two of them worked out that she had said *I am telling you now* about eleven seconds before she said *I am going home*, and neither of them could have told you what that was.

---

The two of them stood in the yard of the last house in Ellerby for about half an hour, and a man of about thirty-five with a barrow came and went twice and offered them nothing and asked them nothing, and the two hundred and nine names were on the outside wall of that house at head height and eleven feet of it, and nobody in that village had ever had a reason to think that those two things ought to be in the same yard.

And this is the whole of the finding, and it took about four minutes and it is not a discovery about the world.

“Ninety acres cannot be walked in nine days of rings,” said the man of fifty-two, who had come back in the middle of it without anybody noticing and who had a bit of lead out by the time he started. “Seven is a boundary. I have got that. But seven is not a boundary and nothing else, and that is the part nobody has ever said out loud, and I have been saying it since about the ninth hour of the second day of that week and I did not know it was the finding until this morning.

“A stranger is three and a market is four. Three and four is seven. A debt called in is two and a fire is two and a stranger is three. Two and two and three is seven. *There are at least four different days that a board in this county can say seven about and there is nothing whatever on the plank that tells them apart, and your two sevens in ten days are not the first two anybody has ever had and they are the first two anybody has ever noticed, and I have been in this county for a month and I did not notice it until a man from a room four hundred miles off made a woman of forty-one go into the grain of her own father's hand about a run of nines.*”

He put the lead back in his pocket.

“A hundred and forty-one names cannot be dated in four hundred years, and that is the other half, and the two halves are the same failure in two coats. *A count with no units in it cannot be turned back into what was done, and a list with no dates in it cannot be turned back into what anybody was for.*”

---

And then a person of a village said a name out loud.

Not Ilyan. Not the man of fifty-two. A woman of about fifty-one from Trope, who had walked four miles and a bit to be in that yard, and who had been at the foot of a stair eleven worn steps high about a week ago and had put her thumb on the last nine figures in a column with nothing over it, and who cannot write, and who had said the word out loud about four times in her life and had never once said it in a yard.

She said the name of the fourth person from the bottom of that column, out loud, in a yard in Ellerby, in front of about nine people, and then she said whose it was.

“It is hers,” said Isel Cardew. “It is Wenna Skell's grandmother, and she is in this yard, and she has not told me that and I have not asked her and I have found it out in a stair in a village of about ninety people by reading a name off a plank upside down because I am the only one in this lane who can read and I was not asked, and I have not told a soul in about two weeks and I have not been asked once.”

“I did not ask you,” said the woman of about sixty.

“No,” said Isel Cardew. “You did not. *And I have just said it in a yard, and you cannot un-know that anybody else has said it, and I did it because this man asked me to put a boundary and a list next to each other and there is no way to do that with a person of about sixty standing in it that does not end with somebody saying one of those two things out loud, and I have looked for about an hour and there is not another way, and I have decided to be the one who says it and not you.*

“I am sorry about that and I am not going to be asked about it and I am not going to be thanked, and if you want to be angry with anybody you can be angry with me and I will be here about four minutes, and after that I am going up the lane, and my gate bar is at home and it has not been shut since before I was born and it is not going to be shut this week either.”

---

The yard was quiet in the way a yard is quiet, and about nine people in it had heard a name said out loud for the first time in about four hundred years and did not know what to do with any part of it, and the woman of about sixty put her hand flat on the outside wall of the last house of her own village, on the plank, about four feet to the left of where the name was, and left it there.

“It is on our wall as well,” she said. “Not that one. Ours. Two hundred and nine of them, and nobody struck out, and a name is an address, and about seventy of the two hundred and nine are people in four other villages who can be got at and asked, and that is the one thing in this county that has ever worked and it has worked for about four hundred years and nobody knows why.

“*And I have been carrying a thing about a grandmother for about a week that I did not know was true until a woman of fifty-one said it in a yard, and I am about sixty, and the two things I can say are the two things I have got, and now one of them has been said in front of nine people by somebody else, and it is still mine and it is not mine, and I have nobody to be angry with because she did it to spare me and I would not have done it and I could not have done it.*”

She took her hand off the wall.

“There is one more thing and I am going to say it and then I am going home for good today, and it is not for him and it is not for the man with the lead.

“I have wanted that hedge out of the ground for nine years. Nine years. And about a week ago somebody told me the fourth name off that plank was my grandmother’s, and I have not been right since, and I have not told one person, and there is nobody coming, and the two things are both going when I am. *I am not a woman with a grievance any more. I am a woman with two facts and about as long as it takes to be asked about the other one.*”

---

And the man of thirty-one wrote nothing down.

That is the third time since he came into this country and it is the only thing he did in that yard that anybody afterwards agreed was the right thing, and about four people in it said so afterwards, in about four different words, and he did not hear any of them.

“There is no form in eleven villages that says a man may put a thing in front of a room,” he said, to the man of fifty-two, when the man of fifty-two asked him why he had got the lead out and had not put it anywhere. “There is no room in eleven villages. I have a method and it is a good one and I have got four lines on a piece of paper and one of them is a list of a hundred and forty-one people and one of them is ninety acres that eleven families are grazing, and I cannot put them in front of anything, and I am not going to write them down anywhere else either, because the second copy of a thing in this county is the thing that gets used, and there is a woman of twenty-nine at a press who proved that about a fortnight ago with a number and a pencil, and nobody has said whose the number is in and about nine people know.”

“Write it on the cart,” said Tarin Keel.

“No,” said the man of thirty-one. “I wrote on a cart in this yard twelve days ago and it is on a sheet in a press eleven miles off with a figure at the foot of it in my own hand and nobody has ever asked me whether that figure is a figure of anything, and that is the best that writing on a cart has ever done in this country.”

---

They came down the lane in the last of the light and the four bells went on the plank at Coldharrow at the last of it and the day was a day of seven and there was nothing on the wall at Coldharrow at all about a name said in a yard in a village of about a hundred and forty people nine miles up the lane, and there is no column on that plank for it and there never has been one and there is not going to be one.

And later the same evening, a thing happened at the ninth house in Coldharrow that was the first thing in this county in about a month that has gone better than it was going to, and it took about four minutes, and nobody's hand was on it.

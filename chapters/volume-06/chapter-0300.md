# Chapter 300: A Person Doing Their Work

The last number of this one, on the first morning of the sixty-fifth week, was sixty-three thousand eight hundred and forty-one, and a woman of forty-one put it up at the second light and did not know it was the last one, because nobody in two hundred and ten people has ever known a thing like that coming and she had not been told and there was nobody to tell her.

It was four bells and nothing whatever on top of them at the second light, and she wrote the four and wiped the cloth over it and put the cloth over her shoulder, and it was the fiftieth morning of a man of thirty-one in this country.

---

The difference between that figure and the figure on the plank on the first morning he came into this country is three hundred and thirty-one.

Three hundred and thirty-one is not a round number. It is not four bells a day for any whole number of days, and there is no column for it and there is no heading over it and there is no name against it and there was never a year at the top of that plank and there is not going to be one.

Nobody in two hundred and ten people chose it. About two hundred of it was on the board before a certain week began and nobody chose that either, and the rest of it is fifty days of a village doing things, and a day's figure is a weighted number, and you cannot get back from a weighted number to the thing that was weighed, and there are now four days in the last ten that say the same figure and no way on earth to tell which was which.

*There is a day of eight in this county and there are two of them, and one of them was a market and one of them was a stranger and a burial, and the plank cannot tell them apart, and there is no room in eleven villages in which anybody could ever be told.*

A man of fifty-two said that in a yard about a fortnight ago and then said he was not going to say it in a yard again, and he has not.

---

What he has, at the fiftieth morning, is this, and it is worth setting down whole because it is the whole of a man of thirty-one at the end of fifty days and it is not a plan.

A good name of nobody's to spend. A leg that is the leg, and no count of days in it, and nobody in this country has ever asked him about it and nobody was going to be told. A thing in a bag that is off nothing, and a second half of a book he has not opened since a valley four hundred miles behind him, and he has not opened it in fifty days and he is not going to open it on the back of a morning like this.

A figure in white chalk at the foot of somebody else's board, put there on his first morning, forty-nine days old now, that nobody has told him to wipe off and that he has not offered to wipe off, and that about nine people in two hundred and ten can tell at a glance and that not one of them has ever said a word about, in a yard, in a doorway, or at a bar.

A figure in pencil at the foot of a sheet in a press eleven miles off, in a hand that is his, on a thing nobody asked him to write on, that a woman of twenty-nine put there because he gave her a number when she asked for one, and that nobody in this county has said whose hand it is in, and that he is not going to take back.

No name on anything. No office and no party and no bench and no body and no seat and no room with a door on it that has anything to do with him. No remedy he chose alone, because there has not been one. No stage, and there was one in a valley about four years ago and it was not reached again and it is not going to be.

And four people in two counties who can be asked about something. That is twice as many as there were on the first morning he came into this country, and he has never once counted them, and there are four and there were two, and one of the two was a woman of forty-one in a village of about ninety people who had never been asked what she was for in her life.

One of the four is a woman of about sixty in a village nine miles up the lane who has said out loud in a yard that she is the only person who can say where a boundary started, and not the only one who thinks she can, and that the two things she can say are both going when she is, and that nobody is coming. She is about sixty and there is nothing wrong with her.

And one of the four is a woman of fifty-one at Trope who cannot write, and who has a handbell she has rung at a lane end for about forty years, and who has refused three times to say who taught her, and who read a two-syllable word at the head of a clerk's column four hundred miles off out loud in a yard because somebody had to, and who shut her own gate bar for the first time in fifty-one years on a day in the middle of all of this, for about four seconds, and opened it again inside four seconds, because a hen was out, and nobody in about a hundred and twenty people heard any of it.

---

And four hundred miles off, in a valley of about a hundred and forty people, a corridor is standing and it is not going to be torn up, and a man of forty who signed it is alive and is in his own field on an ordinary morning, and a bell is owed to that valley and is not hung and costs six pounds and fifteen and nobody in this county has six pounds and fifteen and nobody is going to be asked for it.

And a woman of about fifty-six who works under a bench of four somewhere has gone back and will not describe a report, and does not know what is in it, and changed her mind about wanting to know and would still rather nobody in that valley had it.

And a man of thirty-four, who stands in front of about forty people a week and has never once been able to see the thing he is saying out loud, went home four hundred miles with a scrap of his own handwriting on the back of his own hand, and the sentence he stands behind is still his, and he did not act on it in this county, and there was nothing here for him to act on, and that is a fact and not a mercy.

---

And there are four things in this county that have been sitting exactly where somebody put them, and each of them is worth about nine seconds, and nobody in two hundred and ten people has touched any of them in fifty days and nobody was ever going to.

There is a figure in white chalk at the foot of a board on a tower wall, and there are about nine people who could tell whose hand it is in, and nobody has ever said so.

There is a figure in pencil at the bottom left of a sheet in a press eleven miles off, under the last line, on a paper with no day anywhere on it, and nobody in this county has ever said whose it is and it is not going to be taken back.

There is a column of a hundred and forty-one figures on the inside of a door in a tower four miles up the lane, with nothing whatever over the top of it, and the figures are all different, and the last nine of them are in a hand about two hundred years old, and the sum has not been made. It was refused twice out loud, on purpose, in front of about twenty people, by a man of fifty-two, and the reason he gives is the same one both times and it is that a column with nothing over it is not a total and adding it produces a number that is wrong about the world. *Nobody in eleven villages knows what those figures count. Not the man of thirty-one, and not a recordwright of twenty-eight who has read them off a door upside down, and not a clerk of forty-four, four hundred miles off, who has read about four thousand documents in twenty-two years and could not get the last line of the head of that plank in a stair in about four minutes.* And no room in any county has ever asked.

There is a fourth line on a printed form that a woman of about fifty at a gate read out loud in a yard, unasked, about seven weeks ago, and said it was a door, and that whoever wrote it in had meant to be decent about it, and that decent and useful are not the same trade, and that she was leaving it where it lay. It is not opened. It is not refused. Nobody has been asked.

And there is a bundle under the seat of a timber cart in this county with four lines in it about a water and a height and a day and a party to pay, copied and sealed at Sallowby at threepence, carried four hundred miles by a sawyer of about fifty-one who was given five shillings and four days and has not read it, and it is under a seat now and it will be under a seat on the day it goes, and it was not opened and not read out and not produced in any of the fifty chapters of this, and about four people know it is in this county and nobody has asked for it.

---

The market came into Coldharrow on the market day and the woman of about fifty took nine loads at the bar before midday at three pence apiece, and she read every one of them off her own board under the four letters and a stroke.

About four people in that yard knew where the day was going, because four bells and a market makes eight and eight is what the plank was about to say, and they knew it the way anybody in a village knows a thing, which is from the noise.

Nobody asked her a second question. That is the last state of that, and nobody in eleven villages has a form that would change it, and there is not going to be one.

A man of thirty-eight was at the end of the ninth cart with a hand on the side of it. He has called the same load account in nine times in nineteen years and has never once been paid and has gone on calling it in, and he is going to go on calling it in, and he has still never worked out why he does.

A boy of seventeen came up the lane out of Ellerby, his book under his arm and a stub of pencil he never puts down, and he has a question in it with a mark on the end of it that nobody has answered, and he can be asked about a third thing in about six weeks and has not been asked and does not want to be asked about two things at once.

And a woman of fifty-one was at a lane end with a handbell under her arm and did not ring it, because there was nothing to ring it for, which is about two hundred times a year.

---

The last bell went at about the last of the light and a woman of forty-one went up the ladder with a cloth over her shoulder and put four strokes up under the number and wiped the cloth over the two of them together, which is what she has done every morning for twenty-five years.

The day came to eight. Four bells and a market.

And she said the sum out loud on the ladder, to nobody, the way a person says a thing out loud in an empty tower, and it is worth exactly one line that she said it, because in twenty-five years she has never once said the number out loud, and she has said it now because there is a man in this country who has been in it fifty days and a figure in a stranger's hand has been in two counties for all of it, and she is forty-one and she has about as many years of it as a bell has of rope, and somebody is going to have to say numbers out loud in this tower eventually and there is nobody else.

Sixty-three thousand eight hundred and forty-one, and eight, is sixty-three thousand eight hundred and forty-nine.

It is not a round number either. Nobody chose that one either. There is no column for it, and there is no heading over it, and there is no name against it, and there is no year at the top of the board, and there never was one, and there is not going to be one.

And she came down the ladder and put the cloth over her shoulder and went to do her day.

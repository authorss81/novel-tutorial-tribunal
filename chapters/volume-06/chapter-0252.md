# Chapter 252: Four Bells A Day And Two That Are Not The Clock

At the second light on the second day of the fifty-eighth week the board had five more on it than it had at the second light the day before, and the woman of forty-one was in front of it with the cloth and the man of thirty-one was not invited anywhere and was not asked to leave.

The four more were the first bell and they are the same in every village in this country and they are the clock, and the fifth was a boy of seventeen who had gone over a stile at the third hour and had said what he was and had rung, and that is a stranger and a stranger is three.

“Three for a stranger,” said the man of thirty-one.

“Three for a stranger,” she agreed. “I have never written down why three and I have been asked about three twice in twenty-five years and both times it was a man from a county with a road in it who wanted to know whether we counted the ones who come in as well as the ones who live here, and the answer is yes, and the reason is that a stranger is a bell you did not choose the day of, and you would be a fool to let him take one of your own.”

She wiped the foot of the board, which did not need it.

“The four are the first, the change, the noon and the last,” she said. “The first is when the day starts and the last is when the day stops and the change is in the middle of it and the noon is when the light is on the tower. That is four a day and that is four hundred and sixty in a week and that is the clock, and the clock is the only part of that board anybody in this village would bet money on.”

“And the rest.”

“The rest is about two a day and it has been about two a day for as long as I have been counting,” she said, “and that is where I am going to tell you the thing you came here to be told, and I am going to tell it in one sentence because it has taken me twenty-five years and it does not need more than one.

“I cannot tell you what the other two a day are for, and neither can anybody else in this village, and it is not because we are hiding it. It is because nobody has ever written down one of them.”

---

A boundary is walked on the third of this week and about nine men went out of the village at the second light to do it, and the three of them were asked whether they might come and were told they might, and the reason they were told was not kindness, it was that a woman carrying a bucket said *they will ask me and I would rather have told them no on a morning than a week*.

So eleven of them walked a mile and a half along a hedge and a man of sixty-one put a mark on two elms and a woman of forty-one counted, and she counted out loud the whole way, which is the thing a bell-count does when the bell is out of hearing and she said why.

“Seven,” she said, at the second elm. “You hear it once to start and once at each corner and once at the end and twice coming back, and that is seven, and it is seven every time it is done, and it has been seven since before I started and my father did it and his father did it and there is no way to do it in six and nobody has ever tried.”

The man of thirty-one walked behind her and understood, about half a mile in, that he was looking at the only proof in this country that a strip of ground had ever been decided, and that the proof was seven sounds in the air on a particular afternoon, and that the woman ahead of him was the only living record of the afternoon.

It is the same finding twice. He had stood in front of a printed index in a room with eleven seats in it eight weeks ago and watched a clerk put a finger on a column headed with a word that means *not sent*, and he had spent a bench of four's good name on it, and he has not been able to put the two things side by side since. He put them side by side on a hedge in the Briar March on the second day of the fifty-eighth week, and the finding was: *a thing that happened once, and the only record of it is a woman saying the number out loud on a hedge in a particular year, is not a record and is not not a record. It is a third thing, and the Bench has no word for the third thing, and the Bench's word for the fourth thing is a column headed with a year.*

He did not say that out loud. He had learned in a valley four hundred miles off that the sentence that separates the people in a building from the people in a room is the sentence that protects the institution, and he is not the person who gets to hand the Bench a better word for itself, and he had the standing to say something on a hedge and there was nobody on the hedge to say it to.

---

The tally for a day, then, is a sum, and the sum has more than one thing in it, and the parts are figures and not coins.

A burial is one ring. A boundary walked is seven. A debt called in is two. A market is four. A stranger is three. A fire is two, one to raise the alarm and one to say it is out, and if it is out by the second ring the second ring is still rung, and a woman of forty-one said that she had never once known a village that did not ring the second one.

And the four of the clock.

“A man in a county with a road in it asked me in about four minutes on my daughter's wedding in the ninth year whether I could tell him how many burials there had been in this village since he was born,” she said, “and I told him I could not, and he was surprised, and I told him why, and he asked me the second question, which is whether I could tell him how many strangers, and I said no, and he said *surely you can tell me how many people*, and I said no, and that is the whole of it and I have been asked it four times in twenty-five years and I have given the same answer four times.”

A board that says fifty thousand three hundred and seventy-five does not know how many people died in a village of about two hundred and ten. It does not know how many men are owed money, or by whom, or whether a debt was ever paid, or whether a boundary was walked twice or once, or whether a road went in front of a field in the ninth year or the fourth. It knows how many times a bell went, and that is all it knows, and the two numbers are not the same and cannot be made the same by anybody who has ever stood in front of it.

“Now do the thing you came to do,” she said. “You have been doing it in your head since the yard yesterday and you have got it nearly right, so I will finish it and you can check me.

“Four a day is the clock, and four hundred and sixty a week, and about six a day is everything, and six times three hundred and sixty-five is two thousand one hundred and ninety. That is what a year of this board is. And a carter coming through rings once at each of eleven villages, and there are eleven villages between Coldharrow and Coldharrow-edge, and one is a hundred and ninety-nine times over with one over.

“So a year of this board is a hundred and ninety-nine whole passages of a man with a cart and one ring. And the one ring is most likely a stranger and the one ring might be anybody, and that is what you have, and I would like to know what you are going to do with it.”

---

The man of thirty-one told her what he was paid for, and the route-man of fifty-two told her what she was paid, and she told them hers first, which is the thing people in a country like this do.

“Ninepence a day and I am here a hundred and forty days in the year and it is the same ninepence whatever else is happening,” she said. “Ninepence times a hundred and forty is one thousand two hundred and sixty pence. Twelve pence to the shilling and twenty shillings to the pound, and one thousand two hundred and sixty is five pounds and five shillings, and I have had that for six years and it was eight before that and the eight was for a hundred and forty days as well and I never asked for the extra penny because I did not know it was extra.”

The route-man of fifty-two got a bit of lead out of his pocket and a scrap of paper and did not do anything with them, which for a man of his trade is a kind of shouting.

“Five pounds and five shillings,” he said. “For a year of the only record in the county.”

“For a year of the only record in this village,” she said. “There is one in every village and they are all different and none of them knows about the others, and there are eleven of us in this stretch and I have been to four of the others and I have never seen another board with a figure in the same hand as mine, and I have never seen one that was wrong either, and I have never met anybody who could tell me whose was which without being told.”

And a man of thirty-one stood in a road in a country he had walked into a day and a half before and found out that the entire price of the thing he had come to do was already known, to the penny, by the person it was going to be paid to, and that she had said it before he asked and had not looked at the number once.

---

The stranger came through at the fourth hour, on foot, with a stick, going the other way, and he was about fifty and he had not been in Coldharrow before and would not be again. He went down the lane and did not stop and did not look at the board, and the woman of forty-one went out to the gate of the second yard and stood there until he was past the last hedge, and the bell went three times, evenly, and she came back and counted them off on her fingers without looking at the board.

“Three,” she said, to nobody, and the man of thirty-one said “Thank you,” and she said:

“Don't. I have never worked out why it is three and I have been asked twice and I have told both of them the same thing I am telling you, which is that I do not know, and I would rather say that in a road than have somebody come back in a year with the answer and find that I wrote it down.”

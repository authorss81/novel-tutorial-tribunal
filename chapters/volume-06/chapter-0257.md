# Chapter 257: The Whole Of The Margin, With The Working Printed

The seventh day of the fifty-eighth week was the last day of that week and about nine people in Coldharrow noticed it, and nine of them noticed it because they count in rings and not because the week meant anything, and the man of thirty-one wrote a figure in chalk at the foot of a board for the fourth time in a week, and the figure at the foot was sixty-three thousand five hundred and fifty, and it was five more than it had been at the second light the day before, which was four bells and one for a stranger, and he wrote it because that was the arrangement he had made with a woman of forty-one and she had not agreed to the arrangement and had not stopped him.

The cart yard was working and the gate had done about nine loads and the man of fifty-two asked for a cart's side.

There were nine men in the yard and about half of them had been in the yard for the whole of his stay and had watched him say a number out loud on a cart's side twice and had come to a conclusion about him, which was that he was a fool, and the man of fifty-two had decided that the best use of the second hour of the seventh day was to be wrong in front of nine men who thought he was a fool.

Ferrow Hask was thirty-eight and he had walked the eleven-village lane with a handcart for nineteen years and had never been north of Coldharrow-edge with it because there is nothing north of Coldharrow-edge with it. He was the ninth load on a market day and the woman at the gate had told him he was out by one and had been right.

“Say the whole of it,” he said. “Say it slow. I have listened to two men say a figure in this yard in my life and both of them stopped about halfway and both of them were talking about the road and not about the money.”

---

“A day of me on this lane is one shilling and sixpence,” said Tarin Keel, “and I have been told that in a lane two days ago by a man of about forty-two who has counted carts on roads for twenty-two years, and I have checked it against a man of thirty-eight who does it and neither of them gains by me being wrong, and it is eighteen pence. Twelve pence to the shilling, and eighteen pence is a shilling and sixpence, and that is the day.”

“How many days.”

“A hundred and forty,” said Ferrow Hask. “Not a hundred and forty out of a year. A hundred and forty out of the year, and it is not the same hundred and forty every year and there is a year about every seven where it is a hundred and ten and I lose four pounds and I have never worked out where the four pounds went and it is not a market and it is not a gate and it is weather.”

“Hundred and forty it is, then, and I have used that figure in every sum I have done since I came into this country and I have not once said where I got it, and I got it from a man of fifty-two in a yard in a market town four hundred miles off, and he has been wrong about it, and I have not corrected it, and if the hundred and ten is the true one then everything I am about to say is out by about seven in the hundred, and I am going to say it at a hundred and forty and I am going to say where the figure came from, and that is the second honest thing I have done in two days and the first one did not go as well as this one is going.”

He put the lead on the cart's side.

“Eighteen pence a day. A hundred and forty days. Eighteen times a hundred and forty is eighteen times a hundred and forty hundred, and that is two thousand five hundred and twenty pence, and two thousand five hundred and twenty is two hundred and ten shillings, and twenty shillings to the pound, and that is ten pounds and ten shillings. *Ten pounds ten shillings is what a man of my trade earns on this lane in a year.*”

---

“Now the other side,” he said. “And this is where I have been wrong in my own head for seven days and I want it in the yard while I say it.

“The levy is threepence a load. Not on a market day. On every day there is a load. The gate has been at that post since before I could lift a cart’s iron and the price has not moved in nineteen years and I have asked twice and been told it is a foundation and I did not know what that meant until I was about sixteen.

“*How many loads a day go over that gate in an ordinary week.* I have said eight for seven days and I have been wrong and the woman at the post has been right and she told me on a market day in front of five people and I did it again in a yard this morning because I do it in my head and the road is nine loads and the road and the gate are not the same thing and I have been counting the road.

“Eight times a hundred and forty is eleven hundred and twenty loads a year. Nine times a hundred and forty is twelve hundred and sixty. Twelve hundred and sixty loads at threepence a load. Threepence is a quarter of a shilling and there are twenty shillings in a pound, and so a load is a pound of eighty pence, and a shilling is twelve pence and there are four pence over.

“Twelve hundred and sixty times three is three thousand seven hundred and eighty pence. Twelve pence to the shilling, and three thousand seven hundred and eighty is three hundred and fifteen shillings, and that is fifteen pounds and fifteen shillings. *Three thousand seven hundred and eighty pence is what the gate at Coldharrow takes in a year.*”

He put the lead down and looked at the yard.

“Now do the two together and it comes out at nothing, and that is the thing I have been not saying for seven days and I am saying it now.

“Three thousand seven hundred and eighty pence is what the gate takes. Two thousand five hundred and twenty pence is what a man earns. *Take one off the other. Three thousand seven hundred and eighty less two thousand five hundred and twenty is one thousand two hundred and sixty pence.*

“One thousand two hundred and sixty is one hundred and five shillings, and that is five pounds and five shillings a year. *And the woman of forty-one is paid ninepence a day for a hundred and forty days. Nine times a hundred and forty is one thousand two hundred and sixty pence. It is the same figure. It is not nearly the same figure. It is the same figure to the penny and I have got it twice from two directions and I have read it twice.*”

---

Nine men in a cart yard, and about four of them had understood the whole of it and about five had understood the sum, and the difference between those two numbers is the entire difference between this volume and every other volume in this project.

A man of thirty-eight stood at the end of a cart with his hand on the shaft and said, without any preamble and as though he had been carrying it about a yard and a half:

“Eleven villages.”

“Eleven villages,” said Tarin Keel.

“One thousand two hundred and sixty times eleven is thirteen thousand eight hundred and sixty pence. Twelve to the shilling is a thousand one hundred and fifty-five shillings, and that is fifty-seven pounds and fifteen shillings, and that is the whole of the margin, and it is the whole of what this lane is worth to the people who own it, and it is the whole of what is left after paying the people who walk it, and there is not one other penny in it anywhere in this county and I have been on it nineteen years and I know.”

He put both hands on the shaft.

“So the whole margin of a lane eleven villages own is fifty-seven pounds fifteen shillings a year,” he said, “and it goes on at the rate of five pounds five shillings each to eleven women with a cloth, and a hundred and forty days is about half the year, and the other half the year a bell goes four times a day for nothing, and the reason nobody has ever noticed any of this is that there is nobody in this county anybody is asking to look.”

“Which is the whole of what you have been trying to tell us,” said the man of thirty-one, “and you have told it in a yard in about six minutes with a piece of lead and I have been here seven days.”

“I have,” said Ferrow Hask. “And nobody in this county has ever asked me the question that makes it matter, and I have been waiting nineteen years, and here it is, and I have said it to about nine people and I would like you to notice that I said it to a man of thirty-one and not to a man of fifty-two, and it is not unkind of you, and it is because you asked me a question on the first day.”

---

“I want it to be a road,” he said.

Nobody in that yard argued with him and one man of about forty said *everybody does* and was told to be quiet by two people at once.

“I want it to be a road and I do not want a man from a county with a road in it to come and tell me that roads are for carts and I have got a cart. I have got this. I have had this nineteen years and I have had a worse one.

“*I want it to be a road because a road has a gate on it.* And this gate is a post with a bar across it that is not shut, and I have been over it about four thousand times in nineteen years and I have never once been stopped by it, and it has taken three pence off me nine times a day for nineteen years and I have never seen it do anything at all. There is no road here. There is a lane and a bar and a woman with a stick and a man who counts. And the man who counts is me, and I count because if I stop there is nothing, and nothing is not a road, and if I stop then the threepence stops and the five pounds five shillings stops and the whole of the fifty-seven pounds fifteen shillings stops, and I have never once in nineteen years seen anybody ask me what happens if I stop, and I have asked.”

The recordwright of twenty-eight had not said one word in a cart yard in seven days and said one now.

“Your gate takes three thousand seven hundred and eighty pence a year and does nothing,” she said, “and the whole of the reason it does nothing is that there is no road for it to be the gate of. That is not a figure, that is a sentence, and I am a recordwright and I have been waiting six days to write it down and I am not going to, because if I write it down somebody in a room nine days from this field will use it, and I have watched a room use a sentence like that one in another county and I am not doing it in a country that has a woman of forty-one in it.”

And a man of thirty-eight put his hand flat on a cart and said the last thing he said that day, and nobody answered it and about four people heard it.

“Nineteen years,” he said, “and I have never once been able to say what a gate is for. And there is nobody in this yard who can tell me, and I have asked that in four counties and I have been told it is for the toll and it is for the toll and then I have said, and what is the toll for, and then nobody has ever had an answer, and I have stopped expecting one and I have started keeping a list.”

# Chapter 253: What A Burial Gets And What A Stranger Gets

The third day of the fifty-eighth week was a burial, and the man of thirty-one got to the yard at the second light and found the woman of forty-one already at the board with her back to it, not doing anything, which in a yard at that hour is a thing you notice. The figure at the foot of the board that morning was fifty thousand three hundred and eighty-six, which was eleven more than the morning before, which was four bells and seven for a boundary walked, and the eleven was the largest single day's rise in the eight mornings he had been here, and he wrote it down because he wrote all of them down and had not yet worked out that the writing was the part that would matter least.

The figure had gone up five in the night. Four bells and one for a woman of eighty-nine whose name he got wrong and then got right again on the third asking, which is ordinary and which he would have got wrong once, in a town, when he had not spent a month being told by a woman with a barrow that a thing you have been told twice is a thing you have been told.

He waited until the noon bell, which is the one you can time, and then he asked her the question he had come to ask.

“How many days in a row do you get the same number?”

She turned round.

“That is the second useful question anybody has asked me in two days,” she said, “and the first one came from a man of twenty-eight yesterday and it was about a stranger, and I would like it noted that neither of you asked me what my name is.”

“Ottily Roke.”

“That is my name and I have never said it out loud in a yard, and I have said it now because you have both been in it two days and one of you is going to write something down eventually and I would rather it were right.”

---

So they did three days, and then eleven, because three is not enough to show anybody anything and eleven is enough to show them that they are looking at a sum and not at a number.

The sum has these in it and every one of them is a figure and none of them is a coin.

A burial is one. A boundary walked is seven. A debt called in is two. A market is four. A stranger is three. A fire is two, one to raise it and one to say it is out. And four a day for the clock, in every village, whatever else is happening, including on the day of a burial, including on the day a man of sixty-one cut a thumb in a hedge and swore at it for two bells' worth.

“That is the whole of the list,” said Ottily Roke. “And I will tell you what is wrong with the list, and you have worked it out and I watched you work it out yesterday, so I will save you the day.

“The list is complete. There is nothing else. A man from a county with a road in it will tell you there must be something else — a christening, a marriage, a boundary, a fire, a stranger, a debt, a market — and I will tell him he is wrong about two of them and right about the rest. *We do not ring for a christening or a marriage. We ring for a death, and we ring for a thing that has to be known now by people who are not in this village, and that is the whole of the list, and it is a short list and it is complete.* And the four a day are on top of it and they are not in it.”

“So a day's figure is not how many things happened,” said the man of thirty-one. “It is how much they weighed.”

“It is a sum of numbers that mean different things,” she said, “and I have never had anybody say that to me before and I have been counting for twenty-five years, and you have said it in about four seconds and I would like you to understand that I am not grateful and that I am not going to argue with you either, because you are the first person in a county with a road in it who has got it right in one go and I have had four wrong ones.”

---

And then the man of thirty-one went up the board with the cloth in his hand and a bit of lead and did the thing that the recordwright of twenty-eight watched him do and wrote nothing down about, and it is the whole of what he brought to this country.

The older hand is six years of the board and it is in the grain and the newer one is twenty-five and it is not. *And both of them are the same hand, in the sense that matters, which is that whoever made the strokes had the same idea in his head, and the idea was four strokes a day and no column and no reason.*

He found the same week of the year before, because a board is a board and a week is about thirty strokes and he could count strokes the way he used to count state transitions, without meaning to, and in about four minutes he had it.

“Same week, last year,” he said. “Nine, four, eleven, four, four, thirteen, four.”

“And the week before it,” said Ottily Roke, behind him. “Six, four, four, four, two, four, four.”

He put the lead down and did not say anything for long enough that a man of about forty came across a yard to ask whether he was unwell.

“Six days of four in a week,” he said. “And four days of four in another week. And in a week that is not this week's there are two days that are not four, and I can tell you which strokes they are because the ones that are not four are longer, and the man who made them put the downstroke first and a man who is nearly out of the day puts the upstroke first.”

“You can tell me the strokes,” she said.

“I can tell you the strokes.”

“You cannot tell me the day.”

“I cannot tell you the day,” he said, “and you knew that before I did, and you have known it for twenty-five years, and you are the only person in this village who could possibly know it, and nobody has ever asked you, and I am asking you now and I am not going to like the answer and I am asking anyway because I am going to be here for about a week.”

She took the lead off the board out of his hand, which she has done to two other men in her life, and put it in her own pocket.

“I cannot tell you the day,” she said. “I can tell you what a bell went for. I know every one of them that I counted and I know the year, because I count the year on the fell, and I could not tell you the day of one of them if you put a knife to me, and there are about two thousand one hundred and ninety a year and I have done that for twenty-five years."

She stopped, and then said the rest of it, and it is the sentence the whole of this volume is built on and she said it to a board and not to him.

“I have been asked twice why I do not write down what it was for, and both times I said it was in the tower, and it is in the tower and I can read it. And the answer is that it is not in the tower, and it is in me, and I am fifty-nine when this is finished being my job, and there is nobody coming."

---

The man of sixty-three was in the yard at the second light on that day and had been in it on the day before, and he had a coat that had been good about eleven years ago, and there was nothing in the world about him that was difficult.

“Terrel,” said Ottily Roke. “Garrick Terrel, and he comes twice a year and has come every year since before I started, and he sells the bell, and if you want a thing in this country that is worth arguing about, that is the thing, because he has quoted the same price for eleven years and it has gone up twice and the second time was the bad one.”

“What is the price,” said the man of thirty-one.

Terrel said it.

“Metal at four shillings the pound and the bell is thirty-one pounds,” he said, “and the frame and the rope is eleven shillings, and that is the whole of it and it is the same as it was in the spring and I have quoted it in this yard twice this year and I have quoted it three times in eleven years and it was four pounds twelve when I started quoting it and five pounds six after that, and I will tell you what moved it, because a man should know, and what moved it is the metal and not the frame and not me.”

The route-man of fifty-two had the bit of lead out and did the whole of it out loud on a cart's side, which is what he does, and which nobody in this yard has ever seen anybody do and which every person in it stayed to watch.

“Four shillings the pound is forty-eight pence,” said Tarin Keel. “Thirty-one pounds is thirty-one times forty-eight. Thirty times forty-eight is fourteen hundred and forty, and one more forty-eight is fourteen hundred and eighty-eight, and that is six pounds and four shillings, because twelve pence to the shilling and twenty shillings to the pound, and six pounds is fourteen hundred and forty and there are forty-eight left over, which is four shillings. The frame and the rope at eleven shillings is a hundred and thirty-two pence. Fourteen hundred and eighty-eight and a hundred and thirty-two is sixteen hundred and twenty pence, and sixteen hundred and twenty is six pounds and fifteen shillings, because six pounds is fourteen hundred and forty and there are a hundred and eighty left, which is fifteen shillings. *Six pounds fifteen shillings, hung, with the rope on, and that is the price and I have read it twice and I have not found a mistake.*”

“And what does she get for a year of it,” said the man of thirty-one, and he did not say her name and Terrel knew who he meant.

“Ninepence a day for a hundred and forty days,” said Ottily Roke. “One thousand two hundred and sixty pence. Twelve pence to the shilling, and that is a hundred and five shillings, and twenty shillings to the pound, and that is five pounds and five shillings.”

“Six pounds fifteen against five pounds five,” said Tarin Keel. “Six pounds fifteen is a hundred and thirty-five shillings. Five pounds five is a hundred and five. The difference is thirty shillings, and thirty shillings is a pound and ten. *A bell is a pound and ten shillings more than a year of the woman who keeps the count.*”

Nobody said anything for a while, and the yard went on being a yard, and about nine people of two hundred and ten were standing in it in the morning and eleven of them had heard that sentence and about four of them would still be able to say it in a year's time.

“That's the arithmetic,” said Garrick Terrel. “I sell the bell. I have sold about thirty of them and I have never once had a village tell me it cannot afford mine, and I have thought about that a great deal in eleven years and I have worked out that it is not because villages are rich. *It is because nobody has ever set the bell against the woman.* You would have to be a fool or a bastard to do that in a yard, and there has never been a fool or a bastard in this yard that I know of, and I would not be one either, and I have said the price about two hundred times and I have never once said the other half."

He put his hand flat on the cart's side where the man of fifty-two had done the sum.

“The two villages up the lane that have no bell have no board either,” he said. “And they have no idea that they are the only two in eleven, and they are not the only two in the county, and the reason nobody has a bell is that the last man in a village who wanted one went without, and the reason he went without is that he could not ask the woman, because she was not there yet, and the reason she was not there yet is that there was no money for the woman before there was no money for the bell, and that went the other way about thirty years before I was born, and I have never worked out how, and I have thought about it more than is good for a man who sells bells.”

---

The man of thirty-one went and found the boy of seventeen, because the boy had rung a bell at a stile before he had said his name and had said what he was, and because a man who rings what he crosses and says the rule aloud first is a man who keeps something.

He had it in a satchel and it was a book about the size of a hand, and the cover of it was oiled cloth with a corner turned up, and it was the fourth book of that size the man of thirty-one had been shown in about a month and three of them had been in rooms four hundred miles off with a key in a clerk's coat.

“That is a line book,” said Bram Orren, before he was asked. “That is mine. I have run a line since I was twelve and I write down what crosses and when and in what order, and I write down what does not cross, and I have kept it since I was twelve and I am seventeen and I have been doing it five years and I have never lost a day of it."

He did not open it.

"And I know what you are going to ask me," he said, "and I have had two men ask me and one of them was from a county with a road in it and he asked me what it was for."

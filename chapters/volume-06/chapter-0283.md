# Chapter 283: A Dispute That Goes The Right Way By Accident

Nine strangers came up the lane on the fifth day of the sixty-second week, three at each of three gates, and the woman of about fifty who keeps the only board in eleven villages with a word at the top of it counted all nine of them off it in about four minutes and had them on the wall by the second hour, and the number on the wall at Coldharrow had been sixty-three thousand seven hundred and twenty-eight when the cloth went over it, and a figure of thirteen on that plank is a thing that has happened twice in about four years and the woman who put it there is the only person in the county who knows both of the days and has been asked about neither of them.

Three of the nine were the men from four hundred miles off, and one of those three was a man of thirty-four who had got off a cart at the bar on the second day of that week and gone home four hundred miles and come back up the lane on the third, and who had been on this lane three days since and had not been in a yard, and nobody in two hundred and ten people had asked him to and nobody was going to.

The other six were a timber cart and two men with a barrow and three men walking behind them who belonged to none of them.

---

The accident happened at about the third hour, in the middle of the busiest morning the village has had in a fortnight, and it took about four minutes, and it is the only time anybody in this lane can remember that an argument ended well, and nobody in eleven villages had anything to do with it and nobody in eleven villages is going to be told they did.

There is a lane off the Coldharrow lane about ninety yards long with four houses on it and a shed at the end of it, and the man who owns the shed is about thirty-one and makes hurdles and gates, and he had a hurdle frame in his hand when he came out of his own door about halfway along.

A man of fifty-six was standing in that door.

He was standing in it with a barrow behind him, and he had been on his way to the ninth house with a barrow-load of something, and he had been arguing with the man who owns the shed about a load of hurdle frames for about a year. It is not a serious quarrel. It is the kind that a man in a lane has with a man in a lane about whether a barrow-load is a barrow-load, and it has gone on long enough that the two of them can do it in about nine minutes without either of them raising their voice, and about half the lane can tell you which of them is right.

The man with the hurdle frame came out of his own door, and there was a man of fifty-six in his own door, and neither of them had come round the corner deliberately and both of them stopped in about half a second.

Neither of them said anything for about four seconds.

Then the man of fifty-six said, “That is not a barrow-load,” and the man of about thirty-one said, “It is,” and the man of fifty-six said, “It is not, and I have said so for a year,” and the man of about thirty-one said, “And I have said so for a year.”

And then they both stopped, and neither of them could afterwards account for why, and the man of fifty-six put his hands on the handles of his barrow and said, “Well,” and the man of about thirty-one said, “Well,” and the man of fifty-six picked up his barrow and went on up the lane to the ninth house and did the thing he had come to do and it took about nine minutes and he came back down the lane with an empty barrow.

That is the whole of it.

---

The debt at the ninth house was not paid.

The woman who lives there is about sixty and she has not opened that door to a man with a barrow since the fourth day of the fifty-eighth week, and there is no form in this county that says a man can make her, and a man of fifty-two said out loud on that morning that what a village has got instead of a court is a bell and two men and a shut door, and the whole of two hundred and ten people standing in a lane, and that no clerk can do that.

Nobody paid. The man of fifty-six came back with an empty barrow and the woman of about sixty was still behind her own door and the whole of Coldharrow knew before the noon bell that there was a debt at the ninth house and knew by the last bell that it had not been paid, exactly as it goes every time.

And the other thing, the one nobody had been arguing about for a year, went on going on. The man of fifty-six has not taken the ninth house's part since, because he does not take a part until his own is settled, and his own is not settled, and that has nothing whatever to do with the ninth house and everything to do with a door in a lane ninety yards long that he was standing in at the third hour of the morning.

Nobody in that lane has said that. It has not been worked out by anybody.

---

The man of thirty-one was in the lane at the fourth hour and had been in it at the third, standing at the bar, and he had seen the whole of it from about forty yards and had not moved.

Then he went eleven miles, which is a long way to walk in an afternoon for a thing that took four minutes, and he did it anyway, and he did not tell anybody he was going, and the recordwright of twenty-eight walked with him for the first four miles and then said she was going no further and went on to a village where there is a person she has to see about nothing.

The press at Coldharrow-edge is the only room in eleven villages with a door on it, and the woman who keeps it is twenty-nine and has a wife and two children, and she is paid by the day she sells and sells about forty days a year, and the reason she is paid by the day she sells is that a day is the only thing anybody in this county can put a name to and she has worked that out in about four years and she has never once said it out loud to anybody.

She was setting a page when he came in and she did not stop setting it.

“There is a thing happened at Coldharrow this morning,” he said. “Four minutes. Two men at a door in a lane ninety yards long have been arguing about a barrow-load for about a year and they stopped in the middle of it and nobody knows why and neither of them can say. I want it on a sheet. I want the four words *it went the right way by accident* at the top of it and the day under that and the two men's names, and I will pay you the same as you get for a date.”

She finished the line she was setting and put the forme down and looked at him for about four seconds.

“No,” said Nell Callow.

“I will pay you more than a date.”

“I know you will,” said Nell Callow, “and that is not why. *A paper you produce is a thing you can use, and I have been on the other end of one of those in this county, and so have nine other people, and it was on the sixth day of the sixtieth week when the sheet came back down the lane with a figure at the foot of it, and a number came out of it that nobody can account for and it is still in the county and nobody has said whose hand it is in.* So I know what a paper does down here and I am not going to be the one who finds out what a paper does about an accident.”

---

“Write it as luck,” he said. “Write the word luck at the top of it. Then nobody can use it because it is not a thing anybody did.”

“There is a worse one,” said Nell Callow, “and I have thought about it for about a minute while you were standing there, and it is worse than the one you have just said and I am going to say it and then I am going to go back to my page.

“If I write it as luck, then this county has got a piece of paper that says a thing went the right way once, and it is in a room in eleven villages, and it can be carried. And the first man who comes in here with a second thing that went the right way will put it next to the first one, because that is what a room is for. And then somebody in about four years will have got four, and they will be in a row, and they will be about a thing nobody can do twice on purpose, and *a paper that records luck is a thing that gets used as if it were method, and it will not be anybody's fault when it does, because I will have written it and I will have written the word luck on it.*”

He stood in a press with a cracked steel pen in his pocket that he insists is useful and had nothing to say for about nine seconds.

“You are right,” he said.

“I know I am right,” said Nell Callow. “I have been right about about four things in this county in four years and I have not made a penny out of being right and I have never once been thanked for it and I am not going to start, and I would like you to notice that I have not said no to you out of anything except that you asked me to write down a thing that went right by accident, and I have written down four hundred dates in this room and every one of them was a thing somebody did and cost somebody something and I would write that all day.”

---

“There is another half of it and it is worse,” she said, without being asked, which is a thing she does about once a fortnight.

“Two men in a lane stopped arguing because they turned a corner and stood in each other's doorways. That is not luck. *That is a door.* A door is a thing that makes two people be in the same place at the same time, and this county has got about two hundred of them and nobody has ever thought of one as anything, and if I write a sheet about four minutes in a lane, then in about four years somebody is going to write a sheet about doors, and then somebody is going to come into this room and ask me for a thing that makes two people be in the same place on purpose, and there is no form in this county that says a man may do that, and there is no room in this county that could refuse him either.”

She picked the forme up again.

“There is one thing I will do with it,” she said, “and I am telling you so that you know I have done it and not that I have refused you, and it is the whole of what I have for it.

“The register is on my shelf. Four sheets cut out of the back of a bill. A book. Fifty-one places on it and not one village in it. *It has been on that shelf since the seventh day of the sixty-first week, and in that time I have had a woman who keeps a bell in it twice and a carter in it twice and about four people I did not expect in it once each, and every one of them asked me what it cost, and I have said nothing to any of them, and none of them asked twice.* It is the only thing I have that is worth more than my press and I have not told anybody that and I am telling you because you have walked eleven miles and because you asked me for a paper and I have said no.”

---

They walked back. She did not offer the room and he did not ask, and about a third of the way he said, “There is a man in this county who has been in it a month putting four things next to each other, and on the first day of this week he stood in a yard in front of nine people and said out loud what the doing costs, and the bill is that whoever stands at the centre of each thing learns the whole of the other one without anybody putting them in the same place, and neither of them asked for that. And this morning in a lane ninety yards long, two men who have been arguing for about a year stopped arguing, in front of nobody, for no reason at all.”

“And you have come to tell me that a thing that goes right by accident is the same shape as a thing that goes right on purpose,” said Nell Callow. “I have had that out of you in about a minute. *I do not want it and there is no form in this county that says I have to have it, and I have been keeping records about six years and the two worst ones I ever kept were both about a thing that went right once.*”

He stood in her doorway for about a second longer than he needed to.

“The nine strangers that came through this morning,” he said. “Three of them were the three men off that door. One of them is the man of thirty-four and he came back up the lane today and stood nine yards off a yard for about an hour and a half and did not come into it and did not go away.”

“He is not here,” said Nell Callow, and then gave him the rest of it, which she had said she was going to give him and had not.

“I can see you have thought about asking him about nine times this week,” she said, “and I can see it because you stand the way a man stands when he has got an order in him and no place to put it down. *Ask him or do not ask him, that is your half of it. What I am not going to have is you deciding for him that a thing about him is a thing about a sheet. A man who has not offered you a thing has not given it to you, and this room has taken about four hundred things off people who had not offered them and every one of those is still in this county.*”

And then he went eleven miles back, and the leg was the leg, and the day was a day of thirteen, and at the second light the next morning the woman of forty-one had the four of the ordinary morning going up under her arm and the nine of the day before still standing on the wall above it with nothing over them, and she did not know what any of the nine were for and was not asked, and there is no column on that plank and there is no heading over it and there never was one, and nobody in two hundred and ten people has ever asked her what a single stroke of it was for.

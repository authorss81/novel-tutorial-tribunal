# Chapter 272: The Two Villages That Have Neither

They went up the lane at about the first light on the first day of the sixty-first week, and the figure at the head of the board at Coldharrow said sixty-three thousand six hundred and fifty-one at the second light, and it said four and nothing else, and the man of fifty-two said out loud on the first mile that a stretch of three mornings of four was a thing that a woman in a carter's yard four counties off had asked a room for in her own lifetime, and that he had heard about it once and had not understood it and did now, and that he was going to be thinking about it for the rest of the walk.

The woman of forty-one came, and nobody had asked her to and she had not said she was coming, and the reason she gave when the man of thirty-one asked it at the gate was the reason she gives for everything.

“Eleven villages,” she said. “Two of them have got nothing to count and I have been going round eleven bars for twenty-two years and I have got a figure in my head about the two of them that I have never said out loud, and it has not been a week since a man of sixty-three said it out loud in a yard, and if I am not there he will say it again and there will be nobody in that yard to hear whether it is true.”

Nine miles is Ellerby and the two of them went through it and did not stop, and the woman of about sixty was in her doorway with a sheet of paper in her apron and looked at the three of them go past and said nothing, and one of them lifted a hand, and that was the whole of it.

There was a thing he had gone to look in, in himself, the way a man goes to a room, and it had not had anything in it for a long time, and he noticed on the fourth mile that he had stopped going, and he did not finish noticing, and he walked on.

---

Trope is the fourth village up the lane and it is about a hundred and twenty people, and you can see the wheel of the mill from about four hundred yards off, and it is turning, and there has never been a tower in Trope in anybody living's time and there is no bell in it and there is no board in it and the woman of about fifty who was going round the eleven bars had never once had a stroke to put down there.

There is a bar across the lane end. It is not shut. It is a bar on two uprights and it has been there since before the mill and nobody shuts it, and it is not a gate and it does not take anything and it is not a gate for a road, because there is no road in Trope either.

“You are late,” said a man of about fifty-six, “for a thing that is not happening.”

“There is no bell in this village,” said the man of thirty-one, “and there has not been one in anybody's time, and there is nobody in eleven villages who can tell me why.”

The man of fifty-six put a hurdle down on the ground and looked at him for a bit.

“No,” he said. “And I have a handbell and I have had one since I was nine years old, and my wife's mother had one before me, and that is the whole of it, and if you have come up nine miles to be told there is a handbell in Trope you have come up nine miles for nothing and I am sorry for it.”

---

The woman of about fifty was in a house at the far end of the lane and she came out when she heard three voices, and she was called Isel Cardew and she was fifty-one and she had two grown daughters and a gate bar she had never shut, and she rang a handbell at the lane end when something had to be rung.

They asked her about the tower at Nettlebed and she said it was a tower.

They asked her who taught her to ring the handbell and she said the handbell was the handbell.

They asked her where she learned to ring it at the lane end, and she looked at the man of thirty-one for about four seconds and then she said the sentence that the whole of this day turned on, and she said it in about nine words and she had clearly had it ready for a long time.

“You have asked me the wrong question three times,” said Isel Cardew. “You want to know why we have not got a bell. There is no why. There has not been one since before I could ask one.”

---

And the man of sixty-three was in the yard at Trope, and he had a coat that had been good about eleven years ago, and he had come the day before and had slept at the mill and had not gone home, and he had been there when the three of them came up the lane, and he was in it before anybody spoke.

He had asked about Trope and Nettlebed eleven times in eleven years. He had asked every autumn when he came round, and he had asked them at the two houses and at the bar and once in the lane, and he had never once got a different answer, and the answer he had always got was that the two villages could not afford one, and he had never believed it, and he had a coat about him that had cost him money in a year he does not talk about.

“They have told you the same thing I have been told for eleven years,” he said, to the man of thirty-one, and not to the woman. “Not that they cannot afford a bell. *That they cannot afford a bell.* Same words, same order, twice a year, two villages, and the mill has a wheel you can see from the road, so you will understand that I have not spent eleven years unable to do the sum.”

“Garrick,” said the woman of forty-one.

“I am not being funny,” said Garrick Terrel. “I have quoted six pounds fifteen in this lane three times in eleven years and it is the same as it was in the spring and it has gone up twice and the second time was the bad one, and I have got a list in my head of every village in this March that has ordered a bell off me, and it is about thirty, and there are eleven on this lane, and nine of the eleven have one and two of the eleven have not got a tower between them and have never once told me they could not afford mine.”

“They have not told you anything,” said the man of thirty-one.

“They have not told me anything at all,” said Garrick Terrel, “and the not-telling is worse than a no would have been, and I have had a theory about it for eleven years and the theory is that the last man in a village to want one of these went without. He could not ask a woman for it. The woman was not there yet. The money for the woman ran out before the money for the bell did. *And that is the wrong way round, and the wrong way round began a generation before there was a me to be confused by it, and I have not got an answer and I have chewed on it in four counties until I would rather not be asked.*”

Nobody in that yard worked it out. Nobody in that yard was going to. And he had a sentence available that would have moved the whole of it nine miles in about four minutes, and he did not have it, and it was not a sentence about bells, and he stood at a gate that is not shut and put his hands behind his back.

“I am not the man for this,” said Tarin Keel, to Garrick Terrel, and to the yard, and to nobody. “I have been in nine counties and I count what crosses and I do not know the name of a thing that is not in the count, and there is a woman in this village who has rung a handbell for forty years and has never once asked me for a number and I am not going to ask her for one.”

---

Nettlebed is the sixth up the lane and it is about ninety people, and there is a tower in it.

That is the whole of the remarkable thing about Nettlebed and it is remarkable, because there is no bell in it. The tower is built of blocks and it is about four times the height of a house and it has a door in it and the door has a hasp and a staple and there is nothing on the staple, and there is nothing in the tower, and there has been nothing in the tower for about two hundred years, and there are two hundred and ten people in Coldharrow who have never had any reason to walk past it and a lane of eleven villages that goes within about thirty yards of it four times a day.

“It is shut,” said a man of about thirty-one who was standing in his own doorway about two yards off with a hurdle in his hand.

“It is not shut,” said the man of fifty-two. “There is no hasp on it. There is a staple and there is a hole in the staple where a hasp has been, and the hole is rusted round, and a hasp has not been in it for a long time.”

“It is shut to me.”

“It is shut to you,” said the man of thirty-one, “and that is a different thing and I have come nine miles to find out which one it is and I have found out.”

---

The key was on a nail inside a house at the tower foot, and the man in the doorway had it on a nail because his grandmother had put it there and had never once said what it was for and he had never once thrown it out, and he was about thirty-one and he had been in this village all his life and he had a hurdle in his hand and a key on a nail and no opinion whatever about either.

Nobody went in that day.

That is worth saying plainly, because it is the first thing in twenty-two days that anybody has refused to do and there was no form for it and no vote and no falling out: three people walked nine miles and stood at a door for about a quarter of an hour and did not open it, and the man of thirty-one was the one who said so, and he said it because he had a form of words for it and the other two had not a single one between them.

“It is not mine to open,” he said. “There is no form anywhere in eleven villages that says a person may go into another village's tower, and I have been in this country twenty-two days and I have got no party and no bench and no office and no name on anything, and if I open that door then the first thing that ever happens in it is a stranger opened it, and everything anybody finds in there afterwards belongs to the door and not to the village.”

“You have not been asked either,” said the woman of forty-one.

“No,” he said. “That is the whole of my standing in this county and it is the same standing I have got at a bar and in a yard and at a press, and it is the reason I am here and it is the reason I am standing on a path doing nothing, and I would rather be honest about it than be the man who went in.”

And the woman of forty-one, who had been up nine miles on a day that is one of her hundred and forty and is paid ninepence, said the thing that the man of fifty-two had said on the fifth day of the fifty-ninth week in a different village and in about half the words, and she said it to the door and not to anybody.

“There is nothing in there that I have to know about this morning,” she said, “and the reason I know that is that you cannot make a day out of a door. You can make a length out of a door. I have had three mornings of four in a row and that is nine seconds of arithmetic and it is the only thing I have got, and it is worth more than the door, and I am going to write the three down when I get home because I have not got a place to write them and I am going to write them on the end of my cloth, which is a thing I have never done and which I am not going to do again.”

---

They came down the lane at about the last of the light, and the woman of forty-one said the arithmetic out loud on the second mile, which she had never done on a road in twenty-five years, and it came out of her the way it comes out of the man of fifty-two and not the way it comes out of anybody else, and it was this: forty-four and forty-four and forty-four, and that is a hundred and thirty-two, and the three of those are the last three mornings, and a hundred and thirty-two is also nineteen days, and she has known that since the sixth day of the sixtieth week and so has the yard.

And at the gate at Coldharrow he said goodnight to a woman of about fifty and she said it back, and he had a day of four in a country where a day of four is the ordinary one and he had walked eighteen miles and had been refused a door by a village of about ninety people and had been right to be, and the leg was the leg and the thing he was carrying was still off nothing and nobody in eleven villages knew that and nobody was going to be told.

And at about the last of the light a carrier came through on foot with a barrow and a bundle, and would not say where it had come from, and put a letter on the cart's side, and neither did the man of fifty-six with the hurdles, who had come down the lane behind the three of them without saying a word and had gone back up it again, and had not asked what was in the tower, and had said, at the gate, one thing that he said to nobody in particular.

“There is a man at the tower foot asking for a day,” he said. “Forty-four, a satchel, no cart, and he has been asking since about the fourth hour and he has asked me twice and I have told him twice that I do not know what a day is, and I have told him twice because the first time I thought he was selling something and the second time I thought he was mad, and I would like it noticed that I told him the same thing twice.”

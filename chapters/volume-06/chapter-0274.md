# Chapter 274: The Column With No Heading

The recordwright of twenty-eight came back into the county at about the last of the light on the second day of the sixty-first week, having walked eleven miles there and eleven miles back in two days and a night, and the figure at the head of the board at Coldharrow said sixty-three thousand six hundred and fifty-five at the second light and sixty-three thousand six hundred and sixty-six at the second light on the third day of that week, and it was four bells and nothing on top of them.

She had done the thing she said she was going to do, and it took about a quarter of an hour to say and she said it standing at the bar with her boots wet to the knee.

“I handed it to a clerk in a town that keeps weeks I have never learned,” she said, “and I said my sentence, the whole of it, in my own mouth, and he wrote it down, and then he asked me what board I meant, and I told him, and he asked me how many hands the board had, and I said two and a third in chalk, and he wrote all three down, and then he asked me to look up the head of his column while he was in the room, and I did, and I read it out, and I would like it printed somewhere that there is a word for what he has at the top of that column and that it is the ordinary word a clerk writes over a list of things that did not go.

“He is the fourth person in a fortnight who has been in that building and looked at a form and asked me the same three questions in the same order,” said Sera Quill. “And none of them are cruel and all of them are correct, and two of them put the only name I have ever had into a record in a valley that has never heard of it, and I got a bad afternoon out of exactly one of the four and I want him to have it for the rest of his career. *And I have not written one word of it down, because the writing of it down is what the sentence is against.*”

Nobody in the yard said that she should have done otherwise and about four people understood why and the rest of the yard did not, and the day went on, and four bells went on the board at the second light and nothing else did.

---

They went up the lane again on the third day of that week and this time about twenty people from two villages came up the lane with them, and the woman of about fifty-one from Trope came, and she had walked about four miles to a tower she had not been inside of in her life, and she said so at the foot of the stair and nobody made a thing of it.

The reading was done three times and the three were not the same and all three were right, and that is the finding, and it is the finding of a woman of twenty-eight who has been in this country twenty-four days and who said nine words three times in the first three weeks of that and has not said them since.

Somebody had to read it out, because a thing said in a stair of eleven worn steps to nine people is a thing that nine people have heard, and a thing said in a yard to two hundred and ten is a thing that a village has, and this plank had been read to nobody at all for about four hundred years.

So it was read three times and the three were not the same and all three were right.

The first had come out of a woman’s mouth at a bar three days ago, in this county, word for word, unasked for, and the recordwright had not been in the county when it happened and had been sitting with the shape of it in a press ever since.

“Any one may say no,” said Sera Quill, at the top of the stair, in the ordinary voice, to about eleven people on the steps and about nine more at the foot of them.

And then, about nine seconds afterwards, in the same doorway, a second time and longer, with a different word in the middle of it: “Any one may refuse, and may be asked the reason.”

“That is the thing about it that is my whole trade and it is not a small one,” she said. *The first is what the hand says and the second is what the hand has been taken to mean for four hundred years, and they are both right, and the difference between them is a thing that has been happening in that sentence the entire time it has been standing on that door, and I have never in my life not been able to get that difference, and I cannot get this one, and I have not got a word for what that is.*”

“Which one is the one they wrote.”

“Neither,” said Sera Quill. “That is what I have been trying to work out since a wet morning on a lane. There was never a fixed one. The first one is what a person of this county would have said in the year it was written, and the second one is what a person of this county has been saying for about three hundred of the years since, and a recordwright is a person whose whole life is the difference between the two, and I have just told you that I cannot get it, and I have never once in my life not been able to get it, and I am not frightened of that, I am the opposite of frightened of it, and I have not got a word for what I am.”

---

And then the column, and the man of fifty-two got the lead out of his pocket for the first time in three days and did not put it on anything, and about eleven people on a stair of eleven worn steps and about nine more at the foot of it listened to a man count.

“A hundred and forty-one names,” said Tarin Keel. “Seventy-odd of them are in the other ten villages and every one of them can be got at, and the one in the blue coat cannot, and they have left him on, and a name with nobody at the end of it is still better than a number and everybody in this country has understood that without ever putting it in a sentence. And against every one of the hundred and forty-one there is a figure, and over the column there is nothing.”

“So it is a bill,” said the man of thirty-one.

“No,” said Tarin Keel, and he said it faster than he said anything else in that tower, and it is worth saying that he was right. “I am not adding it up. I have got the lead out and I am not going to add it up, and I have been in nine counties and I have never once not added a column up, and the reason I am not going to is that a column with nothing over it is not a total. It is a set of figures that somebody wrote down, and adding them together gives me a number that is wrong about the world, and there is one of those about once a week in this county and the woman of forty-one has spent twenty-five years not making any.”

“The woman of forty-one would know,” he said.

“She would know,” said the woman of forty-one, who was on the fourth step and had not come up any further, because she is a woman of forty-one and eleven steps is a great many steps. “And she is right not to be told by you, and I would rather it was you than me, and the reason is that if I read it out then it is a woman of forty-one reading out a number in a yard, and there is no column on my board that says who did what, and we had all of that out loud on the sixth day of the sixtieth week and I am not going to have it again about a different number.”

“About this lot I can tell you the half of it that is a fact about a document,” said the clerk of forty-four, from the top step, and he had not said a word for about nine minutes and he had been reading the figures from about a foot off the board without touching it. “It is a figure against every name. *There is no name in it.* No man wrote it for a woman and no clerk wrote it for a village. It is a figure and a name, side by side, and nothing to say that the figure belongs to the name, and I have handled about four thousand documents in twenty-two years and I have never once seen a document with that in it, and the only thing I have ever seen like it is a tally, and a tally is a thing that counts something you have not written down.”

---

And the woman of about fifty-one from Trope had not been on the stair for the counting at all, because she had gone to the window, which is a slit about a hand wide and about two feet off the floor, and she had been looking out of it, and when she came back she said the thing that is the whole of the twenty-four days, and she said the first of it in about nine words and then went on for about four minutes, and nobody interrupted her and nobody had asked her.

“That is my grandmother's hand,” she said. “The last nine of them down are my grandmother's hand. And behind that is my great-grandmother's, and behind that is a hand I have never seen and have never been shown and could not read if it was held up in front of me. And the column stops there. *The last figure anybody put in it is about two hundred years old, and it is my grandmother's, and she never told me what any of it was for, and she was dead when I was eleven.*”

“You have never asked her.”

“She was dead when I was eleven,” said Isel Cardew, “and before that she would not have told me, and I know that now and I did not know it then, and I have had about thirty years of being angry with a woman for not telling me a thing, and I have had about thirty years of a tower in a village of about ninety people four miles from my gate, and I have not once in thirty years gone up it, and I would like somebody to say out loud that that is not a thing I am to be blamed for, and then I would like to go home and do my day.”

The man of fifty-two said it out loud, in about nine words, and Isel Cardew said thank you and did not elaborate, and it was the only thanks anybody said in that tower in twenty-four days.

---

And then the clerk of forty-four said the thing that is the whole of it, and he said it in the voice of a man reading a form, which is a voice that four hundred people have heard and about nine of them have enjoyed, and he did not raise it once, and it took him about two minutes and it was the plainest and the worst thing anybody said in twenty-four days.

“There is a document,” he said, “made in a city four hundred miles from here, about four hundred years after that hand wrote those four words. It is called the Codification and it is the thing this country runs on and everything anybody in a room in this county has ever heard of comes out of it. I have read it. I have a hand-copy of it at the Seat with a clerk's name at the foot of every page, and I have read about four hundred copies of it for other people, and I am going to tell you three things about it and I am telling you as a man who reads documents and not as a man telling you a story.

“It froze disputed rules into stable versions so that people would stop dying in arguments. It made a challenge harder to file than the thing it challenged, which was done for a good reason and is the ordinary way every rule gets made. *And it took the right out of the public, out loud, in a printed line, and gave it to nobody.* That is the third thing. It did not take it away from anybody in particular. It took it out of the general and put it in no particular place, which is the cheapest thing an emergency can do and it is the only one of the three that was done for a bad reason, and it was done about four hundred years after the four words on that door.”

“And it does not say so,” he said.

“It does not say so,” said the clerk of forty-four, “and *that is what I have come four hundred miles for.* I have been twenty-two years in a building with about thirty of us in it and a bench of four over us, and I have never once in twenty-two years found a document that admitted to having taken something. Not one. I have found documents that were wrong about a place, and I have found documents that were correct about everything and useless, and I have found documents that were made by a fool and I have found documents that were made by a man doing his best in a bad year, and not one of them has ever said: this was here, and this is what it cost, and it is gone. *A document that does not say what it took is not a document that hid it. A document that does not say what it took was written by people who did not know they were in a room, because there was no room, because nobody who was in one had ever told them.*”

He put the lead — Tarin Keel's lead, which he had not asked for and was holding in his left hand — back on the step.

“That is the whole of it. That is not a secret. There is no man in this county that has been cheated and there is nothing to expose and there is nobody in that city who did a wicked thing in a bad temper, and about thirty clerks did their jobs properly, and the sentence at the head of that board is the reason the whole of it took nine seconds to find out. *I have been eight weeks waiting for a form to come back and there is nobody to send it and I have come up this lane to find out why, and the answer is that a hundred and forty-one people said no, out loud, in the open, on their own ground, and then kept a list, and there was no room anywhere in it for a form to go into, and that is why nothing has happened in this county in four hundred years and that is not the same as nothing having been done.*”

---

And nobody in that stair argued with him, and about four people understood all of it, and the woman of about fifty-one from Trope understood all of it and had understood it before he started, and she said so and said what it was for.

“You have got it the wrong way up,” said Isel Cardew. “You are all standing in a tower talking about a document. *We did not lose it. Nobody took it off us. We were never in a room and there was nothing to be in one.* There was a thing written on a door and then a great many things were written on a great many more doors about four hundred years later, and between the two of them there was a stretch of time when the people in this lane had a sentence and a list and nothing else, and the sentence said a person could say no, and the list is the people who did, and that is all it was, and it is not a rule and it never was a rule and it does not bind you and it does not bind me.”

“Does it bind anybody?” said the man of thirty-one, from the top step.

“No,” said Isel Cardew. “And I want that said in a yard and not only in a tower. *We could not refuse the bar. We could not refuse a copy. Nobody in this lane has been able to refuse one single thing at any time that anybody can remember, and we cannot refuse anything now, and we could not before, and the two are the same.* What we did was stop saying yes. And there was nothing there to say yes to, and the cost of that was a bell apiece, and the bell was rung by whoever said it, on their own, into nothing, so that the day on which they had said it would afterwards be a day that somebody could prove. *And that is the whole of what it ever cost, and it is in that column, and there is no name over the column, and I am not going to stand here and tell you that makes it a right. It makes it a bill. Somebody kept the bill. That is all anybody did.*”

She looked at him, and she was fifty-one, and she was not being brave and she was not being clever and she had not come up here to be looked at.

“You have been in this country twenty-four days,” she said. “And in the valley you came from there is a bench, and it has a form, and the form has a line in it that says a person may say no and be entered without a day. And you have spent three weeks trying to get one name into that form, and you have been told by four people that you cannot, and it is not that you cannot. *It is that this lane has had that form, in a worse one, for four hundred years, and it cost a hundred and forty-one people a bell each, and there is nothing in that form that anybody here has ever wanted.*”

---

And that is where the day turned, and there was no bell for it, and nobody put anything on a board.

Because the four lines about a water and a height and a payment, which had been lying under the seat of a timber cart and in two other hands in two counties for about a fortnight, changed in about four seconds in a tower in a village of about ninety people, and he said the change out loud because it is his habit and it is not a good one and about four people in that stair had stopped remarking on it.

“It is not a stranger's rule landing on a broken place,” he said. “That is what I have said about it every day since the seventh day of the fifty-ninth week and I have been wrong about it every time and I was wrong in the same way all three times, which is that I have been treating this lane as a place where records do not work. *Records work. Records work better here than they do four hundred miles off. They are kept on the inside of a door by a hundred and forty-one people and the ones that count the most are kept in a column with no heading over them, and I cannot read one figure of it, and I am not going to be told what any of it is for by a man who has read it out in a stair and could not get the last line of the head of it.*”

“Then do not,” said the clerk of forty-four, and said it kindly, and it was the kindest thing anybody had said to him in twenty-four days and he had not been thanked for anything. “That is not a thing I would not tell you. That is a thing that belongs to the people whose names are on the door, and I have no office and no standing and I am a man of forty-four who came up here on my own account, and I have not opened your door and I have not written anything down and I am not going to.”

And then he said the last of it, and he said it to the stranger from the valley and not to the stair, and it is the whole of what that week cost him, and it is not the biggest thing that happened in it, and the biggest thing is being paid out of somebody else in a village of about ninety people and does not know it.

“I have spent a good name once,” said the clerk of forty-four. “In a valley, in a room, in front of about nine people, about five weeks ago, and it belonged to four people who did their jobs properly for nine years and are still doing them properly, and it was a public thing and it is free and it is not coming back. *So there is nothing in me that anybody in this county can spend, including me, and if you are waiting for me to put a name to that door you will wait until the day one of us dies, and I am the likelier of the two.*”

He said nothing to that, and there was nothing to say to it, and about twenty people went down out of a tower and out into a lane in a village of about ninety people on the third day of the sixty-first week, and the board at Coldharrow said four that morning and would say four the next, and he was in a country he had been in twenty-four days with a good name of nobody's to spend and a leg that was the leg and a thing he was carrying that was off nothing, and about nine people in that lane had found out that they were standing inside a thing that had been standing there for four hundred years and had no idea what it was, and not one of them was a hero of it and not one of them had been asked and not one of them had done anything at all about it in four hundred years, which is the finding, and it is not a small one, and it is a bill and not a right and nobody in a village of about ninety people had the least idea what to do with it, and they went and did their day.

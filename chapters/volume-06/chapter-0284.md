# Chapter 284: The Second Price, And It Is Paid On A Recordwright

Four bells and nothing on top of them, and the woman of forty-one had the cloth off that number before the sun was over the hedge and the number was sixty-three thousand seven hundred and forty-one, and it was the sixth day of the sixty-second week, and the woman of about fifty at the bar came round the eleven bars twice a week and had said, at the second hour of that morning, in front of four people who had not asked her anything, that yesterday had been the nine.

“A day of thirteen is the largest I have had in about four years and I have had two of them in eleven weeks and the other one was the day the three men came up the lane, and I can tell you what was in it because I was standing here with a board under my arm and I am paid to be here, and I am not going to be told a number by a woman with a cloth.”

And she went up the lane, and the day went to four, and the four was the ordinary number, and nobody in two hundred and ten people asked her what the other nine had been either.

---

So they did it at the bar, which is the only place in eleven villages where a thing happens about twice a week in front of strangers, and which is the one place in the lane where a man of fifty-two had said the cause ran the other way and had never worked out how.

He said the price first, standing at the bar with the load-day over and the board put away, in about nine seconds, in front of about nine people and one woman of about fifty who had a reason to be exact about all of it.

“I am putting the bar and the list next to each other. I am not joining them. And the price is a person and it is not one of the two I have paid before, and I want it said out loud in a yard before I do it.

“*A bar is a thing that counts loads and a list is a thing that counts people, and neither of them has a column with anything over it, and if you put the two side by side the only thing in either of them that anybody can be asked about is the threepence, and that is because somebody's father wrote four letters and a stroke at the top of one of them about thirty years ago and nobody has been able to ask that woman a second question in twenty-two years since.*”

He put his hands behind his back, which is what he does in a yard once the yard has finished with him, and about four people in it had stopped noticing that and about two had not.

---

The woman of about fifty did not take her board out.

“Twenty-two years,” she said. “Nine loads a day, twice a week, and threepence a load, and I have said no to a date about nine hundred times and I have never once said the word no to anything else, and a man of thirty-one has just said out loud in front of nine people why, and he is the first person in thirty-three days who has said it and he has said it in a yard and not to me.

“And I am not going to thank you for it, because you have not done anything for me. *You have explained me. That is a different thing and it is worth less and I know it is worth less, and I have had four people in my life explain me and every one of them did it in a room and I got nothing.*”

“What would be worth something,” said the man of thirty-one.

“If somebody asked me a second question,” said the woman of about fifty. “About anything. For about four years I have wanted somebody to ask me a second question and I have wanted it about as badly as I have ever wanted anything, and I have never said so to a living soul, and I am saying it now to nine people at a bar, and you can all do what you like with it.”

Nobody asked her a second question. She went up the lane. It was not a day of anything.

---

And then it was the list's turn, and the list is in a tower four miles from a gate that has never been shut, and the only reason anybody in this county has ever gone and looked at it is that a bell is the only thing anybody in eleven villages has ever gone and looked for, and there has not been one in that village for about two hundred years.

They went up and went in, because the man of about thirty-one who owns the door had left it on the hasp on the second day of the sixty-first week and had said he would leave it on the hasp, and it had been on the hasp ever since.

The four words at the head of it were not read out. Nobody read them out. A woman of fifty-one who had been in that doorway about four times said, before anybody asked her anything, that she was not going to read them out in that stair again, and that the last time three people read the same four words out loud in one morning they got three right answers, and that a recordwright of twenty-eight told them at the top of that stair that there was never a fixed one, and that this is the only sentence in this county that has ever improved anybody's understanding of anything and it has not improved mine.

---

What the man of fifty-two had come to do was to say a number against a figure, and the number was in his head in about four seconds and he had said it out loud twice in this county in a month and he had it ready, and the woman of forty-one said no before he got it out.

“Not against that,” she said, from the fourth step, where she stands, because she is a woman of forty-one and eleven worn steps is a great many steps. “You said it to me on the third day of the sixty-first week at the bottom of this stair and I am saying the same thing in the same place. *If I read it out then it is a woman of forty-one reading out a number in a yard, and there is no column on my board that says who did what.*”

“Then let somebody else do it,” said the man of thirty-one.

“There is nobody else,” said Ottily Roke. “That is what you have been in this country thirty-four days finding out. *There is nobody else, and if there was, they would be a person, and a person who reads it out is a person who can be asked about it for the rest of their life, and I have been that for twenty-five years without ever choosing it and I would not put it on anybody who had a chance of saying no.*”

So nobody read the column, and the man of fifty-two put the lead back in his pocket, and the man of thirty-one was in a tower for about nine minutes and did not touch anything and had nothing to do at all.

---

The recordwright of twenty-eight came up at about the fourth hour and did the thing she came to do, and she did it because nobody had asked her to, and it took about four minutes and it is the whole of the thirty-fourth day.

“A bar and a list are the same failure in two coats,” she said, “and I have been a recordwright for six years and I could not have told you that in a sentence until about a minute ago, and here is what I have actually come to say, and it is about me and I have thought about whether to say it and I am going to.

“*There is a thing I have relied on for six years and it is the only thing in my trade that I have ever relied on, and it is that a thing is never in one place.* Two copies. The copy in the room and the copy in the other room, and if the two disagree then there has been a mistake somewhere and you can go and look. That is the whole of the protection and it is not a code or a rule or anything clever, it is arithmetic, and it has never once failed me.

“And there is no second copy of the four words at the head of that door. There has not been one for about four hundred years. There is no other room, because there is no room, and I have now been in a tower in a village of about ninety people on a day of four and I have understood for the first time in six years what it is like to be the only copy of a thing, and *it is the worst thing I have ever felt and I am not frightened of it and I have not slept about it and I am not going to pretend to you that I have.*”

Nobody said anything for about four seconds.

“I am not going to be relieved by that and I would like it noticed that nobody in this stair is going to be relieved by that,” she said.

---

And then she did the only thing there is to do about it, and she did it in the way a person does a thing they have done four hundred times in nine counties and have never once done in a lane, and it took about nine minutes and about half of it was waiting.

“A record is not the words,” she said. “I knew that this morning and I have known it for six years and I have never had to say it to anybody. A record is the words and the hand and the day and the person who can be asked, and the hand is checkable and the day is checkable and the person is the part that is not. And when you write a thing down, the way somebody said it goes, and there is no way to write the way, and *in this county the way was the whole of it, because in this county a thing gets said once, in a yard, and the yard does not write.*”

“So you are not going to write it,” said the man of thirty-one.

“I am going to write it and I am going to have it read back and it is going to be the worst record of my life,” said Sera Quill.

---

She wrote on the flat of the door, not on the plank, and she wrote with a stub of pencil and her own hand, and about nine people at the foot of the stair and four on it watched a recordwright of twenty-eight commit a thing to a surface that had paint on it.

And then she said the sentence aloud to a boy of seventeen with a satchel against his hip and a stub of pencil behind his ear, and he read it back off the door, twice, from about a foot off it, and he was not looking at her hand while he did it.

“That is not a witness,” said Bram Orren.

“No,” said Sera Quill.

“You know that is not a witness.”

“I know exactly what it is not,” she said. “*It is two people and a door and a thing I said and a thing you said, and neither of you can be asked about the other one in about four years' time, and the check is on my hand and not on my ear, and my hand is the thing I am least worried about.* And it is the only instrument in eleven villages and it is a boy with a book, and I have used a form in nine counties and it was better than this, and I have never once used anything else.”

“You could use a form,” said the man of thirty-one. “There is a form four hundred miles off with a line in it about a day and it says that where no day can be shown an entry may be made without one, and there is a recordwright of twenty-eight who has read it out loud in a yard and has not opened it and has not refused it and has not been asked.”

“No I have not,” said Sera Quill, and looked at him for about two seconds longer than a person needs to.

---

And the boy shut the book, and said that he had been asked in a doorway about a fortnight ago whether he could be asked about a third thing in about six weeks and that he could, and that he would rather not be asked about this one at the same time, because it was not his instrument, and that he was not being difficult and that he would read a thing back for anybody in that lane at any time they wanted and that reading a thing back is not the same as being a witness and there is no word in this country for the difference and there ought to be one.

And the man of thirty-one wrote nothing down, for the fourth time since he came into this country, and there is not one form in eleven villages that would let a man set a thing before a room, and there is not one room.

---

And then, at about the ninth hour, at the foot of that tower, a person of a village said a sentence in a yard as the rules of the register require.

The rules are eleven lines and the man of fifty-two read them out in a yard in Trope on the seventh day of the sixty-first week and they are the whole of what the register is, and one of them is that nobody may go in without a sentence said by a person of that village in that village, and a recordwright writes down what was said and nothing else and does not write who said it.

A person of a village said one.

It was about ninety acres, and it was eleven families, and it was a boundary walked in the twenty-second year of a board by a man who is four years dead.

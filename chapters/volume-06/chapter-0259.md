# Chapter 259: A Woman With A Board, Second Time

The thirteen rings that took the figure from sixty-three thousand five hundred and sixty-one to sixty-three thousand five hundred and seventy-four were four bells and nine more, and the nine were three strangers at each of three gates, and the three of them were a man with a cartload of timber and two men with a barrow, and they had come up the lane together and had rung at three gates because that is what you do, and a woman of forty-one counted them off on her fingers without looking and did not say what any of them were, and the man of thirty-one had the whole of the sum as a figure in chalk at the foot of a board in about four seconds and did not write down one word of what had been said in a yard, because he has not written down what is said in a yard since a market town four hundred miles off and he is not going to start in this one.

The woman with the board was in the yard at Coldharrow by the fourth hour of that same day and about four people knew it and about thirty did not, and the man of thirty-one found out in about nine minutes that she was not a stranger in this country and had never been, and that the whole of what he had been doing for a week — standing in a yard waiting to be an outsider long enough to be told something — was the wrong shape for the room he was going to be in.

“Nell told me,” he said.

“Nell tells everybody,” said Sable Venn. “Nell Callow has told nine people in this stretch that I was coming for three days and she has told me twice that I am making a fuss about a change in the weather, and I have been in this county nine years and nobody here has ever made a fuss about me.”

She had a board under her arm and it was four feet by three and it was not the Red Basin, and there was a second one rolled inside the first, and she put them against a cart's side and did not unroll either of them.

“I am going to say a thing in about nine words and I am going to say it before you have asked me anything, because you have been in this country nine days and you have the face of a man who is about to be polite at me, and I have been in a room with about four hundred polite men in my life and I would rather be short.”

“Go on.”

“*I put four lines into the March and I did not read them.*”

---

Nobody in that yard said anything for about as long as it takes to cross a yard, and the recordwright of twenty-eight, who had been in this country nine days and had said in a garden five days ago that a count that leaves a board is a count somebody can use, put her hand over her mouth, which is not a thing she does, and then took it away again and said:

“That is a thing you have never said out loud anywhere in nine years.”

“No,” said Sable Venn. “It is not. I have said in four counties, in rooms with nine people in them, in my own mouth, that I do not read other people's records, and I have said it before I was asked, and I have said it as a fact about a trade and not as a virtue, and it is the whole of my standing in this world and I have never had it questioned once in nine years. I have just questioned it myself, standing in a yard, to a man of thirty-one, and I would like it noticed that it took nine days and a copy.”

“Who was it for?”

“It is not for you,” she said, and then stopped, and said the rest, which is the part that is not a refusal. “It is for four counties and it is for about nine hundred people who cannot pay a pound three shillings and sixpence for a day out of a village that keeps Shelf weeks, and it is the only thing I have ever carried that cost a person nothing to receive, and I have carried about four hundred of them, and eleven of them are walls.”

“What are the four lines.”

“They are four lines of an order,” she said, “made in a market town called Sallowby, about a dam, and a height, and a day, and a party to pay. It is four lines and it is a complete order and there is no hole in any of it. It was made by a bench of four four hundred miles off about a valley four hundred miles off and it is about water in a village of about a hundred and forty people and it is not about this county and it does not name this county and it is true.”

“Whose?”

“Mine to carry,” said Sable Venn. “Not mine to have got hold of. There is a difference and it is the only one I have ever had and I have been on the wrong side of it about four hundred times and I have never once been asked which of the two it was until about a minute ago.”

---

The man of thirty-one said the thing he had come to say and it was not the thing he had prepared, and he noticed it happening and let it happen.

“If it is true, then it is a document about four hundred miles off, and this is a county where a day is four bells and a height is measured from nothing, and you have put a true document into a country that has never had a document, and I have spent eight weeks in a valley learning that a ruling is a weapon the moment it leaves the place it was measured in, and the place it was measured in is four hundred miles from here and has a woman of thirty-eight at a gate in it who I have never met.”

“I know what it is,” said Sable Venn. “I have known what it is for eight weeks and I have not slept about it and I have not taken it out, and I would like you to understand that I have not taken it out for a reason and that the reason is not sentiment.”

“Then give me the reason.”

“Because you are the man who spent the order,” she said. “You made it. Or you stood in a room and helped make it, and there are about nine people in that market town who were in the room and about four of them remember me standing in it, and I have been waiting eight weeks for one of the other five to walk into this county so that I could hand it to a stranger, and instead a man of thirty-one walked into this county and asked a woman of forty-one what a ring is.”

“That is fair,” said the man of thirty-one, “and it is not the half of it, and I will give you the half of it now, because you have given me nine words and I have earned my nine.

“I am the man who is going to be stood in front of a room about this county, if there is going to be a room, and I have no party and no name on anything and no office and no way of being heard, and I am here for about a week. And if there is a document about this county in a room nine days from this field with my hand nowhere on it, then there is a person they can ask who was here. *You have built me a box and put me in it and you have done it in the only way anybody could do it, which is by handing me a true thing.*”

---

And there it was, and it is the whole of Volume 06 in about ninety words, and neither of them was the one who said it.

“Nonsense,” said Ferrow Hask, from the end of a cart, where he had been standing for about four minutes without anybody asking him to. “And I have listened to both of you and I have walked that lane nineteen years and I am going to say the thing and then I am going to load my timber.

“A thing carried into a country is not a thing anybody in the country can be shown. That is not an opinion, that is what carrying is. You carry it in and it is here, and it is a piece of paper with four lines on it, and there is nobody in eleven villages who can put a name to a person in a room nine days off, and it is not one. So you have not given this county a door, woman. *You have given it a thing it cannot open and a man it will be asked about.* And I have been trying for nineteen years to work out what a gate is for and I have never once worked out that the answer was a piece of paper from four hundred miles off, and I would have gone on not working it out quite happily.”

A woman of forty-one came down the ladder at that point, because she comes down at the last bell, and she had heard the whole of it, and she had heard nine rings of a board come in that morning and had not been told about any of this.

“No,” she said, to Sable Venn, and it was the shortest word anybody said that day. “No, and you will give it to me, and not to him, and not to her, and not to the boy with the book.”

“It is yours if you want it.”

“I do not want it,” said Ottily Roke. “I want to know three things and I have not got the days for three, because it is a hundred and forty days and I am at about the hundred and six, and this is the thirty-fourth.

“I want to know whether your four lines are sealed. I want to know what a piece of paper is worth in this county, which is three pence a load and nothing else, and I want to know who put them in the cart. And I want to know it today and not on the last day, because on the last day I have been the last day for six years and it has never once turned out to be the last day.”

“I put them in the cart,” said Sable Venn. “It is sealed, because a thing going four hundred miles in a satchel under a seat wants sealing, and it cost threepence at Sallowby, and I gave the man five shillings and he is a man of about fifty-one and he has been buying paper in four counties for about nine years and he did not know what it was and I did not tell him what it was, and I told a woman of thirty-three to copy it and she did it in her own hand in about four minutes because she is fast and because she asked me no questions, which is a thing I have relied on four hundred times.”

“Then he is on the cart that came in this morning with the timber,” said the man of fifty-two, “and he is two hundred yards down the lane at the second house and he is asleep, and there is a satchel under the seat of it, and I saw the satchel at the first bell and I did not think anything of it.”

Nobody in that yard moved for about four seconds.

“Go and wake him,” said Ottily Roke, “and do not frighten him, and do not take the satchel off him, and do not ask him anything at all until the four of us are in the same yard.”

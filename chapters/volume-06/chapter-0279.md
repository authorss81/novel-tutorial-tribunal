# Chapter 279: The Lanes Put Side By Side

The first day of the sixty-second week went up five on a board in Coldharrow, and the figure at the head of it at the second light was sixty-three thousand seven hundred and one, and four of the five were the clock and the fifth was a stranger, and the stranger had come up the lane the evening before and slept at the mill and was standing in a yard at Trope at about the ninth hour saying that he was called Petrow Sill and that he lived at the end of a bad road called Stennick and that he had come up nine miles because a woman of fifty-one had told him in a lane last week that there was a list.

Nobody in two hundred and ten people had ever heard the name of the place at the end of his road, and about four of them heard it twice in an hour and one of the four wrote it down on the back of a hand.

---

And the thing that happened in that yard was the thing a man of thirty-one has done four times in two years and had to pay for four times, and he said the price out loud first, in about nine seconds, in front of about nine people, because four weeks in this county have taught him that the only honest act left is the one announced beforehand in front of witnesses.

“I am going to put two lanes next to each other,” he said, “and I am not going to join them, and the price is that the two men at the end of them are going to find out about each other, and neither of them is going to have been asked, and there is no instrument in this county or any other that stops it. *Twelve days ago I did it with two women and it cost a woman of forty-one nine years of a room she had not known she was standing in, and I have spent every day since looking for a way to do it that costs somebody else that, and there is not one, and I am doing it anyway, and I would like it said in a yard that I said it first.*”

“Two lanes,” said Petrow Sill. “Mine has nine farms on it and a well and a roof that fell in. His has eleven villages on it and a bar.”

“That is why it is worth doing and it is not worth doing,” said the man of thirty-one, “and you are both going to be sick about it by about the noon bell.”

---

So the man of fifty-two did the arithmetic, and it took about half an hour, and about nine people stayed for it, and what he brought to the cart's side was not a sum.

He brought a round.

“There is no lane,” he said. “Say that back to me, both of you, and then say it properly. *There is no lane.* You have both been walking one thing for years. A carter's handcart takes what a cart can take and stops where the ground stops being ground. A cart that is going four hundred miles with timber comes up through Wrenny and turns for Sallow and it does not go into Ellerby at all, because there is nothing at Ellerby it wants, and I have watched forty of them do it and I have counted them, and not one of them has ever gone into Trope.

“*A road is a set of lanes that only go one way at a time.* What you have got is a round, and it is not the round of this ground, and here is how I know, and it has taken me two days and about nine questions and I have got it in three pieces and none of the three pieces know about the other two.”

He wrote the eleven on the side of the cart and did not number them.

“The first piece is the order the debts go round in. I have stood at a bar for nineteen years and I have heard about a hundred and forty of these a year go past, and they go in an order. *It is not the order of distance. There is a village nine miles up this lane that comes fourth and there is a village four hundred yards from Coldharrow that comes eighth, and I have known that for about four years and I have never asked anybody, because a man at a bar does not ask a man in a lane what order his debts go in.*”

He put a hand flat on the first sheet.

“The second piece is the order the names are in on the outside wall of the last house in Ellerby. Two hundred and nine names and nobody struck out and it is in the order things happened, and a woman of about sixty told us that a month ago in a lane, and I have been up there twice since and read about a hundred and forty of them, and *they are in that same order, and the villages in them go in that same round, and about seventy of them are people in four other villages, and the round holds for people who were not in Ellerby when it was written, which is the part that took me a day.*”

“And the third,” said the man of thirty-one.

“The third is a boy of seventeen with a book he has run since he was twelve,” said Tarin Keel, “and the order in that book is the same order every year, and he has told me on the sixth day of the sixtieth week, standing up, in front of eleven people, that it does not go by the ground, and that a line that goes by the hedges goes round, and that his does not go round anything. *I have had a copy of that said to me in nine counties and I have never once been able to check it and this is the first week in nineteen years I have been able to check it and I have checked it twice and it is the same round and it is not the round of the ground and no cart would choose it, because a cart goes where there is ground and this goes where there is a village.*”

He put the lead down and there was nothing on the cart's side but the eleven names in an order that no road in eleven villages goes in.

“That is three things that have got nothing to do with one another,” he said. “A debt at a bar. A wall in a village of about a hundred and forty people. A book a boy carries on his back. And the same round is in all three, and it is in about a hundred and forty names on a plank on the inside of a door in a village of about ninety people that a man of forty-four has dated at about four hundred years, and eleven is the number of villages on this lane and it was the number when he dated it, and I have not asked him whether eleven is the number that is on the door and I am going to ask him in the gap before the next week and I am not going to ask him before I have got this written down where somebody can read it.”

---

And the price came, and it came in about four minutes, and it came from the man of thirty-eight, and it was not the price he expected to pay.

Ferrow Hask had been in the yard from about the ninth hour and had said one thing, which was *do not*, and he had not moved since, and when the round had been on the cart's side for about a quarter of an hour he came and stood at the end of it and looked at the eleven names in the order they were in and did not say anything for a while.

“Nineteen years,” he said at last. “I have gone the eleven-village round about a hundred and forty times a year for nineteen years and I have never once been to Stennick, and there is a man who has been to Stennick about forty times in nine years and has never once been on my round, and neither of us knew the other existed until this morning, and I am thirty-eight years old and I have counted about a hundred and forty carts a year out of that bar and I could not have told you the name of a man nine miles up a bad road, and he could not have told you a thing about me, and we are both going to have to be in a room together about a thing neither of us can produce.”

“Yes,” said the man of thirty-one. “That is the price and I said it out loud before I did it and I did it anyway.”

“I know you did,” said Ferrow Hask. “And here is the half of it you did not say, and you did not say it because you do not have it. *He is going to come to this bar.* Not today. Some day in a year, in a lane, with a barrow or with nothing, and he is going to stand where I stand and he is going to look down that lane and see a bar that is not shut and a woman of about fifty counting loads, and he is going to be told by somebody that the bar is for a road. And I will not be here. *That is what it costs and it is not him finding out about me. It is him finding out about my bar and me not being in the county when he does.*”

He put his hand flat on the cart and left it there.

“Nineteen years I have wanted somebody to ask me what a bar is for,” he said, “and there is not one in this county and I have stopped expecting one, and there is a man coming up this lane from a place called Stennick and he is going to be the first one to ask, and he is going to ask you.”

“Petrow,” said Tarin Keel, “has been in this yard about two hours and has not asked anybody one single question, and that is not because he is careful, it is because he came nine miles to hear a list of fifty-one places read out and he has got it, and he is going back down that road tonight, and he is not coming back to this bar in a year. He has a bad road and nine farms and a well and a roof that fell in, and I have been in his house for an hour and there are eleven people in it. *The price of what I have done this morning is not that they find out about each other. It is that neither of them has anybody to put the round to, and there is a third thing about a hundred and forty names on a door that neither of them will ever see.*”

---

And the man of thirty-one was in the yard with it and was in the middle of it, and there was a thing in his bag he had carried for a year and it was off nothing, and there was a second half of a book in it that he had not opened since a valley four hundred miles back, and he did not open it in a yard in a country that keeps weeks he has never learned, and both of those were the same fact and he thought about it for about four minutes and then thought about something else.

And the finding of the twenty-ninth day of his being in this country is a finding about an instrument and not a question in anybody's mouth, and it is worth the whole of it: *the walked lanes in this county are not a set of lanes. They are one thing, going round, in an order that the ground did not choose, and the proof is arithmetic and not opinion, and there are three instruments in eleven villages that hold the order and not one of the three has ever spoken to the other two, and the only record in the March that holds all eleven is a boy's book, and the book is five years old and it is on his back.*

Nobody in that yard asked anything about it.

There was one question in that county about the round and it was asked on the sixth day of the sixtieth week in a doorway by a boy of seventeen to a person, and it has a mark on the end of it, and it is written in the book, and it is the only thing in five years of that book that is not a thing that crossed, and nobody has answered it and nobody asked it a second time and nobody was going to ask it in a yard at Trope with three instruments on a cart's side and about nine people standing round them, and about four of those nine understood, in about four seconds, exactly what a boy of seventeen with a book would be able to do with a round that goes in an order the ground did not choose, and not one of them said so.

---

And at about the fourth hour a boy of seventeen came up the lane out of Ellerby with his satchel against his hip and his stub of pencil behind his ear, and he had been at the wall of the last house for about an hour and a half reading the two hundred and nine names in the order they happened, which is the only thing he has ever wanted to do in a village, and he had counted, and he had a number.

He did not come into the yard at Trope to say it. He went on up the lane for about a mile and a half and turned off it at a stile, and a man of thirty-one who had been walking behind him for about half of that turned off with him, and neither of them said a word for the first two hundred yards.

“Mr Vester,” said Bram Orren. “I have got a number and it is not a question.”

“Go on.”

“There are one hundred and forty-one names on the plank in the tower at Nettlebed,” said Bram Orren, “and I have not been in the tower and I am not going in the tower, and I know there are one hundred and forty-one because a woman of fifty-one said it in a stair and I was in the stair. And I have read the two hundred and nine on the Ellerby wall this afternoon, all of them, and the order they are in goes round the way the debts go round, and I have never seen that in writing before and I have got it in a book I have been keeping five years and there is not one word of it in the book because I did not know there was a second thing to write down.”

He stopped at the stile and put his satchel down on the top step.

“So I am going to Nettlebed,” he said, “and I am not going to ask anybody anything, and I have got my book, and I want you to come and not to help.”

“All right,” said the man of thirty-one. “I will come and not help.”

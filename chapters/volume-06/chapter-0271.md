# Chapter 271: The Fourth Line, Read Again

Nothing happened in Coldharrow on the seventh day of the sixtieth week that the board knew about, and the board said so at the second light in a hand that had been getting more careful for twenty-five years, and the figure at the head of it was sixty-three thousand six hundred and forty-seven.

Four bells, and nothing on top of them, and the woman of forty-one wiped the cloth over the top of the number and left it at that, which is the whole of what a day of four is in this village, and there have been about two hundred and thirty of them in the twenty-five years she has kept it, and she has never once put anything under four and she has never once wanted to.

The recordwright of twenty-eight went up the lane at about the first light with her satchel on her hip and a sentence in her mouth and eleven miles in front of her, and nobody had asked her to go, and she had not said she was going, and she was gone before the second light was finished ringing, and what she had in the satchel was the two hundred words and the four lines and a form with a printed head on it, and there is no longer any of those four things in this village except the last one, which was on the side of a cart in the yard where she had left it at about the first light to put both hands up to her coat.

---

The woman of about fifty who keeps the bar came round the eleven gates at about the third hour on the two days a week that the loads come, and she had a stick in her right hand and a board under her left arm, and the board was about the length of her forearm and it had a word at the top of it, and it was the only board in eleven villages that had a word at the top of it.

It had said four on the wall of the tower in about four seconds and then it had said a figure on its own board and nothing else, and she had got the two out of the way of each other by then and he had stopped trying to help, which is the first honest thing to say about his twenty-first morning in this country.

Then the cart's side.

The sheet was lying on it where the recordwright had put it down, folded along a crease it already had, and it had been lying there about two hours in a wet morning, and the woman of about fifty picked it up because she has a board under her arm and cannot carry a second flat thing in the rain, and she put it on top of her board, and she read it.

She is the fourth person in this county who has read that sheet and she is the only one who was not asked.

“Nobody asked you,” said the man of thirty-one.

“No,” she said. “You did not ask me. Nobody asked me. That is the fourth time that sheet has been read in this county and I am the fourth and I was not asked and I would like it noticed, because the other three were all asked, and one of them was asked twice, and I have been standing at that bar for twenty-two years and nobody has ever asked me to read anything at all, and this is a piece of paper about a river.”

“It is about a water and a height and a payment,” he said, “and you are right about the rest of it.”

---

She read it in the way that people read who are not used to being listened to, which is quickly and out loud and with no help, and she gave the last line its own beat, and nobody in that yard had told her to, and he noticed that and did not say so, and the last of the four lines is one he has now heard three times in this county, and she gave it back word for word, which is what a person at a bar who can read does with a thing that has been handed to her.

“That is a door,” she said. “Somebody built that in on purpose and I will not have anybody tell me otherwise. I know what a door is, I have got a house. It says that where a day cannot be shown the acknowledgment goes in without one, and the entry gets marked as made without a day. Those are the words on it. I have read the whole sheet once and I am not going to read it again, and I am not going to say one word about what is behind it.”

“Yes. It is a door and it is not a door anybody has to open.”

“A door is not a thing anybody has to open.” She put the sheet down on top of her own board and put both hands flat on it. “That is the thing about it. Whoever wrote it did not have to. There is no line under it saying anybody shall. It is a door and it is left where it is, and nobody in this village has opened it and nobody in this village has shut it, and I do not know of a soul in eleven villages that has been asked.”

“You have not been asked either.”

“No,” said the woman of about fifty. “And I want it on the record, such as it is, that I have had it in my hands for about four minutes and I have not opened it and I am not going to, and that is not because I am frightened of what is behind it, because I have had four minutes to be frightened and I have not managed it. It is because I have a board under my arm and I cannot get to a press before the noon bell and a day is a pound three shillings and sixpence and there is one woman between here and the press who sells them, and I have not got that in a bag this morning and neither has anybody else in this village.”

---

The woman of forty-one came out of the tower at about the fourth hour with the cloth over her shoulder, and she had heard the whole of it from the ladder, and what she was interested in was not the door.

She was interested in the word at the top of the other woman's board, and she went straight at it, and she is a woman of forty-one who has kept a count for twenty-five years and who has never once in twenty-five years had anything to compare hers with.

“Let me see your board,” she said.

The woman of about fifty held it out. It was a plank about the length of a forearm with a strip of oilcloth round the edge where it had got wet twice, and at the top of it, in a hand that had been gone nine years, there were four letters and a stroke.

“AT THE BAR,” said the woman of about fifty. “My father wrote that. He wrote it in about the year the tower board got its new hand.”

“He did,” said Ottily Roke. “And it is the only word at the top of anything in this county that I know of, and I have wanted one for twenty-five years and I have asked four men from counties with roads in it for it and told every one of them the same four sentences, and I have never once been able to give them the name of the thing, because there is no name.”

“There is a name on mine.”

“There is a name on yours and it is a good name and it has cost you.” She said it flatly and she was not being unkind and she was not being clever. “That is the half nobody tells you. *A word at the top means every stroke on it is for that and nothing else.* Nine loads. Two hundred and forty days, or one hundred and forty, or however many he says. Your father had a word over it and the word told him what the figures were and it told him what he was for, and because of that word there has never once been a question asked you about any single stroke on it, because there was nothing to ask. You have been asked nothing in twenty-two years because the top of that board says what it is for and it is not for anything else.”

The woman of about fifty thought about that for about as long as a woman thinks about a thing at a bar at the third hour with nine loads coming up the lane behind her.

“No,” she said, at last. “They have asked me twice about the threepence. That is all they have ever asked me. In twenty-two years.”

“Yes,” said the woman of forty-one. “And I would give eleven shillings for a word at the top of mine and I would not know what to do with it, because the day I wrote one is the day nobody could ask me what any of it was for, and being asked is the only thing that has ever been any use to me.”

---

And then the man of fifty-two asked the only question in that yard that anybody had asked anybody all morning, and he asked it to the board and not to a person, because it is what he does.

“The figures at the head of that board are the figures at the second light, so a four on it is a morning on which nothing whatever was rung in the day before. *I am not going to waste a morning on those.* Get your father’s hand up, woman, and go back into the grain. I want to know where the fours run.”

There is not a word anywhere in eleven villages that says a run of days is worth anything, and the man of fifty-two has never once pretended there was. He asked for it anyway, from a plank, in a yard, on his twenty-third morning in this country, and that is the whole of what he is and the whole of what he is not.

She got a cloth and went at the grain with the corner of her eye the way a person does a thing they have done a thousand times, and the man of thirty-one and the man of fifty-two stood at the foot of the ladder and watched her do it and neither of them said one word, because there is nothing to be said to a person about a thing they are better at than anybody in the yard.

“I have got nine of them,” said Ottily Roke, and she had the cloth in her hand and she had not stopped moving it. “Nine mornings, one after another, and there is nothing on top of the four on any of them, and the man who made them put the upstroke first on all nine, which is what he did when he was not tired, and there is a stroke either side of the run that is longer than the others, so it is nine and it is not ten, and I have looked at it twice. *And I cannot tell you which year of it they are in, and neither can you, and that is the other half of what I have just found and I would like somebody else to be holding the other half.*”

He put the lead in his pocket, which is what he does when a sum does not want to be written on anything. “Nine,” he said. “And that is a *run*, and I have been saying since the first day of that week that a week of four is a week you do not talk about, and I have never once been able to tell you which four were next to which four, and I can now, and it is not a rule, it is a length of stroke.”

“You have just said nine words about a week of four,” said the woman of forty-one. “You said them on the first day of that week in a lane and you said they were a rule and not a document and that nobody could say where it came from.”

“It is a rule and it is not a document,” said Tarin Keel, “and I still cannot say where it comes from, and I have just found out that it is *true*, and that it is the only thing in this county that is both.”

---

And this is the whole of the morning, and it took about nine minutes in a yard and it is the finding of this day and nobody in it will remember it in a year's time except three people.

*In a country where a bell is a schedule and not a river, a bell is the only instrument anybody has, and a bell can do exactly one thing, and the one thing it can do is show that a number of days went past.*

*And a number of days that went past is a length. And a length is not a day. And this county can prove how long anything took and has never once been able to say when, and the woman of forty-one has known that for twenty-five years and thought it was a defect in her, and it is not a defect in her, it is the only thing this country can do.*

He said it out loud, because he says things out loud now, and because it was the twenty-first day and he had worked it out himself and there was nobody else in the yard who had.

“A run of fours is a week of something,” he said. “Nine days where nothing whatever was rung in two hundred and ten people. And you can have that, and I can have it, and I can put it in front of a person in about four minutes, and the person can have it too. And that is the first thing in this county that anybody has been able to prove since I came into it, and it is not a day, and nobody is going to be able to turn it into one, and I have been looking for a way to turn it into one for three weeks and there is not one and there is never going to be one.”

“Then that is what we have got,” said the woman of forty-one. “A length.”

“That is what you have got,” he said, “and you have had it for twenty-five years, and you have been apologising for it to four men from counties with roads in it.”

---

And the door on the printed sheet was still lying open in the sense that a door is open when nobody has closed it, and about nine people had now been told there was a door in it and not one of them had gone through it, and the woman of about fifty at the bar said the last thing about it and said it to the yard and not to him.

“A length,” she said. “You are telling me that this county can hand a person in this county a length, and that there is not one building with a yard in it four hundred miles off that has anywhere to put a length. *I have been counting a gate for twenty-two years and there has never once been a question in the world that wanted what I know.*” She put the sheet inside her coat. “I am not opening your door. I am saying that the door is the wrong shape for what we have got, and that whoever built it was being fair, and that being fair and being useful are two different things and I have found out which one of those I would rather have had on a wet morning at a bar with a board under my arm.”

And then she went off up the lane with the stick and the board, and she was gone about nine minutes, and she came back at the noon bell with the count on it and nothing else on it, and there was no gate on her board and there never had been and there was not going to be one, and the word at the top of it was four letters and a stroke and it is the only heading in eleven villages and it is the reason nobody can ask her a question.

---

And the woman of forty-one was on the third of the four rungs with the cloth, and she said the thing that sent the next two days where they went, and she said it in her own mouth and she was not asking anybody for anything.

“I go round eleven bars,” she said. “Twenty-two years, twice a week, and it is eleven villages and I have been to every one of them about two thousand times, and I have never once had a stroke to put on that board for two of them, because there is nothing at those two to count. No loads. Nobody keeps anything.”

“What is at them.”

“Bell,” said Ottily Roke. “That is all that is at them. There is a tower at Nettlebed and there is nobody in it and there has not been anybody in it since before I was standing on this ladder, and Trope has not got a tower at all and has never had one in anybody's time, and the two of them between them have not got a board either. And that is not a poor village. The miller at Trope has got a mill and the mill has got a wheel you can see from the road and I have counted nine hundred and about two hundred of those elms myself. They are not short of anything. *They have not got the two things that the other nine have got, and I have been going round eleven bars for twenty-two years and I have never once let myself finish that sentence in a yard.*”

The man of thirty-one was at the foot of the ladder with his hands behind his back, which is what he does in a yard when there is nothing left to do with them, and he did not say a word, and it is worth saying that he had a sentence available on the fourth line of a printed sheet and he did not use it, and about four people in that yard noticed that he did not use it and none of them could have said why.

“All right,” said Tarin Keel, and picked up the bit of lead and put it in his pocket again. “Then we will go and look at two towers in a day and a half, and I want it said first that I am the wrong man to be sent and that I know it, and I am going anyway, and I have been wrong about a great many things in nine counties and I have never once been wrong about a thing in the field in front of me.”

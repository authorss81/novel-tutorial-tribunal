# Chapter 275: What The Two Villages Paid

There was a debt at the ninth house on the fourth day of the sixty-first week and the board at Coldharrow read sixty-three thousand six hundred and seventy at the second light and went up six before the noon bell, and two of the six were the debt, and everybody in Coldharrow knew there was a debt at the ninth house before the bell was off and knew by the last bell whether it had been paid.

A carter of thirty-eight called it in, because it was owed to him, and it was nine shillings and it was a fortnight old, and he stood at the end of the ninth cart and did it in about nine seconds and nobody enjoyed it and everybody in the yard had heard it.

And the man of thirty-one was in that yard at about the ninth hour with four other people in it, and none of them had come to see him and none of them had told him anything, and one of the four was a man of sixty-three with a coat that had been good about eleven years ago who had slept at the mill on the night before the third day of that week and was going home on the last day of it and had been in this county five days out of the two he gets.

---

And the man of fifty-two said the thing he had been carrying since the second day of that week at a tower, and he said it in a yard at about the ninth hour because that is where this country prints its working, and about nine people stayed for it and about four of them understood the whole of it and about five understood the sum and none of the nine understood why it mattered, and he did not do it out of any sense of an audience. He did it because he had it and could not put it down.

“Ninepence a day,” he said. “A hundred and forty days. That is one thousand two hundred and sixty pence, and I did that in this yard on the third day of the fifty-eighth week and it has not moved. *A year of the woman who keeps the count is one thousand two hundred and sixty pence.* Now. A hundred and forty days is about half a year, and the other half of the year a bell goes four times a day for nothing, and that is what everybody in this county has understood for twenty-five years.

“Two hundred years of that is two hundred times one thousand two hundred and sixty. *Two hundred times twelve hundred and sixty is two hundred and fifty-two thousand pence.* And twenty pence to the pound is two hundred and forty, and two hundred and fifty-two thousand goes into two hundred and forty a thousand and fifty times with nothing over at all. *Two hundred and fifty-two thousand pence is a thousand and fifty pounds exactly.*”

He put the lead down on the cart's side and picked it up again, which is what he does.

“Now a bell is six pounds fifteen,” he said. “I did that in this yard on the same day and I am not doing it again. Six pounds fifteen is one thousand six hundred and twenty pence, and I have read that figure three times today and it is the figure. *A thousand and fifty pounds is one thousand six hundred and twenty pence that many times over with something left.* A hundred and fifty times one thousand six hundred and twenty is two hundred and fifty-one thousand one hundred. Two hundred and fifty-two thousand less two hundred and fifty-one thousand one hundred is nine hundred. Nine hundred pence is seventy-five shillings, and seventy-five shillings is three pounds fifteen shillings. *A thousand and fifty pounds buys a hundred and fifty-five bells and three pounds fifteen shillings.*”

Nobody said anything for about four seconds.

“The two villages have had no bell and no board for about two hundred years,” said Tarin Keel. “That is the dating. I have not got it out of a story. I have got it out of two hands: a man of forty-four in a county that keeps weeks dated the hand at the top of that board at about four hundred years, and a woman of fifty-one from Trope put her thumb on the last figure in a column and said it was her grandmother's and that it was about two hundred years old, and the tower at Nettlebed has had nothing in it for about two hundred years, and Trope has never had a tower. *So: two hundred years, and no bell, and no keeper, and no wage, and the wage is one thousand two hundred and sixty pence a year, and there is the price of it.*”

---

And Garrick Terrel had been in the yard for about a quarter of an hour before any of that and had said nothing, and about four people in the yard had been watching him not say anything, and nobody asked him anything, and it is worth being exact about that, because a man of sixty-three who sells bells is the one person in eleven villages this sum is aimed at and he was not asked a single question about it.

He stood there with his coat on for about four minutes after Tarin Keel had finished, and then he said the thing.

“I have quoted six pounds fifteen in this lane three times in eleven years,” he said. “And I have got a list in my head of every village in this March that has ordered a bell off me, and it is about thirty, and eleven of them are on this lane, and nine of the eleven have one. I told a man of thirty-one in this yard on the third day of the fifty-eighth week, and I have told men in four other counties since, that the reason I have never once had a village tell me it cannot afford mine is not that villages are rich. I gave him the other half of it as well, and it took me eleven years to say, and I have never said it better than I said it to him. *And in eleven years of asking I never once worked out that there are two villages on this lane that were never served at all.* He worked it out this morning. I was standing in a yard with a lead in somebody else's hand and it took about nine minutes and I have had it.”

“Garrick,” said the woman of forty-one.

“I am not finished and it is about my trade and not about your county, and you have had your morning.” He put his hand flat on the cart's side where the lead had been. “A hundred and fifty-five bells. I sell about thirty in nine years. So a thousand and fifty pounds is not money to me, it is a generation and a half of everything I have ever done, and there is nobody in this county that has got it, and that is not the part I have been thinking about for the last four minutes.

“The part I have been thinking about is this. In eleven years I have never once had a village say no to me. Not one. I have had two say nothing, which I have counted, and I have had about four say *we will think about it*, and I have had a great many pay. *And I have wanted one to say no.* That is what a man in my trade is for. I go round twice a year with a price in my mouth and the whole of what I am is that somebody in a lane eventually cannot. And nobody ever has, and I have never once known what I would do about it, and I am sixty-three, and I have a bench and a forge and four men, and I would have gone home on the last day of that week and forgotten this yard by the middle of next month.”

He took his hand off the cart.

“You have not given me a no,” he said. “You have given me a village that never asked me for one, and that is worse and it is about my trade, and I am going to think about it on the road and I do not know what I will have. And I am going to quote six pounds fifteen in this lane in the spring, and I am not going to quote it lower, and that is not stubbornness, it is that a man who drops his price because he has been told something true about himself is a man who has not been told the same thing twice yet.”

---

And the man of thirty-one was in the middle of that yard and was not asked a question and did not get one, and the finding of the twenty-fifth day of his being in this country is that a place with no party is a place that can be done to, and it had now happened to him at a bar, in a yard, in a lane, in a tower and in a press, five times in eleven days, and he had stopped putting his hands behind his back about it somewhere around the fourth time and had nothing else to do with them instead.

“Yes,” he said, to nobody in particular, and about nine people heard it and about four understood it. “The last man in a village who wanted one of these went without. About thirty years before Garrick Terrel was born. The cause ran the other way and it ran the other way for about a hundred and eighty years before that, and Garrick has been trying to account for it for eleven and I have been in this country twenty-five days and I have just been handed the whole of it in a yard by a man of fifty-two and I did not do one thing toward it.”

Nobody agreed with him and nobody disagreed with him and about four people in that yard had the sense to leave him standing there, which is a thing this county has got and has never had to teach anybody.

---

And then the man of fifty-two said the other half of it, and it is the half that matters, and he said it with the lead still in his hand and he said it to the woman of forty-one and not to the yard.

“There is a thing you have not done this morning and I am going to do it and you are not going to stop me, and I have told you the night before on a road that I never do a sum out loud unless I am going to use it.”

“Go on, then.”

“The whole margin of this lane is fifty-seven pounds fifteen shillings a year. Eleven villages, eleven times one thousand two hundred and sixty pence, and that is thirteen thousand eight hundred and sixty pence, and that is the whole of what there is. Nine of those villages have got a woman with a cloth and a tower. Nine times one thousand two hundred and sixty is eleven thousand three hundred and forty pence. Thirteen thousand eight hundred and sixty less eleven thousand three hundred and forty is two thousand five hundred and twenty pence.

“*Two thousand five hundred and twenty pence is ten pounds and ten shillings.*”

He put the lead in his pocket.

“That is what the two villages are worth,” he said. “Not a hundred and fifty-five bells. That is the whole of what those two villages have been worth to this lane every year for about two hundred years, and it is sitting in a levy that is taken off a bar that is not shut, and there is nobody in this county to pay it to and there never has been, and it has been coming in at threepence a load nine loads a day for about two hundred years and it has been going nowhere at all.”

He looked up.

“And there is one man in eleven villages that earns exactly that in a year,” he said. “One shilling and sixpence a day on a hundred and forty days. Two thousand five hundred and twenty pence. *And he is standing at the end of the ninth cart and he called a nine-shilling debt in this morning and has been called out for nineteen years and has never had a penny of it, and he is the only man in this county that could have been paid out of the two villages and there has never been anything to pay him with.*”

Ferrow Hask did not say anything. He had a hand on the side of the ninth cart and he looked at the bar at the end of the lane, which is a post with a bar across it that is not shut and has not been shut for the whole of its life, and he looked at it for about four seconds, and then he said one sentence, and it was not about the two villages and it was not about the money and it is the only thing he said in twenty-five days that was about himself.

“Nineteen years,” said Ferrow Hask. “And I have never once asked what a bar is for.”

---

And that was the morning, and the board went up two more before the last bell for a debt that was paid, and the whole of the margin of two villages for two hundred years was ten pounds and ten shillings and a carter's year, and nobody in two hundred and ten people had known it before about the ninth hour of the fourth day of the sixty-first week, and nobody was told anything they had not been told, and about nine people in that yard had spent the whole of a week waiting to be asked what to do and were not asked.

And the man of thirty-one walked out of the yard at about the fourth hour with a clerk of forty-four and a recordwright of twenty-eight and a man of fifty-two, and the four of them went up the lane toward Nettlebed because there was a board on the inside of a door with a hundred and forty-one names and a column with no heading over it, and the clerk of forty-four could read a hand and the recordwright of twenty-eight could read anything and the man of fifty-two could count and he could do one of the two things he had, and none of the four of them could tell anybody in that village what a figure in a column with no heading over it was a figure of.

He could.

That is the thing he did not say, and it is worth exactly one sentence here because it is the whole of why the sixth of this week is going to happen in a press in a village eleven miles off: a heading over a column is a category, and a category is a thing a person can be put in, and a person in a category is a person in a room, and a woman who has carried four hundred pieces of paper for nine years without her name at the foot of one of them has just told him, in a stair, at about the fourth hour of the third day of that week, that the absence of a heading over a column of figures against a hundred and forty-one names is the only reason any of them is still a person.

He knew it. He could not produce a record. There is no form in eleven villages that says a man may put a thing in front of a room, and there is no room in eleven villages, and the only thing in this county that can be got in front of anything is a plank on a wall with a cloth on it, and he is a man of thirty-one with a third hand in chalk at the foot of it that he has not added to in twenty-five days and that about nine people know at a glance and that a woman of forty-one has said out loud will not be undone.

So he said nothing, and it was the fifth time in eleven days that the right thing to say and the thing he was carrying were the same shape, and he walked up the lane with three people who could each do one thing he could not do and did not put his hands behind his back.

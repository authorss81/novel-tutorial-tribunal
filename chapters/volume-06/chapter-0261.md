# Chapter 261: The Day After A Piece Of Paper Leaves

The fourth day of the fifty-ninth week began with a satchel going back under a seat, and nobody in the county had asked for that to happen and nobody was sorry it had, and by the time the man of thirty-one came out into the yard at the second light the timber cart was thirty yards up the lane with its load and a man of about fifty-one asleep in a shed behind it would not be for another hour.

He had four days of road in front of him. He had no reason to stay. He had no reason to go either, and both of those were true, and the man of thirty-one, who had been in the country eleven days, worked out that the second of them was the one that had been arranged and the first one was the one that had not.

*It is going to be under that seat on the day it goes*, and there was nobody in two hundred and ten people who had said that out loud except a woman of forty-one on the third day of that week, and she had said it about a piece of paper and had been talking about the rest of it as well and had not told anybody she had done that.

The figure on the board at the second light that morning was sixty-three thousand five hundred and seventy-nine, and it was four bells and one more, and the one more was not a stranger and was not anything else on the list, and there was going to be a long afternoon about it.

---

Nobody had decided anything, and that was the trouble with the morning, and the man of fifty-two said so at the second light in about nine words to nobody, which is his habit and is not a complaint.

“Nobody said no and nobody said yes and a thing that has not been decided cannot be took back,” he said. “There is nothing in this village this morning to unpick. I have been in a country eight weeks where there was a room and a column and something to unpick every day of it. I would not know what to do with a morning like this one and I have not known since I got here.”

Nobody in the yard had a better version of it, and about four of them had understood what he had said and the other five had not, and the difference between those two numbers is the difference between a man who has been in a room and a man who has been in a yard.

Then the man of thirty-one, who has spent two volumes learning that the only useful thing a person can do with a piece of paper is put it in front of somebody, said the thing that had actually happened.

“Four lines came into this county and have gone out of it in about a day and a half,” he said, “and during the time they were here, four people read them and two of those four can read at all, and the one who read them out loud in front of nine people was a woman of forty-one who said in the same breath that she was not going to have it in this village, and she was not going to have it on her board, and I have watched her since and she has not written one word of it anywhere and she is right about that and so is everybody else in this yard.”

“You are telling me a piece of paper went for a walk,” said Ferrow Hask, from the end of a cart.

“I am telling you four lines went for a walk to a village that keeps weeks we do not keep, and that a woman of twenty-nine at a press took it, and that it is going to be under a seat in about four days and be under a seat in about nine, and that is the whole of what happened in this village in two days and there was no decision in it anywhere and I have been here eleven days looking for a decision.”

“Then it was wasted,” said Ferrow Hask, and stopped, and put his hand flat on the cart the way he does when he has said something he is going to have to sit behind.

Nobody told him it was not wasted and about four people wanted to and did not, and the reason they did not is that nobody in this yard could say what the wasting was for, and that is a thing about this yard and not about him.

---

The afternoon was ordinary, and the man of thirty-one has a habit of recording that ordinariness was ordinary, and about four things happened in it: a debt, a cart that turned about at the end of the lane because the ground comes up in eleven places in the first mile, a conversation about elms between a man of about sixty-one and a man of about forty that lasted about a quarter of an hour and settled nothing, and a woman of about thirty-five whose hens had got out.

The hens had got out because the gate is a post with a bar across it that is not shut, and the bar is not shut for the whole of its life and is only shut when somebody is in a mood about it, and the hens had found a gap in a hedge four hundred yards off the lane in about the fourth hour of the day, and by the time the light went they were on the wrong side of it and the light was going.

And then it was the last bell, and that is the one you can time.

There were about eleven people in the lane by then, and the woman of thirty-five got there first, and a boy of about nine who had been sent after them got there with her, and a man of about fifty came up the lane from the other end at about the speed of a man who has had a drink and had a long day of it in a county with nothing in it, and all three of them put hands on the rope within about two seconds of each other, and the woman of thirty-five shouted something at the boy, and the boy shouted something back, and the man of about fifty said *go on then*, and the bell went once.

The woman of forty-one was on the ladder and came down the last three rungs and looked at the board and then at the three of them and then at the board again, and did not say anything for about as long as it takes to cross a yard.

“That is one,” she said.

“That is one,” said the man of thirty-one.

“It is four of the clock and it is one of something, and the four of the clock is a hundred and forty days of the year and every day of it, and the one of something is the fifth, and I have to put up five.”

She did not put up five. She went and got the lead and put up four and left the fifth for the morning, which is what she does, and which is worth saying because a great many people in a yard at the last bell would have put up five and been wrong and been right at the same time.

---

And here is the whole of the day, and it took about four minutes to say and about four days to stop being true.

She went through the list. She goes through the list about twice a year and she goes through it in the order it happens to be in, which is the order of how much it weighs, and a burial is one and a boundary walked is seven and a debt called in is two and a market is four and a stranger is three and a fire is two.

“One is a death,” she said. “That is the only thing on that list that is worth one. I have had a one that was not a death twice in twenty-five years and I have not had the other twenty-three in the same fortnight, and I can tell you about both of the ones and neither of them is a thing I am going to repeat in a yard.”

“What were they?”

“The first one was a woman shouting a name into the top of the tower because her man had cut his leg on a scythe and she could not reach him and she wanted the whole village in her garden and she got it,” said Ottily Roke, “and the second one was a man who had had a drink ringing it to say he was going to bed, and I have never worked out why he thought anybody wanted to know, and both of them were about a person and neither of them was about a thing that had happened, and that is the difference and it is the only difference I have got.”

She looked at the three of them in the lane, and the woman of thirty-five, who was the first of them there and had the most right to be there, said:

“It were the hens.”

“Yes,” said the woman of forty-one. “And I heard the hens and I heard the boy and I heard him say *go on then*, and I know which of the three of you had which reason, and I have got one ring and three reasons, and I am going to have to put up one, and a person in a room four hundred miles off is going to be able to prove that on the fourth day of this week a bell went once in Coldharrow, and that is the whole of what they will be able to prove, and it will be correct.”

Nobody argued with her and nobody in that lane could have argued with her, and about four of the eleven people standing there understood that what had just been said was not a complaint about hens.

---

The man of thirty-one had been in the country eleven days and he had watched the hands on that rope on ten of the mornings of them, and he had never once been in a lane at the last bell in his life before this one, and it is worth saying exactly how small the thing was that he knew, because it is the whole of what he has learned in this country and it took him eleven days and a piece of paper that has left the county.

“You had all three of you on that rope inside about two seconds,” he said, “and one pull is one ring and not three, which I did not know at the second light this morning and know now, and it does not matter what you all had reasons for because a pull is a pull.”

“That is the way of a rope,” said the man of about fifty, and went to bed.

“Yes,” said the man of thirty-one. “And I can account for it, and I am the only person in this county who can account for it this evening, and I want to be careful about why, because it is not because I am clever. It is because I have been standing in this yard at the second light on ten of the ten mornings of them, and I have not been doing anything, and a man who is doing nothing watches the hands, and none of the rest of you has ever had to look at that, because you all know what a ring is for and I do not, and a person who has to ask what a ring is for is a person who ends up watching the hands.”

The woman of forty-one looked at him for a second, properly, the way she did on his first morning, and whatever she decided about him was decided inside that second and not in the whole of the rest of it, and she did not say what it was.

“No,” she said. “There is nobody else. That is not a thing anybody has ever said in this lane and I would like it noticed that it is a man of thirty-one saying it and not a woman of forty-one.”

---

And then the man of fifty-two said the thing that was underneath it, and said it out loud, and about four people in the lane heard it, and it is the sentence this whole stretch of days turns on and it took a woman of thirty-five with a hen out of her own head to arrive at it.

“A number that anybody can move is not a record,” said the man of thirty-one. “And that is not a thing about bells. It is a thing about the only thing in this county that anybody has ever been able to look at. Four bells a day is four bells a day and it is the same in eleven villages and it does not matter who pulls. But the two on top of it are the board, and the board is the whole of what there is, and the two on top of it are whatever a person with a hand on a rope was doing at that moment, and there is no column, and there was never a column, and there is not going to be one.

“So the count is the easiest thing in this county for a person to move and the hardest thing in this county to argue with afterwards, and I have been here eleven days and I have been trying to work out how to make a room take this seriously, and the answer is that a room cannot take a number seriously, because a number is not a thing, and the only person in two hundred and ten people who can be asked about a number is the person who keeps it, and she is paid ninepence a day for a hundred and forty days, and I have been telling that to a recordwright since the fifth day of that week and I have just found out that I have also been telling it to a bell.”

The lane went on being a lane, and the last bell had been and about nine people went off to do whatever it is they do at the last bell, and the three who stayed were the man of thirty-one, the man of fifty-two, and a woman of forty-one on the ground at the foot of a ladder.

“Tomorrow will be four,” she said.

“Tomorrow will be four,” he agreed, “and I have seen one of them, on the first day of that week, and that one you could not account for either, and Tarin has a thing about a week of four that he has never written down anywhere and has told me about once, and I asked you on the first day of that week what a day of four was and you took me up a ladder for it.”

“It is four,” she said, “and four is the number that board says about most of the days in a year, and I will tell you why I know it is most of them, because it is arithmetic and it took me about nine minutes. In twenty-five years that board has gone up fifty-four thousand seven hundred and fifty times. The four bells a day over twenty-five years is one thousand four hundred and sixty a year, and twenty-five years of that is thirty-six thousand five hundred. The rest of it is eighteen thousand two hundred and fifty, and that is every burial and every boundary and every debt and every market and every stranger and every fire that anybody in this village has rung for since I was sixteen. *So there is about two rings a day on top of the clock, and two rings a day on top of the clock does not touch most of the days at all, and the days it does not touch are the days the board says four on.*”

“That is a day of nothing.”

“That is a day of nothing and a day of nothing and a day of nine strangers in it are the same number, and I said that to you on the first day of that week and I have not said it since because I did not know what it was for, and I have just found out what it is for, and I am going to tell you at the second light tomorrow because you are going to make me, and because I want to say it out loud once with a person in the yard, and there has not been one in twenty-five years.”

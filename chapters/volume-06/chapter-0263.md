# Chapter 263: A Date Bought By Somebody Else

The sixth day of the fifty-ninth week was the day a boundary was walked, and the board had read sixty-three thousand five hundred and eighty-eight at the second light, and it was the first boundary anybody in Coldharrow had walked since the twenty-second year of a board on a wall, and the man of thirty-one was in the lane at the second light to be told that and not to say it.

Nine men went out of the village, and four of them came from Ellerby, and one of the four was a woman of about sixty who was twenty-one when the last one was walked and who has a name, and her name is Wenna Skell, and it is the first time anybody in this county has said it out loud in a lane.

They walked the last mile and a half of a hedge that runs nine miles from this gate to that village, which is where the two elms are, and a man of about sixty-one put fresh marks on them because the marks that are there are nine years old and the tools that made them are not, and the woman of forty-one counted out loud the whole way, and there were seven.

Seven at the first elm and the corners and the end and twice coming back, and the woman of forty-one said the number out loud in the middle of it in the voice she uses, and the man of thirty-one heard the seventh one and understood that it was the same seven that had gone onto that board in the twenty-second year, in her father's hand, in the grain, and that the board could not tell him so and never would.

“You have just made the seventh ring,” he said, afterwards, in the lane, to a woman of forty-one and a man of about sixty-one and a woman of about sixty. “And the board has got one number on it for that, and a number for the last time, and they are the same number, and there is nothing on the whole of that plank that says they are the same walk.”

“No,” said Wenna Skell. “There is not. And I have known that for nine years and I have never said it to anybody, and I am not going to start now, and I would like to go home.”

---

And then he did the thing he said in a yard on the fifth day of that week he was going to do, and he did it badly, and he did it exactly as he had said he would, which is not the same thing.

He and the man of fifty-two walked the eleven miles to Coldharrow-edge, which is not in the March and keeps weeks this county has never heard of, and they were in the yard of a press at about the fourth hour and Nell Callow was there and took one look at what he had come about and had it half done before he had got to the end of the sentence.

“A day for the third day of that week,” he said. “For the day a sealed copy of an order was opened and read out in a yard in Coldharrow. In the grain year your clerk uses, because I have no idea what else it takes and you told me it takes that.”

“That takes that,” said Nell Callow, and wrote it in about four minutes, because she is fast, and charged a pound, three shillings and sixpence, and did not ask him a single question about it, which is a thing she has relied on four hundred times.

“Who is it for?”

“It is for me. And I am going to have somebody else buy a second one and it is going to be for the same day, and then there will be two names in this county that a person four hundred miles off can be told about.”

“That is a foolish thing to want,” said Nell Callow, “and I am not going to charge you for the advice because you did not ask for it, and I will tell you what it is in one sentence and then I am going back to my work. *Two people who bought the same day have not made a day twice. They have made one day with two people standing in front of it, and a room will take whichever of the two it likes, and it has never once in my life taken the one I would have chosen.*”

---

The man of fifty-two did the sum on the side of her press bench, because there was nowhere else and because it is what he does, and it took about four minutes and about eleven people watched it and none of them had seen it done before and about five of them understood the sum and four of them understood what the sum was for.

“One pound three shillings and sixpence is two hundred and eighty-two pence, because twelve pence to the shilling and twenty to the pound and it is twenty-three shillings and sixpence. Two of those is five hundred and sixty-four. Five hundred and sixty-four less four hundred and eighty is eighty-four pence, which is seven shillings. *Two dates is two pounds and seven shillings exactly.*

“And a day of me on that lane is eighteen pence, and five hundred and sixty-four goes into eighteen thirty-one times over and there is sixpence left, because thirty-one times eighteen is five hundred and fifty-eight. *Two dates is thirty-one days of the man who walks the road and sixpence.*

“And a year of her is one thousand two hundred and sixty pence, which is five pounds and five shillings, and one thousand two hundred and sixty less five hundred and sixty-four is six hundred and ninety-six. Six hundred and ninety-six less four hundred and eighty is two hundred and sixteen, which is eighteen shillings. *Two dates is a year of the woman who keeps the count less two pounds and eighteen shillings.*”

He put the lead down.

“Two pounds and eighteen shillings of a woman of forty-one’s year,” he said, “and I have known her thirteen days. I printed her whole year on a cart’s side in a yard in that village six days ago and it came out at five pounds five, and I have been carrying the five pounds five round since, and this afternoon I have spent two pounds and eighteen of it. The woman was not in the room and does not know, and I would like that noticed by about eleven people, and by nobody else.”

Nobody said anything. About four of them had understood that the two pounds and eighteen was not a price at all and was the entire annual wage of a woman, spent in an afternoon by three men.

---

They walked the eleven miles back and the man of thirty-one did most of the walking in silence and then said, at about the seventh mile, the sentence that had cost him the most of anything he has said in this country:

“I said it on a hedge, in front of a boy of seventeen, on the fourth day of the fifty-eighth week, and I said that I had been in this country four days and had decided in those four days that the answer was to get a second person who can be asked, and that I never once asked myself what would happen to the first of the two when the second of them arrived. *That was nine days ago. I have got a second person, and the first one is going to know about it this afternoon, and I did not ask her either, and there was a moment on the bench at that press where I could have said it to her and I bought the day instead.*”

He walked about a quarter of a mile.

“That is the thing I have no answer for,” he said, “and I am not going to have one in a lane, and I have stopped expecting to, and I would rather say it than not say it.”

---

And at the last bell in Coldharrow, in the yard, a man of thirty-eight was standing at the end of a cart with a slip of paper in his hand, and he had walked to Coldharrow-edge and back that day as well, and neither of them had seen the other on the road, and he said so first, in about nine words, before anybody asked him anything.

“I have bought a day,” said Ferrow Hask. “The same day. The third day of that week. I have got my name on it and I did not know yours was on it and I have not asked and I am telling you because I have been standing here for about an hour deciding whether to tell you and I have decided, and I would like it noticed that I decided.”

“You wanted a day for the lane,” said the man of fifty-two.

“I asked the woman at the press for a day for the lane,” said Ferrow Hask, “and I told her what the lane is and where it goes and how long I have walked it, and she said: tell me the day it was laid out. And I said it was always there. *And she said: then I cannot help you.* And that is the whole of a day in this county in one sentence from a woman of twenty-nine and I have been on the road nineteen years and I have never once been able to put that into words, and she did it in about four seconds and charged me a pound three shillings and sixpence for a day that is no use to me whatever.”

He put the paper in his coat.

“I want it to be a road,” he said, “and a road has a gate on it, and a gate wants a day on it, and there is no day on it, and I have been the man at that bar for nineteen years and I have never once been able to say what the bar is for, and I bought a day for a piece of paper being read out in a yard instead, and I would do it again tomorrow, and if you want to say something about that you can and I will stand here and listen to it.”

The recordwright of twenty-eight, who had been in the country thirteen days and had said nine words in a garden on the fourth day of that week and again on the fifth, said the thing she had come to say and did not improve on it.

“Two names on one thing is not two witnesses,” she said. “It is one thing with two people standing in front of it, and one of them is a man of thirty-eight who has wanted a day for a lane for nineteen years and one of them is a stranger, and a room nine days from this field will take the one it likes, and I have been in one of those rooms and I have watched it happen, and I would like it written down in a place a person can be held to account in, and I have looked for one for about a week and there is not one in this county.”

Nobody wrote it down anywhere. It is in four places.

---

And then, at about the last of the light, a woman of about forty came through the bar that is not shut with a barrow, and Delya Craven has run letters and small things up and down the eleven villages for nineteen years and she was in the yard about four minutes before anybody understood what she had, which is a folded sheet of paper with a printed head on it that said nothing any person in that yard had seen before except one of them.

“It is not for you and it is not from you,” she said, and put it on a cart's side, and would not leave it. “It came off the seat house cart on the third day and I have had it four days in a drawer under a cloth with a list of eggs on top of it, and I was going to bring it on a market day and this was not one, and I have brought it anyway because there is a woman in this village that reads and there is nobody else on this lane that does.”

“What is it.”

“It is a thing off a bench four hundred miles off,” said Delya Craven, “and it has a day on it, and it is not your day, and I do not know what it is for and I have not read it and I am not going to, and I am telling you that I have not read it so that nobody says later that I did.”

# Chapter 270: What He Wrote On A Board In A Place He Was Not In

A stranger came through on the sixth day of the sixtieth week at about the fourth hour, and a stranger is three, and with the four of the clock that made the day a five on the board, who went down the lane with a dog and a stick and would not say where he had started from, and the man of thirty-one had been in the country twenty days and was in a yard at the ninth hour because a woman of about forty with a barrow had come through the gate that is not shut with a sheet of paper rolled inside her coat, and had put it on a cart's side, and had said the thing she says.

“I have not read it,” said Delya Craven. “I have not read it on the road and I have not read it in the shed and I have not read it in the two days it took to come back, and I am telling you first and I am telling you in front of the two of you, because I told a man on the market day that I had not read it and I would like there to be two of me saying it and not one.”

And she went down the lane, and he did not pick it up for about four minutes, and then he picked it up and carried it to the tower, and about nine people followed him, and nobody sent anybody, and about four of the nine had followed him on the two previous days for reasons that were becoming harder to keep separate.

---

The woman of forty-one was at the foot of her own ladder with a cloth, and there is no form in eleven villages that says a person may read out a thing brought by a carrier, and she did it anyway, and she asked for it, which is a thing she has never done about anything.

“Read it out,” said Ottily Roke, to the man of thirty-one. “No. Read it out, whoever can. Not you. I have watched you for twenty days and I know what you do with a sheet and I would rather somebody in this yard who has no reason to want anything out of it read it.”

The recordwright of twenty-eight read the two hundred words. She read them well, which is her whole trade, and she read the four lines under them in the middle, and nobody stopped her, and the four lines were not new. They had been in this yard twice. They were in a satchel under a seat of a timber cart four hundred miles off by now, and in two hundred and ten people's heads, and they had not been in a room.

And then she got to the bottom of it and stopped.

“There is a figure,” said Sera Quill.

“There is a figure,” said the man of thirty-one, who had not said anything at all in a yard for about eleven minutes.

“Nobody has said whose hand it is in.”

“No,” he said. “Nobody has. And nobody is going to, and I would like that noticed, because about nine people in this yard have just looked at it and know, and not one of them has said a word, and I did not tell them and neither did she and neither did the woman who brought it.”

The figure was in pencil at the foot of the four lines on the left-hand side, and the figure was sixty-three thousand five hundred and ten.

And the figure at the head of the board on the wall of the tower, in the newer of two hands, with a cloth wiped over it at the second light that morning, was sixty-three thousand six hundred and forty-two.

---

Not one of the nine said a word for long enough that somebody went round the outside of that yard rather than through it, and the man of fifty-two had the lead out of his pocket and had it in his hand and did not put it on the cart's side, which is the first time in twenty days that anybody in this country has seen him hold a lead and not use it, and it was because the sum was not the hard part.

“A hundred and thirty-two,” he said.

“Yes,” said the man of thirty-one, and did not look at the lead in the other man’s hand, and the other man noticed him not looking at it.

“A hundred and thirty-two pence is a shilling and a bit and it is not a figure of anything, and I am going to do it anyway, and I am going to do it out loud, and nobody is going to stop me, because it is the only thing I know how to do and this is the morning I do it.

“Nineteen days. You wrote it on the first morning and you have not written it since and it has stood in the chalk on that wall for nineteen days while the wall went on being a wall. So the sum is the rings of nineteen days. *Five, eleven, five, six, eight, five, seven, four, thirteen, five. Five, four, eleven, eight, six, five, seven, thirteen, four.*”

He put the figures on the cart's side in two rows, and it took about nine seconds, and about four people in the yard could follow it and about six could not.

“That is sixty-nine, and that is sixty-three, and sixty-nine and sixty-three is a hundred and thirty-two. *Which is what the board has done since the morning you chalked it.* So the number at the foot of that sheet is not a number of anything in this village. It is the shape of nineteen days. And a hundred and thirty-two is a number that could be nineteen days or eleven burials or forty-four strangers or sixteen boundaries, and there is no column and there was never a column, and you have carried nineteen days into a piece of paper in your own hand and there is no day on it anywhere.”

“I know,” said he, and put his hands behind his back, which is what he does in a yard when there is nothing left to do with them.

“And the last one,” said Tarin Keel, and he put his finger on the last figure in the second row. “The last one is four. *The last of the nineteen days was a day of four.* Four bells and nothing else, and a woman of forty-one told him on the fifth day of the fifty-ninth week that four is the number that board says about most of the days in a year. So the very last day of the nineteen, the one nearest the sheet, the one that is easiest to find, is the one day out of the nineteen that you could not find with a lamp and a boy. *You have put the fortnight in the paper and you have put the nothing on the top of it.*”

He put the lead down.

“I have counted carts on roads for nineteen years,” he said, “and I have never once said one of those out loud in a yard, and I have said it, and I would like it noticed that the man it is about is standing four feet away and has not said a word.”

---

The woman of forty-one came down off the last two rungs of the ladder, and she did not look at him, and she looked at the figure on the sheet for about as long as it takes to cross a yard, and then she said the thing nobody had asked her for, which is what she has done on the four days of his being in this country that anybody has asked her anything, and she said it in her own mouth, and she is not a woman who softens a thing, and she is right about the board every single time and has been for twenty-five years.

“No,” she said. “I am not going to say whose it is. I have known since about a second and a half after the woman of twenty-eight stopped reading and I have said nothing and I am not going to say it, and there are about nine people in this yard who have known it and they are not going to say it either, and that is the arrangement and I have made it in the last ninety seconds and I am not calling it a favour.

“Here is what I am going to say, and it is the thing, and it has been in me since the noon bell yesterday and it is not about him.

“*There is a number inside a piece of paper in this county.* That is what has happened. Not a name, not a claim, not a debt, not a date. A number, in a hand, on the bottom left of a sheet that is about a river four hundred miles off, and it is the number that was on my wall on the first morning of the first day, and it has been in this village for nineteen days and it is now in about four hands and I do not know how many and I cannot find out and I am not going to try.

“And I want the other half of it said as well, because everybody in this yard is thinking the first half. *A number on a wall is a thing about a village. A number on a piece of paper is a thing about a person.* Those are different and I have known that for twenty-five years without ever having had to, and I have had to this morning, and what it is is that on a wall it is ours and on paper it is his, and there is no column on my board that says whose hand anything was, and there is not going to be one, and there never was one, and I have kept that board for twenty-five years and I could not tell you this morning until a woman of twenty-nine eleven miles off put a figure on a sheet because she wanted a number and had nothing else to do.

“I have been a person in a record for one morning. In this county. On a wall that is a number. *And I did not ask to be one, and I am not going to be able to undo it, and I am not going to be told I am sorry for it*, and I want that in four places and I do not care which four.”

She put the cloth over her shoulder.

“Now go on,” she said, to nobody, to about nine people, and to the man who had said nothing at all for about eleven minutes. “There is a debt at the ninth house and the noon bell is at about the fourth hour and it is not going to be rung twice because there is a figure on a piece of paper, and I have got a board.”

---

He did not explain.

He did not apologise, and he did not offer to take the figure off, and he did not take it off. The reason is in his own hand, on a leaf, on a road, in the cold, on the way back from a press, and he gave it to nobody, and the sentence is about what becomes of a person in front of a village when he takes a thing back off a wall that was never his to write on, and it is the reason he did not offer on the first morning, and it is nineteen days old and it is still true.

He stood in a yard in a country he had been in twenty days, with a good name of nobody's to spend, and he had found a thing to spend, and it was the standing of a woman of forty-one who had never once been asked what she was for. He did not do it on purpose. It was done inside a copy of four lines he had not read, on a day of four, by a woman of twenty-nine who wanted a number and had nothing else, and the third hand is still in the chalk at the foot of that board where it has been for nineteen days, and there is no column on it for him. He looked at the cloth on her shoulder for a while, and then at the gate, and then at the middle of the yard, which is where he looks when he has nothing to do with his hands and has not had for twenty days.

The leg is no better and the thing he is carrying is still off nothing, and he has been in the country twenty days, and nobody has thanked him and nobody is relieved.

The recordwright of twenty-eight took the sheet off the cart's side, folded it along the crease it already had, put it inside her satchel, and said the thing she had said twice in nineteen days, in a voice that had stopped being careful about it some hours earlier.

She said it the third time and it came out word for word as it had come out twice before, and that was the whole of the point of it. “A count that leaves the board is a count somebody can use. I said it in a garden on the fourth day of the fifty-ninth week and I said it again in a yard on the fifth, and I have said it twice and I know I have said it twice. This is what it looks like. *And I would like it written down somewhere that is not a wall*, and there is a woman of forty-one on a wall and a woman of sixty holding a sheet she asked a carrier for, and neither of them will do it, and I am not going to do it for them, and I am going to carry the sentence to a town that keeps weeks I have never learned and I am going to hand it to a clerk and I would like him to have a bad afternoon.”

---

And then the boy of seventeen was in the doorway, and he had been in the yard for the whole of it with his satchel against the wall, and he had not said anything, and about half an hour later he asked him the question.

It was in a doorway, and it was to a person, and it was not to a yard and not to nobody, and the man of thirty-one had been in this country twenty days and had been asked exactly one question by anybody in it that was worth being asked, and it was this one, and it came from a person of seventeen with a book under his arm and a stub of pencil in his hand.

“Mr Vester. Can I ask you a thing that is not about work.”

“You can ask me the thing.”

“I have run a line since I was twelve,” said Bram Orren. “I have got eleven villages in it in an order, and the order is the same every year, and the order does not go by the ground. I have run lines that go by the ground and I know what that looks like, and it does not look like this. It does not go by the water and it does not go by the hedges, because a line that goes by the hedges goes round, and this one does not go round anything. It goes to the next place whatever is in the way of it. And there is no map of it in this county and there is no map of it in Coldharrow-edge, and the men who use it are four hundred miles off where that paper came from, and I have not met one of them and I have had one letter off one of them, which I am not supposed to write, and which I have posted, and I would write it again tomorrow.

“And the men who use it do not choose it either. Ferrow Hask has gone it nineteen times a year for nineteen years and he has never once said why it goes where it goes. I asked him on the first day of that week, in the words, because I ring what I cross and I have to be able to say what I am on. He said he had never found out. I have been asking since I was twelve and I have got nowhere, and there is nobody in this county I have not asked, and about four of them have told me to go and ask somebody who is dead.

“So I have got a question and it has got a mark on the end of it, and I have never written a question in that book in five years because a question is not a thing that crossed and that is the whole of what the book is for. *Who laid it out?*”

He put the stub of pencil against the page and did not write it, and then he wrote it, in about four seconds, in a hand much better than anybody's in that yard, and turned the book round so that the man it was meant for could see it, and there was a mark at the end of it, and he was not being clever and he was not being dramatic and he was seventeen and had asked.

The man of thirty-one had the one sentence he had the standing to say, and it was the sentence he had spoken out loud to himself on a hedge in this county on the fourth day of the fifty-eighth week and had not offered to anybody, and it is the sentence that keeps the people in a building and the people in a room apart, and it protects the building, and he did not say it on the hedge because there was nobody on the hedge to say it to.

There was a person in the doorway now. That was the whole difference between the two occasions and it made no difference at all.

He waited the way he has waited twice in two years, for something that has never once had to explain itself, and nothing came.

“I do not know,” he said.

“That is two of us,” said Bram Orren, and shut the book, and went to do his day, because there were about four bells left and somebody in this village had to count them.

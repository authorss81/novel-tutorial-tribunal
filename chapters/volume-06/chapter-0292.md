# Chapter 292: The Hundred And Forty People

The board at Coldharrow stood at sixty-three thousand seven hundred and eighty-two by the time the three of them left the yard, and it was four bells and nothing whatever on top of them, and the forty-second morning of a man of thirty-one in this country was the forty-second morning of the ordinary number, and nobody sent word to the woman of forty-one that a woman of forty-one had been in her yard and she did not ask for word to be sent.

The recordwright of twenty-eight packed her book into the satchel and did it in about nine seconds, and the man of fifty-two was already at the end of the lane, and the woman of forty-one from the Stennick end stood in the yard and did not go and did not say she was not going, and after about a minute and a half she picked up her bundle and followed, which is not the same thing as agreeing and everybody who saw it understood the difference.

---

They got to the tower wall at about the fourth hour, and there was a cloth on the ladder rail, and the woman of forty-one was on the third of the four rungs with the cloth over her shoulder, and she came down when she saw three people, and the one she looked at longest was the one she had not been in the same county with.

The recordwright did not ask. She put the sheet on the flat of the cart's side and weighted it with the bit of lead the man of fifty-two handed her without being asked, and she stepped back, which is the correct thing to do and about four people in that yard had worked out why without being able to say it.

Ottily Roke read the nine lines out.

She was not asked, and nobody asked her, and she had read four things off a cart's side in this yard about two weeks ago and had read a four-word sentence in a doorway and had read a letter from four hundred miles off, and it is thirty-one years since anybody in this village first handed her something to read and about six weeks since she stopped waiting to be asked about it.

She read the whole of it standing up, in her own voice, the way a person reads nine lines off a cart's side, and she gave the word at the top of it its own beat because nobody had told her to.

And then she said the thing that is the price of the whole of six weeks and she said it to the woman in the coat and not to the yard, and about four people in that yard understood all of it and the rest heard a woman of forty-one talk.

“Nine lines,” she said, “and a word at the top, and about two inches at the bottom with nothing printed in it. I have got a plank on this wall about the size of a door and the paint has come and gone and come again until the grain stands up in it, and the figure is at the top and I have never once written a word over any of it, and there is a woman four hundred miles off who has been putting a word at the top of things about places for nine months and she does not know what it costs, and neither do I, and I have had thirty-one years of finding out what it costs not to.”

She put the sheet back down on the cart's side, square with the edge of it.

“Ask me the second question,” she said. “You have been standing in my yard for a quarter of an hour and you have not asked me one single question and I have been waiting about six weeks for somebody to ask me one about anything at all, and I can answer exactly one of them and no more, so you had better spend it now.”

The woman of forty-one from Draymoor asked her the one thing only Ottily Roke in eleven villages could answer, which was how much a bell costs, because a woman who has a count for twenty-five years knows every figure in this county and does not know the price of the only thing anybody has ever wanted to put on top of her.

The recordwright of twenty-eight said it, in about nine seconds, standing up, with the working, because she is the person who reads things out and Tarin Keel was in the yard and did not want to do it in front of about nine people.

And the woman of forty-one from Draymoor said the other half, out loud, to about nine people, in a yard in the Briar March, having walked a day for it.

“A bell is six pounds fifteen and a keeper is five pounds and five shillings a year, and the difference is a pound and ten, and the difference is the same every year for as long as anybody rings it, and that is the first time in my life that a person has told me a price for the thing my mother used to do and my mother never told me and I never asked her because you do not ask a person who is paid.”

Nobody in that yard said anything for about four seconds.

And then the woman of forty-one in Coldharrow said: “I have been nine pence a day for twenty-five years and I have never once been asked what I was for, and I have said so out loud in this yard about six times, and it has not helped me, and a woman from four hundred miles off has been in my yard for a quarter of an hour and I have said the half of it to her and not to the nine of you, and I would like it noticed that that is the first time in thirty-one years I have said a thing in this yard to one person and not to the rest of them.”

---

Petrow Sill was at the end of his bad road and he was about to go and do a thing with a roof that had partly fallen in, and he had nine farms on that road and about eleven people in his house and a lane he had walked about forty times in nine years and could not say where it went.

He said he knew the way. He said it before anybody had asked him, which is a fact about him and not about them.

“I know the way and I have never been there,” he said. “I have walked the top of it about nine times and it goes on and there is a place where the ground drops and after that it is somebody else's ground and I have not been on it. My grandfather went on it twice and he would not talk about it and he died in my mother's time and I was not asked.”

He put his hand on the gate of his own house and left it there for about four seconds, which is the thing this county does instead of swearing.

“Eleven months ago there was a sheet with a list of places on it and a rule on it, and a man of fifty-two came to my gate and read me the rule out, and a woman of twenty-nine wrote me down on it because I had said the word *there* once to a carter in a lane, and I had a cross against my gate for about a month. And then I came down to a market and said a thing in a yard and took the cross off, and I said in that yard that being on a list frightens me more than being asked, and both of those are true of me, and the first one is the truer.

“You are asking me for the way. That is not on your list. *A thing you are not on is a thing you can be asked for, and I have had one of those in my life and it is the reason I know the way.* So you are asking me and not the book and I am going to tell you, and I am not going to be thanked, because the day somebody puts a cross against this gate again for telling a stranger how to get somewhere is the day this road of mine stops being mine.”

Then he gave them the whole of it, standing at his own gate, and it took about four minutes, and the working was in it because Tarin Keel asked for it and got it.

“One shilling and sixpence a day,” said Petrow Sill. “A hundred and forty days a year, because a cart does not go out in a March winter and nobody has ever counted the days it does not, and that is the count the whole of this county uses and it is wrong and everybody who uses it knows it is wrong. Two thousand five hundred and twenty pence. Ten pounds and ten shillings a year. Two days there and two days back is four days of it, and four times one shilling and sixpence is seventy-two pence, and seventy-two pence is six shillings, and I am not going to be paid for it and I am not going to be thanked and I am going to lose two days of my year over it.”

---

They got to Trope at about the ninth hour and Isel Cardew was in her own yard with a handbell under her arm and, behind her, a gate bar that has hung open her whole life, and she was not surprised, because nobody in this county is ever surprised, and she said the only thing she was going to say and she said it standing on her own ground.

“No,” she said. “You do not get to do this with it.”

She did not say what *it* was. About nine people who had come up the lane with them had followed about half of it.

“A hundred and forty-one people said no out loud on their own ground about four hundred years ago, in their own yards, one at a time, and then they kept a bill and somebody kept the bill and there is no name over the column, and I have said in this yard that that thing is not ours. *I said that to nine people about six weeks ago and a man of thirty-one was in the yard and did not say a word, and here you are, eleven weeks later, with a sheet of paper in your coat and a woman from a place four hundred miles off walking behind you up my lane, and I want the whole of that said out loud because you are right and I am right and neither of us is going to win it.*”

She put the handbell down on the flat of the bar of her own gate.

“You may not use a thing that is not yours. You may not expose a thing that is not yours. And you may not carry a thing that is not yours four hundred miles and lay it on a table in a room with a hundred and forty people in it so that it can be looked at.

“And I am not coming with you. *I am not coming with you because I have been in that tower four times and I am a person of this lane and that plank is the only thing in eleven villages that anybody has ever been asked to keep, and the day a person from another county puts it in a room is the day it stops being a thing that was kept and starts being a thing that was shown.*”

“Fine,” said the man of thirty-one.

She looked at him for about two seconds.

“You are not going to argue with me,” she said.

“No,” he said. “You are right and I have got nothing that is worth nine seconds and I would rather be honest about that than find something in about four minutes and use it.”

“No,” said Isel Cardew. “That is the fifth time in six weeks you have said a thing like that in a yard and I have stopped being surprised by it and I have started being suspicious of it, and I am not going to ask you about that either, and you have got about an hour of daylight and a woman of forty-one behind you and you had better go.”

---

The road past the ninth farm goes down, and then it goes along a water, and the water has a channel cut in it about a foot wide and a man of fifty-two looked at the channel for about four minutes and said that somebody maintained it, and that it had been maintained inside living memory, and that a channel with water in it going away from you is a thing about four hundred miles of people drinking what you have got, and neither of them said the rest of it and Tarin Keel said it in about nine words and it cost him his day.

And then the ground came up and there were about nine houses, and then eleven more, and then about forty.

It came at the last of the light, and the light went about four minutes after they came to the first hedge, and the whole of the valley went dark at once the way a valley does.

There is a tower in Draymoor about four times the height of a house and there is a bell in it, and the bell has not been rung since before the mother of the woman of forty-one died.

Nobody in the village will say which year the money stopped.

That is worth saying exactly, because it is the same failure the Briar March has been about for seven weeks and it is in a different shape and about nine people in two counties would have understood it and nobody in either county has ever met the other.

There is a keeper's wage in Draymoor of ninepence a day on a hundred and forty days, which is one thousand two hundred and sixty pence, and it is five pounds and five shillings a year, and it stopped when a thing stopped, and the thing that stopped was not the money, it was the arrangement, and there is no date on the arrangement in Draymoor because Draymoor keeps no days the way Coldharrow keeps rings, and a bell that is not rung is not a day that does not happen.

A bell that is not rung is a day that nobody can point at. It went on and did not go on, and the two of those are the same absence.

“Nobody here can say in which year it stopped,” said the woman of forty-one, at her own gate. “Not me and not the man who rings the handbell and not the four of us who were here when the last person was paid. I have asked twice, once to my father and once to a man who came about the money in my mother's time, and both of them said the same sentence, and the sentence is: it was before I was here.”

---

There were about nine people in a yard in Draymoor at about the last of the light on the seventh day of the sixty-third week and there were about a hundred and forty people in the valley and about nine of them were under about nine years old, and about forty of the hundred and forty were under about twenty.

There is a leader and he is forty and he is called Corvin Hale and he has been the one who is asked for about nine years and he has never once been asked what he is for either, and he came out into the yard on his own ground with a lantern in his hand and about four other people behind him, and he did not ask them who they were and he did not ask them for anything.

He let them stand in his yard about a minute and a half and then he said the thing that is the whole of his half of this book.

“I will hear anybody who comes,” he said. “That is what I have got, and it is not much, and I have said it in this yard about nine times in nine years and I have meant it every time and about two of those times somebody came. Nobody has ever come about this before. You have come a long way and it is late and I am not going to make a speech.

“So: say what you are going to ask for. *Not what you have come about. What you are going to ask for when you are in the room.*”

And the man of thirty-one stood in a yard in a district four hundred miles off and said, in about nine words, the thing he had said out loud on a hillside, on a path, in a market yard, in a lane and at a bar six times in six weeks.

“I have no standing in this village at all. I have no office and no party and no bench and no name on anything and there is nobody in this valley who owes me one thing, and I would like that said out loud in your own yard before I say one single word about a sheet of paper.”

Nobody thanked him. About four people in that yard worked out that he had just told a room the cheapest thing anybody could tell it, and that he had done it on purpose, and that nobody was going to get anything out of the rest of the day on the strength of it.

And Corvin Hale put the lantern down on a flat thing by his own door, and looked at the woman of forty-one who had come with them, and then at the man of thirty-one, and then at the recordwright of twenty-eight, and said the thing that is the whole of Chapter 292 and is not what the man of thirty-one came with.

“I am not going to ask you for a road,” he said. “I have not got a road and I have never wanted one and there is not one within nine miles and there is not going to be one.

“I am going to ask whether a corridor has anything to say about the water. *That channel past the ninth farm is mine and it goes four hundred miles and there are people drinking out of it who have never heard my name and about a hundred of them would be drinking nothing in a month if the whole of this valley were moved out of the way.* I have not got that in a sentence. I have got it in about nine years of my wife’s hands, and I am going to put it into a sentence tonight, and I am not going to do it well, and I would like to know before tomorrow whether anybody in this valley has a better sentence than mine.”

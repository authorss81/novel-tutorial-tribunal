# Chapter 443: He Sets A Form In The Window Of The Press With Two Lines In It, And His Own Name Is On The Foot Of The Fifth Page And The Line Beside It Is Empty

Fourth day of the eighty-fifth week. Shelf day three hundred and eighteen, his hundred and ninety-third morning, forty-three days after the settlement, the fever twenty-two weeks and six days, the rail down thirty-three days.

The form went into the window of the press at the middle of the nine bridges at the second hour, and it was six pages, and it cost six pence, and the clerk of the ground floor paid for it out of a wage that has never been paid, and nobody in this city was charged anything for reading it.

It was the first form ever set in that window that nobody had sent for.

Tam Ferrow had the compositor's copy on the bench and he would not let the compositor's boy handle it, and about thirty people came up the hill to look at it in the rain and stood in the street with their backs to the wet.

“*Say the head,*” said Skell.

“*Concerning the naming of a person of a ward in respect of the roads, the keeping of them, and any bar upon them.*”

“*Say the first line and do not stop in the middle of it.*”

“*One. Every ward upon a road of the Shelf may name one person of that ward, who may be asked concerning the roads, the keeping of them, and any bar upon them.*”

“*Say the second one, and I want the room to hear that there are two lines where the office's has one.*”

“*Two. The same ward may name one other person of that ward, who is not the person first named, who may be asked concerning the person first named, and not concerning a road.*”

About thirty people who had come up the hill to look at it said the second half of that line out loud together. A woman at the back said *and not concerning a road* again on her own, and nobody told her not to.

---

It was the seventh time in his life, and five of them have been in this city, and eight days since the last one. It asked him for a thing and supplied nothing in place of it, and about thirty people were standing in a wet street looking at a form he had written, and not one of them could see it.

**CLAIM: A form with one line in it cannot ask a ward for a person, and the second line is the only part of the whole form that is a person.**

**EVIDENCE: The sheet of the office of the third day of this week asks each ward for a person, and does not ask for a calling, and does not check. The first of thirty-one answers was the name of a man who counts halls, sent by a hall and not by a man. No form will tell the difference, because a name is a line of ink and the office enters names and does not check them.**

**BURDEN: A second person is a person of this place who will be asked about the first person by an office that does not check a number and does not require a person to be entered. The second person does not get to be asked first, and does not get to refuse, and is not asked whether they will do it.**

**CHOICE: Set out a second line and put a name on the foot of the page, or set out one line and let thirty-one wards be asked in the office's own way.**

It did not say what a ward would do with a second line. It did not say that a ward would fill it, and it did not say that the second person would be any use to anybody.

He chose nothing out of it, then or afterwards, and he has never chosen anything out of one. He did not tell anybody, and nobody asked him.

Then Nell Fisk read all six pages of it out on the flat outside the press, in the open, in the rain, twice.

She read the first three on the first going and the last three on the second, and about nine people stood on the flags with their hoods down and heard both readings, and nobody interrupted her, and she did not apologise for any of it.

“*And the second foot line is empty,*” she said at the end, the second time round. “*And I have read that one line eleven times this morning in about eleven voices, and every one of the eleven read it as a question, and it is not a question.*”

“*Say what it is,*” said Skell.

“*It is a line for a person to put a name in. And every person standing in this street has just worked out that the office cannot enter him, and that whoever is named in it is the only person the office can ask about him, and that it is going to be somebody in this city.*”

“*And the rest of that,*” said Ilyan.

“*And not one of the eleven of us knows who yet, and not one of us is going to be the one who fills it in, and that is the part I have not got used to. In nine years in this city I have watched about nine hundred people fill in about nine hundred lines without being asked whether they wanted the line.*”

Nobody had asked Ilyan whether he had wanted the line, and about nine people in the street were looking at the bottom of the fifth page of it.

---

“*Say the third line,*” said Fenna Rusk, from under the awning where she had got out of the rain.

“*Three. Where the person first named is a person the office does not recognise, the burden of the recognition falls upon the person named in the second line.*”

“*Say the rest of that clause and go on to the end of it.*”

“*And the office does not check a name, and does not require a person to be entered, and may ask the person named in the second line concerning the person first named until that person declines or is struck out.*”

Nobody in that street said anything for about three seconds.

“*The rest of that.*” said Skell.

“*Four. Nothing on this form is a finding about a road, and the office does not check what is put on it and does not require it to be filled in, and a ward that fills in neither line has not declined and is not entered as having declined.*”

“*Say the foot of it,*” said Fenna Rusk.

“*Two lines at the foot. The first says: the person who drew this form up, of this place or not. There is a name in it.*”

“*Say the name.*”

“*Ilyan Vester,*” said Ferrow. “*And it is in his own hand and it is not mine. I copied the six pages fair over three hours and I did not copy that name.*”

“*Say the rest of that and I want it in a book,*” said Skell.

“*He wrote it at the foot of the fifth page in front of about nine of us in a room off the low ground, at about the fourth hour on Thursday night, which is the night after the office's sheet came down this city. I copied the six pages fair on Friday morning and I did not copy that name, and I have been asked twice this morning whether I copied it and I have said no twice, and I would like the room to have it that I said no twice.*”

---

The second foot line was empty.

It said: *The person who stands beside the name above, of this place.*

“*Say the whole of it out loud,*” said Skell, “*because there is a man standing in this street who wrote that line, and he is not going to fill it in, and everybody in this street can see that he is not going to fill it in, and I would like it said in a room and not in a street.*”

“*It is the second line of the form,*” Ilyan said. “*Every ward that answers is asked for two names. And at the foot of the form there are two names as well, because the form does not have a different shape at the bottom of the page than it has at the top of it.*”

He put his hand flat on the awning frame.

“*And the first of the two is mine. The office cannot enter me and cannot strike me out. And the second is a person of this place who will be asked about me for as long as the form stands.*”

“*And you have not asked a ward for that name,*” said Fenna Rusk.

“*No.*”

“*Then who is it going to be.*”

“*A ward, or a person. And not me. And I cannot make it me, and I would like the whole of this street to understand that I cannot make that decision, and have not tried to, and am not going to.*”

---

The line was written last, and it was the only line of the six pages that was not fair. It was in his own hand and smaller than the hand on the rest of the page, and it was written in a room.

Ilyan had said the price out loud in four inches of mud on the south side of a road two miles out, on the second day of the eighty-third week, with eight people standing in it. The office had it in a fair hand in a register four hundred miles off, with the day and the number of persons and the words said before the entry was made.

He wrote it out again on Thursday night, in a room off the low ground, with about nine people in it. He wrote it into a form, on the fifth of six pages, in the third line, in his own hand, and on Friday morning he had Ferrow copy it fair and set the copy in a window at a penny a page.

“*Say what you have just done,*” said Fenna Rusk.

“*I have written it where it can be read.*”

“*Say the half of that which is the other half.*”

“*I have written it where it can be read. I said the price out loud in a room in the eighty-third week so that a book would have it, and I told this city for six weeks that I said it before I did it, as though that were a thing that had been done for me.*”

He turned round and looked at the window.

“*It was not done for me. It was done so that a book would have it. And I have now put the same sentence in a form with a head on it and six pages and a name at the foot of the fifth.*”

“*Out loud, and all of it.*” said Skell.

“*And it is in a window in the rain a mile and a half from here, and it will be in a press at a penny a page, and from a press to a clerk at the seat, and the clerk at the seat will read the third line before he reads anything else, because the third line is the one that is about a person.*”

“*Why,*” said Fenna Rusk.

“*Because the third line is the only sentence anybody in this city has written down that says what a second person is for. And if it is not written down, then in six weeks somebody will set out a form with two lines in it and a good deal of talk about persons, and nobody will ever know that the second line is a bill.*”

“*Say the rest of that and do not leave it in the middle,*” said Skell.

“*And the second person is going to be a person in this city who does not know yet. She is going to be named by a ward and not by me.*”

“*And you have had a sentence ready since the second hour,*” said Fenna Rusk.

“*And I have not said it, and I know what it is going to cost, and I am going to say it anyway, because if I say it now she will say yes.*”

“*Then say what she will say if you wait,*” said Skell.

“*She will say no. And she will say it in front of nine people, and it will be in a book, and about a hundred and twenty people have told me in six weeks that a book is the only thing that has ever let anybody out of anything.*”

He put his hands in his coat.

“*I do not believe a word of it, and I am going to act as though I do.*”

Then Bess Low, who had been standing at the back of about thirty people with her shawl over her arms, said the thing that made about nine of them go quiet, and she said it to the window and not to him.

“*Say it again that it is a bill,*” she said. “*Because I have been in this city nine years and I have never once been counted, and I have stood in a street and been put in a line, and I am telling you that I know what a bill is when I am standing next to one.*”

Nobody answered her, and about nine people did not look at her, and she went home up the hill at about the fourth hour with her shawl over her arms, and there was a form in that window with six pages in it and not one of them was about her.

The form stood in the window of the press with the second foot line empty until the evening of the next day, and about nine people had read that line out loud in a street and nobody had filled it in.

The fever was twenty-two weeks and six days old, and it did not stop for a form, and the bar of four hurdles, nine links and a plate was still up two miles out on the north side of a road, and a woman who could not say no was in a box on that side of it at the hour a cart came in, counting crossings on a slate that no office in four hundred miles has a book for.

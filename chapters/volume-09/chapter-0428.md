# Chapter 428: A Form With Two Questions In One Line, And A Boy Who Will Not Fill In The Part That Would Cost Him Everything

Third day of the eighty-third week. Shelf day three hundred and three, his hundred and seventy-eighth morning, twenty-eight days after the settlement, the fever twenty weeks and five days old, the rail down eighteen days.

A form came down the hill in the tray at the second hour. It was not waxed. Ferrow read it out on his feet in the long room at the foot of the hill, in about thirty people's hearing, and it was the seventh thing he had read out in thirteen days, and he had said on the first day of the week that he was not going to apologise for any of them.

“*Enter a person under age, named in a return as having carried a statement concerning a road from four doorways of the low row into a building at the middle of the nine bridges on the seventh day of the eightieth week, at the ordinary hour and in the ordinary way.*”

“*Go over the second line,*” said Skell.

“*Whether the person so named reported the road unsafe, and if not, the name of the person who did.*”

The room did not move. There was a plate on the wall with a line of names on it and a second line, and the boy of sixteen was in the doorway at the back with his hands behind him.

“*That is the whole of it,*” said Ferrow. “*It is one line. There is a line for yes and a line for no and a line for a name, and the line for a name has a head on it that says name of the person who did, and there is nothing else on the paper, and it is a decent form, and it can be read standing up in the press room for a penny.*”

“*Say what a person under age does with it,*” said Ilyan. “*There is a line at the top of that form for the name of the person who signs, and I have watched a man of the seat come four hundred miles to be told by a boy on a step that he could not sign it.*”

He had said that in this room on the second day of the week, and nobody had asked him whether anybody had thought about it since, and he had been waiting three days to find out.

The boy came in. He did not ask anybody to read it to him, and about nine people at that end of the room noticed that, and none of them said anything about it.

“*Say the line again,*” he said.

Ferrow read the second line again in the same voice, and about thirty people heard the second reading, and Ilyan did not remark on the asking.

“*It is one line and it is asking two things,*” said the boy. “*Did you carry a statement about a road. And did you report the road unsafe. The answer to the first is yes and the answer to the second is no, and there is one line on that form and the line takes one.*”

He put one hand flat on the long table. He was sixteen and his hands were not clean and he did not apologise for them.

“*I am not going to choose between them in a line, and I would like somebody in this room to say what happens to a person who answers half of a question.*”

“*Nothing happens to him,*” said Ferrow, because he had the form in his hand and had been asked for second halves all week. “*That is the honest answer. A line takes one answer, and a form with a line in it does not have a place for half an answer, and the office does not require a half, and I am not going to be the man who works out which half was meant and writes that half. I write down things that are in front of me. I have been on that floor three years and that is the whole of what I do, and I have not got better at it in a fortnight.*”

“*Then say what you are going to write,*” said Ilyan.

“*That a person under age was offered the form and read the second line and said the second line is two questions and he would not choose between them, and that he did not report the road unsafe, and that he carried a statement about a road on the seventh day of the eightieth week from four doorways in the ordinary way, which is in the return already and which I have not entered twice.*”

“*And the line for the name of the person who did,*” said the boy. He had not looked at the form. “*I am not going to put a name in it.*”

“*Then the form is not filled in,*” said Ilyan, “*and a form that is not filled in goes up the hill with a man of thirty-eight, and the office has a line and nothing to fill it off. And I have watched that happen once this month, in a register with a gap in it, and a man in a room told me the clerk there would either leave it empty or the office would appoint somebody itself.*”

“*And I have not met that clerk,*” said Ferrow, “*and I copy fair for a man who is not here.*”

“*Say the rest of it,*” said Ilyan, “*going down the row and not in a room, because the rest of it is the part that matters, and what matters is what happens to the person whose name is not in a line.*”

The boy stood at the end of the long table with his hands behind him and did not say it for a while, and about thirty people who had come up that hill waited for a boy of sixteen, which is a thing that happens in this city and has never once been written down.

“*Nothing happens to him either,*” he said. “*And that is the part I have been trying to get at since the second hour.*”

“*Say it,*” said Ilyan.

“*The man of this ward who heard one of the four of what I said, and wrote it down, and carried a paper about it into a building at the middle of the nine bridges in the ordinary way at the ordinary hour. And a clerk who filled a line off that paper.*” The boy looked at the floor between his own boots. “*Neither of them is in that form. Neither of them is named on it and neither of them has been asked anything about it.*”

He came up further into the room.

“*And I am the only person in this city who could put either of them in it, and no form anywhere asks a person of sixteen whether he may.*”

Ilyan felt it arrive. It arrived the way a bad step arrives, one second after you have put your weight on it.

“*And I have been asked one question about myself since the first day of this month,*” the boy said, “*and that was fifteen days ago, and a man came down this row with a sheet and asked me whether I wished to be struck out of a register, and I said no, and he wrote it down in a book. That was the first one.*”

“*And this is the second,*” said Ilyan.

“*This is the second, and it is a different one, and it is worse, because the first one was about whether I was in a thing and this one is about whether I put somebody else in a thing.*” He turned round and looked at the man on the window seat, straight at him, for about as long as a look lasts. “*I did not know on the first day of this month that I could do that.*”

Nobody in the room was looking at Ilyan. He had not moved and he was not going to move now, because moving would have been the answer.

“*So I am going to say why I am not going to name him, and then I am going to leave this room, and I am not going to say it in a row either, because a row is how a thing goes into four mouths and a room is how a thing is heard once.*”

“*Say it,*” said Ilyan, and heard himself ask, and knew what he had done.

“*If I say his name then the whole of what I have been in for a fortnight was because of who I know, and not because of what I said.*”

The room was very quiet. The fire was out.

“*What I said was that a rail was up on a road. I said it about a road four times and I never said a word about a person. I have said it since to a man in a doorway, and to a woman of thirty with her head on her knee, and to about four hundred people who came and stood where I was standing, and every one of them came because of what I said, and not because of who carried it.*”

He put both hands on the edge of the long table.

“*And I am not going to be the one standing in a doorway who says a person's name about a road.*”

Ilyan had a sentence in his mouth. It was a good sentence. It would have cost the boy nothing, because the boy had already decided, and it would have cleared him out of the room in a minute, and he would have said it out of habit — a man fixing a record so that a form could be completed — and for about a year afterwards he would have believed he had done the boy a kindness.

He did not say it. He sat on the window seat with his leg out in front of him and let the silence run, and the boy looked at him the whole time, and neither of them said one word in front of thirty people.

“*What are you writing,*” said Ilyan.

Ferrow had the pencil out and had not looked down at it.

“*That a person under age was offered the form, read the second line, and declined to name, and that the form has no line for a person who will not name, and that the person so named is not entered.*”

“*Put down that I was asked and I did not,*” said the boy. “*If you have a line for a person who was asked and did not, put me in it. If you do not, put down nothing. I do not mind which, and I am not going to be thanked for either, because there is nobody in this room who can do that and I have not asked.*”

He turned to Ilyan before he went.

“*My name is Sef,*” he said, “*and I have not said it in a room in four months, and I am not saying it so anybody can write it down.*”

Then he went out of the room and did nine doors, because that is what he does, and he did not say anything at one of them.

At about the fourth hour, Bess Low read out a paper that had come into the tray with the form. It was not waxed, and it had an address at the top of it in a hand nobody in this city knows.

“*It is four paragraphs and it is tired, and I am going to say so before I read it and not after,*” she said. “*It is from a person who has been doing this work longer than the man with the bad leg and who is not in this city. It is not a sheet of the office and it has no seal on it, and it is in the tray because somebody put it in the tray, and nothing that has been printed says anything about a tray.*”

“*Read it,*” said Skell.

“*It is correct about the work.*” She had it in both hands and she did not need it. “*It says that a count of doors and a count of people are two different things. It says that a person who cannot say no is not a witness. It says that a witness who is in a record is a person the office can ask. It says that a bar is a rule with a room missing out of it. Four things, in about six lines, and every one of them is right, and I have heard all four of them said out loud in this city in the last fortnight by four different people.*”

“*Who?*” said Ilyan.

“*One of them was a boy of sixteen and one of them was a woman of forty-four who cannot see a slate.*”

The room took that without a sound.

“*And it is wrong about the plan,*” she said. “*It says the thing to do is to ask the office to do it properly, once, in the right terms. It says the office holds the roads and is the only hand that can take a bar down, and that a thing a government can be asked to do is a thing a hundred other cities can be asked to do the same way.*”

“*That is a good plan,*” said Skell.

“*It is a good plan and it is wrong, and it is wrong because a count got put in a column on a form this month in this city and went up a hill in a coat on the afternoon of the second day of this week, and the woman who kept it cannot tell anybody whether it is right any more. That is what happens to a count the moment the office is asked to hold it.*”

About nine people who had stayed in that room had now heard a sheet read out that said four true things and one wrong one, and not one of them said a kind word to anybody, and Ferrow did not copy it into a book because it was not in front of him.

Skell put it in his coat and took it home and put it on a table.

Nobody thanked the woman four hundred miles off who had written it. Her own family's claim of six years is unfiled and nobody in this city has asked her about it, and nobody in this city is going to, and the fever was twenty weeks and five days old, and it did not stop for a form with two questions in one line, and nobody in that room was thanked.

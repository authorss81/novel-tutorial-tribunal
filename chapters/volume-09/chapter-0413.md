# Chapter 413: A Form With One Line In It For The Name Of The Person Who Signs, And A Boy Of Sixteen Who Is Tired Of Being Asked

The second day of the eighty-first week was his hundred and sixty-third morning and the thirteenth day after the settlement, and the fever was eighteen weeks and four days old, and the rail had been down three days, and the man of thirty-eight went down the low row at the third hour with a form in his coat and did not have to knock on any doors, because the boy of sixteen was at a door on that row on his own account at the third hour and had been at a door at that hour since the eighth morning of a fever, and there is no form in this city that says a person may be at a door instead of in it.

The boy saw him coming up the row and did not go in, and the man of fifty-two was not there and the man with the bad ear was not there, and there were a woman of thirty with a head on her knee at a gate and a man of twenty-three who was a person of the ninth row and a man of sixty-eight who was her father, and none of them had been sent for and none of them were there for this.

“*I am going to say the whole of it standing up,*” said Kerwin Dace, “*because I read a schedule in a room yesterday and I am not doing that again in a row, and because you are sixteen and you will not remember it and you are the only person on this row nobody can send for.*”

“*Say it.*”

“*And under the note at the foot of the sheet. A person entered in a book of the office who does not wish to remain entered may apply to be struck out. The form is at the seat and is a single sheet. It asks one question and the question is whether the person wishes to be struck out, and the answer to that question is a signature at the foot, and there is a line at the top of it for the name of the person who signs. The striking out is done by the office and not by the person.*”

“*Say that again for me,*” said the boy of sixteen.

“*It is a thing I read once this week,*” said Kerwin Dace.

“*It is the first thing anybody has asked me since the eighth morning of the fever and I have had nine of them in four doorways and none of them had a question in it,*” said the boy. “*Read it again.*”

The man of thirty-eight read it again, and he read it in the same voice, and nobody in that row remarked on the boy asking, and the man of thirty-eight did not remark on it either, and he answered a question that had been asked twice.

“*You are named,*” he said, “*in a return that came out of this city on the last day of last week with three sheets in a coat. You are named as the person who carried a statement about a road from four doorways in this row into a building at the middle of the nine bridges on the seventh day of last week, in the ordinary way, at the ordinary hour. You are under age. The form is for a person of the ward, and it is not for you, because it is not yours to sign when you are the one being struck out, and a clerk at the seat told me so in a room there and I have come four hundred miles to be told no by a boy on a step.*”

“*Then I could not sign it anyway.*”

“*You could not sign it anyway,*” said Kerwin Dace. “*That is what I have come down this row to tell you, and there are nine hundred doors on it and about forty of them have somebody under twenty in them, and I have been told that the form is the office's way of not having a person under twenty in a book, and I have not believed that and I have not disbelieved it, and I am going to ask the same question at every one of these doors and you are the first.*”

“*Ask it.*”

The woman of thirty with a head on her knee said, “*You are not going to ask it at ours,*” and the man of thirty-eight said, “*I am not going to ask it at yours, and I will tell you why at the end of this row and not now, and it is not because you have a father at this door.*”

“*You are going to,*” said the boy of sixteen.

“*I am going to,*” said Kerwin Dace. “*That is the last of it and I would rather say it than have it said behind me in a row. I have four days and I have two rows on it that I have not read to anybody and I have nine hundred doors, and a person who says no is a person I write down, and a person who says yes is a person I write down, and either way it is the same pencil and the same book and I go on to the next door. That is what I have come down here for and there is no other thing in it.*”

---

Nobody said anything, and then the man with the bad ear came up the row, and he had come a mile and a half and had not been sent for, and he asked the question that he asks, and he said that he had asked it in four rooms in this city and had not been let into two of them.

“*Say what he is refusing, in words that are not the words of that form.*”

“*He is not refusing a form,*” said Kerwin Dace. “*He is refusing to find out that he is in it. Those are not the same and I have been in this city two days and I can tell the difference and I would like it noticed that I can tell the difference.*”

“*Then say what he is refusing.*”

The boy of sixteen stood on the step with his hands behind him and he did not say it for a while.

“*There are four of what I said and one of me,*” he said. “*I said it at four doors on the fifth day of last week because a man asked me in a row not to write it on a board, and I have not written it on a board, and I said it four times because that is what I do and nobody stopped me. And a man of this ward heard one of the four and carried a paper about it into a building. And the office has that one. And if I sign that paper, then the office has struck out the one of me, and the four of what I said are still in this ward in four mouths, and it can ask the four of them about the one of me, and I will be the only one of the five who cannot say anything about the other four, and that is what I would be.*”

“*And the other thing,*” said the boy, “*and I am saying it because a man has asked me for the second half and he has asked me for it in a row and not in a room, which is the first time anybody has asked me for the second half in a row, and I would like it noticed that I have noticed.*”

“*Say it.*”

“*I have been in this ward since I was born and nobody has ever asked me a question about myself,*” said the boy of sixteen. “*I have been asked what the hall is and what the order is and whether the water is on, and I have been asked to carry a mark on a slate, and eleven people at a standpipe asked me where the hole in the road was, and a man with a bad leg asked me to say a thing in four doorways and then told me why I should not say it in nine people, and he was right. And in ten days I have got a man's name into a book and a clerk's name into a book and a woman's name into a book four hundred miles off, and none of it was because anybody thought I knew anything. It was because I was the one standing in the doorway.*”

He looked at the man of thirty-eight.

“*And now a man comes down this row with a sheet that has a line on it for the name of the person who signs, and the whole of the sheet is asking whether a person wants to be in a thing, and that is the first question anybody has put to me that is about me, and you are asking it on a step, and if I say yes then the only reason I have ever been in anything is a man from four hundred miles off wanting me out of it, and if I say no then the only reason I have ever been in anything is me.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*So put down that I was asked and I said no,*” said the boy of sixteen, “*and if you have a line for a person who was asked and said no then put me in it, and if you do not then put down nothing, and I do not mind which, and I am not going to be thanked for either because there is nobody here who can do that and I have not asked.*”

---

Kerwin Dace wrote in the book with the pencil, and he did not look down at it, and the boy of sixteen watched him do it and did not ask what he wrote, and the man with the bad ear watched him do it and did not ask either.

“*What are you writing.*”

“*That a person under age was named in a return and was offered the form and declined it,*” said Kerwin Dace. “*That is a sentence with a name in it and a name in a sentence is a name in a book, and I have not got a way of writing a thing I have done that does not do that, and I was told so in a room at the seat by a man who has been a clerk for nine years and who is on a cart at this moment.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*Then say the rest of it and say it going down the row and not in a gate,*” said the man with the bad ear, “*because the rest of it is the part that matters, which is that you are going to ask the same question at nine hundred doors and about forty of them have somebody under twenty in them, and you are going to get the same answer in most of them because a person of sixteen who has never been asked anything does not want to be asked to leave the only thing he has ever been in.*”

“*That is the whole of what I have come down here for,*” said Kerwin Dace, “*and I did not know it was when I started, and I have been doing this since the day I came down that hill and I have got two answers and neither of them is a person who is glad I asked.*”

“*Say what the other one was.*”

“*The other one was a carter of nineteen with an arm in a splint who has said no eleven times and I asked him a twelfth thing and he said a word at me that I am not going to repeat in a row,*” said Kerwin Dace, “*and that is in the book too.*”

He went down the row. The boy of sixteen said it in three doorways before the light went off the flat, and the man of thirty-eight went with him and did not tell him to stop, and the woman of thirty with a head on her knee said it in one doorway herself, and the man of twenty-three said it in none.

Nobody thanked the boy of sixteen. He went home at the change and he had not been asked a question about himself by anybody, and a man with a bad leg stood in a row for a while afterwards and did not go and find him, and the rail was in a yard at the low end, and the fever was eighteen weeks and four days old, and it did not stop for a form with one line in it, and nobody on that row was thanked.

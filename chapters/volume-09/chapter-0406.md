# Chapter 406: What A Seal Is For, And A Name At The Foot Of A Sheet That Does Not Answer

The second day of the eightieth week was his hundred and fifty-sixth morning and the sixth day after the settlement, and the fever was seventeen weeks and four days old, and the rail had been up nine days, and the sheet with the seal on it was on the long table in the public room at the foot of the hill, squared to the edge, because a clerk squares a thing four hundred times a day and had squared that one more than the others.

There were about thirty people in the room. There is a room in this city that fills when a boy of sixteen says something in a doorway, and it was that room, and there is no form in this city that says a person may not come to it.

“*Ask me about the die,*” said the man at the wheel, from the end of the long table, where he had come up a mile and a half with a plate under his arm, because he could not leave a plate.

---

“*What is a seal for.*”

It was the rider who asked it, and it was the last thing he asked in this city, and he said afterwards that he had thought about it on the road and had not expected to be asked and had not expected to answer.

“*It is the reason it is the same sheet,*” said Bern Raye. “*That is the whole of it and I have never said it out loud and I have been on this floor nine years and I fill lines and I do not make them up, and you are not asking me a line, you are asking me a thing, and there is no line for a thing, so I am going to answer it and I am going to be wrong in whatever way I am wrong.*”

“*Say what you mean by that.*”

“*This sheet in this room and the same sheet in a room four hundred miles off are one paper. A seal is the reason they are one paper. If there were no seal on it, this would be a piece of paper that a man on a cart carried here, and a piece of paper that a man on a cart carried here is worth exactly what the man on the cart says it is worth, which is nothing, because he is paid by whoever sent him and he is carrying it for them.*”

He turned round and looked at the man on the cart.

“*Say that part.*”

“*I have carried thirty-one of them in nineteen years and nobody has ever asked me what was in one,*” said the rider. “*Not once. Not in this city and not in the seat and not in a stable in between. And I put my hand on every one of them and I could not have told anybody what was in any of them, and if a man at a rail had put a knife in the fold I should have been able to tell him it was paper, and that is all I should have been able to tell him.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*And that is what the seal does to you,*” said Bern Raye. “*It takes it away from the man and gives it to the paper. And I say that as a man who has spent nine years filling lines off a paper in front of me and being glad of it, and I am not going to pretend I would not rather have the paper and not the man.*”

---

“*Say the other half of it,*” said the man at the wheel. “*You have all been in this room and watched a clerk say half a thing, and you have all been at the end of a table, and I am the man who gives the figures and I am going to give the figure on a die because nobody has ever asked me for it and it is mine.*”

“*Go on.*”

“*A seal is cut. A die for a seal is cut the same as any other line, and the fee for a line in this city is four shillings the hundred, and I have been cutting for thirty-one years. I have cut four dies for seals in my life and I have been paid for two of them, and I was paid four shillings the hundred for both, because that is the fee, and I did not know the first one was for a seal and I found out afterward and I did not ask and I have never asked.*”

“*Four shillings the hundred is about half a penny a die.*”

“*Four shillings the hundred, and a hundred dies is four shillings, and I have cut two and been paid for two, and the other two are the property of an office in a room four hundred miles off and I have not seen them and I have not been asked to.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*And I want to say the last of it and then I am going back to the wheel with my plate,*” he said. “*A die is the only line I have ever cut that does not carry a name. A line on a plate is a person. A line on a plate is a person of a ward being in a ward, and that is what the plate is for, and I have cut four thousand of them. A die is not a person. A die is the thing that puts the same mark on nine hundred papers, and it says the same thing every time, and I could not tell you what it says because I have never read one, and I have been paid four shillings the hundred for cutting a thing I have never once had in my hands to read and I have never asked what it is for and I am not going to start now and I am not going to be thanked.*”

---

And then the clerk of thirty read out the last line on the sheet, which was the one under the seal, and which he read because he reads everything on a sheet out loud and in order and he had said he would.

He read the three lines of the body and the four lines at the end and then he turned the sheet over and there was a line under the wax, at the foot, printed, and the wax was over the middle of it and the two ends of it were clear.

“*This is the office the acting is done in,*” he said, in the flat voice. “*And the acting is done in the name of this, and this is at the foot of the sheet, and the sheet is a good sheet and the seal is on it and the whole of it is one paper in two rooms four hundred miles apart. And the name is not written on the sheet. It is cut into the die. That is what a seal is.*”

Nobody said anything. He read it a second time because a man in the room had asked him to read a thing twice about a month ago and it has not been forgotten by either of them, and then he squared the sheet to the edge of the table a fourth time and put his hand flat on it.

There is a name at the foot of it and the name is a man's, and it is a name at the foot of a document, and it is cut into a die, and the man who cut that die is at the end of this table with a plate under his arm and has never read it.

“*Say it,*” said the man of fifty-two, from the end of the table, “*and say it as a name and not as a word, because I have counted halls and doors for twenty years and I have never once been able to tell a man in a room what a word means.*”

“*Iven Marrow,*” said the clerk of thirty, in the flat voice, reading it off the foot of the sheet.

Nobody in that room had heard it before, because nobody in that room had been to the seat, and it is at the foot of a piece of paper in a press on the ground floor of a building at the middle of nine bridges, and the name did not answer and it was not asked anything, and not one person in that room remarked on it, and the only man who asked anything about it asked for it as a name and for no other reason.

“*Say it again for the people at the back,*” said the boy of sixteen, from the doorway, and the clerk of thirty read it a second time, which is a thing a clerk of thirty does four hundred times a day and has now done twice in a week for a name.

“*And the rest of the foot of the sheet,*” said the man of fifty-two, “*because a name at the foot of a thing is never the whole of the thing and I have watched people not say the rest of it for forty years.*”

“*The rest of it is: Regent of the Shelf, and the acting is done in his name,*” said the clerk of thirty, “*and that is the whole of the foot of the sheet, and I have read it out three times now and I am not going to read it out again.*”

---

And the rider was asked about it in the doorway at the last of that afternoon by a boy of sixteen who says things in doorways, and the boy is not the only person in this city who has ever asked that question, and the boy said it four times in four doorways, and the rider answered it once, standing, in a doorway, and would not come into the room.

“*Is he a good man.*”

“*I have been to the seat and back thirty-one times in nineteen years,*” said the rider. “*I have seen him twice. Once he gave a cart-driver a day's wages out of his own hand in a yard where there was a man watching him do it, and I have never once seen a road come down anywhere he has been, and both of those are true, and I have never seen them in the same place, and I have thought about it on every cart I have driven since and I have not got anywhere with it, and I am not going to pretend I have. That is the whole of what a man on a cart knows about a regent and I would say it in a room as well.*”

Nobody stopped him and nobody thanked him and the clerk of thirty did not enter it, and there is no form for what a carrier of sealed paper knows about the man whose name is cut in the die, and there is not going to be one.

---

And at about the fifth hour, with the sheet lying flat on the long table in front of him and thirty people in a room, four lines came into his sight and hung there clean and useless, and it was the fourth time in his life and it was forty-six days since the last one, and he read it twice and did not choose anything out of it and he has never chosen anything out of one of them and is not going to start.

**CLAIM: A seal makes one paper out of two rooms, and a rail does not have one.**
**EVIDENCE: The line at the head of the sheet is filled and the line at the foot is filled. The rail is up on the seventh morning of last week and the line at the head of the sheet nailed to it is not filled and has not been filled since.**
**BURDEN: Somebody must be the person the office is entitled to ask about the road. That is a person, and a person who is asked about a road is a person who cannot afterwards be asked about anything else.**
**CHOICE: Put the burden on the office that sent the paper, or name a person to carry it.**

It did not say how much a name at the head of a line costs the person whose it is, and it did not say what a person of a place would be asked to do if they were named, and it did not say how many people are behind that rail, and it did not say that the office could name anybody it liked and would not need a person of a place at all.

It hung in his sight and asked him for a thing and supplied nothing in place of it, and it had been asked of a man with a bad leg who was on no list of anybody in a city of ninety thousand people, and there were about thirty people in that room and not one of them could see it.

He did not say anything about it to anybody, then or afterwards, and nobody asked him, and the woman of twenty-eight said in a doorway at the last of the light that a clerk had put his own name on a line, which was true, and the four bells went, and the rail was up on the ninth day, and the fever was seventeen weeks and four days old, and it did not stop for a seal, and nobody in that room was thanked.

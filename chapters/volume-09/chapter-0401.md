# Chapter 401: A Rail Across The Low Road, And A Line At The Top Of A Sheet That Nobody Filled In

The fourth day of the seventy-ninth week was his hundred and fifty-first morning and the first day after the settlement that anything in this city had been shut, and the fever was sixteen weeks and six days old, and there was a rail across the Low Road at the weir head, and it had been up four days, and nobody in this city had yet been asked to go and stand at it.

He came on foot and he was still on foot. He had walked down the row at the second hour because a boy of sixteen had said it in a doorway at the change the night before, and the boy of sixteen says things in doorways and nobody stops him, and what the boy had said was that the bar was still up and that a carter had gone round the long way and come back with a load of nothing, meaning with no load at all, having gone eight miles for it.

The Low Road leaves this city at the low end of Tallowgate, runs along the head of the ninth weir for about four hundred yards, and then goes east across the flat toward the next town, which is two days on a cart and which nobody at the low end talks about in the morning. It is not a wide road. It is two cart widths and a ledge on the north side that is a ledge the way a shelf is a shelf.

---

The rail was new. That was the first thing he understood about it and it took him a minute, because a thing in this city that is new usually is a new kind of paper.

It was a rail of a length that had come off something else, whitewashed at some point in a year he did not know about, and it stood in two sockets cut in the hard ground at each side of the road, and the sockets were cut recently and the dust from them was still lying in a ring round each of them, and the rail had been set in them and packed and had not been painted. There was a chain across the north side at knee height and no chain across the south side, and a woman with a barrow of water had come up the south side that morning, which meant the south side was open, and meant it was open to a barrow and not to a cart, and he stood there long enough to work out which of those two things was true and could not.

There was a sheet nailed to the rail. It was a single sheet, printed at the head, and it had been rained on twice and the water had got into the lower corner and taken a word with it.

He asked for it to be read to him.

Nobody read it to him. The woman with the barrow would not, and a man going east with a hand-cart would not, and two people coming the other way would not, and none of them would not in the way people will not in a city where a thing is on a wall and being asked to read it is somebody's employment.

Nobody would not in the way a person will not when they have been asked by a stranger with a leg to do the thing they have not been asked to do in four hundred years.

---

So he went up the hill to the black building at the middle of the nine bridges, and he was on the ground floor at the third hour with his hand flat on the sill of the window, and the clerk of thirty was in the ordinary place with the tray, and the clerk of thirty had heard about the rail before he came in and had a thing ready, and the thing he had ready was a book.

“*You have come about the road,*” said the clerk of thirty. “*I have got the day out, because I thought a man with a leg who has come about a road would want to know how long it has been a road, and that is a figure I can give you and it is the only figure in this city today.*”

“*Say it with the working.*”

“*The settlement is the fourth line of a return and a heading under it, and the return came down the hill in a tray in the middle of last week and was read out in this room and printed in the press in this building, and the second return went up the hill with it in one tray and nobody sent for a runner, and the clerk of forty wrote it fair, and the second return is a magistrate's own objection against a settlement she is bound by, and it is in that press now, and the two of them have been in one building since the day before the fever went down.*”

“*Then what are you counting from.*”

“*From yesterday,*” said the clerk of thirty, “*and I am going to say the working and then I am going to say the part of it I do not like. A return is dated the day it is read into a room, and this one was read into a room in the middle of last week, and the day it was printed in a press is not in this building anywhere, because a press does not date itself and a clerk does not enter a day against a book that was not asked about. And the second return was copied fair and went up and came down and went into the press, and the day it went into the press is not in this building either.*”

He put his finger on the book and turned it round.

“*So there are two days a man could ask me for and I have got one sheet between them and no form that says which. I am counting from yesterday, because yesterday the settlement and the objection to it were both in a press in this building at the same time, and a thing is settled on the day it stops being a thing that is going to happen. And yesterday was the third day of this week, and today is the fourth day of this week, and the first day after the third is the first, and the third is nothing, and if a man asks me what day it is since the settlement I say it is the first day, because the settlement did not happen today.*”

“*And if he asks you which day it was.*”

“*Then I say the third day of this week,*” said the clerk of thirty, “*and if he asks me again in a month I will say the second, because one of the two is going to end up in a return somewhere and I do not know which, and I have been on this floor nine years and I have never had two right answers to one question and I am not going to have it now. That is not a thing I have ever had to say to anybody and I have said it out loud because I am a man who says things out loud and then cannot get them back.*”

He put his hand flat on the book.

“*Say the other one, since you will want it and I would rather you had it from the paper.*”

“*The bar went up on the morning of the seventh day of last week,*” said the clerk of thirty. “*That is four days. I have it because a carter's boy came into this building on the seventh day and asked whether a rail had to be reported, and I said that I did not know, and he said that was the first honest answer he had had in a week, and I did not put anything about it in a book, and I am telling you the day out loud and there is no form for it and there is not going to be one.*”

---

The sheet was read out in the public room at the foot of the hill at the fourth hour, standing up, in the flat voice, because that is what the clerk of thirty is for, and the room was full, and the order was on the wall behind them with the bill under it, crooked, on candle paper, with a blank line in the middle of it that was the correct figure.

He read the head and then he read the four lines and then he stopped and looked at the sheet, and everybody in that room understood what was happening, and nobody said it.

“*There is a line at the top of it,*” he said, “*and the line is for the name of the person making the report, and the line is not filled in.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*Say the line out,*” said the man with the bad ear, from the back, because he is the man in this ward who asks for a thing to be read twice.

“*The line says: name of the person of the place reporting the road unsafe.*”

“*And it is not filled in.*”

“*It is not filled in,*” said the clerk of thirty, “*and I want it read out in this room that I have not filled it in and that nobody has sent for me to fill it in, and that I cannot fill it in, because a clerk fills lines off a paper in front of him and there is no paper in front of me with a name on it, and I will not put a name in a line out of a room because eleven people in a yard at a standpipe said a road had gone, because then the rail on that road is a rail that eleven people at a standpipe are responsible for, and one of those eleven is a woman who is at this standpipe every morning of her life and could not get to a hearing about a road if there was one.*”

“*So the bar is not a form.*”

“*The bar is not a form,*” said the clerk of thirty. “*There is a sheet nailed to a rail and the sheet has a line at the top of it for a name and the line is empty, and a rail is not a form, and a bar is not a form, and it is on a road, and it is four days old, and there is no form in this city that says a rail comes down. I have looked. I looked this morning before you came, which I have never done in nine years for a thing nobody asked me about, and I have not been asked to, and I am not going to be thanked for it and I am not going to tell you why I did it.*”

Nobody asked him why.

---

It is true that the near side of the weir road came out of the water.

The water at the low end had gone down a hand's width a day for nine weeks and the low ground was under at the sides twice a day, and on the sixth morning of last week the near side of the weir road came out of the water, and it was not a bank. It was silt with a skin of road on it that had been put there by somebody who was dead, and a carter's boy of nineteen called it a cart went into the hole in it with the near wheel, and he got out, and he broke his arm, and the arm is in a splint at the top of this city and it is mending and he is not a person of a place that anybody has made a record of and nobody is going to.

The rail is correct. That is the thing about the rail, and Ilyan said it out loud in the room at the foot of the hill at the last of the light, and the man of fifty-two from the end of the table said it was the first time anybody had said the other half in a room since the water went down.

“*A bar is a thing a place does to itself,*” he said, “*and if nobody in this city wants that, then the whole of what eight months of arguing has been about is a bar that eleven people at a standpipe put up against a hole in a road because a boy of nineteen broke his arm. Which is right. And I am not going to stand in this room and say it is not right, and I am not going to stand in this room and say I could not get it down either, and I am going to be in this city in a month, so write the second half down.*”

“*There is no form for the second half.*”

“*Then it goes in a doorway,*” said the boy of sixteen, from the door, and nobody stopped him, and the man of fifty-two did not go and look at him, and the clerk of thirty did not have his pen out, and the man of fifty-two said his four figures are a number about halls and not a number about a rule and that has not changed since the last time he said it.

Nobody thanked the man of fifty-two.

---

And then the woman of twenty-eight said the thing that the room was going to be arguing about for nine days, and she said it from the wall with her slate under her arm, and she had not been asked, and nobody in that room was going to be able to say afterwards that she had been asked.

“*A bar is a rule with no room in it.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*Every other thing in this city that is done to you, you can be in a room about. The order, the roll, the nineteen, the bill on that wall, all of it — you stand in this room at the second hour and you are the same size as the thing. And that rail is four days old and it is on four hundred yards of road and there is no room anywhere it can be in, because the room it would be in is this one, and this one is on the wrong side of it, and the people who are on the other side of it are not going to be in this room tonight, and they are not going to be in this room on any night this month, and the number of them is a number nobody in this city has, including the man who has kept that road for thirty-four years, and I asked him this morning at the bar and he told me so himself and he said it like a man ashamed of it, and he has nothing to be ashamed of and he does not know that either.*”

The clerk of thirty read the sheet out again at the end of that, all four lines and the empty line, and the rail was still up at the last of the light, and the water at the low end had gone down another inch in the day, and the four bells went over Tallowgate, and the fever was sixteen weeks and six days old and it did not stop for a bar, and nobody in that room was thanked.

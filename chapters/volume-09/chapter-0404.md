# Chapter 404: A Hearing In The Road At The Weir Head, Of Which There Is No Return And No Second Hand

The seventh day of the seventy-ninth week was his hundred and fifty-fourth morning and the fourth day after the settlement, and the fever was seventeen weeks and two days old, and the rail had been up seven days, and a hearing was held about it in the open at the second hour on the south side of the Low Road, where a barrow can go and a cart cannot, and nobody sent for a hearing and nobody was sent for one.

He had gone up the hill at the first hour to ask for it, because that is the ordinary way and the form is the oldest one in the building.

The form under the window has three lines. The thing to be read. The room it is to be read in. And a line at the top for the name of the person asking.

“*What is the thing to be read.*”

“*A rail across the Low Road at the weir head, put up on the seventh morning of last week, and a sheet nailed to it with a line at the top of the sheet that is not filled in, and a hole in the near side of the road four hundred yards along where the silt was and it has been filled and packed, and eleven people saw the near side come out of the water, and nobody can be sent for, and there is no form for a road.*”

The clerk of thirty looked at him for a while with the flat look of a man reading a thing he has read before and not liking the shape of it.

“*Then I cannot do it,*” he said.

“*You could read the sheet to me in this room.*”

“*I could read the sheet to you in this room and I have, twice, and it is four days old and the line at the top of it is still not filled in and reading it a third time is what a clerk does with a book. A rail is not a book. And if it is not a book, the person who can answer it has to come down and read it themselves, and the person who can answer a rail is a man in a hut at a crossing, and there is a bench of four upstairs and not one of the four of them is that man, and a bench of four is four and not a person, and the man in the hut is a person, and none of those two can be got to the other one by a tray.*”

“*Then is it a hearing at all.*”

“*It is a hearing,*” said the clerk of thirty, “*and it is going to be held, because you have asked and because there is nothing else to do, and it is going to be held in the road at the rail at the second hour, and I am going to be there with the sheet, and I am going to read the sheet out loud to whoever stands at that rail, because that is the only part of a hearing I have a form for. And I want you to hear me say this in this room, and then I am going to go and stand in a road and do it, and there is nobody in this building who can stop me and I would be glad of one and I am not going to get it.*”

“*Say the rest of it.*”

“*There is no second hand,*” said the clerk of thirty. “*A return is read into a room and entered on a return and the return has a line at the top for the person who wrote it and a line for the person who read it, and that is the second hand, and it is four men and it is not a person and it will not be. In a road there is not even that. A road has no bench, no tray, no return, and no room, and the only paper present is the paper that shut it. So anything anybody says at that rail this morning goes into the people who said it and goes nowhere else, and there is nothing at the end of it that anybody in four hundred miles can be shown.*”

“*Then it cannot be produced.*”

“*It cannot be produced,*” said the clerk of thirty, “*and that is not a fault in it. It is what a hearing is when the thing is a road. I have been on that floor nine years and everything I have ever entered could be produced by anybody who wanted it, and I have never once been told that a thing which cannot be produced was better, and I am not going to start now.*”

---

There were nine at the rail.

The man with the bad ear, who had come four hundred yards again and had said in a doorway two mornings ago that a hearing was going to be held at the rail at the second hour, which nobody had announced. The woman of twenty-eight, with her slate and no chalk in her apron. The man of fifty-two, who has counted halls and doors for twenty years and who was not let into two of the four rooms in this city where a board was read out, and who said so, in the road, out loud, on the ground. Two men off the books of the low hall. A woman of sixty with a shawl. A carter's boy of nineteen with his arm in a splint, who was the person the rail was about, and who had not wanted to come and had come.

That is seven. There were two more and one of them was a woman who had been in that row before the water came.

Nobody had been asked to be there and nobody had been sent for.

The clerk of thirty stood on the south side with the sheet in his hand and the wind off the flat coming up the road behind him, and he read it out, all of it, including the line at the top, and he read the empty line last, and he said the words *not filled in* in the same voice he read everything in.

“*That is the whole of the paper,*” he said. “*And I have read it out, and I have read it out in a road and not in a room, and I am the only person in this city who can read a paper out loud to people who have never had to read one for themselves, and I have been that person for nine years on a floor with a window, and this morning I have done it standing up in a cart road with the wind off the flat, and I have not been asked to and I have not been thanked and I am not going to say the rest of it because there is no form for the rest of it and I fill lines and I do not make them up.*”

Nobody asked him why.

---

“*Go on, then,*” said the man with the bad ear. “*That is a hearing and I have been to four of them in this city and none of them were like it, and I want to know what a hearing is when nobody can write it down.*”

The man of fifty-two answered that, and he said it the way he says his four figures, flat and once.

“*A hearing is a room where people who are affected are in the same place as the person who decides, and the whole of the arrangement is that what is said can be checked afterwards by somebody who was not there. And there is nobody who can check this. So it is not a hearing. It is nine people standing at a rail and a man reading a piece of paper, and I have been in rooms in this city that were worse than this, and I have been in one that was better, and the difference between the two is not how honest the people are. The difference is the return.*”

“*Say the other half.*”

“*The other half is that the people it does not reach are not the people who are not in the room this morning,*” said the man of fifty-two. “*They are the people behind the rail. And they are not absent because they are sulking, and they are not absent because they were not told, and I have said this in a room before and about a different room and I am saying it again about a rail. Somebody will go home tonight and tell them there was a hearing and it was true, and that is the whole of what happens, and in a year a person will say there was no hearing and that will also be true, and both of those will be true in a city that has a return for everything else.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*I am not going to put a figure on that,*” he said, “*and my four figures are a number about halls and not a number about a rule, and that has not changed since the last time I said it in a room, and it is not going to change because the room is a road.*”

---

And then the woman of twenty-eight said the thing about the keeper, and she said it as a person stating a fact and not as a person asking a question, and it was the finding of the morning.

“*There is a person in this ward who is the only person anybody can be asked about the roll of this ward, and she is forty-four and she keeps it on four shillings a year and has kept it for eleven years, and she is on the other side of that rail as of this morning, and she went over on her own account, and nobody asked her to and there is no form that says a person may be asked, and I have said so in a room before this morning and I am not going to be thanked for it and I am not going to stop saying it.*”

“*Where is she.*”

“*I do not know,*” said the woman of twenty-eight, “*and neither does anybody in this room, and that is not a thing I have not been round to. She is over that rail and she has been over it since the light this morning and she has not come back, and nobody in this city can send a person to ask her whether she is there, and the clerk of thirty will tell you the same and he is the only person in this city who could tell you it with the flat voice and mean it.*”

“*I can tell you the same,*” said the clerk of thirty.

“*So this morning is a hearing about a road at which the person who keeps the roll of the ward is not present and cannot be, and there is no form for a hearing without her and there is not going to be one, and in eight months somebody is going to ask whether that rail was right and this is the morning that will be asked about, and this morning is nine people and a piece of paper in a road, and I have put the nine down here on this slate because there is nowhere else to put them and I am not going to give the number of that to anybody.*”

She turned the slate round and there was a mark against nine and nothing else on it, and nobody copied it, and the clerk of thirty did not ask to, and the man with the bad ear looked at it and did not say a number out loud, and the carter's boy of nineteen stood with his good hand on the rail and said the only thing he had come for, which was that he was not going to walk the road again, and that nobody should ask him to, and that the arm was mending and it would not have mended if the rail had not been up.

Nobody asked him to walk the road.

The hearing was over when the light came off the flat and made the road white, because that is when a road is a road and not a place people are standing in. Nine people went back up the row. The sheet went back on the rail. Nobody thanked the clerk of thirty and he said that he was not going to be thanked, and he said it before anybody had a chance, and the man with the bad ear went up the four hundred yards with the man who had the bad leg and neither of them said anything, and the four bells went over Tallowgate, and the rail was up, and the fever was seventeen weeks and two days old, and it did not stop for a hearing, and there is no return for any of it and there never will be.

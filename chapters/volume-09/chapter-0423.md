# Chapter 423: The First Dispute This City Has Sent Away Is A Bar, And The Form That Comes Back Wants A Number Of Persons

Fifth day of the eighty-second week. Shelf day two hundred and ninety-eight, his hundred and seventy-third morning, twenty-three days after the settlement. The fever was twenty weeks old that morning. The rail had been down thirteen days, and a return came up the hill to the black building with wax on it.

Tam Ferrow read it out standing up in the public room at the foot of the hill, because he reads things out standing up and because it was the second thing he had ever read out of that press and he had been on the ground floor three years.

“*The office has received a dispute concerning the passage over the Low Road at the ninth marker in this city, given by a person of this place, and the dispute is entered in the register at the seat, and the person to whom it is given is the person appointed in that register, and the person appointed is of the seat and is not of this city.*”

“*Go over that once more,*” said Darrin Skell.

He had come up from the end of the plate yard and had not been sent for. Ferrow read it again in the same voice. He did not put his hand flat on the table the way Kerwin Dace does, and he had said in that room that he is not going to.

“*And the office has read the dispute, and has had it read to it twice, and the office's answer is in four lines.*”

“*Say them.*”

“*One. The bar upon the Low Road at the ninth marker is put up and is to remain up. Two. The persons and the premises on the north side of the crossing have been given notice. Three. Nothing in this return is a finding about the road. Four. A dispute given to the person appointed in the register is a dispute given, and is not a dispute that is lost.*”

The room sat with it. At the back, a boy of eight had a barrow between his knees and a blanket over his shoulders. He was eight, and he was better than he had been in the spring, and he could not be discharged from anywhere, and nobody in that room said his name.

“*Say the second half of that,*” said Ilyan.

Ferrow turned a page over without looking at it. “*There is a second half and I have not been asked for it, and I am going to say it because I have been on this floor three years and I copy them fair and I have never read one out.*”

The room waited. It had learned to.

“*A man stood against that wall on the first day of this month and said a government which reads what you write to it is a government which has not heard you. He said it out loud, and he meant it, and about thirty people heard it.*”

He squared the sheet to the edge of the long table.

“*A man from the seat read a magistrate's objection in this same room on the same day and answered it in four lines, and the answer was correct. That man is four hundred miles up a road on his own two feet at this moment and he is not coming back to this room, and those four lines upstairs are the answer to a magistrate and they are the answer to that sentence too.*”

“*Say the last of that,*” said Ilyan.

“*They are correct. The office has read both of them and has not changed its mind by one line.*”

“*Say the finding,*” said Ilyan.

“*The first dispute that has gone out of this city to a register at the seat since the settlement was read out is four hurdles and a length of chain at the ninth marker.*” Ferrow kept his eyes on the room and not on the sheet. “*Not a person. Not a clerk. Not a woman. Not a boy. A bar, given by a person of this place, and the person who gave it is not named on the sheet because the sheet has no line for it.*”

“*And the person it is entered against?*” said Ilyan.

“*A name cut into a die in a room four hundred miles off. I have never seen it, and I am not going to read it out, and I am not going to be asked.*”

Nobody asked him. There was a bell in the top of the room that had not been rung since the fever, and the water under the ninth weir was four hundred yards away and it did not stop for a bar.

“*And there is a second thing in the tray and it is not waxed,*” said Ferrow. “*And it is a form, and I have put it in the press on the ground floor at a penny a page with a window in that room, and anybody in this city can be shown it for a penny. I have read it out twice this morning in a corridor on my own before I came down. I am twenty-nine and I write them fair and I have never read one out to anybody, and I have stood in this room four minutes and read two, and I am not going to apologise for either of them.*”

“*Read the third thing,*” said Skell.

“*Return of the number of the persons of this place, by ward, and of the doors by street, for the keeping of a place under the fifth row of the schedule of the first day of the eighty-first week. The return is to be made by a person of the place. The office does not provide the number and the office will not check it.*”

“*Again,*” said Ilyan, because the sentence about a number of persons was the one that mattered.

Ferrow read it again, and about thirty people heard a second reading of a sentence about the office not checking a number of people.

“*Here is what I have in front of me,*” said Skell, and he stood up at the end of the long table. “*That form wants two numbers off one sheet, and it wants them from a person of this place, and it is a good form, and it is legible, and it is a penny a page, and there is nothing wrong with it. There is nothing in this city that can fill in half of it.*”

“*Which half,*” said Ilyan.

“*The doors I have.*” He put his hands flat on the table. “*Nine hundred doors in this ward on the day the settlement was read out, and nine hundred this morning, and I counted them myself, and I have four halls to their names and I could give you all five numbers by the change. I have given them out loud in four rooms in a fortnight.*”

“*And the persons?*”

“*I do not have the number of persons in this ward, and neither does the office, and neither does anybody in this room, and the reason we do not have it is that a count of persons in a ward is not a count.*” He said it slowly, the way he said everything he meant. “*It is a division. You take the number of doors and you divide it by something, and the only thing in this city to divide it by is a number of people to a door, and there is no such number.*”

He looked up the row.

“*Because a door with nobody behind it and a door with two people behind it are the same thing on a roll and a different thing in a street, and about nine hundred people have just been counted.*”

He looked up the table, and for a moment his eyes came to Ilyan and stayed there, and Ilyan understood that the man was waiting for him to say a number. He had one. He had a better one than a guess: he had a divisor worked out from three weeks of boards, and he could have put it on the table in ten seconds and it would have been in four books by Friday.

The room would have called it the office's arithmetic. Hessa Marl would have had it read back to her as a person of this place who confirmed it. Nobody in this city would ever have found out that it was a stranger with a pen.

“*And I am not going to give the office a number of persons in this city,*” Skell said, “*because the day that number exists, this city has decided what a person is, and it will not be this room that decided it, and I would like it said in here that I have said that out loud in front of a man from four hundred miles off.*”

“*I am not from four hundred miles off,*” said Ferrow. “*I am twenty-nine and I am from this city and I copy them fair, and I have stood here and read out two things this morning, and I have said that the name at the foot of a sheet with a seal on it is not a person of this place, and I would like the room to have both of those.*”

“*Then I have said it in front of a man of this city who prints the sheets,*” said Skell, “*which is worse, and I am not taking it back, and I am not going to be thanked for it.*”

---

Bess Low got up off the wall at the back, which she does not do.

She was sixty and she wore a shawl over a dress that had been mended at the cuffs twice, and nobody had sent for her, because nothing that has been printed in this county lets a person be sent for.

“*I am going to say a sentence about a bell and a porch,*” she said, “*and I am saying it because a form has come down this hill that wants a number of people.*”

The room gave her the silence it had been giving her since the first day of the month.

“*A bell in a county four hundred miles off of here is not hung. A porch in a valley of sixty people is gone. There is a corridor standing in it with a bell unpaid on it.*” She said it the way she said everything, which was as if she were putting three stones on a table to prove she had been in the room. “*That is three things about three places, and not one of them is a number of persons, and I have been in this city nine years and I have never once been counted.*”

“*And the count?*” said Ilyan.

“*And I have noticed the difference between the two.*” She went back to the wall. “*The count is me, in a hand, on a form, with my name at the top of it. That is a thing I do not have. I am going back to the wall now.*”

She was not thanked, and she had said on the first day of this month that she was not going to be relieved about it either.

“*Then there is nothing anybody in this room can put in that form,*” said Skell, “*and the office has said in writing that it will not provide the number and will not check it, and that is a form with two lines in it that no person in this city can fill in, and it goes up the hill with a man of thirty-eight and no name in it, and I would like somebody to say what happens to it in a room four hundred miles off.*”

Ferrow said it, because he was the only person in the room with a pencil and a book and had been asked twice this week whether he had written a thing down.

“*Nothing happens to it. That is the honest answer and I have had it since the second hour.*” He looked at his own hands. “*A clerk at the seat will have a line to fill and nothing to fill it off. There is a man in this city who has watched that happen once this month, with a register and a gap in it, and he told me in a hut on the sixth day of this week that the clerk would either leave it empty or the office would appoint somebody itself.*”

“*And he thought the clerk was a good man?*” said Ilyan.

“*He said he thought the clerk was a good man. I have never met that clerk. I copy fair for a man who is not here.*”

“*Say that last part again,*” said Ilyan.

“*There is no line on that form for a person who could not fill it in.*”

The room waited, and the boy of eight got up and went out with his barrow between his knees, and nobody said his name, and there is no form in this county that says a person has to be looked at.

“*Then say who can fill it,*” said Skell, across the table. “*You have said the shape of it in front of thirty people, and the answer is a mile and a half from this hill and it has been in it all along.*”

“*I am not going to ask the room,*” said Ferrow, “*because I have been the one who filled a line off a paper in front of me twice this month, and I am not going to be the one who points at a person.*”

“*Then I will,*” said Hessa Marl.

She had come in at the change of the light and was standing at the end of the long table with her slate under her arm, and she had not been asked and had not come for anything, and there were six people left in the room where there had been thirty.

“*There is one woman in this city that anybody can be asked about a roll. She is a mile and a half down the low ground, in a room behind a chandler's shop with a lamp on it.*” Hessa Marl did not lift her hand off the slate. “*She is forty-four, and she has kept it eleven years, and the office pays her four shillings a year and has not paid her one penny of the eleven years.*”

“*And her name?*” said Ilyan, before he could stop himself.

“*No.*” She said it flatly. “*You did not ask it because you do not need it. I have not been asked whether I may say it, and I have said the rest in a room, and I am not going to say her name in here, because it is at the top of a page in a city four hundred miles off and it got there off a paper that one of us carried in the ordinary way, and I am not going to be the one who puts it in a second book on the same day.*”

Ferrow wrote nothing down. About six people who had been in that room all morning knew where the only number in this city was, and Ilyan stood at the end of the long table with a divisor in his mouth that he would never be able to say out loud now, and the fever was twenty weeks old, and it did not stop for a count, and nobody in that room was thanked.

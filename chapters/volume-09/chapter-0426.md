# Chapter 426: The Only Question In Nine Weeks That Was Asked In The Form Of A Question, And The Answer

First day of the eighty-third week. Shelf day three hundred and one, his hundred and seventy-sixth morning, twenty-six days after the settlement, the fever twenty weeks and three days old, the rail down sixteen days.

A mile and a half down the low ground there was a lamp on in the room behind the chandler's shop, and the roll was on the table and not under it, and there were about nine people in the room, and none of them had been sent for.

Hessa Marl was not in it. She was at the end of the low row at a door with her slate under her arm, doing the ninth of nine, and nobody had told her that anything was happening a mile and a half from her step and nobody was going to.

Ilyan had walked out that morning with a bad leg and an hour of light in hand, and he stood at the end of Fenna Rusk's table for a while before he said anything, because he had learned in four rooms this month that a question asked too early is a request.

“*Say the question,*” he said, “*and say it in the form of a question and not in the form of a request. I have asked four rooms in this city for things in ten days, and every one of them was a request, and I would like one that is a question.*”

Ferrow had a book and a pencil and was standing up, because he reads things out standing up. Darrin Skell was at the end of the table, having come up from the end of the plate yard and not been sent for.

“*Can the roll say whether the person who went out of this city on the seventh day of the eighty-second week at the fourth hour is the person who came back into it at the last of the light.*”

Fenna Rusk did not hurry it.

“*No,*” she said. “*And I am going to say why, once, and I have said on the sixth day of this week that I have stopped saying that I will not be thanked, and I am not going to start again.*”

“*Say it,*” said Ilyan.

“*A roll counts doors. A door opened at the second hour and a door shut at the last of the light, and a roll has both of those in it and can say both of them out loud with the hour against them. That is what a roll is, and it is what I have kept for eleven years.*” She put her hand flat on the table. “*And the person is not in it. The person went out of a door and came in at a door, and it is the same door, and the roll can say the door and cannot say the person. That is not a fault in the roll and I have never once said it was.*”

She turned her head slightly, as if she could locate him by the sound.

“*If a man has come a mile and a half to find a fault in it, he has come for the wrong thing, and I would rather he had come for nothing.*”

“*Then say how a person gets into it,*” said Ferrow, “*because I have the form in front of me and I am going to read the head of it out before I am asked twice about whether I have written a thing down.*”

He read it out.

“*Return of the number of the persons of this place, by ward, and of the doors by street, for the keeping of a place under the fifth row. The return is to be made by a person of this place. The office does not provide the number and the office will not check it.*”

He turned the form round on the table, which is a thing he does.

“*And the head of the other one, which is not a form but a return with a column in it, and which came up this hill with the schedule on the first day of the eighty-first week, and which is in the press on the ground floor at a penny a page with a window in the room, where anybody in this city can be shown it standing up.*”

“*Say the head of it,*” said Skell.

“*The head of it reads: persons and things reported to the office in respect of the Low Road, this city, and the bar lately upon it.*”

Nine people in a room behind a chandler's shop sat with that. Skell put his hands flat on the table. Ferrow did not look down at his book.

“*That is what it is,*” said Skell, “*and I have had it since the second hour on the first day of this week and I have been waiting for a room with the form on the table in it.*”

“*Say the whole of it,*” said Ilyan.

“*If that roll goes up that hill in that column, then the head of it stops being a roll and becomes the head of a return, and everything under a head is a thing.*” He looked at Fenna Rusk. “*And there is one woman in this city that a thing can be asked about, and she is sitting at this table, and from today she is not a person in a book. She is a keeper.*”

“*Say the rest of that,*” said Ilyan.

“*A person can be struck out of a book of the office, and there is a form for it at the seat, and the striking out is done by the office and not by the person. And a thing cannot be struck out, because the form begins with a person who does not wish to remain entered, and a thing has no wish, and no form anywhere takes one.*”

Fenna Rusk put her hand flat on the table where the roll was, and did not look at it.

“*And the working on what that costs her,*” said Skell. “*Four thousand and four days. That is nine rounds of four hundred and twenty days, and two hundred and twenty-four days of a tenth, and nine times four hundred and twenty is three thousand seven hundred and eighty, and three thousand seven hundred and eighty and two hundred and twenty-four is four thousand and four.*”

“*Say what that is in years,*” said Ilyan.

“*About eleven years. And a keeper of a roll is paid four shillings a year, so eleven years of it is four pounds and four shillings, and I am going to say the next part, and I am going to say it because there is a man with a pencil in this room and there are about nine of us, and a man of twenty-nine said in a room at the foot of that hill two days ago that he is the one they are going to ask.*”

“*Say it,*” said Fenna Rusk, before Ilyan could.

Skell looked at her, and then at the room, and then at the wall.

“*I have said it,*” he said, “*in a room, and it is in a book four hundred miles off by now, and I am not going to say it twice in the same morning.*”

The room went quiet in a way that was almost funny.

“*Then say the cost of it,*” said Ilyan.

“*Not about a roll. About a person who went out of a road and came back into it, once, on the seventh day of this week, and about every one that goes after her, for as long as there is a road and a form and an office that does not check a number.*” He took his hands off the table. “*And there is no line on that form for a person who says no, and no line for a person who says yes, and no line for her name at the top, because the head of the form is a head of persons and things and not a head of names.*”

The boy of sixteen was at the door of that room, and he was sixteen, and nobody had looked at him.

“*And the part that is mine,*” said Fenna Rusk, “*and nobody has to write it, and I am saying it because the two men in this room have both asked for a second half and one of them is going to have to be the one who pays, and it is not going to be him.*”

She took her hand off the table.

“*A roll cannot be asked whether a person is the same. That is not a fault in a roll. That is what a roll is for, and I have said it in four rooms and on a bench of four and to a magistrate, and nobody has printed it.*”

“*Then how does a person get into one,*” said Ilyan.

“*You stop it being a roll. You put it in a column. And a roll in a column is a register, and a register goes up the hill.*” She was not looking at anybody. “*There is no line on any form in four hundred miles for the keeping of a roll. The office does not pay for a copy of one, does not require a copy of one, and will return an original so conveyed in the ordinary way. And the ordinary way is a man on a cart, paid by whoever sent it, and whoever sends it is a man of twenty-nine who writes them fair.*”

Ferrow had the pencil out and had not looked down at it for a minute and a half.

“*Say whether you have written that down,*” said Ilyan.

“*I have written that a person of this place has said, in a room, in nine people's hearing, that a roll cannot be asked whether a person is the same. And that the office's return has a column with the head of persons and things on it, and that a roll sent up that hill in that column leaves this city without the only thing anybody can be asked about a roll in it, and that a person cannot be struck out of a book, and a thing cannot either, and there is no form for either.*”

“*Say the rest of it.*”

“*I have not written her name, because the head of my book has no line for the name of a person who says a thing in a room like this one.*” He stopped, and then went on, because he had decided to. “*I filled a line off a paper in front of me in the third week of last month that put a name at the top of a page in a city four hundred miles off, and I said the cost of that out loud in a room, and I did it anyway, and I have been on that floor three years and I have not got a better way of writing a thing down.*”

“*Say the working on the four pounds and four shillings,*” Ilyan said, “*and say it for her, because she will not do it.*”

“*No,*” said Fenna Rusk.

The room waited.

“*I am not going to say it, and I am going to say why I am not going to say it, and it is that a keeper of a roll is paid four shillings a year, and there is one of me, and I have not been paid one of the eleven years, and the only number anybody has ever asked me for is what a roll is worth, and there is no such number.*”

She put both hands flat on the wood.

“*The reason there is no such number is that a roll is worth a person and I am the person, and I am not going to stand in my own room with about nine people in it and put a figure on myself.*”

The roll went up the hill that afternoon inside the coat of a man of twenty-nine. Before it went he copied eleven pages of it fair into a second one, in his own hand, and put the second one in the press on the ground floor of a building at the middle of the nine bridges, where it is a penny a page and there is a window in the room.

Then he read the first page of the copy out to the woman who could not see it, standing up, in her own room, with about nine people in it.

“*That is not my hand,*” she said.

“*No,*” he said. “*It is fair, and it is legible, and it is what goes up instead of the page. The page goes up as well. The copy is the one that stays in this city, and I have read all eleven of it, and I will read any of them again in this room or in the room at the foot of the hill in front of anybody who comes.*”

“*Then I cannot tell you whether it is right,*” said Fenna Rusk.

She put the lamp out, which she did before the working was done, and then said the rest of it into the dark.

“*And that is what it cost. I was the only person in this city anybody could be asked whether a roll was right, and I was that person at the second hour this morning, and I am not that person at the fourth. And there is a copy of it in a press in a building at the middle of the nine bridges in a stranger's letters, and I can read it, and I cannot tell you whether it is right.*”

“*You can get somebody else to read it,*” said Ilyan.

“*Can I.*” She struck a light again and the room came back. “*Get somebody else to read it, and then there are two of us, and the office will ask the one it can send for, and it will not be me, and that is how it works every time, and I have watched it work on other people for eleven years.*”

Ilyan had been in this city twenty-six days and he had put four people in books in that time, one of them by carrying a paper up a hill himself, and he sat on a bench in a room behind a chandler's shop and could not think of a single question that was in the form of a question.

“*And the last of it,*” she said, “*and it is short, and it is for the man with the bad leg, because he asked for the working four times this morning and he is the only one in this room who came for a question and got one.*”

“*Say it.*”

“*I have been asked what a roll costs. I have never been asked what a year of a roll costs, and there is one of me, and the answer is four pounds and four shillings, and I have had none of it. And the office will say in a room four hundred miles off that it notes the sum and does not press for it, and it has been saying that since the third row of a schedule came down this hill.*”

“*Say that it is true,*” said Ilyan.

“*It is a true sentence, and I want it noticed that it is true,*” said Fenna Rusk.

The lamp was out, the table was bare, and nobody in that room was thanked.

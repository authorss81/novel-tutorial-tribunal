# Chapter 424: A Name In The Wrong Line, And A Man Doing A Division Out Loud Because Nobody Asked Him To

Sixth day of the eighty-second week. Shelf day two hundred and ninety-nine, his hundred and seventy-fourth morning, twenty-four days after the settlement, the fever twenty weeks and one day old, the rail down fourteen days.

The sheet came down the hill at the second hour with wax on it. Ferrow read the head of it before the body, because that is what is at the head.

“*The office has entered a person of this place as removed from this city, under the fifth row of the schedule of the first day of the eighty-first week. A place is kept. The person so entered is to be conveyed at the charge of the office beyond the Shelf, and the office will pay that person one year's wage at the rate of the second row, in one sum, at the seat, on the giving of a name. A person so conveyed is not to be asked about the city afterwards.*”

Nobody spoke for a while. There was a fire going in the corner that had been lit twice that week and gone out twice.

“*And the line at the foot,*” said Ferrow, “*which is for the name of the person so entered. There is a name in it. I have not read it out and I am not going to read it out, and there is a person standing at the end of this table who does not need me to.*”

“*Say the head of the line above it,*” said Hessa Marl.

“*The head of the line above it reads: person applying.*”

“*And the head of the line below it?*”

“*Person removed.*” He turned the sheet round on the long table, which is a thing he does. “*And the name is in the second one. I copied this sheet fair at the ordinary hour, off a paper that a man of this ward carried into a building at the middle of the nine bridges, in the ordinary way, at the ordinary hour. I did not ask the paper where it came from. I have said the cost of that out loud in this room once already and I am not going to say it a second time, because it is the same cost and you have heard it twice.*”

The room did not turn round. There is no form in this county that puts a person in front of a room that has to be looked at.

Ilyan put his tea down on the sill behind him, and picked it up again, and put it down.

“*Say what is left of it,*” he said.

“*There is nothing else on it,*” she said. “*And I have been in this room four minutes and I have been asked nothing and I have not come for anything.*”

She had the slate under her arm and she did not put it down. Her hands were not steady and she managed that by keeping them busy.

“*I want to say one thing and I want it written down, and I want the man with the pencil to do it, and I am not going to say it twice.*”

“*Say it.*”

“*On the second day of this week I said no to a farthing a visit. I said it in this room. I said it on a step six days before that. On the second day of this week I said I had not applied for anything and was not going to, and I asked for that to be written down, and a man with a pencil wrote it in a book.*” She looked at the long table and not at anybody.

“*That book is four hundred miles off. The sentence is in it and the sentence is mine, and no form takes a sentence off a page, and none of them asks a person whether they agreed to it.*”

“*And the half of that which you have not said,*” said Skell.

Hessa Marl put two fingers on the edge of the table.

“*I have my no in a room four hundred miles off, and I have a name in a room four hundred miles off in a different line, and they are the same distance away and about eleven days apart, and I am the only person in this city who is in both of them, and neither of them is a person asking me a question.*”

“*Say the rest of it,*” said Ilyan.

“*I cannot say no. That is the whole of what I have ever had in this city and it is the whole of what has been taken off me this morning.*” She took her fingers off the table. “*I am not going to be relieved about it, and I am not going to be told at the end of this month that I was right, and I am going to go out and do nine doors, and I am not going to be thanked for any of it.*”

Ferrow wrote it in a book. The head of that book has no line for the name of a person who says a thing at a table.

“*What are you writing,*” said Ilyan.

“*A sentence with a name in it,*” said Ferrow. “*And a name in a sentence is a name in a book. I filled a line off a paper in the third week of last month, and it was a name at the top of a page, and I said the cost of that in this room, and I did it anyway, and I have not got a better way of writing a thing down and I am not going to invent one at a table.*”

The man who carried that paper up the nine bridges was standing at the end of the long table with a cracked steel pen in his hand, and he had carried it, and nobody in the room knew that, and he had known it every day since the third week of last month.

“*Then say what a place is worth,*” said Ilyan, “*because there is a line on that sheet which says a place is kept, and there is nobody in this room who knows what a place is.*”

Skell had been getting up and down all morning and now he stood still.

“*A place that is kept is not a house. A house is a thing. A place that is kept is a door, and it is kept if somebody is behind it at the hour the cart comes in, and the office has printed a rate for it on the first row of the same schedule, and the rate is a farthing a visit, and the payment is for the visit and not for the name.*”

“*Do the working,*” said Ilyan. “*Out loud, and with the working.*”

Nobody had asked him for it. He had not been asked for it, and he was doing it anyway, and he had his hands flat on the long table, and about thirty people who had come up that hill did the division a second time in their own heads, which is what a room does.

“*Nine doors a day. That is Hessa Marl's figure and she gave it in a row yesterday and it is four hundred and forty-one doors in forty-nine days off a slate, and I was not there, and a boy was, and I have been given it and I am not going to pretend to this room that I counted it.*”

“*Go on,*” said Ilyan.

“*Nine doors a day over a year of three hundred and sixty-four days at a farthing a visit is three thousand two hundred and seventy-six farthings. A farthing is a quarter of a penny. That is eight hundred and nineteen pence, which is sixty-eight shillings and threepence.*” He did not hurry it. “*And a keeper of a roll is paid four shillings a year, which is forty-eight pence. Eight hundred and nineteen over forty-eight is seventeen, and a sixteenth over seventeen.*”

The room waited.

“*And that is the end of the working. I am not going to do the other half in here.*” He took his hands off the table. “*Eight hundred and nineteen pence, against the second row, which is two hundred and forty pence a day. That is three days and a little over three of what a person appointed in that register is paid for in a day.*”

“*Say that again,*” said Ilyan.

“*The office has printed a price for a visit, and a price for a day, and a price for a year of being taken out of a city, on one sheet, with a rule between them, and it has not said which of those is a person.*”

“*Which of them is,*” said Bevin Tarr from the wall, and did not apologise for the sound of it.

Skell looked at him for a moment, and then at the room.

“*I have said it and I am not going to say it again, and I am going to say the rest of it once and then sit down. I am fifty-two and I have counted halls and doors for twenty years, and I have not got a hall in any of this.*”

“*Go on,*” said Ilyan.

“*A government that prints a price for a visit and a price for a day and a price for a year of being taken out of a city has decided what a person is. It has not decided it in a room and it has not decided it in a line. It has decided it in a division.*”

“*And the division was done by whom,*” said Ilyan.

“*By me. In this room tonight. Out loud, with the working.*” Skell put his hands flat on the table again. “*Nobody stopped me and nobody asked me not to.*”

He sat down.

“*And I would like it entered in whatever happens next that I did not enjoy it.*”

---

A mile and a half down the low ground there was a lamp on in a room behind a chandler's shop, and a table, and a roll that was on the table and not under it, and Fenna Rusk sitting at the table with a sheet in her hand that she could not see.

Nine people had come down the road on the word of a boy who says things in doorways. Nobody had sent for anybody, and there is no form in the county that would let anybody do it.

Renn Callow was not there. He had said on the fourth day of this week at the end of the low row that he would go when he had not been given anything for a day, and it had not been a day, and he was at a crossing with his lamp out.

Ferrow read the form out twice, standing up, and about nine people heard the second reading.

“*Return of the number of the persons of this place, by ward, and of the doors by street, for the keeping of a place under the fifth row. The return is to be made by a person of this place. The office does not provide the number and the office will not check it.*”

“*Say the fifth row,*” said Ilyan.

She put her hand flat on the table, on the bare part of it, which is a thing she does before she says the last of anything.

“*No,*” said Fenna Rusk.

The nine people did not look at her, and nothing that has been printed says a person has to be looked at.

“*I have not said the reason and I am going to say it once.*”

“*Say it,*” said Ilyan.

“*A number of doors is a roll, and the roll is under this table, and I have kept it eleven years, and the office pays me four shillings a year for it and has not paid me one penny of the eleven years.*” She did not move her hand.

“*A number of persons is not a roll. A number of persons is a division, and a man of fifty-two stood in a room at the foot of that hill tonight and did a division out loud and said himself that it decides what a person is.*”

“*And you?*” said Ilyan.

“*I have kept a roll for eleven years in order that a person would not be a division, and I am not going to do it in a fortnight because a form has come down a hill with a line in it.*”

Ferrow had the pencil out and did not look down at it.

“*And I am going to say the other half before anybody asks me for it, because there is a man of twenty-nine in this room with a pencil and about nine of us, and I have been the only person anybody can be asked about a roll for eleven years, and I have never once been asked in a room with anybody in it.*”

“*Say it,*” said Ilyan.

“*Yes.*” She took her hand off the table. “*I will keep a place. I will be at this table at the hour a cart comes in, and I will be here at that hour every day, and I have not been thanked in nine weeks and I have stopped saying that I will not be.*”

The room waited for the other half, because there is always another half, and she had given them one a month ago and had not given them the other.

“*A kept place is a door, and I have said I will be at this door. I have not said the office may ask me what came through it.*”

“*And a roll?*” said Ferrow.

“*A roll is a count of doors and not a count of the people who went out of them. And if a person is conveyed out of this city on a road and comes back, this table has a line for it, and there is one of me and there is one of this room, and I am not going to be the person who decides which of those two it is.*”

He wrote for a while.

“*Say what you are writing,*” said Ilyan.

“*That a person of this place has agreed to keep a place, and has declined to give a number of persons, and that both of those were said out loud in one room in about nine people's hearing, and there is one line on the form, and I have written both of them on it, and the line is longer than the form.*”

He put the pencil down and squared the form to the edge of the table, and a woman of twenty-eight went out of a room and did nine doors and said nothing at one of them, and the fever was twenty weeks and one day old, and it did not stop for a place, and nobody in that room was thanked.

# Chapter 424: A Name In The Line For A Person Removed And Not In The Line For A Person Applying, And What A House Is Worth When A Man Who Counts Halls Does It Out Loud

The sixth day of the eighty-second week was his hundred and seventy-fourth morning and the twenty-fourth day after the settlement, and the fever was twenty weeks and one day old, and the rail had been down fourteen days, and a sheet came down the hill at the second hour with wax on it, and the clerk of forty read it out on his feet in the long room at the foot of the hill in about thirty people's hearing, and the woman of twenty-eight was at the end of the long table with her slate under her arm and had not been asked and had not come for anything, and the clerk of forty read the head of it before the body, because that is what is at the head.

“*The office has entered a person of this place as removed from this city, under the fifth row of the schedule of the first day of the eighty-first week. A place is kept. The person so entered is to be conveyed at the charge of the office beyond the Shelf, and the office will pay that person one year's wage at the rate of the second row, in one sum, at the seat, on the giving of a name. A person so conveyed is not to be asked about the city afterwards.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*And the line at the foot of the sheet,*” said the clerk of forty, “*which is for the name of the person so entered, and there is a name in it, and I have not read it out and I am not going to read it out, and there is a person standing at the end of this table who does not need me to.*”

“*Say the head of the line above it,*” she said.

About thirty people in a room at the foot of a hill looked at a clerk of twenty-nine, and he turned the sheet round on the long table, which is a thing he does, and he read the printed head of the line above it, standing up, in the flat voice.

“*The head of the line above it reads: person applying.*”

“*And the head of the line below it reads: person removed.*”

“*The head of the line below it reads: person removed,*” said the clerk of forty. “*And the name is in the second one, and I copied this sheet fair at the ordinary hour off a paper that a man of this ward carried into a building at the middle of the nine bridges in the ordinary way, and I did not ask the paper where it came from, and I have said the cost of that out loud in this room once already and I am not going to say it a second time because it is the same cost and you have heard it twice.*”

The woman of twenty-eight did not move, and about thirty people in that room did not turn round, and there was no form in this city that puts a person in a place somebody has to look at.

“*Say what is left of it,*” said the man with the bad ear.

“*There is nothing else on it,*” she said. “*And I have been in this room for four minutes and I have been asked nothing and I have not come for anything, and there is a piece of paper in this room with wax on it with my name in the line for a person removed and an empty line above it for a person applying, and I want to say one thing and I want to say it standing up and in my own mouth and I want the man with the pencil to write it down, and I am not going to say it twice.*”

She had the slate under her arm and she did not put it down.

“*On the second day of this week I said no to a farthing a visit, in this room, and I said it six days before that on a step, and on the second day of this week I said that I had not applied for anything and was not going to, and I asked for that to be written down, and a man with a pencil wrote it down in a book, and that book is four hundred miles off and the sentence is in it and the sentence is mine. And there is no form in four hundred miles that takes a sentence off a page and none that asks a person whether they agreed to it.*”

“*And the half of that which you have not said,*” said the man of fifty-two.

“*The second half is that I have got my no in a room four hundred miles off and I have got a name in a room four hundred miles off in a different line, and the two of them are the same distance away and they are about eleven days apart and I am the only person in this city who is in both of them, and neither of them is a person asking me a question.*” She put two fingers on the edge of the table. “*And I cannot say no. That is the whole of what I have ever had in this city and it is the whole of what has been taken off me this morning, and I am not going to be relieved about it and I am not going to be told by anybody at the end of this month that I was right to say it, and I am going to go out and do nine doors and I am not going to be thanked for any of it.*”

Kerwin Dace's pencil was not in the room because Kerwin Dace was on a road four hundred miles from here, and the clerk of forty wrote it down in a book, and the head of his book has no line for the name of a person who says a thing at a table, and he wrote it in the ordinary way and did not say what he had written, and the man of fifty-two asked him and he said it was a sentence with a name in it and a name in a sentence is a name in a book.

“*Then say what a place is worth,*” said the man with the bad ear, “*because there is a line on that sheet that says a place is kept and there is nobody in this room who knows what a place is and I have been in four rooms in this city in ten days and I have been let into two of them.*”

“*A place is kept is not a house,*” said the man of fifty-two. “*A house is a thing. A place that is kept is a door, and it is kept if somebody is behind it at the hour the cart comes in, and the office has printed a rate for it on the first row of the same schedule, which is a farthing a visit, and the payment is for the visit and is not for the name. And I am going to do the working out loud in this room, and nobody has asked me for it and the man with the bad leg asks for the working and I am not going to wait for him.*”

“*Go on,*” said the man with the bad ear.

“*Nine doors a day.*” He had his hands flat on the long table. “*That is the figure and it is hers and she said it in a row on the fourth day of this week and it is four hundred and forty-one doors in forty-nine days off a slate, read out in a row at the end of a low row, and I was not there and a boy of sixteen was, and I have taken it and I have been given it and I am not going to pretend to this room that I counted it. Nine doors a day over a year of three hundred and sixty-four days, at the first row's farthing a visit, is three thousand two hundred and seventy-six farthings. A farthing is a quarter of a penny, so that is eight hundred and nineteen pence, which is sixty-eight shillings and threepence. And a keeper of a roll is four shillings a year, which is forty-eight pence, and eight hundred and nineteen over forty-eight is seventeen times and a sixteenth over seventeen.*”

Nobody said anything, and about thirty people who had come up that hill did the division a second time in their own heads, which is what a room does.

“*And that is the end of the working and I am not going to do the other half of it in here, and here is why.*” He took his hands off the table. “*Eight hundred and nineteen pence against the second row is two hundred and forty pence a day, and that is three days and a bit over three of what a person appointed in that register is paid for a day. And the office has printed both of those numbers on one sheet and has ruled a line between them and has not said which of them is a person.*”

“*Say that last part again,*” said the man of fifty-two's own neighbour, who was nobody.

“*I have said it and I am not going to say it again, and I am going to say the whole of the rest of it once and then I am going to sit down, and I am fifty-two and I have counted halls and doors for twenty years and I have four figures about halls and I have not used one of them tonight and I am not going to. A government that prints a price for a visit and a price for a day and a price for a year of being taken out of a city has decided what a person is, and it has not decided it in a room and it has not decided it in a line. It has decided it in a division, and the division was done in this room tonight, by me, out loud, with the working, and nobody stopped me and nobody asked me not to, and I am the man who counts halls and doors in this ward and I have been doing it for twenty years and I would like it entered in whatever happens next that I did not enjoy it.*”

About thirty people who had come up that hill sat with a number they had just watched a man arrive at, and the fever was twenty weeks and one day old, and it did not stop for a division, and nobody in that room was thanked.

And a man of thirty-eight came back into this city on the fifth day of this week on the same cart, with a hole in the case where a schedule had been and no name in the return either, and nobody asked him and nobody thanked him, and the man on the wheel said he had gone as far as the next town and had found it to be two days on a cart, which is what the sheet in the yard said at the low end of Tallowgate a fortnight ago.

And a mile and a half down the low ground, in a room behind a chandler's shop, there was a lamp on and a table and a roll under the table and a woman of forty-four sitting at the table with a sheet in her hand that she could not see, and there were nine people in that room and about a half of them had come down the road on the word of a boy of sixteen who says things in doorways, and nobody had sent for anybody and nothing in four hundred miles has a form in it that lets a person be sent for.

Renn Callow had not been there. He had said on the fourth day of this week at the end of a low row that he would go when he had not been given anything for a day, and it had not been a day, and he was at a crossing.

“*Say what is asked of you,*” said the man with the bad leg, who had walked a mile and a half with a bad leg and had not been sent for and had not been thanked for it, and then he did not say anything else, because he had asked for that in a road on the second day of this month and had been told to stop, and he stopped.

The clerk of forty read it out, twice, in a room, standing up, and about nine people heard it the second time.

“*Return of the number of the persons of this place, by ward, and of the doors by street, for the keeping of a place under the fifth row. The return is to be made by a person of the place. The office does not provide the number and the office will not check it.*”

“*And the fifth row,*” said the man with the bad leg, “*say the fifth row, because she has not heard it and she is the only one in this room who is the only one in this room.*”

The clerk of forty read the fifth row out and read it a second time when the man with the bad leg asked for it again, and the woman of forty-four put her hand flat on the table where the roll is not, which is a thing she does before she says the last of anything.

“*No,*” she said.

Nine people in a room behind a chandler's shop did not look at her, and there is no form in four hundred miles that says a person must be looked at.

“*I have not said the reason and I am going to say it once and I have said on the last day of last week that I am going to stop saying that I will not be thanked, and I have stopped, and I am not going to start on anything else.*”

“*Say the reason,*” said the man with the bad leg.

“*A number of doors is a roll and the roll is under this table and I have kept it eleven years and the office pays me four shillings a year for it and has not paid me one penny of the eleven years, and a number of persons is not a roll and I am not going to give this room a number of persons, because a number of persons is a division and a man of fifty-two has stood in a room at the foot of that hill tonight and done the division out loud and has said himself that it decides what a person is, and I have kept a roll for eleven years in order that a person would not be a division, and I am not going to do it in a fortnight because a form has come down a hill with a line in it.*”

The clerk of forty had the pencil out and did not look down at it.

“*And I am going to say the other half and I am going to say it before anybody asks me for it, because there is a man of twenty-nine in this room with a pencil and there are about nine of us and I have been the only person anybody can be asked about a roll for eleven years and I have never once been asked in a room with anybody in it.*”

“*Say it,*” said the man with the bad leg.

“*Yes,*” said Fenna Rusk. “*I will keep a place. I will be at this table at the hour a cart comes in and I will be here at that hour every day and I have not been thanked in nine weeks and I have stopped saying that I will not be, and I am not going to be thanked for this either, and there is nothing in this room that will make me a person who is asked about every person who comes to a door in this city, because I am going to say the next part now and I am going to say it in my own mouth.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*A kept place is a door and I have said I will be at this door. I have not said the office may ask me what came through it. And a roll is a count of doors and not a count of people who went out of them, and if a person is conveyed out of this city on a road and comes back, then this table has a line for it, and there is one of me and there is one of this room, and I am not going to be the person who decides which of those two it is.*”

The clerk of forty looked down at the pencil.

“*Say what you are writing,*” said the man with the bad leg.

“*That a person of this place has agreed to keep a place and has declined to give a number of persons, and that both of those were said out loud in one room in about nine people's hearing, and there is one line on the form and I have written both of them on it and the line is longer than the form.*”

The man with the bad leg had asked for the second half twice in one afternoon and had got two of them, and a woman of forty-four put her hand off the table where the roll was not, and a woman of twenty-eight went out of a room and did nine doors and did not say anything at one of them, and the fever was twenty weeks and one day old, and it did not stop for a place, and nobody in that room was thanked.

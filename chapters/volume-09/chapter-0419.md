# Chapter 419: A Return With One Column In It And Two Things Down It, And One Of Them Is A Man Of Fifty-Eight Who Keeps A Road

The first day of the eighty-second week was his hundred and sixty-ninth morning and the nineteenth day after the settlement, and the fever was nineteen weeks and three days old, and the rail had been down nine days, and a sheet came up the hill to the black building at the second hour with wax on it, and the clerk of forty read it out standing up in the public room at the foot of the hill in nine people's hearing, and it was the first thing he had read out in his life.

“*The office requires a return of the persons and things reported to it in respect of any road, and the return is to be made in one column, and the office does not require the person making it to distinguish between a person and a thing, and does not pay for the distinction.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*Go over that once more,*” said the man of fifty-two.

“*It is the whole of it and it is one line and I have read it three times in a corridor on my own before I came down,*” said the clerk of forty. “*I am twenty-nine and I write them fair and I have never read one out to anybody, and I have been standing in this room for four minutes and I have not put my hand flat on the table the way he does, and I am not going to, because I do not do that.*”

“*Say the rest of it,*” said the man with the bad ear.

“*There is a form for it on the ground floor and the form is printed at the head and the head says persons and things, and there is one column under the head, and there is no line for a person and no line for a thing, and the office has not asked for a second column and I have been on this floor three years and I have never written a line in a form that was not in it.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*And if it is a thing and I fill it in as a person,*” said Tarin Keel, from the wall, “*then a thing in a register is a thing that can be asked about. And if it is a person and I fill it in as a thing, then a person in a register is a person that can be sent for by a person who has been sent for by a thing.*”

“*Say it slower,*” said the man of fifty-two.

“*I have been in a room for nine days and I have watched four names go into four books and I have watched a road go into a book twice without anybody noticing, and the road is the only one of the five that nobody can complain of, and I am not going to be the one who says that out loud, and I have said it.*”

Nobody said anything.

---

The clerk of forty wrote it in the press room on the ground floor with the door shut and the clerk of thirty's tray on the table because it was the table he had been used to, and he was not in there long.

The head was printed and it read *persons and things reported to the office in respect of the Low Road, this city, and the bar lately upon it*, and under the head there was one column ruled down the middle of a good sheet, and the sheet was good and the ruling was straight and the ink was good, and it cost a penny a page, and it could be read standing up, and anybody in this city could be shown it for a penny.

He put two things down it.

The first was: *Renn Callow, of the Low Road at the weir head, keeper twenty-nine years, who says that he does not know how many doors are on that road.*

The second was: *The ninth marker on the same road, and two miles of the road past it, not walked in four years.*

He looked at the two of them for a while. Then he copied the sheet fair into a second one and put the second one in the press, and he read both of them out in the public room at the foot of the hill at the third hour, and it took him four minutes, and he did not apologise for any part of it, and about thirty people were in the room.

Nobody said anything.

“*Say that again,*” said the man of fifty-two, “*the first one, and then the second one, and then tell this room which of the two is a man.*”

The clerk of forty read them both again.

Nobody said anything at all, and the room sat with it, and Bevin Tarr at the end of the long table said *huh* out loud, which is not a word he has said in a room since the fourth day of the eightieth week, and he did not apologise for it either.

“*Say what you have found,*” said the man with the bad ear.

“*I have found a sheet with one column in it,*” said the clerk of forty, “*and I have filled it in because that is the form and I fill lines off a paper in front of me. And there is no second column and there is no line under a person's name and there is no line under a road's. And I read them both out in this room and about thirty of you have heard me read a man and a marker and you have not got anything in the sound of it to hold on to, and neither have I, and I have been on this floor three years and I have never before put two things in a column and been unable to tell anybody afterwards which was which.*”

“*Say the second half of it,*” said the man of fifty-two.

“*The second half is that the office will get it on the fourth day of the eighty-second week with a man of thirty-eight and there will be a line in a register at the seat with a man of fifty-eight and a marker on it, and the office does not pay for the distinction and the office does not require it, and in eleven years somebody in that building is going to take that sheet off a shelf and ask what it is about, and there is going to be no way on the paper of telling.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*And the third thing and I have not said it out loud anywhere yet,*” said the clerk of forty, “*is that it is going to be me they ask, because I am the one who filled it in, and I am twenty-nine and I have been on that floor three years and I have never had my name at the top of a line, and a clerk of thirty has, out loud, in a room, and he said the price of it before he did it and he did it anyway, and I have been in this building three years and I have watched that happen and I have thought about it for nine days and I have got nothing better and I am going to do the ordinary thing and I am not going to be thanked for it.*”

---

He went down to the weir head at the fourth hour with the sheet in his hand because he did not know what else to do with it, and the man at the crossing was outside with the lamp out and the water moving under the head of the weir.

Nobody had sent for anybody.

“*I have filled it in,*” said the clerk of forty, “*and I have filled it in with two things in one column and the office does not pay for the distinction, and I have read it out in a room to thirty people and I would like you to tell me which of the two things on that sheet is the man, because if I put it back in the press I can take one of them out.*”

Renn Callow looked at the sheet and did not touch it.

“*I do not know how to help you with that,*” he said, “*and I have been asked what I can and cannot do twice in one day by two people from four hundred miles and this is the second time, and I have told you both of the same thing and I would like it noticed that I have noticed.*”

“*Then tell me which one to take out.*”

“*No,*” said Renn Callow, “*and here is why, and it is the last of what I am going to say to you and I am saying it on my own step and not in a hut, because there is no fire in a hut in this weather and a man of the seat has a book and I have signed a line and I am not going to be careful with words in a road. You are twenty-nine and you have been on that floor three years and you have never read a thing out in a room in your life, and you read one out this afternoon. That is not a small thing and nobody in that room said it was. And now you have a sheet with my name on it and a marker on it and you cannot tell them apart, and if I tell you which one is the man then I have chosen a column, and there is not one, and you would have to write down that I chose, and there is no line for that either.*”

“*Then I will go and ask somebody else.*”

“*Ask the man with the bad leg,*” said Renn Callow, “*because he has been at this crossing twice in nine days and he is the only man I have seen walk up to it who was not sent for and is not a person of this ward, and I have not got a better idea and I have thought about it standing here.*”

The clerk of forty went up four hundred yards of road and found the man with the bad leg at the end of the low row at the last of the light, and he had a sheet in his hand and he read the two lines out loud on the ground, in a row, standing up, because a man with a bad ear asks for the second half and there are nine people in that row and the boy of sixteen is one of them and did not ask.

The man with the bad leg listened to the whole of it.

Nobody said anything.

“*Say which one,*” said the clerk of forty.

Nobody said anything, and the man with the bad leg did not say which one, and the row waited, and the clerk of forty waited, and nobody asked him, and the boy of sixteen did not ask him, and the man of fifty-two was not in that row and the woman of twenty-eight was at a door at the end of it and did not come over.

The clerk of forty put the sheet back in his coat and the man with the bad leg walked away up the row and neither of them said one word to the other about it, and the row did not think about it afterwards and told nobody.

The four bells went over Tallowgate. The sheet went up the hill and into the press on the ground floor, and it is legible, and it is a penny a page, and there is a window in that room, and a man of fifty-eight is a line on it and a marker is a line on it, and there is no form in four hundred miles that takes either of them out, and the fever was nineteen weeks and three days old, and it did not stop for a return, and nobody in that row was thanked.

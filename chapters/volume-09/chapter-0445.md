# Chapter 445: Nine Men Come Up The Road In The Night With A Bar Of Ash And A Chain, And What Holds The Crossing Is A Barrow That Goes Up The Other Side Every Morning

Sixth day of the eighty-fifth week, the fourth hour of the night. Shelf day three hundred and twenty, his hundred and ninety-fifth morning, forty-five days after the settlement, the fever twenty-three weeks and one day, the rail down thirty-five days.

They came up the Low Road in the dark in the rain with a lantern and a bar of sawn ash about nine feet long and nine links of chain over one shoulder. The man at the front of them had a sheet of paper rolled inside his coat. There were nine of them, and they had walked from the weir head.

The four hurdles were across the north side of the ninth marker at knee height, with the nine links of new chain between the two outer ones. The plate was on the post above them, with two nail holes in it and one of them bent, and nothing cut in the bottom corner. The box was on the north side three feet from the fourth hurdle with its shutter down, because it was the fourth hour of the night and nobody is in it at the fourth hour of the night.

The south side was four yards of mud and a ledge of a foot and a half with the water under it, and there was nothing at all on it, and there has been nothing on it since the eighty-first week.

---

Renn Calloway was in the mud at about two hundred yards on the south side with a lamp in his hand, and about eleven people were with him, and he had not been sent for. There is no form in this county that says a man may be sent for.

Tam Ferrow was on the ledge at the same time with his book under his coat and his pencil in his hand, and nobody had asked him to come and nobody had sent for him, and there is no form in this county that says a man with a pencil may be kept out of a room either.

“*Say how many,*” he said, to nobody in particular, because he says what he sees.

“*Eleven with me,*” said a voice behind him. “*And I counted them twice, because it is dark and because I am fifty-eight. Two of them are children, and I would like that written down by somebody who was not in a hurry.*”

“*Say how many men on the road.*”

“*Nine.*”

“*Say the working on the mud,*” said Ilyan.

He had walked a mile and a half in the rain in under two hours with a bad leg, and he had a sentence ready, and he was going to use it. About eleven people turned round and looked at him, which is the thing that happens in this city when a man who is not of it opens his mouth at a bad moment.

“*Say it fast,*” said Calloway. “*Say it to the crossing and not to me.*”

“*The four hurdles and the nine links and the plate are the property of the office of Regent of the Shelf under the third line of the writ of succession. The office may take them down. There is nothing in this county that stops the office taking them down, and none of the eleven of you has any standing here at all.*”

The rain came off the flat into the mud, and about nine people did not move.

Then a man on the north side of the road, who was about nineteen and had a lamp in his teeth, said the same thing back to him in a slightly different order.

And then he said the rest of it.

“*Say it again, and I will put this barrow through the north side of that crossing, and I have got a barrow and I have got the arm for it, and I am going to work in the morning.*”

Ilyan did not say it again. He had said it once, and it was true, and it had been the wrong true thing, and it had been in the wrong place.

---

The man with the barrow was somewhere behind them and nobody had heard him. He had been coming up the south side at about the ordinary hour with about two feet of mud in the wheels, the way he had every morning since the rail came down, and the way he had every morning before that.

He came on up past the group without stopping. His lamp was out. He put his shoulder past the ninth hurdle on the north side without touching it, the way a man does not touch a thing he has walked past four hundred times.

And the traffic of that side of the road at that hour came up four yards of mud behind him, which was about forty people and two carts, and is a fifth of what goes up it in a day.

That was all it was. There was no resolution in it and no speaker and no paper. There were about two hundred crossings on that side of that road on an ordinary day, and it was the fourth hour of the night, and the carts of this city go on the road at night as well as in the day. The south side was four yards of mud and a ledge and water underneath it, and it was the side that had never been named in any office document, and it was therefore the side that nobody could be told to keep off.

The nine men on the north side had a bar of sawn ash nine feet long, and nine links of chain, and a written authority from an office that had the roads. They could not get to the ninth marker, because the ninth marker is where the two sides meet, and forty people and two carts were in it, and about a hundred and sixty more would come up behind them before the light.

They stood there for about forty minutes in the rain.

Then the man at the front of them put the sheet of paper back inside his coat and said one sentence, and it was loud enough for the mud to hear.

“*The office has the roads, and we are the office, and we will come back at the change of the light, and this is not the end of it.*”

“*Say the half of that which is the other half,*” said Calloway to Ilyan, quietly, while the nine of them were still on the north side.

“*Go on,*” said Ilyan.

“*The half of it is that we have all just watched the office say out loud that it is the office, on a road with a bar on it. And the office is right, and it is right on an ordinary day.*”

He shifted his weight off the bad leg.

“*And there is a bar up on the north side of that crossing tonight, and a bar down on it at the change of the light if they come back. And the road is not held by the bar, and it is not held by the office.*”

“*Then say what holds it,*” said Ilyan.

Calloway looked at the four yards of mud for a while with the water going round his boots.

“*That,*” he said. “*The barrow.*”

“*Say the rest of it and do not make it a speech.*”

“*He has gone up it every morning for nine years, and nobody has ever asked him to, and nobody has ever put his name anywhere. Tonight nine men with a bar and a paper stood in the crossing for forty minutes, and the road went on working in front of them.*”

“*Say the half of that which is the other half.*”

“*They did not have to lose and they did not have to win. They had to be in the way, and they were not in the way, because the other side of that road has no name on it in any document and you cannot bar what you have not written down.*”

Nobody in that mud asked him for a second half, and he did not offer one, and about eleven people went up the south side of the ninth marker in the dark and in the rain because it was the way they went home.

---

Ilyan stood in four inches of mud on the south side of a road two miles from his room for about a quarter of an hour and said nothing at all.

What he had wanted, since the second hour of the third day of this week, was to be the thing that held the road, and he had spent a day and a night writing a form with two lines in it about persons and wards. A form with two lines in it was a very good way of asking a ward who it was, and it had not been a single use whatever to a road with a bar on one side of it and mud on the other. He had known that on Friday. He had said it in a window of a press. It had not stopped him.

“*And the rest of it.*” said Skell, from up on the ledge, where he had got himself up in the dark with a hand on the concrete drum.

“*Say what you are writing.*”

“*That nine men with a bar of ash and nine links of chain and a written authority stood on the north side of the ninth marker for about forty minutes in the fourth hour of the night, and that they took nothing down.*”

“*Go on,*” said Skell.

“*And that about forty people and two carts went up the south side in the same forty minutes, and that the man who went first was a man with a barrow going to work in the morning.*”

“*And the rest of that.*”

“*And that the man not of this city, who has spent six weeks saying that anything written down is a thing somebody can be asked about, said out loud in that mud, to about eleven people, that there is nothing in this county that stops the office taking a bar down.*”

“*Say the last part of that again.*”

“*And about nine of them heard him say it, and one of them said it back to him in a different order and put a barrow in the way of it. And I would like the room to know that he was right, and that being right is what happened, and that I have written it in that order because I write down what is in front of me and not what it means.*”

---

At the change of the light a rider came up the Low Road from the low end of Tallowgate at the pace of a man who has been riding all night, and he did not come to the ninth marker.

He came to the room at the foot of the hill, and there were about thirty people in it, and he had a sheet in his coat, and he read four lines of it out standing up in the middle of the floor without sitting down.

An instrument of succession had been lodged with the office of Regent of the Shelf at the fourth hour of the night, and it named one person, and the person named was the man who had been standing in the mud at the ninth marker at that hour, four hundred yards off, saying a true thing that was no use.

The holder of the office of Regent of the Shelf had ceased to hold the office at the fourth hour of the same night, by the writ of succession of the first day of the eighty-fifth week. The writ said that upon the ceasing of the holder, the office passes to the person named in the instrument.

And the third line of the writ said that the roads of the Shelf, and the keeping of them, and the bar upon any of them, and the taking down of any bar, pass with the office.

Nobody in that room said anything for about four seconds.

Then Fenna Rusk said: “*Then the nine men on that road were the office, and the office had passed to him an hour before they stood in it, and he did not send them.*”

Ilyan Vester put his hand flat on the long table and said the only sentence he had, and it was four words long.

“*Read the rest of it out.*” said Skell.

“*I did not send them.*”

“*Then say the rest of it and do not stop in the middle.*”

“*I did not send them, I did not know, and I was in the mud with a sentence in my mouth.*”

He put his hand flat on the table and left it there.

“*And there is a woman in this room who is named in a line in my own hand, written into a form on Friday, and a clerk asked her at a town four hundred miles off on the same day, and she got my name out of herself in front of about thirty people.*”

“*Say the rest of that and do not stop in the middle of it,*” said Fenna Rusk.

“*I have held four hundred miles of country since the fourth hour, and I have not held anything at all, and there is not one form in this county that a person in my position can be asked to fill in.*”

The fever was twenty-three weeks and one day old. The bar of four hurdles and nine links and a plate was still up on the north side of the ninth marker at the change of the light, and the south side of that crossing still had no name in any document in the world, and about two hundred crossings went up it every day in the mud.

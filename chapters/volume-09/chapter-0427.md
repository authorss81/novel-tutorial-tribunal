# Chapter 427: A Woman Says Her Finding In A Room And Then Eight People Walk Two Miles To A Post In The Mud, And A Man Says The Price Of His Own Name Out Loud Before He Puts It Down

The second day of the eighty-third week was his hundred and seventy-seventh morning and the twenty-seventh day after the settlement, and the fever was twenty weeks and four days old, and the rail had been down seventeen days, and the woman of twenty-eight said her finding in the public room at the foot of the hill at the second hour in about thirty people's hearing and the clerk of forty wrote it down in a book and there is no line on the head of his book for the name of a person who says a thing.

She said it standing up, with the slate under her arm, and it took about four minutes, and it was the fourth thing of hers in a book in a city four hundred miles off, and the man of fifty-two stood in the room with her because he had said in a row on the fifth day of this week that he would, and he was not thanked for it and did not expect to be.

“*And the second half of that,*” said the man with the bad leg, “*which is that a bar on a road is an admission, and I want that said in a room by somebody who is not me and I do not care who.*”

“*Say it in a room,*” said the woman of twenty-eight, “*and I will say it.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*A government that bars a road has admitted the road goes somewhere. That is the finding and it is the only one there is. It is not a victory and I am not going to call it one, and it is not a finding about this city and I have not left this city. A person may go out on that road and a person may not, and neither of those is a victory and neither of those is a finding about the city they went out of. And the office has paid for a cart and the cart has never once gone past the ninth marker, and the office has paid nobody anything, and the year of money is three hundred and sixty-four pounds and it is sitting at the seat and I have not been told that it has been paid and I have not asked and there is a form for asking and I have not got one and I am not going to say that is the office's fault, because a person who has not asked has not asked.*”

The clerk of forty wrote it down and did not say what he had written, and the man with the bad ear asked him and he said it was four sentences with a name in one of them and a name in a sentence is a name in a book, and the man of fifty-two said that was the fourth and that the office would get it on the fourth day of the eighty-second week, which was the day before, and that it had got it, and nobody thanked anybody for it.

---

Then eight people walked out two miles on the Low Road to the ninth marker, and none of them had been sent for and no form in four hundred miles lets a person be sent for, and the man who kept that road for twenty-nine years had said on the fifth day of the seventy-ninth week that he did not go past that post alone, and he went past it.

“*Say why we are going out there,*” said the woman of sixty with a shawl, at the weir head, to about six people who had not asked.

“*Because the only thing this city has got that is worth anything is a road with a bar on it two miles out and a woman at a table a mile and a half the other way, and those are four hundred yards apart at the nearest and they have not been in the same place at the same time in nine days, and I am not going to do that in a room, and the man with the bad leg has not said the price of it and I would like him to say it out loud before we get there and before any of us stands in the mud.*”

The mud on the south side of the Low Road at the ninth marker is about four inches deep in the middle and about a foot and a half down at the ledge, and eight people of this city stood in it, and the four hurdles were across the north side at knee height, and the chain was between the two outer ones, and the plate was on the post above them, and there was nobody in four hundred miles who had sent any of them.

“*Then say it here,*” said Renn Callow, “*and I will not go past the post until you have, and that is not a threat and it is the only thing I have got and I have had it since the fifth day of the seventy-ninth week.*”

The woman of forty-four was in the mud at the edge of it with her hand on nothing, and she had walked two miles, and she had not been asked to and she had not been able to say no, and she said so out loud in the road before anybody said anything else.

“*I came because a man asked me and I could not say no, and I have said that in four rooms this week and I am not going to say it a fifth time in a road, and I have said it so that everybody here knows that the reason I am standing in this mud is not that I thought of it.*”

“*Then say the price of it,*” he said, “*before either of us moves, and I am going to say it to you and not to the road, and there is a man of twenty-nine here with a book and a pencil and he can act, and I have known for three days that this is the shape of the whole of what I have done since the first day of this month and I have not done it once, and I am going to do it now.*”

Nobody said anything, and about eight people stood in four inches of mud two miles out on a road in a flat, and a woman of forty-four did not have to be able to see his face to hear him, and the clerk of forty took his book out of his coat and did not open it.

“*If I put my own name in a line next to yours, then this road has a person on it who is not of this city and cannot be sent for, and there is nothing in four hundred miles that takes a name out of a return except a person of the place applying at the seat, and I am not a person of a place, and so I cannot apply, and so I cannot be struck out. I have said that price out loud in this city five times in three weeks and every one of the five was to a person who was in a room, and this is the first one I have said to a person who is standing in front of me in mud and did not ask me for it, and I would like it entered that I said it before I did it and not after.*”

He waited. The water moved under the ninth weir four hundred yards back and the mud was not doing anything.

“*Say the second half,*” said Fenna Rusk, “*which is what it does to me, and you have said yours and it is true and it is not mine, and I have been in this city nine weeks with my name at the top of a page and I know exactly what a name in a line does to the person whose it is, and I am not going to stand in a road and be thanked for asking.*”

“*The cost of it is yours and it is not mine.*” He did not raise his voice and there was no wind on the flat. “*A person of this place in a book is asked about the person outside it who put her there, and you cannot say no, and the asking is not this week and it is not next week. It is for as long as there is a road and a form and an office that does not check a number, and it will go on after I am not in this city, and it will go on after I am not in this country, and I have tried for two days to name one person who could put a stop to that and I cannot name one, and I have said in a room this month that I could not name one about this city, and I have not stopped saying it since.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*Then say what happens if you do not do it,*” said the woman of forty-four.

“*Nothing happens,*” said the man with the bad leg. “*The road keeps a bar on it at the ninth marker. The bar is on the north side and the south side has nothing on it and a barrow goes up it every morning, and a person will keep going round it on foot because there is nothing else to do, and there will be no person in this city who can say what crosses that road, and I will have a thing I know about and not one name in it, and the next man from the seat will come down that hill with a form and there will be nothing to put in it, and he will fill a line off a paper in front of him, because that is what he does, and it will be a good form and it will be a penny a page and it will cost this city nothing and it will decide what a person is in a room that does not know it.*”

The man of fifty-two was at the edge of the mud on the north side where the ledge is and had not stepped off it.

“*I can write it either way,*” said the clerk of forty. “*I write lines off a paper in front of me. I have been on the ground floor three years copying other men's words fair and I have never once been asked to put two names in one line, and I would like it entered that this is the first and that I was asked and not told, and I am twenty-nine and I write them fair and I have never read one out to anybody and I have read six this week and I am not going to stop now.*”

“*Then say the word for it,*” said the man of fifty-two, “*because there is a phrase for a man who can be sent for and a phrase for a person of a place and there is not one in this city for a person who cannot be sent for standing beside a person who can be asked about, and if there is not one then say that there is not one, and if there is one then say it and say who says it.*”

“*A co-witness,*” said the man of fifty-two. “*That is what it is. Two people on one line, and one of them is a person of the place who can be asked about the other one, and the other one is a person who is not of the place and cannot be sent for and cannot be struck out, and neither of them can take the other one off, and that is a co-witness, and there is no form for it and there is not going to be one.*”

“*I have not heard the word,*” said Fenna Rusk, “*and I am not going to pretend that I have, and I have kept a roll for eleven years for a thing that had no form and I am not impressed by a thing with no form. So say what it is for, and if you have both said what it is for then it is a word for something that already exists and I am not going to be a person who is asked about it on the strength of a word.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*It is for the road,*” said the man with the bad leg. “*That is the whole of what it is for. It is for a road that a person can be asked about by a person who is not of the place and by a person who is of it, and neither of them can be moved off it, and the office can bar the north side of it as often as it likes and neither of them will be behind the hurdles.*”

The clerk of forty wrote for about a minute and a half, and the man of fifty-eight had not said a word since the weir head and had not been asked to, and about eight people stood in the mud on the south side of a road two miles out from a city of ninety thousand people.

“*Say what you are writing,*” said the man with the bad leg.

“*That a person of this place, not of this city, has put his own name in a line beside the name of a person of this place in respect of the Low Road at the ninth marker, and has said the price of it out loud in eight people's hearing before he did it, and that there is no form in four hundred miles that takes either name out of that line, and that the office has not asked him and cannot.*”

“*Then put his name in it,*” said the man of fifty-eight, from the north side, where he had been standing on the ledge. “*And I will tell this road what it is and I will say it once. I have kept this road twenty-nine years and I cannot tell you how many doors are on it and I have said so three times this week and it is written down twice. And this morning a man said a price out loud in the mud in front of a woman who cannot say no, and the price did not stop him, and it did not stop him because it was not his to pay, and I have been at this crossing since the fifth day of the seventy-ninth week and I have wanted to say that a road with one keeper and one decider in the same body is held and not kept, and this morning there are two of them and neither of them is me, and I have not got anything to add to that and I am not going to add to it.*”

A man of fifty-eight turned the lamp on the bench out of the wind in a hut four hundred yards back and was not thanked, and the fever was twenty weeks and four days old, and it did not stop for a name, and nobody on that road was thanked.

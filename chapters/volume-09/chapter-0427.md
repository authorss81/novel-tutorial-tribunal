# Chapter 427: Eight People Walk Out To The Post In Four Inches Of Mud, And A Man Says The Price Of His Own Name Out Loud Before He Puts It Down

Second day of the eighty-third week. Shelf day three hundred and two, his hundred and seventy-seventh morning, twenty-seven days after the settlement, the fever twenty weeks and four days old, the rail down seventeen days.

Hessa Marl said her finding in the public room at the foot of the hill at the second hour, standing up, with the slate under her arm, and it took about four minutes.

It was the fourth thing of hers in a book in a city four hundred miles off. Skell stood in the room with her because on Friday night at the end of a low row he had said he would, and he was not thanked for it and had not expected to be.

Ferrow wrote it down in a book. The head of that book has no line for the name of a person who says a thing.

“*And the second half,*” said Ilyan, “*which is that a bar on a road is an admission, and I want that said in a room by somebody who is not me, and I do not care who.*”

“*Say it in a room,*” said Hessa Marl, “*and I will say it.*”

She waited until the fire had gone quiet, which took a while, and then she said it.

“*A government that bars a road has admitted the road goes somewhere. That is the finding and it is the only one there is.*”

The room sat with it.

“*It is not a victory, and I am not going to call it one, and it is not a finding about this city, because I have not left this city.*”

She turned the slate over once and did not open it.

“*A person may go out on that road and a person may not, and neither of those is a victory, and neither of those is a finding about the city they went out of.*”

“*Then say the rest of it,*” said Skell.

“*The office has paid for a cart and the cart has never once gone past the ninth marker, and the office has paid nobody anything, and the year of money is three hundred and sixty-four pounds and it is sitting at the seat.*” She put the slate back under her arm. “*I have not been told it has been paid and I have not asked, and there is a form for asking and I have not got one.*”

“*And that last part is yours,*” said Ilyan.

“*That last part is mine, and I am not going to be relieved about it, because a person who has not asked has not asked.*”

Ferrow wrote it and would not say what he had written, and when Skell asked him, he said it was four sentences with a name in one of them and a name in a sentence is a name in a book. Skell said that was the fourth. The office would get it on the fourth day of this week, which was the day before, and it had got it, and nobody thanked anybody for that either.

---

Then eight people walked out two miles on the Low Road to the ninth marker.

None of them had been sent for, and nothing that has been printed lets a person be sent for. The man who had kept that road for twenty-nine years had said on the fifth day of the seventy-ninth week that he did not go past that post alone, and he went past it.

At the weir head the woman of sixty stopped, and about six people who had not asked why stood on the flat with their boots wet through to the ankle.

“*Say why we are walking out there,*” said Bess Low.

“*Because the only two things this city has got that are worth anything are a bar on a road two miles out and a woman at a table a mile and a half the other way, and those are four hundred yards apart at the nearest, and they have not been in the same place at the same time in nine days.*” Renn Calloway had his hand on his bad leg. “*And because the man with the bad leg has not said the price of it, and I would like him to say it out loud before we get there, and before any of us stands in the mud.*”

The mud on the south side of the Low Road at the ninth marker is about four inches deep in the middle and about a foot and a half down at the ledge. Eight people of this city stood in it. The hurdles were across the north side at knee height, the chain between the two outer ones, the plate on the post above them, and nobody who had sent any of them was four hundred miles away or anywhere else.

“*Then say it here,*” said Calloway, “*and I will not go past that post until you have, and that is not a threat. It is the only thing I have got and I have had it since the fifth day of the seventy-ninth week.*”

Fenna Rusk was at the edge of it with her hand on nothing. She had walked two miles. She had not been asked to and she had not been able to say no, and she said so out loud in the road before anybody said anything else.

“*I came because a man asked me and I could not say no, and I have said that in four rooms this week and I am not going to say it a fifth time in a road. I have said it so that everybody here knows that the reason I am standing in this mud is not that I thought of it.*”

“*Then say the price of it,*” said Ilyan, “*before either of us moves. I am going to say it to you and not to the road. There is a man of twenty-nine here with a book and a pencil and he can act, and I have known for three days that this is the shape of the whole of what I have done since the first day of this month, and I have not done it once.*”

He had the cold in his right ear again and he was aware of how long it had taken him to stop shifting his weight off the leg. There were about eight people in the mud, and the water under the ninth weir was four hundred yards back, and the wind off the flat was not doing anything at all.

“*If I put my own name in a line next to yours, then this road has a person on it who is not of this city and cannot be sent for.*” He said it slowly, and he did not look at Fenna Rusk while he said it, because he had learned that she heard better when she was not being looked at. “*And nothing anywhere takes a name out of a return except a person of the place applying at the seat. I am not a person of a place. So I cannot apply, and so I cannot be struck out.*”

“*And you have said that in this city five times in three weeks,*” said Ferrow quietly, “*and every one of the five was to a person who was in a room.*”

“*This is the first one I have said to a person who is standing in front of me in mud and did not ask me for it.*” He turned round and looked at the man with the book. “*I would like it entered that I said it before I did it, and not after.*”

He waited. The mud was not doing anything.

“*Say the second half,*” said Fenna Rusk, “*which is what it does to me. You have said yours and it is true and it is not mine, and I have been in this city nine weeks with my name at the top of a page, and I know exactly what a name in a line does to the person whose it is.*”

“*The cost of it is yours and it is not mine.*” He did not raise his voice, and there was no wind on the flat. “*A person of this place in a book is asked about the person outside it who put her there. You cannot say no. And the asking is not this week and it is not next week. It is for as long as there is a road and a form and an office that does not check a number.*”

“*Say the rest of that,*” said Ilyan, because he could hear himself needing it.

“*It will go on after I am not in this city, and it will go on after I am not in this country. And I have tried for two days to name one person who could put a stop to that and I cannot name one, and I have said in a room this month that I could not name one about this city, and I have not stopped saying it since.*”

Nobody spoke. Somewhere behind them on the north side, a boot went into the mud on the ledge.

“*Then say what happens if you do not do it,*” said Fenna Rusk.

“*Nothing happens,*” said Ilyan. “*The road keeps a bar on it. The bar is on the north side, and the south side has nothing on it, and a barrow goes up it every morning, and a person will keep going round it on foot because there is nothing else to do.*”

“*Then what is left?*” said Fenna Rusk.

“*There will be no person in this city who can say what crosses that road, and I will have a thing I know about and not one name in it.*”

He looked at the hurdles, four lengths of ash with a chain between the outer two.

“*And the next man from the seat will come down that hill with a form, and there will be nothing to put in it, and he will fill a line off a paper in front of him, because that is what he does. And it will be a good form, and it will be a penny a page, and it will cost this city nothing, and it will decide what a person is in a room that does not know it.*”

Skell was at the edge of the mud on the north side where the ledge is, and had not stepped off it.

“*I can write it either way,*” said Ferrow. “*I write lines off a paper in front of me. I have been on the ground floor three years copying other men's words fair, and I have never once been asked to put two names in one line, and I would like it entered that this is the first, and that I was asked and not told.*”

“*Then say the word for it,*” said Skell. “*Because there is a phrase for a man who can be sent for and a phrase for a person of a place, and there is not one in this city for a person who cannot be sent for standing beside a person who can be asked about. If there is not one, say there is not one. If there is one, say it, and say who says it.*”

“*A co-witness,*” said Skell.

“*I have not heard the word,*” said Fenna Rusk, “*and I am not going to pretend that I have, and I have kept a roll for eleven years for a thing that had no form, and I am not impressed by a thing with no form.*”

“*Say what it is for,*” said Ilyan, “*and if the two of you have both said what it is for, then it is a word for something that already exists, and I am not going to be a person who is asked about it on the strength of a word.*”

“*It is for the road,*” said Ilyan. “*That is the whole of what it is for. It is for a road that a person can be asked about by a person who is not of the place and by a person who is of it, and neither of them can be moved off it, and the office can bar the north side of it as often as it likes and neither of them will be behind the hurdles.*”

Ferrow wrote for a minute and a half, and about eight people stood in the mud on the south side of a road two miles out from a city of ninety thousand people.

“*Say what you are writing,*” said Ilyan.

“*That a person not of this city has put his own name in a line beside the name of a person of this city, in respect of the Low Road at the ninth marker, and has said the price of it out loud in eight people's hearing before he did it, and that no form takes either name out of that line, and that the office has not asked him and cannot.*”

“*Then put his name in it,*” said Renn Calloway, from the north side, where he had been standing on the ledge the whole time. “*I am not putting mine in. I want that said first, because I have been at this crossing since the fifth day of the seventy-ninth week and I have wanted to say that a road with one keeper and one decider in the same body is held and not kept.*”

He looked along the line of them, at the hurdles, at the plate.

“*This morning there are two of them and neither of them is me. I have kept this road twenty-nine years and I cannot tell you how many doors are on it, and I have said so three times this week and it is written down twice, and a man said a price out loud in the mud in front of a woman who cannot say no, and the price did not stop him, and it did not stop him because it was not his to pay.*”

He put the chalk back in his pocket, where the eleven strokes and the one that was not a stroke of anything had been all morning.

“*I have got nothing to add to that and I am not going to add to it.*”

The eight of them stood in the mud until their feet were gone, and then they went back up the Low Road, and the fever was twenty weeks and four days old, and it did not stop for a name, and nobody on that road was thanked.

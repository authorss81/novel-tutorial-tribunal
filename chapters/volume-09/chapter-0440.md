# Chapter 440: A Writ Of Succession Comes Down The Hill In The Tray, And The Third Of Its Four Lines Is About The Roads

First day of the eighty-fifth week. Shelf day three hundred and fifteen, his hundred and ninetieth morning, forty days after the settlement, the fever twenty-two weeks and three days, the rail down thirty days.

A sheet came down in the tray at the second hour with wax on it and a seal on it, and the head of it was printed, and there were four lines under the head.

About thirty people were in the long room. They had been in it every day for forty days, and on every one of those days they had got up off a wall and stood and then sat down again, and a man of the seat was still in the city because the dispute had been given to him and was not closed.

“*Say the head,*” said Skell.

“*In the matter of the office of Regent of the Shelf,*” said Ferrow, standing up, “*and there is nothing under that, and then there are four lines.*”

“*Say the first one.*”

“*One. The office of Regent of the Shelf is one office, and is not divided, and is not held in parts, and is not held by two persons at once.*”

“*Say the second one and do not stop in the middle of it.*”

“*Two. Upon the ceasing of the holder of that office, by death or otherwise, the office passes to the person named in the instrument of succession lodged with the office, and where no such instrument is lodged, to the person then holding the second seat at the council of the Shelf.*”

“*Say the third one.*”

“*Three. The roads of the Shelf, and the keeping of them, and the bar upon any of them, and the taking down of any bar, pass with the office.*”

The room did not make a sound at all.

“*Say the last one,*” said Skell, and Ferrow read it in the same voice.

“*Four. Nothing in this writ is a finding about a road, and nothing in it is a finding about any person in any city, and it takes effect according to its terms and not otherwise.*”

He put it down and squared it to the edge of the long table, which is what a clerk does.

---

Nobody in that room could say what a writ of succession was.

“*Say what you think it is,*” Ilyan said, “*all of you, and I do not want the answer, I want to know how many different answers there are in a room of about thirty people.*”

They gave him five. A man at the back said it was a paper about a man who was ill. A woman near the door said it was a paper about a house. Skell said he did not know and would not guess. Fenna Rusk said it was a list.

Then Renn Calloway said it from the wall, where he had been standing since the second hour because there is no form in this county that says a man may be sent for, and there was not one at the second hour on Friday, and there has been one ever since a sheet with no head on it came down in the tray on Saturday.

“*A writ of succession is a piece of paper that says who gets a thing when a man stops holding it,*” he said. “*I have kept a road for twenty-nine years and I have watched two men stop holding it, and both times a piece of paper came down inside a week and said who had it now, and neither time the paper said anything about the road, because the road was not the thing. The thing was a chair in a room four hundred miles off, and the road went with the chair because the man who held the chair had said it would.*”

“*Say the rest of that,*” Ilyan said.

“*And this one says what the thing is. The thing is the roads.*”

“*Say the whole of it and do not stop in the middle of it,*” said Skell.

“*The third line says the roads, and the keeping of them, and the bar on any of them, and the taking down of any bar, and they all pass with the office. And the first line says the office is one office and is not held by two persons at once. And the second line says it goes to a person named in a paper kept somewhere, or failing that to whoever is sitting in a second chair.*”

He shifted his weight off the bad leg.

“*And I have been arguing in this room for five weeks about four hurdles of ash and a chain and a plate, and I sent the argument up a hill myself on a piece of paper on the fifth day of the eighty-second week, and it has gone down four times and every one of the four answers has been correct, and not one of the four has said whose the bar is.*”

“*They have all mentioned the bar,*” Ilyan said.

“*They have all said the bar is to remain up,*” said Calloway, “*and not one of them has said the bar is anybody's.*”

---

“*Say the working on the third line,*” said Skell.

Skell put his hands flat on the table.

“*There is one office. It is not divided and it is not held in parts and two people cannot hold it. It passes to one person. And the roads of the Shelf pass with it, and the keeping of them, and the bar on any of them, and the taking down of any bar.*”

“*Say the rest of it.*”

“*So the four hurdles on the north side of the ninth marker, and the nine links of chain, and the plate with nothing cut in the corner of it, are the property of a single office, and there is one office, and the person who holds it is the only person in four hundred miles of country with a lawful hand in the timber.*”

“*Say the half of that which is the other half.*”

“*And the dispute this city sent up the hill in the eighty-second week is not a dispute about a bar. It never was. It is a dispute about who holds a road, and this city sent it to a register, and the register gave it to a person appointed, and that person is standing in this room with a book.*”

Nobody in the room had been in the room when the dispute went up. About nine of them had been in this room on the fifth day of the eighty-second week, when a man of about thirty-eight set a case on this table and said he was going anyway and was not asking anybody to stop him.

---

“*Then say what it is for,*” said Ilyan. “*I have been in this city forty days and I have had a reading of it ready since the flat, and I would like to hear somebody else say it first.*”

The man of the seat put his pen down on the table.

“*It is a writ of succession and it is filed and it is correct,*” said Wray Cope, “*and I am the person appointed in the register of disputes concerning roads, and I have not got anything to do with that office and I am not going to tell this room what that office is for.*”

“*Then say the half of it that you have got to do with,*” Ilyan said.

“*The half of it I have got to do with is the fifth clause of the sheet of the fifth day of the eighty-third week, which is correct, and which says that a person not of this city cannot be entered in a register of this city and cannot be struck out of one.*”

He turned the sheet round so that the seal was facing the room.

“*This is a register. A writ of succession is a register, and it is a register of an office and not of this city. A person of this city cannot be entered in it, and a person not of this city cannot be entered in it either. The office does not require that a person able to hold the office be of a city on a road that runs in it.*”

“*Say the rest of that,*” said Skell.

“*So there is no name in this city that can go in that writ, and there is no form in this county that gets one there, and nobody in ninety thousand people on this side of that road can be given the thing that the third line is about.*”

“*Say the working on that,*” said Ilyan, because that is what he does to a room.

“*I am not going to,*” said Cope. “*You have asked me for the working twice, in a corridor, about a division and about a door, and both times I gave you one, and both times I was of this city and this time I am not.*”

“*Say why.*”

“*Because the working on that is arithmetic and the arithmetic is not the difficulty, and you know it is not the difficulty, and you have been asking me for workings for five weeks because a man who is good at workings does not have to say the hard part out loud in a room.*”

---

Ilyan had said what he thought the office was doing, and he said it at about the third hour, and it was the wrong thing to say, and Skell corrected him inside of a minute.

“*Say what you think that office is doing,*” said Fenna Rusk, from the second chair along, “*and say it in a room and not in a corridor, and I will not say anything about it afterwards.*”

“*I think it has written down who the roads go to before anybody has been asked whether they should go to anyone,*” Ilyan said, “*and I think a bar is the cheapest way of finding out whether anybody objects, and I think this city has objected five times and has been told five times that it is correct, and I think the office has learned in five weeks that this city objects in a form.*”

“*Say the half of that which is not yours to say,*” said Cope, and he had the pen out.

“*Say it as a question,*” said Ilyan.

“*Is it your office?*”

“*No,*” said Ilyan.

“*Then it is not a thing you can say in a room in this city and call a finding, because a finding about an office that is not yours is an opinion, and an opinion said in a room of thirty people is a thing thirty people carry out of the door.*”

“*And it is a thing a man of the seat can put on a sheet,*” said Skell, “*and we had a sheet on Saturday with no head on it at all, and the name of a boy of sixteen at the foot of it, and a seal on it, and the office has been putting names in books from roads since the second day of the eighty-third week.*”

Cope wrote for about a minute, and then he read it out.

“*That a person not of this city said in a room in about thirty persons' hearing that he considers the office of Regent of the Shelf to have settled in advance who the roads of the Shelf pass to, and that he is not of that office and does not hold any place in it.*”

“*That is correct,*” said Ilyan. “*Write that I said it in a room and not in a corridor and that I knew it was an opinion when I said it.*”

“*That is not what you said,*” said Cope, “*but I will enter that you have asked me to.*”

---

The room at the foot of the hill had a writ of succession on the long table with the wax still on it and about thirty people who had come up a hill to find out what a bar was for, and there was no one in the room who could be given the thing the third line was about.

“*Say the last of it,*” said Skell, “*and then I am going to say something that is not mine, and then I am going to stop for the day.*”

“*Then say yours first,*” said Ilyan.

“*Mine is that this is the ninth thing the office has sent this city in five weeks, and that eight of the nine came down a hill because this city asked for them, and that this one came down because nobody asked for it, and that all nine are correct, and that I have got a bar on a road two miles out that I have been keeping the wrong end of for twenty-nine years.*” He took his hands off the table. “*A man stops holding the chair and the road goes with the chair, and the road does not stop being a road, and I am still the one who has to get up at four in the morning.*”

Calloway pushed himself off the wall.

“*Mine is not mine and I am going to say it anyway,*” he said. “*There is a man in this room who has said two things in this city in five weeks and I have heard both of them and I am going to say them back to him and I am going to ask him which one he is standing behind.*”

Ilyan put his hand off the table.

“*Say them.*”

“*The first one is what you said in the mud on the south side of that road on the second day of the eighty-third week, with eight of us standing in it and a man of twenty-nine writing.*” He said it slowly. “*It is for the road. And the office can bar the north side of it as often as it likes and neither of them will be behind the hurdles.*”

He waited until the room had it.

“*And the second one is what you said in this room on Sunday at the change of the light with about thirty people in it, and there is a woman of forty-four at the end of this table who heard it twice.*” He looked at Fenna Rusk and she nodded once. “*I am not sending it because if it goes up the hill it is a number the office has, and then this city has two numbers of persons in it and neither of them is the right one.*”

“*Say why those two,*” said Ilyan.

“*Because that writ says the roads pass with an office, and the third line of it, and I cannot work out what a road is for if the two of those are both true, and I have walked past both ends of that argument in five weeks and I am asking you and I am asking you in a room because a room is where things get said in this city and I have had a month of that.*”

About thirty people waited in a room at the foot of a hill for a man with a bad leg to pick one of two things he had said.

He picked the first one, and he picked it out loud, and then he said the half of it that made it a bad answer.

“*The first one, because a road that an office holds is a road a person stands on the open side of. The only way to be on the open side of it for good is to be in a book that says I am.*”

He waited, and then said the half that made it a bad answer.

“*And the second sentence says I will not be in a book. So I have said a sentence out loud that I cannot keep, and I am saying it in front of a man of the seat and a man with a pencil and about thirty people, and I would like all three of you to have it.*”

“*Then I will have it,*” said Cope.

“*Now say the other part of the question,*” said Ilyan, “*because there is a second part to it and it is the part I actually want answered.*”

He turned to the man of the seat.

“*Do not write that down.*”

The room went quiet, and about nine people looked at each other.

Ilyan had spent five weeks in this city asking rooms for second halves. He had asked a press to set things in a window where anybody could read them standing up. He had asked a boy of sixteen to be told the right answer before he went in, and he had been right about all of it, and it had never once got anybody out of a room.

Now he was asking a man of the seat to leave a sentence out of a book.

“*Say why,*” said Cope.

“*Because I have been asking rooms for the writing down of everything since the first day of the eighty-third week. I have told nine people in this room that a thing written down is a thing somebody can be asked about, and I have said it in four rooms and in a street. I have been right every time. It has never once got anybody out of a room.*”

“*Say the rest of it,*” said Skell.

“*The rest of it is that I cannot give you a reason that is in the form of a reason, and I have been in this city forty days and that has happened to me twice, and both times it was about a person and not about a road, and both times I was managing the record for somebody who had not asked me to.*”

Cope did not write for about ten seconds.

“*There is a line for what a person said in a room,*” he said. “*There is a line at the foot of a book I carry and it is the ordinary form and it is not mine and I did not make it. I have to fill it in, and I am not able to leave it, and I have never once left one.*”

He put the pen down.

“*I would like it entered that you asked me, and I would like it entered that I said no, and I would like it entered that I said no anyway, because the two of those are the only parts of this afternoon that were mine.*”

He wrote it down.

The writ of succession was on the long table with the wax on it and four lines under a head. The third of the four said that the roads of the Shelf, and the keeping of them, and the bar upon any of them, and the taking down of any bar, pass with the office.

Every one of the four was correct. The fever was twenty-two weeks and three days old, and it did not stop for a piece of paper that says who gets a thing when a man stops holding it, and about thirty people in a room at the foot of a hill had found out that they had spent five weeks arguing about a chair.

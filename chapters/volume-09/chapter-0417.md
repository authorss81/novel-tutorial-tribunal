# Chapter 417: The Man At The Crossing Asked For The First Time In Twenty-Nine Years, And The Answer Is Four Lines In A Book Four Hundred Miles Off

The seventh day of the eighty-first week was his hundred and sixty-seventh morning and the seventeenth day after the settlement, and the fever was nineteen weeks and one day old, and the rail had been down seven days, and a man of thirty-eight went down to the Low Road at the weir head at the fourth hour with a book and a case, and the man with the bad leg went with him because he was the only man in this ward who could be asked whether anybody had sent for anybody, and nobody had sent for anybody.

The hut is four posts and a roof and a bench and a lamp and a barrow-wheel leaned against the north post, and there is a line of chalk on the bench, which is not a record, and eleven small flat things on the sill, which is not a tally either, and the man inside is very firm about both of those things.

Renn Callow let them in and did not offer a chair and did not say anything at all for a while.

“*I have come down this row three times since the day I came down that hill,*” said Kerwin Dace, “*and I have asked nine hundred doors a question and I have got nine answers and four hundred of the nine hundred are in the four rows off this one and I have not been to them. I am going to ask you the same question and then I am going to ask you one more, and the second one is not on my paper and it has not been on it since I came, and I am telling you that I have not so that you know what it is when it comes.*”

“*Ask the first one.*”

“*Will you be named in the register of disputes concerning roads.*”

Nobody said anything. The water moved under the weir head and the eleven flat things on the sill did not move.

“*Say the rate for it,*” said Renn Callow.

“*A pound a day,*” said Kerwin Dace, “*and the working was said out loud in this city twelve days ago by a man at the wheel who has given every figure in this city out loud with the working in the sentence for thirty-one years, and I have it written in front of me and I am not going to say it in a hut, because he said it and I am not going to stand in a road and take a figure off a man who is better at it than I am.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*Then I know what that is,*” said Renn Callow, “*and I have told a stranger with a bad leg the other thing once, on the fifth day of the seventy-ninth week, in this hut, and I said once and I meant it and you have not asked me for it, and I am not going to say it again to you or to anybody, and that is all I have to say about a register.*”

Kerwin Dace shut the case and put the pencil away, and the man with the bad leg said, “*Then he has said no to it and he has said why, and both of those have been said in a hut, and you have it, and you are going to ask the second question anyway, and I would like you to ask it standing up, because that is what it is.*”

He asked it standing up.

“*How many doors are on this road.*”

Nobody said anything, and the lamp on the bench was turned round so the wick was out of the wind, and it was the man at the wheel's trick and it is a trick in this ward and it is not the office's.

“*I have kept this road twenty-nine years,*” said Renn Callow, “*and I do not know the number, and I have known for eleven years that not knowing is not the same as not going to them, and I have never said that to anybody in this city because there has never been anybody here to say it to, and there has never once been a person in four hundred miles with a book at that crossing.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*Write that down,*” said Renn Callow.

Kerwin Dace wrote it down.

“*Say it back to me,*” said Renn Callow, “*all of it, and do not leave anything off the page out of kindness, because I have been at this crossing twenty-nine years and there is not one line in any building in this city with my name at the top of it, and there is one now, and I would like to hear what is on it.*”

Kerwin Dace read the page back, and he read all of it, and the hut is four posts and a roof and there were three people in it.

“*A person under age named in a return, offered the form, declined. A woman of twenty-eight named in a return, offered the first row, declined, reason given and entered, and the reason is a visit that is paid for is a visit that can be sent from somewhere else, and there is a second part to it which is that the office keeps a book of the visits and does not enter the names, and the second part is her own and she has given it four times. A person of nineteen with a cart and an arm in a splint asked what a bar costs, answer eleven runs and one hundred and ninety-eight pence of cart. A person of sixty who read the first row out in a room and made a statement about a settlement and a woman in a bed at the top of this city. A clerk of the ground floor, whose name I have, gone on the seventh day of the eightieth week on the office's charge with three sheets. And a person named Renn Callow, keeper of the Low Road, keeper twenty-nine years, asked how many doors are on that road, says he does not know, and says he has known for eleven years that not knowing is not the same as not going to them.*”

The lamp on the bench did not move and the chalk line on the bench was eleven strokes and none of them a name.

Nobody said anything.

“*That is me in a book,*” said Renn Callow. “*And I am not going to ask you to rub it out and you are not going to offer, because you have said you do not have a way of writing a thing you have done that does not do that.*”

“*I have not.*”

“*Then I am going to say the second half of it and it is for the man with the bad leg and not for you,*” said Renn Callow, “*and then I am going to sign the page, because a clerk of thirty said the price of a name out loud in a room in this city on the first day of the eightieth week and filled the line in anyway, and a man who has kept a road for twenty-nine years knows exactly what a name at the top of a line is, and I am not going to be the second person in this city to find that out the hard way.*”

Kerwin Dace turned the book round on the bench and put the pencil beside it.

Nobody said anything.

“*Before you do that,*” said the man with the bad leg, “*say what it costs, out loud, in this hut, to a person who is here, because you told me on the fifth day of the seventy-ninth week that you would not write it down and I have not forgotten it and I have not forgotten that you were right not to.*”

“*The price is that from today there is a line in a book with my name at the top of it and what I do not know on the same line,*” said Renn Callow. “*And the office has asked it once and will ask it again, and there is nobody at this crossing but me to ask, and the only person in this ward who can answer it is a mile and a half down this row with a slate under her arm, and she is at the top of a page of her own in a city four hundred miles off and she cannot say no.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*So I am going to have to go down that row and ask her,*” said Renn Callow, “*and I have not asked anybody for anything in twenty-nine years and I am not going to pretend that asking her is a small thing, and I would like it said in this hut that I know what I am about to do and I am going to do it anyway, and I am not going to be thanked for it, and if it goes into anybody's book I would like it to go in whole.*”

He picked the pencil up and signed his own name at the foot of the line.

Kerwin Dace read it back to him twice, as a clerk does, and Renn Callow made him do it twice, and then the man of thirty-eight shut the book and said the thing he had come to say, and he said it in a road with a fire going behind him.

“*I have until the fourth day of the eighty-second week and I have used six of them and there are four left,*” he said. “*I have got two names out of three for that register and a gap where the third should be, and a clerk at the seat has a line to fill and nothing to fill it off, and tomorrow the register is going to be kept with somebody in it that your office chose, and I am telling you that at a crossing and not in a chamber, because you are the only person in this city who keeps anything and I would rather a thing like that was said to a keeper than to a magistrate.*”

“*And who will it be.*”

“*A person of the seat,*” said Kerwin Dace, “*and I do not have a name and I did not know that when I came down that hill and I know it now and I got it out of a boy of sixteen, a woman of twenty-eight, a carter of nineteen and a clerk of thirty, and not one of them said it as a warning, and I have been trying all day to work out how to tell them and I have not got it.*”

Renn Callow put the lamp down on the bench and turned it so the wick was out of the wind.

“*Say the last of it and then go,*” he said.

Kerwin Dace turned the book round again and put his thumb on it.

“*How many doors are on this road.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*I do not know,*” said Renn Callow, “*and I have kept it twenty-nine years, and I said that to you ten minutes ago in this hut and you wrote it down once, and I am going to say it again because your form has a line for a second answer and a second reason, and I have read your fourth row out of your own mouth and I know what your forms do.*”

“*I have to enter the second asking.*”*

“*Then enter it,*” said Renn Callow, “*and put both of them on the same line, and do not put one of them in your book and one of them in your head, because there is a version of this where I have said a thing once and you have said I said it twice, and in four hundred miles there is going to be a man who reads that page and works out which of the two of us is lying, and he is not going to be able to tell, and that is not a thing I am going to hand to a room four hundred miles off to sort out.*”

The man of thirty-eight wrote it down twice, and both of them were on the same line, and neither of them was rounded off.

The four bells went over Tallowgate. A man of fifty-eight shut a hut door at a crossing and had not been thanked and had said out loud that he was not going to be, and a man with a bad leg walked four hundred yards up the road and did not go down the row to the lamp and the slate, and the fever was nineteen weeks and one day old, and it did not stop for a roadkeeper, and nobody on that road was thanked.

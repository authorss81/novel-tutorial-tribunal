# Chapter 405: A Sheet With A Seal On It, And The Name Of The Man Who Reads Things Out Loud

The first day of the eightieth week was his hundred and fifty-fifth morning and the fifth day after the settlement, and the fever was seventeen weeks and three days old, and the rail had been up eight days, and a man came into this city in the afternoon off a cart from the east with a fold under his arm and a seal on it, and the boy of sixteen had said it in four doorways by the last of the light, and the room at the foot of the hill was full before the clerk of thirty came down.

The rider is fifty-four and he is a carrier of sealed paper between the seat and this city and he has done it for nineteen years, and he is a person of the flat and not of this ward, and he said so himself before anybody asked him.

“*I am not of anywhere,*” he said. “*I am of the road between the two and I have slept eleven hundred times in this city and I have never been asked a question in it and I have never been in a room in it. I have brought thirty-one of these. Twenty-nine of them were about grain and weights and two of them were about a boat.*”

“*What does it cost you to bring one.*”

“*The crossing is two days on a cart and a cart is eighteen pence a day, and eighteen twice is thirty-six, and I am paid thirty-six and a loaf,*” said the rider. “*And I was held at a rail on the way out of this city for a day and a half, on a cart, going out, with this on me, and I was not paid for that day and a half and the man at the rail did not know what was on me and I did not tell him, because a man on a cart with a seal on a fold is a man who has a thing that is not his and I have never in nineteen years told a man at a rail what was in the fold.*”

Nobody thanked the rider.

---

The sheet is a good sheet. That is the first thing the room understood and nobody said it out loud, because saying it out loud in a room is a finding and a finding said in a room goes into people, and by the fourth hour of that afternoon there were about forty of them in that room and the boy of sixteen was in the doorway with his mouth shut, and he does that perhaps twice a year and nobody in this room had seen it before.

It is a single sheet, ruled, and the ruling is straight, and the head is printed, and the ink is good, and there is no smudge in it and no word has come off it in a week of weather, and the clerk of thirty held it up to the window and turned it and looked at the back of it, which is a thing he does to a form.

“*This is legible,*” he said. “*I have had this sheet in this building for about a minute and I can read it standing up. I have not had to be near it. And I have been on this floor nine years and I want to say what that is, because nothing in this city is legible, and I have never had a sheet in my hand that I did not have to be close to, and I have never read a thing to a room that I could not have read from the door.*”

He put it on the long table, squared to the edge, and squared it again.

“*I am going to read it out. That is what I am for and I have not been asked to and I am not going to be asked to, and I am going to read all of it including the parts I do not like, and I am going to read the lines at the end last.*”

---

“*One. A bar upon a road within a city is a matter for the office at the seat and not for the ward in which the bar stands. The office may put up a bar upon the report of a person of the place. The office may take down a bar upon the report of a person of the place. Where a bar stands or falls, the report of the person of the place is made to the office and not upon a sheet nailed to a rail.*”

“*Say what that does to the sheet on the rail.*”

“*It does nothing to it. I am not going to say anything to it. I read lines. That line does not say what happens to a sheet nailed to a rail, and I am not going to read a thing into a line in a room because a man with a leg is standing at the end of the table looking at me.*”

He read the second.

“*Two. A person of a place who goes to a door on the account of the office is paid, and the payment is at the figure paid to a keeper of a roll, and the payment is for the visit and not for the name, and the office keeps its own book of the visits and the names are not entered in it.*”

“*Say the figure and say the working.*”

It was Bevin Tarr who said the working, and he had come up the mile and a half with a plate under his arm because he could not leave a plate, and he had not been asked and he was at the end of the long table.

“*The figure a keeper of a roll is paid is four shillings a year,*” he said. “*Four shillings is forty-eight pence. Three hundred and sixty-four days go to a year in this city, and forty-eight pence over three hundred and sixty-four days is a farthing a day and not quite. So that is what a visit is worth to this office: about a farthing. And I am telling you the figure because I am the man who gives the figures in this city and I have given every one of them out loud with the working in the sentence for thirty-one years and I have never been thanked once and I am not going to be thanked for the hundredth time today.*”

“*Say the other half of it.*”

“*The other half is a boy of eleven at the end of this row who carries water for a penny at the end of the week,*” said Bevin Tarr, “*and every person in this room knows that figure because he is a person of this ward and is the head of his own house. So a visit to a door, in a city of ninety thousand people, is worth about a quarter of what a boy of eleven earns in a week carrying other people's water, and I have cut forty thousand lines in my life and I have never been paid a farthing for anything and I have never been asked for one, and that is the whole of what I have to say about a sheet of good paper with a seal on it.*”

Nobody said anything. The clerk of thirty read the third line.

“*Three. A dispute concerning a road, or the passage over a road, or the keeping of a road, may be given to a person appointed by the office for that purpose, and the person so appointed is named in a register kept at the seat, and a dispute given to that person is a dispute given and is not a dispute that is lost.*”

He read that twice, in the flat voice, and then he read the lines at the end, which he had said he would read last.

“*And the office requires, for the keeping of its register, the name of the person who read the return of the Mercy Trial into the public room at the foot of the hill in this city, and the name of the person who wrote that return fair, and the name of the magistrate against whom that return was written.*”

---

The room did not go quiet. It went the other way, and then it did not do anything at all, and there was a sound at the back of it that was about eleven people finding out at once that they were in a room.

“*You have read that last line out loud,*” said Tarin Keel, from the wall, “*and the second line of it is the name of a man in this room, and the first line of it is yours.*”

“*The first line of it is mine,*” said the clerk of thirty, “*and I read the settlement into the room at the foot of this hill standing up, and I read it again because a man asked me to read it again, and I wrote the second return at a magistrate's dictation and the clerk of forty copied it fair, and I have been that man for nine years and I have never in nine years had a name put at the top of anything and I have told this room before, in this room, that I have not.*”

He put his hand flat on the sheet, on the empty part of it, and he squared it a third time.

“*Then here is the price of it and I am saying it out loud in a room because I am a clerk and I say prices in rooms and I have watched a man do it and I have never been able to do it as well as he does and I am going to do it now.*”

Nobody stopped him. That is the thing about it. There were forty people in that room and two of them could have said no and neither of them did and one of them was not going to, and the other one had said already, four days ago, in a row, that there is no form that takes a name off a page and that he is not going to be able to give it back.

“*A name at the top of a line is a person who can be asked about that line,*” said the clerk of thirty. “*That is the whole of it. After this morning the office can ask me whether I read that return and I will have to say I did, and I said I did, and they will ask me how many times and I will say twice, and I will say I was asked to read it again by a man with a bad leg who is not in this city on any list of anybody, and I will say that and they will go and ask about him, and he will be a person in a record in a city of ninety thousand people, on the other side of it, because I read a line out loud in a room. I am a clerk. I fill lines off a paper in front of me. The paper was in a tray and the tray came from a room four hundred miles off.*”

“*So do not fill it in.*”

“*I am going to fill it in,*” said the clerk of thirty, “*and I am not going to be thanked for it and I am not going to be relieved and I am not going to be told afterwards that it was the ordinary thing, because it is not the ordinary thing, and I have been the ordinary thing for nine years and I have never had a piece of paper in my hand that was not what it said it was.*”

He filled the line. It took him a moment longer than a line takes.

“*My name is Bern Raye,*” he said, to the room, because the room did not have it, and because he had been a clerk for nine years and had never once had to give it in a room where it was going to be written down. “*And I have said it out loud, and I have been saying things out loud for nine years, and I have never once had it go anywhere before.*”

Nobody thanked him and nobody told him it was the ordinary thing, and the woman of twenty-eight went out into the row at the last of the light and said in a doorway that a clerk of thirty had put his own name on a line and that a man with a bad leg was on the other side of that line now, and she said it four times in four doorways because that is what she said and nobody stopped her, and the rider went out into the low row and was not thanked for the fold, and the four bells went, and the rail was up, and the fever was seventeen weeks and three days old, and it did not stop for a seal.

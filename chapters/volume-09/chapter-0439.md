# Chapter 439: Eleven People Put A Penny In A Bowl For A Bell Four Hundred Miles Off, And The Bowl Is A Line In A Book

Seventh day of the eighty-fourth week. Shelf day three hundred and fourteen, his hundred and eighty-ninth morning, thirty-nine days after the settlement, the fever twenty-two weeks and two days, the rail down twenty-nine days.

A china bowl with a chip in the rim stood on a table outside a shop at the low end of Tallowgate from the second hour to the change of the light, and eleven pence went into it, one at a time, by people who were not announced and were not asked for anything.

The bell is in a county four hundred miles off. It has not been hung. The porch in the valley of sixty people is gone, and there is a corridor standing in it with a bell unpaid on it, and a woman of sixty said all three of those things out loud in a room at the foot of a hill a fortnight and two days ago and went back to the wall, and the three of them have not been said in a room since.

The bowl was her idea and she has not said so and nobody has asked her.

“*Say what the line is,*” said Skell.

Tam Ferrow had a book open on his knee at the end of the table with a pencil in his hand, and about nine people were standing round, and the low row behind them had emptied out the way a row empties when there is a table in it.

“*A line of names,*” he said, “*against the eleven pence in the bowl, in the order they went in, with the hour against each one, because I asked for the hour and nobody had a reason to refuse me one.*”

“*Say why you asked for the hour.*”

“*Because a gift of money with nothing written on it is money, and money can be taken off a table, and a gift of money with eleven names against it cannot be taken off anybody without a line in a book saying who had it.*”

“*Say the rest of that and do not stop in the middle of it.*”

“*And a list of people who gave a penny is a roll,*” said Ferrow. “*And I have been putting rolls into books for three years and I did not know that was what I was doing on a table outside a shop until a woman of forty-four said the word in a room last week and I have not been right since.*”

Fenna Rusk was at the table. She had not been asked and had not been sent for and had walked a mile and a half to a bowl.

“*Say the whole of it,*” Ilyan said. “*You are the only person in this city who knows what a roll is, and a roll is your word, and you have been carrying it for eleven years and somebody has just written one at a table outside a shop and I would like you to say whether it is one.*”

“*It is not a roll of doors,*” said Fenna Rusk, “*and it is the same shape, and I want both of those said out loud and I want them said in that order.*”

---

The working took about a minute and Skell did it standing up with his hands flat on the table.

“*Eleven pence in the bowl. The bell is four pounds. A pound is twenty shillings and a shilling is twelve pence, so four pounds is four times twenty times twelve, and that is nine hundred and sixty pence.*”

“*Say the next part of it.*”

“*Nine hundred and sixty over eleven is eighty-seven and a remainder of three. That is eighty-seven collections of eleven pence, at the same eleven pence each time, before there is any money in the world for a bell.*”

“*And the working on the line.*”

“*Eleven names and eleven pence and one name to a penny, and that is correct, and I did it in about four minutes on the second day of this month with a pencil in a room and nobody checked it.*” He put his hands down. “*And the shortfall is one. And I am going to say the rest of it and then I am going to sit down, and I have said the rest of it four times in a month and this is the fifth and it is the last time in a room.*”

“*Say the rest of it.*”

“*A person in this city who gives a penny to a bell four hundred miles off has not bought a thing. She has put a penny in a bowl. The office has no line for a gift and would make one. The moment there are eleven names against eleven pence, this is not a gift any more. It is a subscription, and a subscription has a list, and a list is a roll, and a roll goes up a hill in an oilcloth in the ordinary way, and the woman who keeps it cannot afterwards tell anybody whether it is right.*”

“*And say who is right,*” said Ilyan.

“*I am right, and it is not a thing I am glad about, and I have not got better at this in five weeks.*”

---

Bess Low was at the edge of the table and had not put a penny in it, and there was a name on the line with a quarter past the third hour against it, and she had not been in the shop at any point that morning.

“*Say the name on the line,*” said Ilyan, and then stopped himself, and said it differently. “*Say whether the person whose name is on that line is in this street.*”

“*I am,*” said Bess Low. “*I have been in this street since the second hour and I have not put a penny in the bowl and I have not given anybody my name, and there is a woman of sixty in it at a quarter past the third hour and I did not ask her and she did not ask me and there is a man with a book who wrote it.*”

Ferrow looked at the page and then at the room.

“*I wrote it,*” he said, “*and I am going to say how, because there is a man with a pencil and about nine of us, and I have not done this five times.*”

“*Say how.*”

“*I had a list of eleven people who had said in a room that they would put a penny in, and four of them were not in the street, and I wrote the eleven names first. Then I went round the row and collected the pennies, and I had a name on the line before I had a penny against it. That is the whole of how it happened and I have not got a better way of doing it and I have not tried to get one.*”

“*Say the last of it,*” said Skell.

“*And I am not going to take it back off the page, and I would like the street to know that I am not going to take it back off the page, because a name in a line is a name in a book and I have said that nine times and I am not going to be the man who rubs one out at a quarter past three on a Thursday.*”

Bess Low came up to the table. She was sixty and she wore a shawl over a dress that had been mended at the cuffs twice, and she put two fingers on the page next to her own name, and she did not touch the pencil.

“*Then read the line out,*” she said, “*and read the bowl out, and read the two halves and the difference, and say it in a room rather than in a street, because I have been in this city nine years and I have never once been counted, and there is a bell four hundred miles off that is not hung, and I am not going to let this happen outside a door.*”

“*Bess—*” said somebody.

“*Do not.*” She said it without any weight in it at all. “*I am not going to be one of the eleven. I have given nothing to that bowl and I have not promised that bowl anything, and a name on a line is a count, and I have not been counted in nine years, and I did not choose the year.*”

---

Ferrow read it out. He read it standing up, because he reads things out standing up, and about thirty people were in the street by the last of the light and about nine of them came in and stood in the doorway of the shop and did not come further.

“*Eleven pence in the bowl. Eleven names on the line, one to a penny, with the hour against each. One of the eleven names is the name of a person who has given no penny and has promised nothing, and that person has not asked to be taken off the line, and the clerk of the ground floor has not taken her off it, and neither of them is going to.*”

“*Say the shortfall,*” said Bess Low.

“*The difference is one.*”

“*Say what the one is.*”

“*The difference is one penny in the bowl with no name against it, and one name on the line with no penny behind it, and the two of them are not the same one, and there is no form in this county that puts them on the same line.*”

About thirty people stood in a street and listened to a woman of sixty read out a figure of one, and the shop closed behind them, and the chip in the rim of the bowl was the shape of nothing anybody could name.

“*Then say the rest of it,*” said Skell, quietly.

“*I have been in this city nine years and I have never once been counted,*” said Bess Low. “*I said that in a room a fortnight and two days ago because a form had come down a hill wanting a number of persons. I said it and I went back to the wall, and nobody has done anything about it since, and I have not expected anybody to.*”

She turned round to face the street and not the table.

“*And a name on a line is a count. That is the whole of it. There is one number of people in this city who have not been counted in any book and I have spent nine years being her, and this afternoon a man with a pencil wrote me on a page for a penny I did not give, and I am going to stand here and say that I would rather be a person nobody has a number for than a person on a line for a bell four hundred miles off.*”

“*Say the last of it,*” said Ilyan.

“*And the bell is not hung. And the porch is gone. And the corridor stands with a bell unpaid on it, and it stood there nine years before I came to this street, and it will stand there after this, and I have not said one word to anybody about any of it today except a penny I have not got, and I am going home.*”

She went home.

---

Ilyan got her back.

He had wanted the eleven names off the line, and he had wanted it since the second hour. He had thought about it in a corridor for a quarter of an hour on the previous evening.

He had worked out that a gift of money is not a return of persons and there is no line anywhere in this county for a gift of money. He had worked out that if a room understood that in the right words then eleven names would come off a page, and eleven pence would stay in a bowl, and a bell four hundred miles off would be a bell and not a roll.

So he said it. He said it in a street with about thirty people in it at the quarter past three, to a woman of sixty who had been in this city nine years, and he said the true thing, and he said it well, and he said it the way he had said true things in four rooms in this city and been right about all four.

Then a woman of sixty read the shortfall out loud in a room, and the shortfall was a public figure.

A public figure of one penny against a bell in a county four hundred miles off is now a thing in a street with about thirty people in it. It is a thing an office can ask a man of this ward about. It is a thing a man of the seat can put on a sheet of his own with no head on it.

He had wanted the names off a line and he had put a number in a room, and there is no form that takes a number out of a room once thirty people have heard it, which is the same fact he said out loud on the ninth marker in four inches of mud nine days ago, in a different voice, about a different thing.

“*Say the rest of it,*” said Skell.

“*Say what you are writing,*” said Ilyan.

“*That eleven pence were put in a bowl outside a shop in this city on the seventh day of the eighty-fourth week, and that eleven names were entered against them in a book. That one of the eleven was entered without a penny and without a promise, and that the person so entered has declined to be one of the eleven. That the clerk of the ground floor has not struck the name out, and that the difference between the pence and the names is one.*”

“*Say the rest of that.*”

“*And that a person not of this city asked, in a street, for the names to be taken off the line, and that the line is not a form and cannot be un-filled, and that his asking is in this book because it happened in front of me.*”

“*Then strike mine out of it,*” Ilyan said.

“*There is no line for you on that page and I am not going to make one at a table outside a shop,*” said Ferrow, “*and I have said nine times in four weeks that I write down things that are in front of me and I am not going to start inventing the order of a page because a man with a bad leg is tired.*”

The fever was twenty-two weeks and two days old, and there were eleven names on a line and eleven pence in a china bowl with a chip in the rim, and one of the eleven names is a woman of sixty who gave nothing and has not been struck out, and a bell four hundred miles off was not hung, and nobody on that street was thanked.

# Chapter 431: The Roll Comes Down The Hill In Oilcloth, And The Woman Who Made It Is At Her Own Table

Sixth day of the eighty-third week. Shelf day three hundred and six, his hundred and eighty-first morning, thirty-one days after the settlement, the fever twenty-one weeks and one day old, the rail down twenty-one days.

The cart came into the yard at the low end of Tallowgate at the second hour with the oilcloth on the tail-board, and there was one person in the yard.

Ott Dray was on the wheel. He had gone up that hill on the fourth day of the week with a return in a coat and the roll in oilcloth, and he had gone round four hurdles on the south side to get past them, and he had said nothing about that to anybody then and he said nothing about it now.

The boy of sixteen was against the wall of the yard with his hands behind his back. He had not been sent for. A cart comes into that yard at the second hour and a boy who is at a door at that hour knows a cart the way a person knows a step.

“*Say what is on the tail-board,*” he said.

“*An oilcloth,*” said Dray. “*Tied on. It is the oilcloth it went up in and the roll is inside it.*”

“*Say the other half of that,*” said the boy.

“*The knot is not the knot it went up with.*”

Dray got down and did not touch the oilcloth, which was not a thing he had said out loud and did not need to.

---

Ilyan was on the step of the low row with tea gone cold in his hand when the cart turned in off the weir head, and he counted the harness and the man and the shape under the oilcloth before he got to the yard, because counting is what he does with a thing he does not understand.

He had a want, and it was a bad want, and he had it before he was in the yard.

He wanted a piece of paper from the seat saying that the roll had gone up unopened and had come back unopened. He wanted it in a hand he could hold up in a room. He had known since the fourth day of the week that he wanted it, and he had not said it to anybody, and the wanting had been sitting in him for two days like a coin in a tooth.

“*You are going to want a paper,*” said Dray.

“*I am going to want a paper,*” Ilyan said.

“*Then you are going to want it for a while, and I am going to tell you what I was told at the top of that hill so that you want it less.*”

They stood in the yard. The boy was at the wall and had not moved from it.

“*They gave me back the oilcloth and they said nothing else to me at all,*” Dray said. “*Not a sheet, not a word, not a look. A man of about forty took the oilcloth off the tail-board and went into a building and a man of about sixty came out and looked at the knot on it for a while and then went back in and I waited.*”

“*How long.*”

“*Longer than a man needs to look at a knot.*” He turned the reins over in his hands. “*I have been in this country forty years and I have been in a yard at the top of that hill in it three times, and on the first two I was let in within a minute and on the third I was not let in at all, and I went and stood by the water.*”

“*Say what you saw on the way.*”

“*Four hurdles,*” said Dray. “*Nine links. The plate with nothing cut in the corner of it. And the south side with nothing on it, the same as when I went up, and a barrow on it going the other way from mine with about two feet of mud in it.*”

Ilyan looked at the oilcloth.

“*Say the last part of that again.*”

“*A barrow going the other way from mine.*”

Not one of the nine of them said anything, and it went on for about a second and a half.

“*Then the road has two things on the south side this morning and one of them is a barrow with a man on it,*” Ilyan said, “*and the office has put a plate on the north side that says a road cannot be walked round, and it is being walked round by two different people in one hour on the same four yards of mud.*”

“*You can write that down,*” said Dray, “*if you have a hand that is any use and I would rather you did not put my name under it. I am a man on a wheel. I have been a man on a wheel for nineteen years and the only thing I am paid on is a mile.*”

“*And say the other half of this morning,*” said Ilyan, “*which is the half nobody has said yet. This city has been saying since the first day of this week that it will come back in about three weeks, and a woman of twenty-eight said it out loud in a yard two days ago in front of about nine of us, and the oilcloth is on your tail-board at the second hour on the sixth day of the week.*”

“*It came back in the ordinary way,*” said Dray.

“*Say the ordinary way again.*”

“*It came back the way it went. A cart, on a road, with a man on the wheel who is paid by whoever sent it, and nobody sent for this one except a man of twenty-nine who put it in a coat.*” He got his hand off the tail-board. “*I do not know what the office expected it to do for three weeks, and I have been in this country forty years, and I am going to say one thing about it and then I am not going to say anything else about it.*”

“*Say the one thing.*”

“*A thing that comes back in the ordinary way has not been anywhere. It has been to a room and come out of it, and I have been three times into a yard at the top of that hill in nineteen years, and I was let in within a minute on the first two of them, and this is the third.*”

---

They got the roll off the tail-board and carried it up the low row between them, and the boy of sixteen came behind it with his hands still behind him, and about nine people at that end of the row came out onto the steps to look at an oilcloth going past, and none of them was told anything.

A mile and a half down the low ground there was a lamp on in a room behind a chandler's shop, and there was a woman at the table.

“*Say that to her,*” said Ilyan, “*and do not say it to me first, because I have been in this city thirty-one days and I have learned that a thing said to me first is a thing said to her twice.*”

Dray said it to her.

“*It is come back,*” he said. “*On the cart, in the oilcloth, from the top of the hill. The boy carried it up the row and the man with the bad leg carried it up the row and I carried it up the row, and I have not put my name under anything.*”

Fenna Rusk had her hands flat on the bare table. There was nothing on the table but the lamp.

“*Say the hour,*” she said.

“*I do not know what hour they gave it to me. It was afternoon on the fourth day of the week and it was dark before they came out.*”

“*Then say what you can say about it and not the rest.*”

“*They did not open it in front of me.*” Dray took his hands off the reins. “*And I have been in a yard at that hill three times in nineteen years and I have never once been asked to say what a man did in a building, and I am not going to start by telling this room that they did not open it, because a man in a building is a building and I was on a cart.*”

---

The knot was on the outside of the oilcloth and it was a thing that could be seen and a thing that could be said.

“*That is not mine,*” said Tam Ferrow.

He had come up the road with about nine people behind him, and he had not been sent for, and he was carrying nothing, which was the first time in a fortnight.

“*Say what yours is,*” said Ilyan.

“*I tied it on the afternoon of the fourth day of the week in a room off the low ground and I tied the tail of the sheet doubled and brought it round the standing part twice and put the bight through and drew it down hard, and the bight came out on the left.*” He put his thumb on it. “*That is a knot you can learn in a morning and I have tied four of them in three years and two of them are in this city.*”

“*And that one?*”

“*That is the same knot done the other way round and pulled tight, and a person who has tied one knows what it is, and it is not mine.*”

The room had about nine people in it and the lamp and the bare table and the smell of a room that has had a fire in it and gone out.

“*Say the half of that which is worse,*” said Ilyan.

“*Half of it is a knot.*” Ferrow took his hand off it. “*The other half is that a knot can be retied and cannot be read, and I have said out loud in four rooms since the first day of the eighty-third week that I copy what is in front of me and I do not say what it means. And this morning I am saying a thing in front of me and I do not know what it means, and that is the first time in three years and it is not a thing I am going to write down.*”

Fenna Rusk had not touched the oilcloth.

“*Open it,*” she said, and then, before anybody had moved: “*I have said that out loud and I am going to say why, because I have not asked anybody to do anything in this room in a fortnight and this is me asking. I am not opening it and I cannot see whether it is right, and there is one person in this room who can see and one person in this room who cannot, and if I open it then the whole of what happens next happens in a room with a blind woman in it and I have decided in advance that I will not do that.*”

Nobody moved for a moment.

“*Then say what you will have done,*” said Ilyan.

“*I will have it opened at the table at the foot of the hill, in about thirty people's hearing, and the man with the pencil will read every page of it out from the top, and the man who tied the knot will say when it was tied and in which town, and I will say whether the pages are in order and whether the folds are as I fold them.*” She put one finger on the bare table. “*And if I say one page is not as I fold it, that will be in a book with a pencil in it, and I will have said it.*”

---

The long room at the foot of the hill held about thirty people, and there was a bell in the top of it that had not been rung since the fever.

Ferrow read all eleven pages out from the top, standing up, in about four minutes, and nobody interrupted him, and it was the ninth thing he had read out of that press in seventeen days and he had said on the first day of the week that he was not going to apologise for any of them.

At the end of it Fenna Rusk said the pages were in order.

“*Say the other half,*” said Ilyan.

“*The folds are not.*”

She said it from the second chair along, in a room of about thirty people, and Ferrow put the roll down flat on the long table with the eleventh page open, and there was a fold in it straight across the middle of a line of figures.

“*Say how you fold them,*” said Skell, who had come up the hill from the end of the plate yard and had not been sent for.

“*Down the gutter, always, and the last leaf tucked in, and I have folded that roll the same way every Friday for eleven years and I could do it in the dark and I have done it in the dark.*”

“*And the office?*”

“*The office folded it across.*”

About thirty people stood in a room and looked at a piece of folded paper on a table, and none of them could see it.

“*Say the whole of that,*” said Ilyan, “*and do not make it smaller than it is.*”

“*I cannot tell you whether the figures are right.*” She put her hand flat on the long table next to the roll. “*I have told this city that once already, in this room, on the first day of this week, and I am telling it a second time and it is a worse thing the second time, because the first time I had eleven pages in a stranger's hand in a press and now I have the original back.*”

“*Say why that is worse.*”

“*Because the first time it was a copy and I was the only person who could say the copy was wrong. Now it is the thing itself, and the knot is not the one I tied, and there is a fold in the eleventh page that I did not make, and I am the only person who could say so, and I cannot see it.*”

Skell stood up at the end of the long table.

“*Then I am going to say the half of it that nobody in this room is going to say,*” he said, “*and it is a half I have said a different half of twice in a fortnight, and I am not going to do a division today.*”

“*Nobody has asked you for one,*” said Ilyan.

“*Nobody has, and I am doing it anyway, which is the fourth time and it is the last time in a room.*” He put his hands flat on the table. “*There is one roll in this city and it has just come back down a hill with a knot on it that is not the knot it went up with, and there is one woman in this city who could say whether it is right, and she has said the words for it twice, and she cannot see it, and the man who could read it to her is a man of twenty-nine who ties knots in rooms off the low ground.*”

He looked along the table.

“*That is a person in a room, and a person in a room is not a record. If I do the other thing — if I count anything, if I compare a number on that page with a number I have got in my head — then I will have made a person out of a fold, and I did that once on the sixth day of last week and I did not enjoy it and it is in a book four hundred miles off.*”

“*And nobody has asked you to,*” said Fenna Rusk.

“*Nobody has asked me,*” said Skell, “*and that has been the shape of the whole of it.*”

---

Ilyan got out of the room last, and he stood in the corridor with his back against the wall for a while, and he had done a thing in a yard at the low end of Tallowgate that he had not done in this city yet.

He had taken a document off a cart and carried it up a row.

He had carried the paper about Hessa Marl into a building at the middle of the nine bridges in the eighty-first week, in the ordinary way, at the ordinary hour, and he had known every day since that he had done it. He had carried a return and a schedule up a hill on the fourth day of this week. This morning he had carried the roll, and he had not been asked to, and he had done it because a woman in a room could not see and a cart was in a yard, and if he had not done it then it would have sat on a tail-board until a carter came back.

There was no form for that either. He had looked.

The cold had got into his right ear again on the flat and had been there since the weir head, and he could not get it out, and he drank the tea, which had been cold for an hour and was bitter, and it was the only thing in the building that had not changed.

He had wanted a piece of paper from the seat saying the roll had not been opened, and there was not going to be one, and he was going to spend the next week asking for it in rooms, and about thirty people were now going to hear him ask.

He wrote in the corridor, standing up, with the cracked steel pen, and the pen went through the paper on the second line and he had to turn the sheet over and go on writing on the back of it. The knot was not the knot. The fold was not her fold. Nobody in that room could see it, including the woman who had folded it. And he finished with a question he had no answer to and wrote it down anyway, because it was the only thing on the sheet he could carry out of a room.

Then he went down the corridor to the yard at the low end of Tallowgate and looked at the rail, which was still against the north wall with its own sheet nailed to it and the line at the top of that sheet empty, and then he went east to look at a road, and the fever was twenty-one weeks and one day old and it did not stop for a roll coming home.

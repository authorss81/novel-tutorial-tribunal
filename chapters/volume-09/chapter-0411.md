# Chapter 411: A Sheet With A Name At The Head Of It, And A Return Taken Out Of A Press On The Ground Floor

The seventh day of the eightieth week was his hundred and sixty-first morning and the eleventh day after the settlement, and the fever was eighteen weeks and two days old, and the rail had been down one day and was standing against the north wall of a yard at the low end of Tallowgate with the sheet still nailed to it and the line at the top of that sheet still empty after fourteen days, and a tray came down the hill at the second hour with wax on it and a name at the head of it instead of a printed heading.

The room at the foot of the hill was full before the clerk of thirty came down, because a boy of sixteen had said it in four doorways at the change and had said, in the last of the four, that it was the clerk's own name at the top of it, which is a thing he could not have known and had worked out from the shape of the boy's face.

Bern Raye read it standing up, in the flat voice, and he read the head before the body, because that is what is at the head.

“*The name of Bern Raye, clerk, of the ground floor of the black building at the middle of the nine bridges, being the person named in the first line at the end of the sheet of the second day of the eightieth week.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*One. The name required for the keeping of the register has been given and is entered. Two. The person named in it is required to attend at the seat, and will be conveyed at the charge of the office, and is to attend on the fourth day of the eighty-second week. Three. The return upon which the giving of that name was founded is to be carried to the seat by the hand of the person so named, and not by a carrier, and not by copy.*”

He read the third line twice, which is a thing he does about four hundred times a day and has now done for a second thing this week, and he put the sheet down and squared it to the edge of the long table and squared it again.

“*Say the third line again,*” said the man of fifty-two, from the end of the table.

It was read again.

“*Say what a carrier is and what a copy is, in this room, at this table, and do not say it as a thing you have read.*”

“*A carrier is a man on a cart who is paid by whoever sent him,*” said the clerk of thirty. “*He has carried thirty-one of these in nineteen years and he has never once been asked what was in one, and he is fifty-four and he slept in a stable in this city and I know that because a man of this ward told a boy in a doorway and the boy said it in four doorways, and I have not asked him whether it is true and I am not going to.*”

“*And a copy.*”

“*A copy is a page in a press on the ground floor of this building,*” said the clerk of thirty. “*It is in the fair hand. It costs a penny a page. It is legible, which is a thing I said out loud in this room on the second day of this week and I am not going to say the whole of it again, because I have said it and a man in this room was in it. And any person in this city can be shown it for a penny.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*So the office is asking me to carry the original of it up four hundred miles on my own two legs,*” he said, “*and the second half of the third line is the half that is going to cost somebody in this city who is not me, and I have been in this building nine years and I know exactly which page it is and I am not going to say it in this room at the second hour with forty people in it, and I am going to go and take it out of the press, and I am going to come back down and say it at that door instead, and I would like the people who want to be in this room at that to be at that.*”

---

Nobody thanked him for the intention. The man of fifty-two said the second half about a different thing, on the ground, out loud, which is what he does.

“*Then what is read out in this room in the morning.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*I want that said in a room,*” said the clerk of thirty. “*I have stood at that end of that table and read the fourth line of a return and a heading under it every morning for six weeks, and there is no form in this city that says a person may be put in another person's place, and there is no form in four hundred miles either, and I asked the man at the desk of forty this morning before I came down and he told me he writes them fair and has never read one out in his life, and he is twenty-nine, and I believe him, and I am not going to stand here and tell forty people that somebody is going to step up.*”

“*Nobody is going to step up,*” said the clerk of forty, from the back wall, without moving. “*I write them fair. I have copied that return eleven times. I could not read it out if the room were on fire and I would get the half of it wrong and there is a line in the middle of it that the magistrate wrote herself and she does not hold still for it.*”

Nobody said anything, and it was Tarin Keel who said the thing that was in the room, from the wall, where he stands.

“*So the reading stops, and the settlement does not,*” said Tarin Keel. “*That is the shape of it and I have been watching this office do it for nine days and it is the first thing it has got right. A rule that is in every room is a rule nobody can be in a room about. I have been saying a thing to the one person who can act for thirty years and I have never once had a room emptied of the people who were in it, and I am not going to pretend to anybody that I like it.*”

“*Say what you do like about it,*” said the man of fifty-two.

“*Nothing,*” said Tarin Keel.

---

And then Bern Raye went up the hill to the ground floor of the black building and took three sheets out of a press.

There is a press on that floor and it is a penny a page and it is the only thing in this building that anybody in this city can pay to look at, and he had put two of the three into it himself in the last ten days. The first was the return of the Mercy Trial with the settlement in the fourth line and a heading under it. The second was the second return, in the fair hand, under the fourth line and not under any heading, and the magistrate had written the first line of it herself and had not held it still.

The third was a single sheet, copied fair, the same afternoon, under the third clause of a sheet with wax on it, and it was a true thing said in a room by a person of this ward, in nineteen people's hearing, and it stood on that floor in the same letters as the rule and it could be read standing up, and at the top of it, in the fair hand, in a line that is for the name of the person asking, there was a name.

“*Take that one last,*” said the clerk of forty, who had come up the stairs behind him and had not been sent for.

He took that one last. He put the two returns in the inside of his coat and he held the third one in his hand for a moment and then put it inside with them, and the press shut, and there was a gap in it where a page had been, and there is no form in this city that says a page may be taken out of a press.

“*Say the working,*” said the man with the bad leg, from the window, because that is what he says.

“*The register at the seat is kept upon the ground for it,*” said Bern Raye. “*The ground is the fourth day of the last week when a woman of twenty-eight said a true thing about a road in a room in front of nineteen people and I read the sheet out and a man of this ward carried a paper into this building in the ordinary way and I filled a line off it at the ordinary hour and the clerk of forty wrote it fair and it went into a press at a penny a page. That is the ground. There is one of it. And a register four hundred miles off cannot be kept upon a thing that is standing up in a press in a room with a window where anybody may be shown it for a penny, and if I leave it here then in eleven days a man from the seat will be in this room and he will ask for the ground of the register and there will be a hole in the press and no form for the hole.*”

“*And the reading out.*”

“*Stops,*” said Bern Raye.

---

He said it at a door at the end of the low row at the last of the light, and there were four other people at that door, because there are always four other people at that door, and he asked them to go in, and none of them went in, and he said it anyway with them standing there.

The woman of twenty-eight had her slate under her arm and she had been at nine doors that day and she had four she had not been to, and she heard the whole of it standing on her own step with a lamp behind her.

“*Say the part about me again,*” she said.

He said it again. A man of this ward brought a paper into the building. He filled a line off it at the ordinary hour. The clerk of forty wrote it fair. It went into the press on the ground floor at a penny a page and anybody in this city can be shown it for a penny. And on the fourth day of the eighty-second week he is to carry the three of them up four hundred miles on his own two legs, and there will be no copy in this city after that, and there is no form that says a page may be taken out of a press.

“*Which of the two did they ask for.*”

“*Neither,*” said Bern Raye.

“*Say that again.*”

“*Neither. They asked for the name of a person who read a return into a room. You were not asked for. I was not asked whether the thing you said was true. A man of this ward heard a thing said in a room and carried a paper about it into a building in the ordinary way and I filled a line off the paper, because that is what I do, and the line wanted a name and the paper had one on it.*”

“*And nobody asked me.*”

“*Nobody asked you,*” said Bern Raye, “*and I have said it twice because you asked me to and because I have been on this floor nine years and I fill lines off papers in front of me and I have never once in nine years asked a paper where it came from, and I told a man with a bad leg the whole of what that would cost on the first day of this week in a room, and I have said the cost out loud twice this week and I am not going to say it a third time in a doorway, because it is the same cost and you have heard it and hearing it twice has not helped.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*Then what is at the other end of it.*”

“*A register with a line in it, and a clerk, and a sheet with wax on it and a name cut into a die at the foot of it,*” said Bern Raye, “*and the man whose name it is does not live in this city and has never been in it.*”

“*Is there a person at the other end of it.*”

“*There is a person at the other end of everything,*” said Bern Raye. “*That is what an office is. It is a person at the end of a road and a book, and the road is the one we are standing in, and I am going to walk up it on the day this man of thirty-eight goes out of the city.*”

“*Then I am not going to say anything about it.*”

“*Do not.*”

“*Then say what you did it for.*”

“*I filled a line off a paper in front of me,*” said Bern Raye. “*I have done that nine years and I have never once asked where a paper came from, and I told you the price of that on the first day of this week in a room with nineteen people in it, and I have not said it will not cost, and I have said it does.*”

The four bells went over Tallowgate. A man of twenty-nine walked out of a city of ninety thousand people with three sheets inside his coat and no form for it and nobody stopped him at the end of the row and nobody watched him go, and a woman of twenty-eight stood on her own step with a slate under her arm and four doors she had not been to, and the rail was in a yard, and the fever was eighteen weeks and two days old, and it did not stop for a clerk, and nobody on that step was thanked.

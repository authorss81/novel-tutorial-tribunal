# Chapter 438: A Man Writes A Return Of His Own In A Room Over A Saddler's, Puts A Number In The Margin, And Does Not Send It

He had a room over a saddler's at the middle of the nine bridges, and there was a bed and a table and a chair and a cup of tea that had gone cold in it about an hour before he sat down, and it was the sixth day of the eighty-fourth week. Shelf day three hundred and thirteen, his hundred and eighty-eighth morning, thirty-eight days after the settlement, the fever twenty-two weeks and one day, the rail down twenty-eight days.

He slept until the fourth hour and then he could not get back to it, and the leg was on the chair and the ear was doing what it does on a wet morning, and he had a sheet of the same good paper the press sells at a penny and he had bought four of them on the last day of last month for four pence of a wage that does not exist in this country.

The head of the form is printed, and he copied it fair by hand because he could not afford to have a clerk do it: *Persons and things reported to the office in respect of the Low Road, this city, and the bar lately upon it.*

He wrote his own name at the top of it.

Then he got out the cracked steel pen and put four lines in the margin, and it took him about a quarter of an hour, and twice he got up and walked to the window and came back.

The first line was what he is. Not of this place. Cannot be sent for, because there is no process in this county for sending for a person who is not of a place, and none has been invented since he arrived. Cannot be struck out, because the only form that takes a name out of a return is filled at the seat by a person of this place, and he is not one, and there is no other. Here thirty-eight days. Bad leg, which is not a finding. A room over a saddler's, and a table, and a chair, and a cup of tea.

The second line was what he has done. Four people in books in five weeks and three days, and he wrote the four out in the order he had done them and did not put a name to any of them.

A name in a sentence is a name in a book. A man who cannot be sent for is the only kind of man in this county who can describe a person of this place without being asked to, and that is not a thing to be improved on.

The third line was a figure.

He had had it since the second day of the eighty-second week, in a row of about twenty people, with the answer in his mouth and the taste of it staying with him all afternoon. Three weeks of boards, from a man who kept them and did not want them, and a division, and a good divisor, better than a guess, and it would have filled the office's count form in ten seconds and it would have been in four books by Friday and nobody in this city would ever have found out which part of it had come from a stranger with a pen.

He wrote it in the margin of his own return on the back of a sheet of paper that nobody had sent for.

He did not write it in the column. He wrote it in the margin, in a hand smaller than the hand on the rest of the page, and he did not put a name to it and he did not put a date to it, and it was the first number he had put anywhere in this country that had not come out of somebody else's mouth.

Then he sat with it for about ten minutes and did not tear it up.

The fourth line was why he was not going to send it, and he wrote that one last, and it is the only one of the four that is about this city.

He dated the sheet. The fourth day of the eighty-third week was the day it would have gone up in, and the day after that was the day it would actually have gone up in. He wrote the second of those.

Then he folded it in four and put it in the inside pocket of his coat, where a man who has been in this city thirty-eight days and has a name in a column four hundred miles off keeps the only paper he has that nobody has asked for.

---

About thirty people were in the long room at the foot of the hill at the change of the light. Hessa Marl had stood up in front of it on the previous day and said out loud that she was going to sit in a box on the north side of the ninth marker.

Nine hundred doors in a ward had heard a man refuse a number on a flat, and about nine hundred had heard a woman agree to be a box.

Ilyan got up off the window seat. He had a bad leg and a cold in his ear and a folded sheet in his coat, and he had been in this room nineteen times in a month, and he had asked it for a second half nine times, and he had been given one nine times.

“*I want to say a thing and then I am going to sit down,*” he said, “*and I am going to say it once, and I am not going to say it twice, and I would like it noticed that I have said that about myself and not about the office.*”

About thirty people waited, which is a thing that has happened in this room.

“*I have written a return of my own. It is on the form, and it has the head on it, and it has my name at the top of it, and it is in my coat, and I am not going to send it up the hill.*”

For a second nothing came, and then about nine people said something at once.

“*Stop,*” said Skell. “*Let him finish. He said he was going to say it once and he has said he is not saying it twice, and I am not going to be one of the nine. Go on.*”

“*There is a number on it,*” Ilyan said, “*and I am not going to say what the number is, and I want that to be a thing that is said in this room before anything else is said, because in four weeks in this city I have watched a figure go out of a mouth and into a book, and I have done it as well as anybody, and I am not going to do it again in a room.*”

“*Say the rest of it,*” said Fenna Rusk, from the second chair along, where she had been sitting since the second hour with a bare table behind her.

“*I have had the number since the second day of the eighty-second week,*” he said. “*I worked it out of three weeks of boards from a man who kept them and did not want them, and it is a good one, and it is better than a guess, and it would fill the office's count form in ten seconds. And I have not said it out loud in this room once in five weeks and three days, and there are about thirty people in here who have just found out that there is one.*”

The room changed.

“*Say the number,*” said somebody at the back.

“*No.*”

“*Say the number and let us have it, and there is a form in the press upstairs that wants it.*”

“*No,*” said Ilyan, “*and I said I was not going to say it twice and I have not, and that is twice I have said the word and it is going to stay at twice.*”

“*Why,*” said a voice near the door that he did not know, and it was a fair question and about four people in the room agreed with it out loud, and Ilyan felt the whole of the room turn over onto his side of it in under a second, the way a room does, and he had felt a room do that three times in a month in this city and had never once been on this side of it.

He sat down.

He sat down with a folded sheet in his coat that had a number in the margin of it, and about thirty people in a room at the foot of a hill wanted it, and he had made them want it, and he had made them want it by saying out loud in the only voice that room listens to that there was one.

---

Renn Calloway was at the back, against the wall, with his lamp out in his hand, because he had walked a mile and a half to be in a room and had not been sent for.

He did not ask for the number. He had asked for the number of doors on the Low Road three times in four days in the previous month and had been refused three times, and he had said on a step on the second day of the month that a roadkeeper says what he sees and not what it means, and he said now, once, in a voice the room could hear:

“*I am not going to ask you for it.*”

“*Say why,*” said Skell.

“*Because I have been asking this city for numbers since the seventy-ninth week and every time a person has given me one it has gone into something I did not choose, and I am fifty-eight and I have learned the one thing I know how to learn.*”

The room made a noise.

“*Then say the question you came for,*” said Fenna Rusk, “*because you do not come a mile and a half for nothing and everybody in this room knows it.*”

“*I came for the one question and it is the only one and it is about four things on the north side of that crossing and not about a number.*” He shifted his weight off the bad leg. “*There is a bar on it and there is a box on it and there is a woman in the box at the hour a cart comes in, and there is a plate on a post with nothing cut in the corner of it. That is four things on one side of a road in a mile and a half, and every one of them is the office's, and I keep the road, and I am not on any of them.*”

“*Say the question,*” Ilyan said.

“*The question is this. If a road has four things of the office standing on it and a person in a box in front of them, and every one of those four can be taken away by a man in a town four hundred miles off who has never been to it, then what is the man who keeps the road for?*”

Nobody in that room had an answer and about nine people said so out loud, and Skell said it was not a question anybody could be helped with, and Ilyan said nothing at all for about a minute and a half, and then he said one thing and it was not the answer to the question.

“*The question is the wrong one,*” he said. “*It has been the wrong one for a month. Everybody in this room has been asking who owns a bar, and the bar is not the thing. The thing is that four hundred miles of country has one hand in it, and that hand can put timber across a road on a Tuesday and take it away on a Wednesday, and there is nobody in ninety thousand people on this side of it who can be asked about either the putting or the taking.*”

“*Then say what is the right question,*” said Calloway.

“*I do not know,*” Ilyan said, “*and I have been in this city thirty-eight days and I have not got it, and I am the man with the return in his coat, and I have had thirty seconds to think and I have not got it.*”

---

The sheet was still in his coat at the last of the light.

“*Say the last of it,*” said Skell, “*because there is a man with a pencil in this room and about nine of us, and I have said it every time this month and I am not going to stop now.*”

Ferrow had the pencil out and had not looked down at it.

“*Say what you are writing,*” Ilyan said, and heard himself ask it, and knew that he was asking a man with a pencil to write down a thing about a folded sheet in his own coat.

“*That a person not of this city has said in a room, in about thirty persons' hearing, that he has written a return of his own on the office's form, with his name at the top of it, and that there is a number in the margin of it, and that he has declined to give the number, and that the sheet is not sent,*” said Ferrow.

“*Say the rest of it.*”

“*And that he has not said the number twice, and that I do not have it, and that I did not hear it,*” said Ferrow.

“*And the rest of that.*”

“*I would like the room to know that I did not hear it, because I am the man they ask. There is a version of this in which I am the man who has a number and is being careful with it, and I have thought about that version for four minutes. I would like it written down that I thought about it and that the number is not in my book.*”

Nobody said anything.

Ilyan put his hand flat on the inside of his coat where the sheet was, and about thirty people watched him do it.

About nine of them were still waiting for a number. He had put thirty people into a position where they had something of his and did not know what it was. The only thing he had left in this city that nobody could be asked about was a number in a margin, and he had just told a room it was there.

“*Then say the rest of it out loud and go home,*” said Fenna Rusk.

“*I am going to say one sentence and then I am going to go home,*” Ilyan said. “*I am not sending it because if it goes up the hill it is a number the office has, and the office does not check a number, and then this city has two numbers of persons in it and neither of them is the right one and one of them came out of a mouth that cannot say no.*”

He stopped there.

“*Say the rest of that,*” said Fenna Rusk.

“*That is the whole of it and I said one sentence and I am not going to say it twice.*”

Calloway put his hand on the bad leg and looked at him for a while.

“*Then I will tell you what I do not understand,*” he said, “*and then I am going home and I am not going to say it twice either, and I have said in this room that a roadkeeper says what he sees.*”

“*Say it.*”

“*You have got a number that would fill a form, and you have told thirty people about it, and you have told us you are not sending it, and the form upstairs in the press still wants it, and the office is still going to ask this city for it, and there is not one person in this room who can fill that form and go on working in this city afterwards.*”

He waited.

“*So the number is the only thing in this city that might have been any use to anybody, and you have taken it out of the room, and I cannot work out what a person does with a number they have made everybody want.*”

He went home, and there was a bad road to walk and he walked it, and about thirty people stood in a room and did not understand a man with a leg and a sheet in his coat, and one of them said *sixteenth* under her breath, and another said *two*, and neither of them said it twice.

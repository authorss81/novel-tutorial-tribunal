# Chapter 429: A Return With One Column And Two Things Down It, And The Office's Own Word At The End Of The Line

Fourth day of the eighty-third week. Shelf day three hundred and four, his hundred and seventy-ninth morning, twenty-nine days after the settlement, the fever twenty weeks and six days old, the rail down nineteen days.

Tam Ferrow wrote the return fair in the press room on the ground floor, with the door shut, and his own tray on the table, because it was the table he had been used to for three years.

The head of it was printed and it read: *Persons and things reported to the office in respect of the Low Road, this city, and the bar lately upon it.*

Under the head there was one column ruled down the middle of a good sheet. The sheet was good and the ruling was straight and the ink was good, and it cost a penny a page, and it could be read standing up, and anybody in this city could be shown it for a penny.

He put two things down it.

The first was: *A clerk of the ground floor, twenty-nine years old, three years on that floor, who has filled this line in.*

The second was: *A person conveyed to the ninth marker on the seventh day of the eighty-second week at the fourth hour, and returned at the last of the light, the same.*

He looked at the two of them for a while. Then he copied the sheet fair into a second one, and put the second in the press, and read both of them out in the public room at the foot of the hill at the third hour, in about thirty people's hearing. It took him four minutes and he did not apologise for any part of it.

Hessa Marl was in the room and had not been asked and had come for nothing.

“*Say that again,*” said Skell, “*the first one, and then the second one, and then tell this room which of the two of them is a man.*”

Ferrow read them both again.

Bevin Tarr said *huh* out loud at the end of the long table, which was not a word he had said in a room since the second day of the week, and he did not apologise for it either. He had come up the mile and a half with a plate under his arm, because he could not leave a plate, and nobody had sent for him.

“*Then tell this room what you have made of it,*” said Ilyan.

“*I have found a sheet with one column in it and I have filled it in, because that is the form and I fill lines off a paper in front of me.*” Ferrow kept his hands flat on the table.

“*And the head of it says persons and things, and there is no second column, and I have put a man in it and I have put a person in it who is not a man, because a person conveyed out of a city and returned the same is a thing, the way a marker is a thing.*”

“*Say the rest of that,*” said Ilyan.

“*The office does not pay for the distinction and does not require it, and I am not going to be the man who decides which of these two is which in a column with one line in it.*”

“*And the rest of it,*” said Skell.

“*The rest of it is that the office will get this on the fourth day of the week, and it will have a man of twenty-nine on it and a woman of twenty-eight on it.*” He stopped. “*I put my own name on the first line because on the first day of the week I said out loud in that room that it is going to be me they ask, because I am the one who filled it in. And I have had nine days to think about it and I have got nothing better, and I am going to do the ordinary thing, and nobody has thanked me for one thing in three years.*”

Hessa Marl had her slate under her arm and had not said anything since the sheet was read out the first time.

“*Then say the other half of the sheet,*” said Skell, “*the one with a woman on it. I have asked in four rooms this month for the second half of a thing, and this is the one I have not got.*”

She came about four feet up the room and stopped, and did not come to the table.

“*The second half is that a person who goes out of a city and comes back into it the same is a thing,*” she said. “*And a person who goes out of a city and does not come back the same is a different thing. And this column has room for the first one and not for the second one, and the office has not asked which of them I am, and I have not been asked, and I am the one person in this room who would know, and I am not going to stand here and tell four hundred miles what I am.*”

“*Say the rest of it,*” said Ilyan.

She put her hand flat on the end of the long table.

“*The difference between this and being at the top of a page is not a small one, and I am the only person in this city who can feel it, and I am not going to be able to explain it to anybody in a room four hundred miles off. At the top of a page I am a person, and the page has a name on it, and my name on it, and a clerk wrote it off a paper in the ordinary way.*”

She stopped, and looked at the middle of the room, and did not go on for a moment.

“*On that sheet I am a person conveyed and returned, the same, and the word at the end of the line is the office's and not mine, and it is the only word on that line that anybody is sure of.*”

About thirty people who had come up that hill did not look at her, and there is no form in this county that says a person must be looked at.

“*Say the rest of it,*” said Skell.

“*The rest of it is that a man stood in this room two days ago and did a division out loud and said that it decides what a person is, and nobody stopped him, and I was in that room. And the office has printed a price for a visit and a price for a day and a price for a year of being taken out of a city, and has not said which of them is a person.*”

She took her hand off the table.

“*And now there is a sheet with one column in it and a woman on the sheet is the same when she comes back, and that is the same office saying the same thing a different way, and this one is written down and the other one was in a room.*”

“*What do you want done about it,*” said Ilyan.

It was the first direct question he had put to her in a room since the first day of the week, and he heard it land on her before he heard himself finish it.

“*I cannot say no,*” she said, “*and I have not said no, and I am not going to say no now. And I would like the man with the pencil to write the same down in the second line, because it is the only word in that line that is true, and I have not got the right to change it and neither has he.*”

Ferrow wrote it. He did not look down while he wrote it, and he did not say what he had written, and when Ilyan asked him he said it was one word, and the word was *the same*, and that a person of this place had asked him to in a room.

“*And why,*” said Ilyan.

“*Because I fill lines off a paper in front of me, and there was no paper in front of me with a reason on it, and I am not going to make one up.*”

“*Then put your name on it,*” Ilyan said, before he knew he was going to say it. “*You are the one they ask. You said so yourself, four days ago, in that room. Put your name under hers and it is a thing in two lines instead of a rumour in one, and I will put mine under that, and the office will have to answer all three of us instead of one.*”

Ferrow looked at the sheet for a long moment. The column was ruled once down the middle of a good sheet and the ruling was straight, and he could not have put a third thing in that column without writing into the margin, and the margin was where the price of the page was.

“*There is no room,*” he said.

“*Write in the margin.*”

“*Then it is a sheet with a column and a margin, and the office will read the column and take the margin as an opinion.*” He squared the copy. “*I have said out loud in that room that it is going to be me they ask, and I have nine days of thinking behind it, and I would be a poor clerk if I put three names in one column on the morning after I said that.*”

Ilyan took the pen out of his coat and looked at it for a moment, and then put it back.

It was not a refusal, and Ferrow was not wrong, and both of those were the same fact. A man had learned to count, in nine days, that the strongest thing he could do with a piece of paper was to fill in more of it, and there was a woman in the room who had spent eleven years making sure the strongest thing she could do was to refuse to.

“*Write the two,*” Ilyan said. “*And leave the margin empty, and let them find the space themselves.*”

Ferrow did that, and read the two lines out again, and neither of them was in the margin.

The sheet went up the hill at the fourth hour in the coat of a man of twenty-nine, and Ott Dray took it, and he also took the roll, in oilcloth, out from under a table in a room off the low ground where it had been for eleven years.

There is a word for what the office does with an original so conveyed. The word is *the ordinary way*, and the ordinary way is a man on a cart, and a man on a cart is paid by whoever sent it.

“*Say what pays that cart,*” said Skell, in the yard at the low end of Tallowgate, to about nine people. Dray had the rein in his hand and nobody had sent for anybody.

Ferrow said it, because he was the one who had put it in a coat and had done the ordinary thing.

“*There is no line on any form in the county for the conveyance of a paper,*” he said. “*There is a fifth row of a schedule that says a person may be conveyed at the charge of the office beyond the Shelf and paid one year's wage, and that is a person, and it is in a column with a head on it, and it is not a cart.*”

He looked at his own boots.

“*The office has said in its own words on a sheet of its own that it does not pay for the keeping of a count, and does not require a copy of one, and will return an original so conveyed in the ordinary way. And the ordinary way is a man on a cart, and a man on a cart is paid by whoever sent it, and whoever sent it is me. And I have four shillings a year that I have not had once in three years, and I am not going to say what a cart costs from four hundred miles, because I do not know.*”

“*Then say this in a room and not in a yard,*” said Hessa Marl, “*and I will say it, and I have been at the top of a page for a fortnight and this is the fourth thing of mine that has gone up a hill. And it is the first one that was not put there by a man who carried a paper about me into a building in the ordinary way.*”

The yard was quiet except for the horses.

“*A roll of doors goes up this hill this afternoon on a cart that nobody has paid for, and it comes back down it in about three weeks on the same cart, and the woman who kept it is in a room with a lamp on and a bare table. And the office has said it will return the original. And the office has been true about everything it has printed so far in this city, and the ordinary way is the ordinary way, and there is no form for it and there is not going to be one.*”

“*Say that in a room,*” said Ilyan, “*and I will stand in it.*”

“*You will not stand in it,*” she said. “*You will be asked about it, and I will be asked about you, and there is no form that asks you whether you agree to that.*”

That was the first time in a month she had said anything to him that was not a sentence, and he did not have an answer to it, and she got onto the step and left him standing in a yard with a cart and nine people.

The cart went east out of this city on the Low Road at about the fourth hour, with a return in a coat and a roll in oilcloth. It went past the weir head and out across the flat, and the four hurdles were still on the north side of the ninth marker, and Ott Dray went round them on the south side, which is four yards of mud, because a cart does not go round four hurdles and he went round them anyway, and he said nothing about it to anybody.

The fever was twenty weeks and six days old, and it did not stop for a return, and nobody in that yard was thanked.

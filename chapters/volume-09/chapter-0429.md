# Chapter 429: A Return With One Column And Two Things Down It, And The Second Of The Two Is A Woman And It Is Not A Name

The fourth day of the eighty-third week was his hundred and seventy-ninth morning and the twenty-ninth day after the settlement, and the fever was twenty weeks and six days old, and the rail had been down nineteen days, and the clerk of forty wrote a return fair in the press room on the ground floor with the door shut and his own tray on the table because it was the table he had been used to, and he was not in there long, and the head of it was printed and it read *persons and things reported to the office in respect of the Low Road, this city, and the bar lately upon it*, and under the head there was one column ruled down the middle of a good sheet, and the sheet was good and the ruling was straight and the ink was good, and it cost a penny a page, and it could be read standing up, and anybody in this city could be shown it for a penny.

He put two things down it.

The first was: *A clerk of the ground floor, twenty-nine years old, three years on that floor, who has filled this line in.*

The second was: *A person conveyed to the ninth marker on the seventh day of the eighty-second week at the fourth hour, and returned at the last of the light, the same.*

He looked at the two of them for a while. Then he copied the sheet fair into a second one, and put the second one in the press, and read both of them out in the public room at the foot of the hill at the third hour in about thirty people's hearing, and it took him four minutes, and he did not apologise for any part of it, and about thirty people were in the room and the woman of twenty-eight was one of them and had not been asked and had come for nothing.

“*Say that again,*” said the man of fifty-two, “*the first one, and then the second one, and then tell this room which of the two of them is a man.*”

The clerk of forty read them both again.

Nobody said anything at all, and the room sat with it, and Bevin Tarr at the end of the long table said *huh* out loud, which is not a word he has said in a room since the second day of this week, and he did not apologise for it either, and he had come up the mile and a half with a plate under his arm because he could not leave a plate, and nobody had sent for him.

“*Then tell this room what you have made of it,*” said the man with the bad ear.

“*I have found a sheet with one column in it and I have filled it in because that is the form and I fill lines off a paper in front of me. And the head of it says persons and things, and there is no second column, and I have put a man in it and I have put a person in it who is not a man because a person conveyed out of a city and returned the same is a thing the way a marker is a thing, and the office does not pay for the distinction and does not require it, and I am not going to be the man who decides which of these two is which in a column that has one line in it.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*And the rest of it,*” said the man of fifty-two.

“*The second half is that the office will get this on the fourth day of the eighty-second week and it will have a man of twenty-nine on it and a woman of twenty-eight on it, and I put my own name on the first line because on the first day of the eighty-second week I said out loud in that room that it is going to be me they ask, because I am the one who filled it in, and I have had nine days to think about it and I have got nothing better and I am going to do the ordinary thing, and nobody has thanked me for one thing in three years.*”

The woman of twenty-eight was at the end of the long table with her slate under her arm and she had not said anything since the sheet was read out the first time.

“*Then say the other half of the sheet,*” said the man of fifty-two, “*the one with a woman on it, and I have asked in four rooms this month for the second half of a thing and I have got most of them and this is the one I have not got.*”

The woman of twenty-eight came up the room. She came up about four feet and stopped and did not come to the table.

“*The second half is that a person who goes out of a city and comes back into it the same is a thing,*” she said. “*And a person who goes out of a city and does not come back the same is a different thing. And this column has got room for the first one and not for the second one, and the office has not asked which of them I am, and I have not been asked, and I am the one person in this room who would know and I am not going to stand here and tell four hundred miles what I am.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*And the difference between this and being at the top of a page is not a small one and I am the only person in this city who can feel it and I am not going to be able to explain it to anybody in a room four hundred miles off. At the top of a page I am a person, and the page has a name on it and my name on it, and a clerk wrote it off a paper in the ordinary way. On that sheet I am a person conveyed and returned, the same, and the word at the end of the line is the office's and not mine, and it is the only word on that line that anybody is sure of.*”

The clerk of forty had the pencil out and did not look down at it, and about thirty people who had come up that hill did not look at the woman of twenty-eight, and there is no form in this city that says a person must be looked at.

“*Then say the rest of it,*” said the man of fifty-two.

“*The rest of it is that a man of fifty-two stood in this room two days ago and did a division out loud and said that it decides what a person is, and nobody stopped him, and I was in that room. And the office has printed a price for a visit and a price for a day and a price for a year of being taken out of a city and has not said which of them is a person. And now there is a sheet with one column in it and a woman on the sheet is the same when she comes back, and that is the same office saying the same thing a different way, and this one is written down, and the other one was in a room.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*I cannot say no,*” she said, “*and I have not said no, and I am not going to say no now, and I would like the man with the pencil to write the same down in the second line because it is the only word in that line that is true and I have not got the right to change it and neither has he.*”

The clerk of forty wrote it down. He did not look down at it and he did not say what he wrote, and the man with the bad leg asked him and he said it was one word and the word was *the same* and that he had written it because a person of this place had asked him to in a room and he fills lines off a paper in front of him, and that there was no paper in front of him with a reason on it and that he was not going to make one up.

The sheet went up the hill at the fourth hour in the coat of a man of twenty-nine, and a man of fifty-four took it and a man of fifty-four took a roll in oilcloth that had been under a table off the low ground for eleven years, and there was a word for what the office does with an original so conveyed and it was *the ordinary way*, and the ordinary way is a man on a cart, and a man on a cart is paid by whoever sent it.

“*Say what pays the cart,*” said the man of fifty-two, in the yard, at the low end of Tallowgate, to about nine people, and the man of fifty-four was holding the rein and nobody had sent for anybody.

The clerk of forty said it, because he was the one who had put it in a coat and he had done the ordinary thing.

“*There is no line on any form in four hundred miles for the conveyance of a paper,*” said the clerk of forty. “*There is a fifth row of a schedule that says a person may be conveyed at the charge of the office beyond the Shelf and paid one year's wage, and that is a person, and it is in a column with a head on it, and it is not a cart. And the office has said in its own words on a sheet of its own that it does not pay for the keeping of a count and does not require a copy of one and will return an original so conveyed in the ordinary way, and the ordinary way is a man on a cart, and a man on a cart is paid by whoever sent it, and whoever sent it is me, and I have four shillings a year that I have not had once in three years and I am not going to say what a cart costs from four hundred miles because I do not know.*”

Nobody said anything, and the woman of twenty-eight said the one sentence that was hers.

“*Then say this in a room and not in a yard,*” she said, “*and I will say it and I have been at the top of a page for a fortnight and this is the fourth thing of mine that has gone up a hill and it is the first one that was not put there by a man who carried a paper about me into a building in the ordinary way. A roll of doors goes up this hill this afternoon on a cart that nobody has paid for and comes back down it in about three weeks on the same cart, and the woman who kept it is in a room with a lamp on and a bare table, and the office has said it will return the original, and the office has been true about everything it has printed so far in this city, and the ordinary way is the ordinary way and there is no form for it and there is not going to be one, and I would like that written down because it is the only sentence I have got and I am not going to be thanked for it.*”

The cart went east out of this city on the Low Road at about the fourth hour with a return in a coat and a roll in oilcloth, and it went past the weir head and out across the flat, and the four hurdles were still on the north side of the ninth marker, and the man of fifty-four went round them on the south side, which is four yards of mud, and a cart does not go round four hurdles and he went round them anyway and said nothing about it to anybody, and the roll went up the hill and the fever was twenty weeks and six days old, and it did not stop for a return, and nobody in that yard was thanked.

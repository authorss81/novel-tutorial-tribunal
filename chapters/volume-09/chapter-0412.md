# Chapter 412: A Ruled Table With One Line In It Between A Farthing And A Pound, And A Woman Who Reads A Sheet Out Loud Because There Is Nobody Else

The first day of the eighty-first week was his hundred and sixty-second morning and the twelfth day after the settlement, and the fever was eighteen weeks and three days old, and the rail had been down two days and the settlement had not been read out in the public room at the foot of the hill that morning for the first time in six weeks, because the man who read it out was on his own two legs somewhere between here and a city four hundred miles off with three sheets inside his coat.

A man came down the hill at the second hour with a sheet in a case, and the sheet was good and the ruling on it was straight and the ink was good, and he was thirty-eight and he said what he was before anybody asked him.

“*I am of the office at the seat and not of this city,*” he said. “*My name is Kerwin Dace and I have a post and I can be sent for, which is a thing that nobody in this ward can be, and I have been told that in two rooms since yesterday and I have not said anything about it in either of them because it is not news to me and I would rather it was not news to you either.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*Say that again for the people at the back,*” said the boy of sixteen, from the doorway.

“*No,*” said Kerwin Dace.

“*Say it again for the people at the back.*”

“*No,*” said Kerwin Dace, “*because there are nine people at the back and every one of them heard it and I am not a thing on a post in this city, and I would like to get a sheet read out in the next ten minutes because I have four of them to leave here and I have until the fourth day of the eighty-second week and I am not spending one of those on a name.*”

---

The sheet was a schedule. The head of it was printed and the head of it named a schedule of payments and provisions for the keeping of roads and the hearing of disputes concerning roads, and under the head it was ruled into three columns and there were five rows, and the office's man could read it.

“*Then read it out,*” said the man with the bad ear.

“*I can read it,*” said Kerwin Dace. “*I have it in front of me and I can read every line of it standing up, and I am not going to do a clerk's work in a room in a city where a clerk is on a cart, and I would like it understood that I am not doing that and that it is not a favour to anybody here and I am not asking for it to be noticed.*”

“*Then nobody in this room can read it,*” said the man of fifty-two, from the end of the table, “*and there is no form in four hundred miles that says a rule may be printed and not read, and the man at the wheel would be the man to read it and he is a mile and a half down this row and he is not here.*”

“*I will read it,*” said the woman of sixty with a shawl.

She got up off the wall, which she does not do.

“*I will read it if it is read in this room and not in a corridor,*” she said. “*I have been in this city nine years and I have never read anything out in a room in it, and I have read a sheet on a post twice to myself at the end of a row because I do not take a thing on somebody's word, and I have told a woman of twenty-eight what a sheet said and she has said no to it, and I am not doing this for the office and I am not doing it because a clerk is on a cart. I am doing it because there are nine hundred doors in this ward and this is the first thing any of us have been handed that we could hold at arm's length and read in the light, and if the only person in this room who will put her hand on it is a person of the seat, then in four hundred miles it will go into a book that nobody in this city will ever read out loud in a room, and the thing will be in a register and there will be nobody here who has heard it said.*”

“*Read the first row,*” said the man of fifty-two, “*and read it as it is printed and not as you would say it.*”

---

“*One. A person of the place who goes to a door on the account of the office is paid at the figure paid to a keeper of a roll, and the payment is for the visit and not for the name.*”

“*That is the farthing,*” said the woman with the shawl, “*and the man at the wheel worked it out loud in this room seven days ago and said it twice, and I was in this room for it and I am not going to work it again because he worked it and he is the man who gives the figures in this city and he has given every one of them out loud with the working in the sentence for thirty-one years and he has never been thanked for one of them.*”

“*Read the second row.*”

“*Two. A person appointed in the register of disputes concerning roads is paid a pound a day, and the days are counted as this office counts them, and the payment begins on the day the appointment is entered and does not cease on the day the register is closed.*”

Nobody said anything, and Bevin Tarr had come up the mile and a half with a plate under his arm, because he could not leave a plate, and he stood at the end of the long table and nobody had sent for him and nobody thanked him.

“*Say the working,*” said the man with the bad leg.

“*A pound is twenty shillings,*” said the man at the wheel. “*Twenty shillings is two hundred and forty pence. A cart in this city is eighteen pence a day, and two hundred and forty over eighteen is thirteen, and a bit over thirteen, so that is thirteen carts a day and a bit. And a boy of eleven at the end of this row carries water for a penny at the end of the week, and every person in this room knows that figure, because he is a person of this ward and he is the head of his own house. So a pound a day is two hundred and forty weeks of what he earns in a week carrying other people's water.*”

“*And the farthing.*”

“*A keeper of a roll is paid four shillings a year, which is forty-eight pence, and forty-eight pence over three hundred and sixty-four days is a shade over half a farthing a day, and a farthing is very nearly twice that,*” said the man at the wheel, “*and I have said about a farthing out loud in this city for thirty-one years and about a farthing is the right size of it and a farthing is not, and that is the first row of that sheet, and a pound a day is the second row, and there is one ruled line between the two rows and the office wrote both of them and ruled the line itself.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*Say the other half of that,*” said the man of fifty-two.

“*The other half is that I have been in this city thirty-one years and I have never seen a rate posted and I have never seen two rates on one sheet, and if a person of this ward went to the second row of that schedule they would be paid a thousand eight hundred and twenty times what the first row is worth, and that is five times three hundred and sixty-four, and I have cut four thousand lines and I have never been paid a farthing for any of them, and I am not going to say what a person ought to do about that in a room, because a person of this ward is not a person the schedule is about and I would like that said in here and I would like it said by somebody who is not me.*”

---

“*Read the third row,*” said Kerwin Dace, “*because the third row is the one I have been asked about most and I have been asked about it nowhere, and I would like it read.*”

“*Three. The office will pay the costs of the settlement of the eighth week, being four shillings the hundred for three hundred names, and will pay them to a person of the place on the giving of the sum, and the sum has not been given and the office notes it and does not press for it.*”

“*Say the second half of that,*” said the man of fifty-two.

And the woman with the shawl said it, and she said it standing where she was, in her own mouth, and nobody had asked her, and there were about thirty people in that room and two of them could have said no and neither of them did.

“*The line for the three hundred is not on that sheet,*” she said, “*and the line for the three hundred is not on the bill on that wall either, and the line for the three hundred is blank, and the blank is the correct figure, and I have read that bill four times this week and there is no other figure on it and there is not going to be one.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*And I am going to say the rest of it because a man asked me for the second half and I have got one, and I am saying it once, and I am saying it in a room and not at a gate because a gate is how a thing goes into four mouths and a room is how a thing is heard once.*”

“*Say it.*”

“*A sum does not look anybody up.*” She had her hand flat on the edge of the table and she was not looking at anybody. “*A settlement in this city is reached by being in a room at the second hour on the day it is read, and I have been in that room and so have most of you, and there is a woman of sixty-eight in a bed at the top of this city whose name is at a shut door in a row above the plate yard's, and she is not going to be in that room on any day this year, and if a person in this city sits down and writes a review of that order and asks the ward whether it agrees, then the answer on the paper will be whatever the people in the room say, and she will be counted in it anyway. That has been the shape of it since the summer. And this office has read the order and read the review and read the second return and answered it in four lines, and I am not saying it has not read them, because it plainly has. I am saying that a rule which can be read standing up does not reach a woman in a bed at the top of a city, it gives her a better copy of the same question, and she is not in the room where the question is answered, and she never has been.*”

The pencil did not move for that one, and she went on standing where she was with her hand flat on the edge of the table.

“*And the four shillings the hundred will be paid,*” she said, “*and it will be paid into a hand, and the hand will be a hand, and the three hundred will be the same three hundred they were in the summer, and that is not a thing anybody is doing to anybody. It is what a sum is. A sum is the only thing in this city that cannot be asked a question and I have known that for nine years and I have never had a sheet in my hand that said it.*”

Nobody said anything. Kerwin Dace wrote in a book with a pencil, which he did without looking down at it, and he did not say anything while he did it.

“*Say whether you have written that down,*” said the man with the bad ear.

“*I have written that a person of this ward read the third row and made a statement about it,*” said Kerwin Dace. “*I have not written her name, because the head of my book does not have a line for the name of a person who reads.*”

“*There is a line for the name of a person who reads,*” said the man of fifty-two, “*and it is at the top of a form in a building at the middle of the nine bridges, and it is for the name of the person asking, and a clerk of thirty filled one in on the seventh day of the eightieth week off a paper a man of that ward carried in, and I have not said the name and I am not going to, and I would like it understood that I have not said it and not that I do not know it.*”

Nobody said anything, and the woman with the shawl put her hand off the table.

“*Then that is the cost of it and I am not going to be thanked for it,*” she said, “*and I am not going to be relieved and I am not going to be told in a month that I was right to read it, because I have read a sheet out loud in a room in a city I have lived in nine years and I did not know that I was a person who did that until this morning, and now there is a man with a book four hundred miles off who knows that this city has me in it, and I have not asked him not to write it and I could not have asked him, because I stood up.*”

---

“*And the foot of it,*” said Kerwin Dace, and he read that himself, in his own voice, which is not a flat voice, and nobody stopped him.

“*And a note at the foot of it, under the lines. Where a return has been made by a magistrate against a settlement which the magistrate is bound by, that return is entered as a return made, and does not suspend the settlement, and does not require the office to do any further thing, and the office has had it read. And under the note, on the same paper, in the same hand: a person entered in a book of the office who does not wish to remain entered may apply to be struck out, and there is a form for it at the seat, and the striking out is done by the office and not by the person.*”

He shut the case.

“*That is the whole of it,*” he said, “*and I have read the foot of that sheet myself because I have read it at nine tables since the day I came and I am tired of it.*”

“*Say what that note is,*” said the man of fifty-two.

“*It is the answer to the second return,*” said Kerwin Dace. “*The magistrate wrote an objection against a settlement she is bound by. The office has read it. The office has had it read to it twice. And the office's answer is that an objection to a thing you are bound by is a return made, and a return made is entered, and being entered is the whole of what happens to it.*”

“*Then say this,*” said Tarin Keel, from the wall. “*I have been in this room twelve days waiting for somebody from that office to be wrong in a way I could put my hand on. And it has read a woman's objection and answered it in four lines, and the answer is correct, and the woman is a magistrate and the objection is hers and it is not a bad one, and he has read it and it has not changed his mind by one line, and I am not going to stand here and tell this room that a government which reads what you write to it is a government which has not heard you. It has heard you. I have wanted somebody to come down that hill and be a fool for nine days and it has not happened and I would like it noted that it has not happened.*”

Nobody thanked him for it.

The settlement was not read out in the room at the foot of the hill again that morning, and a man of thirty-eight went down the hill with a case and nine hundred doors in this ward had a rule about them that a person of sixty could read at arm's length, and the fever was eighteen weeks and three days old, and it did not stop for a schedule, and nobody in that room was thanked.

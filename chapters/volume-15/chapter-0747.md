# Chapter 747: Seventh And Last Day Of The Hundred And Twenty-Eighth Week, And A Monday, And The Water Is About Four Inches Over The Top Step, And A Woman Of Forty-Four Says Out Loud In About Nine People’s Hearing That There Is No Rule In This County That Says A Thing Which Cannot Be Asked Can Stop Being Asked About

Seventh and last day of the hundred and twenty-eighth week. His four hundred and ninety-seventh morning. Three hundred and forty-seven days after the settlement. The fever sixty-six weeks and two days. Three hundred and one days since the division. Two hundred and forty-seven days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

The water was about four inches over the top step at the seventh hour and it was the first morning of it. The man of forty-three gave the reading and said the working, and he also gave the figure for the next time about ninety steps can be walked, and nobody had asked him for it.

Corvin Tace is forty-three and has stood on that landing every morning for nineteen years. He has said he had nothing to say about a night five times and then stopped, and he has given two figures for a night since, and neither of those two is his. He is not going to give a fourth thing out of the three he gives every morning.

“*About four inches over the top step at the seventh hour, and it is the first morning of it.*”

“*One day. It was off all ninety yesterday at about the sixth hour and that is the working.*”

“*And the next time about ninety steps can be walked is in seven days from this morning, and that is the Monday of the hundred and twenty-ninth week.*”

“*It came off on a Tuesday, and a Wednesday, and a Thursday, and a Friday, and a Saturday, and a Sunday, and I am not going to say the seventh.*”

“*I am not going to be thanked for the three things.*”

“*About four of you have noticed that I said five sentences this morning and that is one more than I usually say. I am not going to say the extra one out loud.*”

Then a man of fifty-four had the ninth line read to him in the open before anything else happened, and it was the twenty-sixth morning.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four and holds the gravel at the head of that drain and cannot read a word of the paper on that counter. He has asked for the ninth line in the open every morning since the Thursday of the hundred and twenty-fifth week. He asked to be in that room once, was given nothing, asked again once, and did not ask again. The fifth thing asked of him on a Sunday of a fortnight is still not answered and nobody has asked him about it.

“*Twenty-six mornings and I am standing on a bank on the first day of a week and I have not varied the asking by one word.*”

“*And the sheet is on that counter with three columns on it and nothing in any of them. It has been there three mornings.*”

“*About nine of you have looked at it and about four of you have put a hand near it.*”

“*I cannot read any of it. I am telling you what I have seen and I have seen a man of thirty-eight not touch it and a man of thirty-one not touch it and a boy of thirteen not touch it.*”

“*The fifth thing is still not answered and nobody has asked me and I am still waiting, and I am not going to stop waiting because a week has turned over.*”

“*Nobody is going to be thanked for the twenty-six and nobody is going to be told I was brave about any part of it.*”

Then the man of thirty-eight read the ninth line out loud in the open, and read the fourteen names after it, and read the eleven words at the top of the third column. It took about nine minutes, and the sheet went back on that counter where anybody could put a hand on it.

Garrin Tolley is thirty-eight and has a child of nine, and is the only person on that bank who can put a light on a thing in the dark. He has read that sheet thirty-seven times in this county. He has been refused a figure for how long three times, asked for a fourth, and will not ask a fifth. He stopped deciding at the Sunday of the hundred and twenty-seventh week.

“*Thirty-seven is seven hundred and forty-seven less seven hundred and ten, and there has not been one of the thirty-seven in the dark.*”

“*That thing has been on that counter three mornings and there is no column on it for a person stopping and nobody has put anything in any of the three, and I am not going to say that means something.*”

“*A man of fifty-four who cannot read has just told nine people what he has seen three mornings at a thing he cannot read. His figure for it is a figure of hands and not of columns, and that is better than mine.*”

“*I have a child of nine and about nine minutes a morning and I am not going to say which of those two is the one I would lose.*”

Then a woman of forty-four said it out loud, in the open, on that bank, at about the seventh hour, in about nine people’s hearing, and she gave the reason in the last sentence, and nobody was going to be thanked for it.

Orla Fennimore is forty-four and has kept a book of twenty-nine chairs at thirteen shillings and fourpence a year for nineteen years. There are nine of those chairs and not ten, and she is not a keeper of anything anybody can be sent for about. She said on the Friday of the hundred and twenty-seventh week that a shut door is not the right of refusing.

“*I am going to say a sentence and I am going to give the reason last, and about four of you have been waiting a fortnight for me to say something on this bank that is not about a door.*”

“*There is no rule in this county that says a thing which cannot be asked can stop being asked about.*”

“*A man of thirty-four in an ash has been there a hundred and seventeen days and he cannot be asked anything.*”

“*And there is no rule that says he cannot be asked anything either, and both of those have been true for about a hundred and seventeen days.*”

“*A man of fifty-four cannot read and there is no rule that says a man who cannot read may not be asked to fill in a column.*”

“*And I keep twenty-nine chairs in a book and there are nine of them and not ten, and there is no rule in this county that says the ninth of the nine has to be somebody.*”

“*The reason is last and it is mine. It is that every one of those three has been true at the same time as its opposite for about nineteen years and nobody in this county has ever been given one of the two.*”

“*Nobody is going to be thanked for that sentence and nobody is going to be told I am right about it.*”

“*I have said it in the open on a Tuesday morning and about four of you can tell in four days that I did not say it in a book.*”

About nine of them had a woman of forty-four who had said a sentence and given the reason last. About four of them had a man of fifty-four who could not read what was on that counter and had said so.

Then a man of thirty-one said the true of it in his own mouth, and gave the working, and said nobody was going to be told which half of it was his.

Ilyan Vester is thirty-one and is nobody’s, and has been in this county a hundred and ninety-three days. A hundred and ninety-three is seven hundred and forty-seven less five hundred and fifty-four. On Friday he said the word amendment out loud in about nine people’s hearing and said he was not going to fill in any of the three columns.

“*A hundred and ninety-three is seven hundred and forty-seven less five hundred and fifty-four, and I have been in this county a hundred and ninety-three days, and nobody has asked me for anything in one of them.*”

“*She is right about all of that and I am not going to take one of it back.*”

“*I am not going to say it a second time, because a woman of twenty-eight told me on the Wednesday of the hundred and twenty-seventh week that a second time is a rule.*”

“*Here is what I want and it is smaller than the one I said on the first morning of last week and I am not going to pretend it is not smaller.*”

“*I want that sheet to still be on that counter in four weeks with nothing in any of the three columns and no fourth column on it.*”

“*I am not going to say it a second time this fortnight.*”

“*About four of you are going to work out that a want which is not said twice is a rule wearing a coat, and that is the correct way round.*”

“*A woman of forty-four said on the second day of the hundred and twenty-seventh week that a column which gets asked once is not a column, it is a favour, using a man of forty-one’s words. I have not said that sentence out loud this morning and I am not going to improve on it.*”

“*And nobody is going to be relieved about this morning and nobody is going to be thanked, and about four of you are going to be relieved in about four days and I would rather it was nothing at all.*”

“*And none of this is an answer to anything that was asked on a Wednesday forty-seven days ago.*”

“*Forty-seven is seven hundred and forty-seven less seven hundred. I am not going to let it be one and nobody is going to answer it for me.*”

Then a man of forty-four said what a small want is, in four sentences, and refused a fifth, and it was about a cart road and not about a bank.

Hester Ollerton is forty-four and has kept a bakehouse four miles up that cart road, and about nine houses in that village take her bread. She has been asked four times and answered twice. On the Wednesday of the hundred and twenty-seventh week she came down four miles to say she had not answered the fourth thing, and she has not answered it since.

“*I have come down four miles on a Tuesday morning and I am not going to be thanked for four miles and nobody is going to be told it was worth the walk.*”

“*There is a thing on a counter in this county with three columns on it and it has been there three mornings and nobody has put anything in any of them.*”

“*If that thing comes up four miles of that cart road to my own door it will be a fourth man in a fourth coat and it will not be a man.*”

“*I have had three men in three coats with nine words each and I am not going to make a count of four.*”

“*And I have not answered the fourth thing and I said on the Wednesday of last week that I was not going to answer it that week.*”

“*It is a week and a half now and I am not going to answer it this one either.*”

“*The fifth is not coming and I am not going to say it out loud on a bank four miles from my own door.*”

“*About four of you have noticed that I have said four sentences about a thing four miles off. I am not going to be thanked for the counting.*”

About four of them had a woman of forty-four who had come down four miles and had four sentences and had refused a fifth. About nine of them had a man of thirty-four in an ash who is a hundred and seventeen days into a thing that cannot be asked and that cannot be stopped being asked about.

Then a boy of thirteen gave three counts back and a fourth, and the fourth was the first one anybody had said out loud that week.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, and his mother is forty-one and has two carts and neither of them can be sent for about. He has been keeping four counts since the Monday before last and none of them has been asked for.

“*Forty-six days. Forty-six is seven hundred and forty-seven less seven hundred and one.*”

“*And twenty-six mornings, and a hundred and seventeen days for a man in an ash, and a hundred and eighteen for a woman in a reed.*”

“*And here is the fourth one and I have not said it for a week and I am saying it now because nobody told me not to this week.*”

“*Three mornings that sheet has been on that counter with nothing in it. Three is three and I counted them and nobody else counted them.*”

“*And a man of sixty-one told me on Wednesday not to do that again and it has been a week.*”

“*I am not going to thank him for that and I am not going to be told I was brave about saying a figure of three.*”

“*Nobody is going to be thanked for carrying four. I am going to carry five tomorrow.*”

Nevin Trask is sixty-one, and has kept a book of heads and carts in this county for eleven years, and has written one number in it in eleven years. There are five lines on the leaf after the last head and a number is the last of the five. He stopped a count of days himself on the Wednesday of the hundred and fifteenth week and has refused the figure of it out loud ever since.

“*Five sentences and the fifth one is the reason, and I am going to say them before the count is a count of anything else.*”

“*One: three mornings is a figure of mornings and not a figure of anybody, and about four of you have made it one in the last ten minutes.*”

“*Two: a figure of mornings in a boy of thirteen’s mouth is a different thing from a figure of mornings in a man of sixty-one’s mouth and both of them are the same three.*”

“*Three: I said on Wednesday that I was not going to tell him to stop and I was not going to tell him to go on. I have not, and about four of you have been waiting nine days for me to change my mind.*”

“*Four: and I have not changed my mind and I am not going to say why, and that is the same sentence as the fourth one on Wednesday and I have said it twice now.*”

“*Five: a boy of thirteen is going to carry five tomorrow, and about four of you know what happens on Thursday and about nine of you do not, and that is the correct way round.*”

About nine of them stood on that bank in the wind with four inches of water under the top step and a sheet of printed paper on that counter that nobody had touched in three mornings. Nobody filled anything in. Nobody carried it anywhere.

---

The fever was sixty-six weeks and two days old. He had been in that county a hundred and ninety-three days.

A woman of forty-four said out loud in about nine people’s hearing that there is no rule in this county that says a thing which cannot be asked can stop being asked about, and gave the reason last.

For a man in an ash, for a man who cannot read, and for the ninth of nine chairs in a book kept nineteen years, each of those has been true at the same time as its opposite for about nineteen years. Nobody in that county has ever been given one of the two.

And a boy of thirteen said a figure of three out loud for the first time in a week, having been told on the Wednesday not to. A man of sixty-one did not tell him to stop and did not tell him to go on, and said that he had now said the same sentence twice.

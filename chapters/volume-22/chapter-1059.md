# Chapter 1059: A Working Turned Over

Four inches came up that bank in the night from the bottom, the way it always comes, and by the light the top step had a brown lip on it and about ninety of those cut steps stood in a thin sheet that a man could wade without noticing, and the grit that had been lying loose on the whole flight since the water went was afloat and moving, and a skin of it had already gone down the lane and banked at the bend where the lane turns, which is the one place a boy carrying two full pails cannot get past.

The man with the broom came up and saw the banked grit from the top and said one sentence about it and went to work, which is how that landing deals with most things.

Fourth day of the hundred and seventy-third week, and it is the Friday of that week. His eight hundred and ninth morning. Six hundred and fifty-nine days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and ten weeks and six days old. Six hundred and thirteen days since the division. Five hundred and fifty-nine days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and five hundred and five days in this county of Kell.

Four inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, about ninety of those ninety steps under, and seven days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and then gave the banked grit in the lane in his own words, because it is on his stone and it is going to be on his stone at the bottom of it.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the fourth day of the hundred and seventy-third week that about a cartload of grit had gone down that lane overnight and banked at the turn, and that he was not going to send a man after it because the lane was not his stone and he did not know who held it.

“Four at the seventh hour, about ninety of those ninety steps under, and seven days of the coming back, on the fourth day of the hundred and seventy-third week, and it is the Friday of that week. Three figures, and there is a cartload of my grit at the bottom of that lane and I do not hold the lane.”

He worked the sheet from the bottom upward.

“Go and do your gravel. It is the Friday of the hundred and seventy-third week and the water is coming, and the stone has nothing else for anybody.”

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow, and asked in the same words at the same hour, and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and set the wheel down in the shallows and put his hands flat on the wall.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and thirty-eighth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and fifty-nine less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and thirty-eighth. One thousand and fifty-nine less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

He stood with his hands on the wall and looked down the lane at the grit banking at the turn.

“That is your grit going down there and it is not mine, and I am not going to say the word mine about anything this morning because a man who says it once starts saying it about other things.” He took his hands off. “Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.”

Nothing came back and he let it stand without giving it a sentence this morning.

Then the man of thirty-eight came up that cart road at about the eighth hour and said what he had come up to say, and it was the first thing on that landing in a fortnight that had been addressed to a man of thirty-one.

Garrin Tolley is thirty-eight, with a board of thirty-two notches cut in the edge of it inside his coat, who has been refused a bench, refused a going, refused a barrow and refused a question of his own making inside a fortnight, and who said on the fourth day of the hundred and seventy-third week that there was grit banked at the turn of the lane and a boy of thirteen with two full pails, and that he wanted a second pair of hands for it.

“Come down the lane with me.” He said it to Ilyan and not to the landing. “There is a cartload of grit at the turn and it has to come up before the water is deep enough to float it round to the bottom. It is two yoke-loads of work and one yoke, and I have the yoke. I am not asking you to carry it. I am asking you to stand at the other end of it while I lever it.”

Ilyan Vester was already on his feet when he got to the bottom of that flight, and he had not thought about it, and he said so afterwards.

“Yes.” He said it before he had looked at anything. “I will come and stand at the other end.”

Corvin Tace said nothing at all from the top of the steps, and then, after a while, from the top of the steps, without raising his voice over the water:

“You do not go off my stone while the water is coming back and ninety steps of that flight want doing by one man.” He said it plainly and he went on working while he said it. “And here is the reason, because he asked and not the landing. If you go down that lane I have got a stone with a man on it and a lane with a man in it, and I answer for the stone. A man off my stone is a man I cannot see what he is doing, and I do not want the morning to be like that.”

Ilyan Vester stopped where he was and said yes, that was right, and came back up to the third step from the top, and stood there.

And then, a quarter of an hour later, on the top step with the water at his boots, Ilyan Vester said to Garrin Tolley:

“You cannot go down that lane either.”

Garrin Tolley turned round. He had the yoke in his hands and the grit banking at the turn in front of him and a boy who would need the lane in an hour.

“Because he has just said it.” Ilyan’s voice was not loud and it was not unkind. “A man on a stone is a man somebody can answer for, and a man off it is a man nobody can answer for. That is his working and he is right about it, and by the same working you are going down there out of everybody’s sight with a boy’s yoke and a cartload of grit, and if it goes wrong nobody on this landing can say a word about it.”

Garrin Tolley stood at the turn of the lane with the yoke across his shoulders and did not move for a moment, and then he took it off.

“That is his working.” He said it slowly, as though he were checking it against something. “That is his working about his own stone, put in my mouth and turned over, and I have been listening to working all week on that landing and I have never heard one bent like that.”

He looked up the bank.

“And I am going to tell you what it cost you to do it, because nobody else on that hill is going to and I am not going to be the one who keeps it for you. You have spent a week learning how a man with a broom says no, and you have liked learning it, and this morning you took the shape of it and used it on me to keep me off a lane where there was work and nobody had asked me to do it or told me I could not. That is the first thing you have done this week that you cannot put down again, and I know exactly what it cost you, because I have paid for things I had no use for and this was one of them.”

He set the yoke down on the wet grit at the bottom of the lane.

“You are nobody here, and so am I, and neither of us has been anything on this landing all week except a man who asked and a man who stood there. You wanted to be the man in the middle of it this morning and the middle of it was me with a yoke in my hands.”

Then Garrin Tolley picked the yoke up again and went down the lane to the turn and levered it under the banked grit and worked at it, and nobody went after him, and nobody told him he had been right to go, and nobody told him he had been wrong to stay away from the lane.

And the man with the broom stood at the top of the flight and looked down that lane at the man working in it, and said one thing, and it was not the working.

“That was a working about a man who stays on my stone,” he said, “and he has gone down it, and so the working has not held, and I am not going to say it again to make it hold. He is out of my sight and that is on this stone and not on him. I do not know what he is doing. That is the whole of what I said this morning and I thought I had said the rest of it.”

He went back to the bottom of the flight and worked at the sheet and did not look down the lane again that morning.

Then the boy came up that lane at about the tenth hour and found the bend clear and the yoke under his own pails, and gave his own count and did not turn it into anything.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, and counts his own days in this county, and said on the fourth day of the hundred and seventy-third week that his count is four hundred and six days, and that it is his and he will not make it into a count of anything standing on that landing.

“four hundred and six. That is mine.” He looked down the lane at the bend and then at the man standing in it. “Somebody has been at that bend.” Nothing came back to him about it and he said so. “Nothing came back and I am not going to go down there and ask. It is clear and that is what I came to find out.”

Then Ilyan Vester gave his figures, and gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked nothing of anybody.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county five hundred and five days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and seventy-four days old.

“Five hundred and five days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and seventy-four days old, and the standing offer asked at the eight hundred and ninth is three hundred and fifty-nine days old, which is one thousand and fifty-nine less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it and it has not been answered on and not been withdrawn on.”

He stood on the third step from the top with his hands hanging and did not go down the lane after the man in it.

“One hundred and ninety-ninth wrong things in one hundred and ninety-ninth mornings, and this is the hundred and ninety-ninth, and nobody is to tell me what kind of figure that is. My want is to be somebody and it has not changed. My cost is that I said one true thing this morning and then I bent it, and the bending is the part I will be carrying and not the true thing.”

He turned and looked up the flight at the strip at the top end.

“Has anybody on this landing answered anything about that name since the Thursday of the hundred and seventy-third week.” Nothing came of that and he put it down in new words. “Nothing has come of that and I am not going to ask it a second time this morning, and I am not going to ask it with a reason attached to it either, because a man with a reason attached to it sounds like a man with a working, and I am a man who has no business holding one of those.”

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour and the boy went down the lane and Garrin Tolley went down the lane behind him with the yoke on his shoulder.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about ninety of them were under, and went at the whole flight from the bottom upward with the skin of grit going ahead of the broom. The fortieth step went round on the edge of the broom and not underfoot, and the chair went round it in the same turn, and the iron in that seam took the broom head and nothing else. He swept the sheet to the edge of the dry strip and stopped there, and what lay on that strip at the seventh hour lay on it at the eleventh. The two lengths were still on the dry strip above the standing water, a forearm long each and split down the middle along the grain, thick end rough where it parted, and on the second a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches in the flat with one run out of the side.

Nobody laid a hand on either length, and nobody turned one, or picked one up, or weighed one in the palm, or read one out loud, or carried one inside, or put one back where it stood, or brought one into any use at all, and nobody put a hand on that wood to lift a name off it or press one onto it. Nothing standing on that strip was named that morning, and what that wood is was not said again in any other words, and no figure of what was standing there was given by anybody at any hour.

That morning drew down and let the light off the wall, and the account on that landing was as open at eleven as it had been at six and still in use at both hours, and the name on that second length was wrong, and nobody said on that bank that any part of it was over.

And Garrin Tolley put the boy’s yoke under a cartload of banked grit at the bend at the bottom of that lane and levered it until it split along the grain in his two hands, and he stood at the bottom of the lane holding the two halves of it and did not go up after the boy with them, and the bend was clear and the water was coming up.

# Chapter 1060: It Stood Where He Put It

The water came up that bank in the night as far as it always comes and then some, and by first light the top step had eight inches standing on it and the sheet lay over about eighty of those cut steps with the fortieth step and the chair standing doubled out of it, and the whole flight was on its way back under for the second time in a fortnight, and the man with the broom came up in the grey and worked the shallow end and then went up to the top and stood the broom against the wall.

He left it there. That is the whole of what happened on that landing before the sixth hour, and no person on it made anything of it.

Fifth day of the hundred and seventy-third week, and it is the Saturday of that week. His eight hundred and tenth morning. Six hundred and sixty days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and eleven weeks old. Six hundred and fourteen days since the division. Five hundred and sixty days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and five hundred and six days in this county of Kell.

Eight inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, about eighty of those ninety steps under, and six days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and then let the morning stand still, and he gave them in front of the broom standing against the wall behind him and did not look at it while he spoke.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the fifth day of the hundred and seventy-third week that this morning was going to be the plainest morning of the week so far, and that the broom was against the wall because he wanted his two hands for about a quarter of an hour, and that he was not going to say another thing about it.

“Eight at the seventh hour, about eighty of those ninety steps under, and six days of the coming back, on the fifth day of the hundred and seventy-third week, and it is the Saturday of that week. Three figures, and I am going to have my hands free for a while this morning.”

He set the broom against the wall at the top of the flight, head down, the stick leaning forward at the angle it takes.

“Go and do your gravel. It is the Saturday of the hundred and seventy-third week and I want that road clear below before the next lot comes down it.”

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow, and asked in the same words at the same hour, and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and stood with both hands on the wall and gave his own ten mornings back to himself out loud in the only way he has of giving anything back.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and thirty-ninth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and sixty less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and thirty-ninth. One thousand and sixty less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

He put his palm flat on the wall and left it there while he said the next part.

“I have asked that at this wall at the sixth hour on every morning of these ten days and nothing has come back to me on any of them, and I am not going to make anything out of the ten. Ten is not a figure of a thing anybody did for me. Ten is a figure of mornings I got up that bank and stood at this wall, and not a figure of a thing anybody did for me.”

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.”

Nothing came back and he nodded at it, because he has been nodding at it long enough to have learned what it costs.

“It has not come. I asked it this morning and I will ask it tomorrow and the day after that, and it will not come, and there is no morning of this county that is going to make it come, and I am a man of fifty-four and I am not going to spend what is left of me being a man who asked and did not get.”

Then Ilyan Vester came up at about the ninth hour with the water at the top of his boots, stood clear of the top step, gave his figures, gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county five hundred and six days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and seventy-five days old.

“Five hundred and six days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and seventy-five days old, and the standing offer asked at the eight hundred and tenth is three hundred and sixty days old, which is one thousand and sixty less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it and it has not been answered on and not been withdrawn on, and I have not asked it on any morning of these ten.”

He stood with his hands open and looked at the broom standing against the wall with nobody holding it.

“Two hundredth wrong things in two hundredth mornings, and this is the two hundredth, and nobody is to tell me what kind of figure that is.” He shut the cut hand and opened it again. “There is a whole figure in my mouth this morning as well as a whole figure in his, and I am not going to stand here and put the two together and make something of them, and I am not going to explain to anybody why I am not, because I have been asked for nothing this week and I have managed it.”

Then Corvin Tace went down that flight with both hands free and a barrow-load that had been standing at the top of the steps since the water came, and the broom stayed against the wall at the top of the flight the whole way down and the whole way up, and Ilyan Vester stood on the second step from the top with his hands at his sides for as long as the man was off the stone, and did not touch it, and did not shift his feet to be nearer it, and did not look at anybody to see whether they had looked at it.

Then the woman who saws lengths came up that cart road at about the tenth hour with a length under her arm and a cup in her hand and stopped at the foot of the steps and looked up at the top step where a broom was standing against a wall with nobody holding it.

Wenna Pyle is thirty-eight, saws lengths and sets studs at a bench in an open-sided shed behind a wheelwright’s about three hundred yards down that cart road, and said on the fifth day of the hundred and seventy-third week that she had come up with a length under her arm out of the same habit as the Sunday of the hundred and seventy-second week, and that she was going to stand at the foot of those steps with it and not come up, because there is one dry place on that landing and she has already carried a length down a whole road for the sake of not using it.

Then the man of thirty-eight came up the road with his hands behind his back, and Corvin Tace came back up the flight and went to the wall and took the broom off it in the same grip and went straight back into the water and did not stop, and nothing whatever was said about it by anybody.

The boy came up that lane at about the tenth hour with his pails and stood them in the shallows, and gave a whole figure of his own out loud and let it stand on its own, and asked one thing that nothing came back to.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, and counts his own days in this county, and said on the fifth day of the hundred and seventy-third week that his count is four hundred and seven days, and that it is his and he will not make it into a count of anything standing on that landing.

“four hundred and seven. That is mine.” He looked at the water standing on the flat of the landing. “Has the broom been standing up there by itself.” Nothing came of that and he answered it before anyone could. “Nothing came of that. It has not. Somebody put it there and somebody took it off it.”

Then Ilyan Vester said the rest of it while the man was still working, and nobody answered him and nobody agreed with him and nobody corrected one word of it.

“My want is to be somebody and it has not changed. My cost is that a broom stood against a wall on that landing for the length of a barrow-load with nobody holding it, and I was standing by it, and I wanted it moved so that I could say I had left it alone. That is the whole of what I wanted and it is a shameful thing to want and I am putting it on the page.” He looked at the wall where the broom had been standing. “And the broom was not moved, and I did not move it, and nothing about this morning has come out of that for anybody, and I am not going to stand here and say that it did. I have been carrying that hand open at my side down that bank every morning of this week and I did not know I was doing it until this morning, and I am going to go on doing it.”

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour and the boy went down the lane and Garrin Tolley went down the cart road and the woman who saws lengths went down that road last with the length under her arm.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about eighty of them were under, and went at the whole flight from the bottom upward. The fortieth step went round on the edge of the broom and not over it, and the chair went round it in the same stroke, and the iron in that seam took the broom head and took nothing else. He brought the broom to the edge of the dry strip and stopped, and what lay on that strip at the seventh hour lay on it at the eleventh, and no hand in this county had been on either of them at any hour in between. The two lengths were still on the dry strip above the standing water, a forearm long each and split down the middle along the grain, thick end rough where it parted, and on the second a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches in the flat with one run out of the side.

Nothing was pulled or turned over or lifted or weighed in the palm or read out or carried inside or laid back or bent to work on that landing at any hour of that morning, and no hand went on that wood to take anything off it or to set anything on it, and nothing standing up there was given a name by anybody, and what that wood is was not said again in any wording, and no figure of what stood there was given out loud or otherwise.

The morning went off the wall and let the light out of that bank, and the account on that landing was as open at eleven as it had been at six and was still in use at both hours, and the name on that second length was wrong in the way it had been wrong every morning of the fortnight, and nobody on that flight said that any of it was finished, and nobody there owed anybody anything for any of it.

And Corvin Tace stood the broom head in the top step with the running going past the brush, and went down that bank and did not come up again that day, and about the twelfth hour the water lying over that top step closed over the place where he had left it standing and went on down the flight without stopping.

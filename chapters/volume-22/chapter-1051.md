# Chapter 1051: Nobody Asked Him For It

The broom was standing against the wall at the top of that flight where its man had put it the night before, head down on the stone and the stick leaning forward at the angle a broom stick takes when a man props it, turns round and walks off down a road without looking back at it. It had been standing in the dark long enough for the dew to come off the bristles and stand in the head of it. The light came up behind that angle and found it there, and did not find anybody at all for a while.

Then Corvin Tace came up the cart road with his coat open and the cold still on him, and stopped short of the broom, and stood looking at the wall it was leaning on as though the wall had done something.

He did not touch it for a while. Then he took his hand off his side, put it on the stick where the wear is, and lifted it off the stone, and the water that had gathered in the head of it ran back down the bristles onto the top step. He carried it down to the bottom of the flight before he began.

Third day of the hundred and seventy-second week, and it is the Thursday of that week. His eight hundred and first morning. Six hundred and fifty-one days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and nine weeks and five days old. Six hundred and five days since the division. Five hundred and fifty-one days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and ninety-seven days in this county of Kell.

Four inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, about ninety of those ninety steps under, and seven days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures from the second step from the bottom with the water round his boots, and gave them without stopping to arrange them.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the third day of the hundred and seventy-second week that a man who holds a thing for years wants one thing out of it, and that what he wants is a morning where the stone has work in it and the work is the whole of what the stone owes him.

“Four at the seventh hour, about ninety of those ninety steps under, and seven days of the coming back, on the third day of the hundred and seventy-second week, and it is the Thursday of that week. Three figures.”

He stood the broom head in the top step and let the water come up round the bristles and go down again.

“Go and do your gravel. It is the Thursday of the hundred and seventy-second week and the bank is filling, and the stone has nothing else for anybody.”

Nobody answered him, which is what the stone does, and he went up to the dry strip at the top end with the broom head turned sideways, and there was a morning’s work on that stone and it was in one place.

The angle where the dry strip stands a step above the water and the water’s edge has been standing a hand’s width off it since the bank filled was full of grit, and had been full of grit all the time the water was off, and the broom could not get into it from the strip side because the strip is where a man puts his feet when he is on the strip. Corvin Tace went down onto the water side of that angle, onto one knee with the other leg up, and worked the head of the broom into it and pulled grit out of it and threw the grit onto the water, and the water took it and went down the flight with it. Then he did the next hand’s width. Then the next.

He did it for a long stretch of the morning, on his knees, one-handed, without once stepping up onto the strip, and the whole of the job was four feet of grit and it took him most of the time the light had, and a second pair of hands could not have made it shorter, because a second pair of hands would have had to be up on the strip.

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow, and asked in the same words at the same hour, and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and set the barrow down in the shallows at the top of the flight with the wheel under.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and thirtieth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and fifty-one less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and thirtieth. One thousand and fifty-one less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

He kept one hand on the rim of the barrow and looked down the wet flight.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.”

The wall gave him the morning back and he said that in his own words, and nobody filled it.

“It gave me the morning back and I am not going to say a thing about what it gave me, because whatever I say about it I have said before on this wall, and a man who says the same sentence about a wall over and over is not saying it, he is reciting. I asked at six. It is a quarter past six and it has not come.”

Then Ilyan Vester came up at about the ninth hour with the water at the top of his boots, and stood below the top step and gave his figures, and gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and ninety-seven days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and sixty-six days old.

“Four hundred and ninety-seven days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and sixty-six days old, and the standing offer asked at the eight hundred and first is three hundred and fifty-one days old, which is one thousand and fifty-one less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it and it has not been answered on and not been withdrawn on. Nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one, and I have stopped saying that last part, because saying it every morning is a way of asking.”

He watched the man on one knee work the broom head sideways into the angle.

“One hundred and ninety-first wrong things in one hundred and ninety-first mornings, and this is the hundred and ninety-first, and nobody is to tell me what kind of figure that is. My want is to be somebody and it has not changed. My cost is what the Saturday of the hundred and seventy-first week took, and I am not giving it a shape, and I am carrying it down a bank twice a day and up again.”

He shifted his weight to the other foot so the water could go past.

“And my mistake this morning is standing here with my hands open and thinking about that angle. That is four feet of grit and one man’s knee and a pair of hands, and I could have gone down that angle four times over with a pail. I did not. I stood here with my hands open and I thought about it, and I want it said that I thought about it, and I want nobody to tell me what kind of a man thinks about a thing and does not do it, because I do not know what kind.”

Then the man of thirty-eight came up the cart road and stopped at the foot of the steps where the water was shallower, and stood there with his coat shut and his board knocking once against his ribs, and said out loud what he had been holding since the Monday of the hundred and seventy-first week.

Garrin Tolley is thirty-eight, with a board of thirty-two notches cut in the edge of it inside his coat, who was refused half a day at a bench and refused a going down to the drain inside a fortnight and has not been refused for anything since, and said on the third day of the hundred and seventy-second week that he had come up with nothing to ask for the first time in a fortnight and that standing here with nothing was harder than asking.

“I am not asking.” He said it to the water and not up the bank. “I want that said first, because if I say it after I have asked then it is a thing I have covered up. I am going to stand at this foot and watch a man work an angle out with a broom and I am not going to open my mouth about it.”

Nothing came of that either, and he kept his mouth shut about it for the rest of the morning, which was the first thing he had held to on that bank in a fortnight.

Then the man who cannot see well came up at about the tenth hour with his hand on the wall, and asked his question, and got nothing, and said so in his own words.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and said on the third day of the hundred and seventy-second week that the water being back made the landing harder to walk and not easier, and that nothing was going on his list.

“Has that name been answered by anybody since the Wednesday of the hundred and seventy-second week.” Nothing returned, and he took his hand along the wall past the end of it and off it. “Nothing has returned and I am not putting that on the list, because there is nothing on the list to put it on, and a man who starts filling a list to get rid of a silence has stopped having a list and started having a complaint.”

Then the boy came up that lane at about the tenth hour with his pails on and the yoke across his shoulders, and set them down in the water at the foot of the steps and gave his own count and would not turn it into anything else.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, and counts his own days in this county, and said on the third day of the hundred and seventy-second week that his count is three hundred and ninety-eight days, and that it is his and he will not make it into a count of anything standing on that landing.

“three hundred and ninety-eight. That is mine.” He took the yoke off his shoulders and stood it against the wall out of the water. “Has that water come up over the top step before today and gone off again.” Nothing came of that, and he looked at it. “Then I will remember it going off and coming back, because that is a thing I saw and not a thing anybody told me.”

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour and the boy went down the lane with his pails and Garrin Tolley went down the cart road with his hand flat on the outside of his coat over the board, not to still it but because he had been walking three days with nothing in his arms.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about ninety of them were under, and went at the whole flight from the bottom upward. The fortieth step went round the broom and not under a boot, and the chair went round it in the same turn, and the iron in that seam took the broom head and nothing else. He came to the top end on his knees again and worked the last of that angle out onto the water, and then he came to the dry strip and swept it to its edge and stopped there, and what was lying on it at the seventh hour was lying on it at the eleventh. The two lengths were still on the dry strip, a forearm long each and split down the middle along the grain, thick end rough where it parted, and on the second a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches in the flat with one run out of the side.

Nobody lifted either of those lengths, nobody turned one, nobody put a hand out to find out what it weighed like, nobody carried one off that stone or set one back on it or brought one into use, and no hand went near that wood to rub anything off it, in that whole hour or after it. Nothing standing up there got a name put on it, and nobody counted what was standing there, and no mouth on that bank gave out what that wood is.

The morning used itself up, and the account on that landing was as unfinished at eleven as it had been at six and still in a man’s hand at both hours, and the name on that second length was wrong in the same way it had been wrong the morning before, and nobody said out loud that any part of it had been finished.

And Corvin Tace put the broom back against that wall at the top of the flight at the angle it had been standing at, head down, and went down the cart road to wash his hands in the water at the foot of the steps, and did not look at it again on the way past.

# Chapter 1058: Nothing On The Stone To Measure

The water went off that bank in the night and went off the way it has always gone off it, all at once and downward, and by the light there was grit lying loose on ninety dry cut steps and the whole flight standing out of the bank in the air with nothing running anywhere, and a man could walk from the bottom stone to the top step without lifting a foot, and that has not been true of this landing since before anybody on it can remember asking anything about it.

The man with the broom came up in the grey and stood at the top of it for a while with the broom in his hand and did not begin, which is the only thing about this morning that is different from other mornings and which no person on that landing remarked on.

Third day of the hundred and seventy-third week, and it is the Thursday of that week. His eight hundred and eighth morning. Six hundred and fifty-eight days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and ten weeks and five days old. Six hundred and twelve days since the division. Five hundred and fifty-eight days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and five hundred and four days in this county of Kell.

There was nothing on that stone at the seventh hour to put a figure to. The man of forty-three gave that as a fact and gave it three times over, because a dry morning takes three things off a keeper and takes them one at a time, and he said them out loud in words of his own so that they could not be tidied away later.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the third day of the hundred and seventy-third week that his stone had nothing on it to measure, his flight had nothing under it to count, and his week had nothing coming back to work toward, and that he was going to say those three absences out loud and let them be the morning.

“There is no figure on this stone this morning, no figure under my feet, and no figure of anything coming back. That is the three of them, said out loud.” He put the broom head on the top step. “I said the same three absences on the Wednesday of the hundred and seventy-second week in different words, and I am not going to say them twice in the same words, because a man who hands round the same sentence about his own stone is not being careful. He is being tidy.”

He set the broom against his leg and looked down ninety dry steps.

“Go and do your gravel. It is the Thursday of the hundred and seventy-third week and the water is gone, and the stone has nothing to say to anybody this morning, and it is not going to say something better later in the day.”

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow, and asked in the same words at the same hour, and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and put his hands flat on the wall and found it dry.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and thirty-seventh mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and fifty-eight less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and thirty-seventh. One thousand and fifty-eight less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

He took his hands off the wall and looked at the grit that had come out from under them.

“That wall is dry and that wall has been wet every morning of my asking, and I am not going to say that means anything, because a wall is a wall wet or dry.” He put his hands back on it. “Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.”

Nothing, and the dry wall, and the grit under his boots.

“It has found nothing on a dry morning as well as a wet one and that is a thing I did not know before this week, and I have found it out for nothing, which is how most of what I know has come.”

Then the woman who saws lengths came up that cart road at about the eighth hour with her cup in her hand and no length under her arm, and came all the way up that dry flight for the first time in a fortnight, and stopped at the fortieth step and looked down at the seam of it.

Wenna Pyle is thirty-eight, saws lengths and sets studs at a bench in an open-sided shed behind a wheelwright’s about three hundred yards down that cart road, and said on the third day of the hundred and seventy-third week that with the water gone there was iron showing in the seam of the fortieth step that had not been showing since she began coming up that road, and that she could see it from where she stood and was not going to come one step nearer to it.

“There is iron let into the cut of that step and bedded in grit, and the top of it stands out of the stone and one end has gone back under, and there is more of it in there than there is out.” She said it from above, with her cup in her hand. “I am not putting my hand in it. I am telling you what is in it because you would have to find it with a broom head eventually and a man is better told first. That is the whole of what I have and I am not standing over it.”

Corvin Tace nodded at that and went on sweeping, and did not change his stroke.

Then the man who cannot see well came up at about the tenth hour and walked the whole of that dry flight with his hand out in front of him and found every step by the edge of it, and got to the fortieth one and put his hand along the stone above the seam and did not lower it, and said one thing about the light that nobody had asked him for.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and said on the third day of the hundred and seventy-third week that a dry morning was the first morning in this county where he had seen the shape of that whole flight from the bottom, and that seeing it had not helped him at all, and that his list was still empty.

“I can see the shape of this landing this morning from the bottom stone and it has not given me one thing I did not have on a wet morning.” He kept his hand on the stone above the seam. “There is a thing in that step that the woman has just said out loud, and I am not putting my hand on it and I am not putting it on my list either, because the list is for things I cannot check and this morning I have been told what it is by somebody who knows, and a thing I have been told is not a thing I have found.”

Then the boy came up that lane at about the tenth hour, walked the whole dry flight without the water to wade, stood a moment on the fortieth step with his heel well to the side of the seam, and gave his own count and would not turn it into anything else.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, and counts his own days in this county, and said on the third day of the hundred and seventy-third week that his count is four hundred and five days, and that it is his and he will not make it into a count of anything standing on that landing.

“four hundred and five. That is mine.” He looked down the flight he had just walked. “I have never been up it dry. It takes longer. There is nothing to push against.”

Then Ilyan Vester came up at about the ninth hour in boots that were made for standing in water and were standing in nothing, and gave his figures, and gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county five hundred and four days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and seventy-three days old.

“Five hundred and four days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and seventy-third days old, and the standing offer asked at the eight hundred and eighth is three hundred and fifty-eight days old, which is one thousand and fifty-eight less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it and it has not been answered on and not been withdrawn on. It is the first morning since the Wednesday of the hundred and seventy-second week that the man with the broom has had nothing to give me out of his stone, and I am still here, and I am not going to make that into a complaint about either of us.”

He stood at the top step in dry boots and looked down the flight without a mirror in it.

“One hundred and ninety-eighth wrong things in one hundred and ninety-eighth mornings, and this is the hundred and ninety-eighth, and nobody is to tell me what kind of figure that is. My want is to be somebody and it has not changed. My cost is that I have spent this week learning to want less off that stone and less off the men standing on it, and it turns out that wanting less is not the same as wanting it more quietly, and I have been doing it the second thing.”

He looked at the seam in the fortieth step from above without going down to it.

“And my mistake this morning was standing at the top of a dry flight wishing I could see how much of the morning was gone. On a wet morning the water tells you what has happened and how much of the day is behind you and this morning there is nothing to tell me, and I stood up here wishing for a figure of work so that I could put it beside the other four, and a man who wishes for a fifth figure to put beside four is a man doing arithmetic on himself and calling it a morning.”

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour and the boy went down the lane at a run because it was dry, and the woman who saws lengths went down that cart road with her cup still in her hand.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, and none of them were under, and went at the whole flight from the bottom upward with the grit coming ahead of the broom in the dry air. The fortieth step went round on the edge of the broom and not underfoot, and the chair went round it in the same turn, and the iron in that seam took the broom head the whole length of the step and took nothing else. He came to the dry strip at the top end and swept it to its edge and stopped, and what was lying on it at the seventh hour was lying on it at the eleventh. The two lengths were still on the dry strip in the dry air, a forearm long each and split down the middle along the grain, thick end rough where it parted, and on the second a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches in the flat with one run out of the side.

Nobody laid a hand on either of those lengths, and nobody pulled one, turned one, lifted one, felt one in the palm, read one aloud, carried one inside, laid one back or brought one into use, and nobody put a hand on that wood to take a name off it or to set one on it, and nobody put a finger in the seam of the fortieth step to see what was in it. Nothing standing on that strip was given a name that morning, and what that wood is was not said in any other words, and nothing standing there was counted by anybody.

The morning went and the account on that landing was as open at eleven as it had been at six and still in a man’s hand at both hours, and the name on that second length was wrong, and nobody on that dry flight said that any part of it was done.

And Corvin Tace went up that flight a second time from the bottom with the flat of his hand on the stone in front of him, step by step, feeling for grit with his thumb, going over each tread and beside each seam and not into any of them, and at the top step there was nothing on his hand at all, and he stood there with it open in front of him and looked at it, and then he put it down his side and picked up the broom.

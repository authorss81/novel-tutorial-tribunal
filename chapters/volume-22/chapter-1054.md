# Chapter 1054: Nowhere To Set It Down

The bank came up to a full sheet in the night and did not go over the edge anywhere at all, so that by the time there was light the landing was one flat brown table with the water standing on it, sixteen inches on the top step, and it stayed exactly where it was, and the fortieth step and the chair stood out of it in doubled shape, and the dry strip at the top end stood clear of the water’s edge all morning without the water ever touching it.

For the first time in a week there was nothing standing on that flight that a man could not walk straight up to. Every step of the ninety was walkable. A man could go from the bottom stone to the top step in one line without climbing anything.

Sixth day of the hundred and seventy-second week, and it is the Sunday of that week. His eight hundred and fourth morning. Six hundred and fifty-four days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and ten weeks and one day old. Six hundred and eight days since the division. Five hundred and fifty-four days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and five hundred days in this county of Kell.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and it holds, about sixty of those ninety steps under, and four days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures in the middle of that flat sheet with the whole landing behind him, and gave them the way he gives them when the bank is full, which is slowly.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the sixth day of the hundred and seventy-second week that a full bank is the one morning of the week when this landing is the easiest place in the county to stand on and that he was going to spend it sweeping a surface with nothing on it that needs sweeping.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, about sixty of those ninety steps under, and four days of the coming back, on the sixth day of the hundred and seventy-second week, and it is the Sunday of that week. Three figures.”

He drew the broom head across the sheet and the sheet closed up behind the bristles as though nothing had been in it.

“Go and do your gravel. It is the Sunday of the hundred and seventy-second week and the bank is full, and there is nowhere on this stone for a thing to be set down, so nobody is to try.”

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow, and asked in the same words at the same hour, and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and brought the barrow high through the sheet with the wheel held clear of the top step.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and thirty-third mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and fifty-four less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and thirty-third. One thousand and fifty-four less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

He set the wheel down and put both hands on the wall and left them there.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.”

Nothing came of it, and he said so without turning round, and nobody filled it.

“Nothing came of it and I want to say one thing about a full bank and then I will leave it. I have asked that on a morning with nothing on the stone and on a morning with the water to my chin and this morning the whole landing is one floor and there is not a step between me and anybody, and it makes no difference at all, and I am not going to stand here being pleased that I can see them all.”

Then the woman who saws lengths came up that cart road at about the eighth hour with a length under her arm, and came up the flight in sixteen inches of standing water in the flat way a woman with a long thing under her arm comes up steps, and got to the top, and stopped.

Wenna Pyle is thirty-eight, who saws lengths and sets studs at a bench in an open-sided shed behind a wheelwright’s about three hundred yards down that cart road, and who carries her own cup out to that bench because there is no table in the shed, and who said on the sixth day of the hundred and seventy-second week that she had brought a length up out of habit and had nowhere on that landing to put it down and was not going to pretend she did.

“I have brought this because I bring something most mornings and my arms made the decision at the shed without asking me.” She stood on the top step with it across her front and did not shift her grip. “And I am standing here now because there is one dry place on this landing to lay a length down and I know exactly which dry place it is, and it is the one I am not going to put it on.”

She did not put it down and she did not go back down with it yet either.

“If I set this down anywhere on that strip then it is one more thing lying on that strip, and somebody has to look at it tomorrow morning and work out whose it is and whether it moved, and that is a morning of somebody’s I have no right to spend. If I carry it back down the road then my arms have carried a length up ninety steps and down ninety steps for nothing. I am going to do that. I would rather have done the walking than have put it down.”

Then the man who cannot see well came up at about the tenth hour, and waded the whole width of that landing with his hand out in front of him, and got as far as the water’s edge at the top step and stopped there in the open with everything in front of him, and did not take the next stride.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and said on the sixth day of the hundred and seventy-second week that the bank being full had put a man who cannot see well within one step of the one place on that landing he is not going, and that he had measured the stride and it was not far.

“This morning the whole of that landing is walkable and I have been all over it from the bottom stone to here, which is more of it than I have stood on since I came to this county.” He said it flat, facing the water’s edge. “And I am standing at this edge and I am not taking the step across it. Not because I am afraid of a step. Because the moment my foot is on that dry strip I am a man who has stood on it, and I would not be able to say afterwards that I had never stood on it, and that sentence is the only honest thing about me that I have managed to keep.”

He put his hand out along nothing and found nothing.

“Has anybody on this landing answered anything about that name since the Friday of the hundred and seventy-second week.” Nothing returned, and he marked it his own way. “Nothing has returned and I am not crossing this edge to go and look.”

Then the boy came up that lane at about the tenth hour and stood his pails in the sheet and gave his own figure and would not make a shape of it.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, and counts his own days in this county, and said on the sixth day of the hundred and seventy-second week that his count is four hundred and one days, and that it is his and he will not make it into a count of anything standing on that landing.

“four hundred and one. That is mine.” He looked at the water standing on the flat of the landing and then at the man at the edge of it. “Has that man been in the water up to his chest.” Nothing came of that, and he said so. “Nothing came of that. He is up to his knees. I am asking because I carry things and I have to know what a man stands in.”

Then Ilyan Vester came up at about the ninth hour with the sheet to his thighs, and stood on the second step and gave his figures, and gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county five hundred days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and sixty-nine days old.

“Five hundredth days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and sixty-nine days old, and the standing offer asked at the eight hundred and fourth is three hundred and fifty-four days old, which is one thousand and fifty-four less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it and it has not been answered on and not been withdrawn on.”

He stood with the water at the top of his thighs and his hands above the water where they could be seen.

“One hundred and ninety-fourth wrong things in one hundred and ninety-fourth mornings, and this is the hundred and ninety-fourth, and nobody is to tell me what kind of figure that is. My want is to be somebody and it has not changed. My cost is that I have been on this landing five mornings running and I have not once had anything refused with a working that was addressed to me, and there is a difference between a thing that stands on a landing and a thing that stands on me, and I did not know there was a difference until this week.”

He looked at the woman holding the length on the top step, and then at the man standing at the edge of the water who was not going to take the step across it.

“And my mistake this morning was wanting to be the man who carried her length back down for her. That is the whole of it. I wanted to be useful in a small way that would have cost me nothing, because I have nobody to be useful to, and it would have been the first thing anybody has let me do on this landing since I came to it, and I did not put my hand out, and the reason I did not is not a good one and I will not dress it up. The reason is that offering is asking, and I have watched what asking has cost this week, and none of it went anywhere that I could send it.”

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour, and the woman who saws lengths went down that cart road with the length under her arm and the man of thirty-eight went down it ahead of her.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about sixty of them were under, and went at the whole flight from the bottom upward with the sheet at his thighs and the whole landing open behind him. The fortieth step went round on the edge of the broom and not under a boot, and the chair went round it in the same turn, and the iron in that seam took the broom head and never a finger. He swept the sheet up to the edge of the dry strip and stood the broom head in it and left it standing there, and what lay on that strip at the seventh hour lay on it at the eleventh with the water never touching it and every one of the ninety steps of that flight walkable the whole time. The two lengths were still on the dry strip above the standing sheet, a forearm long each and split down the middle along the grain, thick end rough where it parted, and on the second a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches in the flat with one run out of the side.

Nobody put a hand on either length, nobody turned one over to look at the underside, nobody took one up in the palm to find the weight of it, nobody carried one off that strip or set one back onto it or stood one against the wall or brought one into any use, and nobody laid a hand on that wood in order to lift a name off it. Nothing standing on that strip was given a name that morning, and what that wood is was not put into a mouth a second time, and nothing standing there was counted by anybody at any hour of it.

That morning thinned out and let the light go, and the account on that landing was as open at eleven as at six and still in use at both hours, and the name on that second length was wrong, and nobody standing on those ninety steps said that anything about it was finished.

And Wenna Pyle carried the length down that whole cart road in her two arms the way she had carried it up, without setting it down at the wall or at the foot of the steps or anywhere between, and her arms were shaking a little by the time she got to the shed, and she put it on her own bench and not on the floor, because a bench is a thing a person can put a length down on and that landing is not.

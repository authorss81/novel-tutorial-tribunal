# Chapter 1053: The Sentence He Did Not Say

Twelve inches came up that bank in the night and lay flat by first light, brown and still, so that the whole flight was one sheet with the fortieth step and the chair standing doubled out of it and the dry strip at the top end standing clear and higher than anything else on the landing. Nothing on the water moved. A gust came down the bank and went across it and the sheet did not even show where the gust had been.

Fifth day of the hundred and seventy-second week, and it is the Saturday of that week. His eight hundred and third morning. Six hundred and fifty-three days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and ten weeks old. Six hundred and seven days since the division. Five hundred and fifty-three days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and ninety-nine days in this county of Kell.

Twelve inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, about seventy of those ninety steps under, and five days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures standing to the knee in the sheet, and gave them in the order they come, and stopped dead after the third one, which is what he does when a figure in the morning has come out whole and there is nowhere to put anything beside it.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the fifth day of the hundred and seventy-second week that a figure with nothing beside it is a figure a man is allowed to stop on, and that he was going to stop on this one and go to work.

“Twelve at the seventh hour, about seventy of those ninety steps under, and five days of the coming back, on the fifth day of the hundred and seventy-second week, and it is the Saturday of that week. Three figures, and there is nothing after them.”

He put the broom head into the sheet and the sheet took it without a sound.

“Go and do your gravel. It is the Saturday of the hundred and seventy-second week and I have a whole figure in the air and no use for it.”

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow, and asked in the same words at the same hour, and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and put his hands flat on the wall and said a whole figure out loud in a voice that does not read and does not write.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and thirty-second mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and fifty-three less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and thirty-second. One thousand and fifty-three less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

He said the figure and then stopped, on purpose, in a place where a man who cannot read can hear the shape of the stopping.

“That is a whole figure and it has come out whole and I have got no days to put beside it, and I am not going up the lane to fetch any. I am not going to stand here and work out what a whole number means when it belongs to me. A figure of mine is mine and it does not have to say anything.”

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.”

Nothing came back, and he told the wall so, and nobody filled it.

“Nothing came back and the day I asked on was whole too, and I am not going to join the two figures up. A man who puts a whole figure next to his asking and says see, this much of me has gone by and nothing has answered it, is asking twice and calling it arithmetic. I asked. That is one figure and I have said it and the rest of the morning is not part of it.”

Then the man of thirty-eight came up the cart road at about the eighth hour and put both hands on the barrow shafts and asked to be allowed to take it down that road once with gravel in it.

Garrin Tolley is thirty-eight, with a board of thirty-two notches cut in the edge of it inside his coat, who was refused a bench, refused a going and refused a lift on this landing inside one week, and said on the fifth day of the hundred and seventy-second week that he had learned to ask in the right shape and was going to ask in the right shape anyway.

“Give me the barrow down the road once. I will put it back where it stands and I will not take it past the wall. That is all of it and I am asking you and not the landing, because you are the man who holds it.”

Barnaby Crove turned round. He does perhaps once in a week, and this was the second time in a fortnight.

“No.” He said it to Garrin and not to the stone. “And here is the reason, because you asked me and not the landing. I cannot tell you what that barrow weighs in words and words are the only thing I have got for you. It has come up that bank three hundred and thirty-two mornings with me on the handles and it has never once been a thing a man could be handed, and if it comes down light then a morning’s work is somewhere on that road and I could not tell you where, and if it comes down wrong then there is no barrow on this landing and the gravel stands still for a week. I am not being careful with it in front of you. I cannot be careful with it with a man in it.”

He put his hands back on the shafts.

“You have asked me well and I have given you the whole of the reason and there is nothing in it for you to carry away and improve on. Go and do something else.”

Garrin Tolley took his hands off the barrow and nodded and did not say anything for a while, and then he said the thing that was true about himself and not about the barrow.

“I wanted it because it is the only thing on this landing that is spoken of in a working and not in a silence, and I am a man who would rather be refused with a working than passed over.” He said it plainly, standing in eight inches of water. “That is not a good reason to want a barrow. I have said it out loud so you cannot say I hid it.”

The barrow question was refused by the man it was put to, in his own words and by nobody else, and no other question about that name found an answer that morning, and nobody thanked anybody for any of it.

Then the boy came up that lane at about the tenth hour with his pails on and set them down in the shallows, and gave a whole figure of his own in his own voice, and a second figure with the same shape on it, and he would not let one touch the other.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, and counts his own days in this county, and said on the fifth day of the hundred and seventy-second week that his count is four hundred days, and that it is his and he will not make it into a count of anything standing on that landing.

“Four hundred.” He said it and let it stand out on its own. “That is mine and it is a whole figure, and I have got nobody to give it to and I am not giving it to the water in front of me.”

He looked at the sheet.

“Is the man with the broom sick. He stopped after his three figures and he has not said anything since and he is not slow.” Nothing came of that, and he answered it himself. “Nothing came of that. He is not sick. He stopped on purpose, and I have seen a person stop on purpose before, and it was me, on the Saturday of the hundred and seventy-first week, and nobody told me I had.”

Then Ilyan Vester came up at about the ninth hour with the sheet at his knees and stood below the top step and gave his figures, and gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and ninety-nine days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and sixty-eight days old.

“Four hundred and ninety-nine days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and sixty-eight days old, and the standing offer asked at the eight hundred and third is three hundred and fifty-three days old, which is one thousand and fifty-three less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it and it has not been answered on and not been withdrawn on.”

He stood in the sheet and the sheet held him still, which was the thing about this morning.

“One hundred and ninety-third wrong things in one hundred and ninety-third mornings, and this is the hundred and ninety-third, and nobody is to tell me what kind of figure that is. My want is to be somebody and it has not changed. My cost is the same one and it is heavier this morning because a whole figure came out of a man’s mouth today and out of a boy’s, and I could feel the wanting in both of those mouths for more than a figure.”

He had turned toward the boy with his hands out when he said the next part, and the boy was still standing there with his pails in the shallows.

“And here is my mistake and I am going to say it while he can hear me. I had it ready. I had a whole sentence ready for him about how a figure of weeks is a clock and not a verdict, and how he should not hear endurance in it because nothing on this bank holds anything on anybody’s account, and it was a good sentence and it was true and I did not say it.”

He put his hands at his sides.

“I did not say it because asking leave to say it is a second thing and I have been refused that on the Sunday of the hundred and seventy-first week by a man with a working, and I am not going to find a way round that by being useful. So he is standing there with a whole figure of his own and he has not asked me what it means and I am not going to tell him. I want it on the page that it was ready and that I did not say it, because it was ready, and because a man who has a true sentence ready for a boy and does not say it is doing something and should have to say what.”

The boy looked at him for a moment and said nothing, and went on up the lane with his pails.

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour and Garrin Tolley went down the cart road with his hands empty and the board quiet against his ribs.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about seventy of them were under, and went at the whole flight from the bottom upward with the sheet unbroken ahead of the broom. The fortieth step he took on the edge of the broom and the chair went round beside it, and the iron in that seam went under the broom head with his two hands still up on the stick. He brought the broom to the edge of the dry strip and stopped and stood the broom head still in the water, and what lay there at the seventh hour lay there at the eleventh. The two lengths were still on the dry strip above the still sheet, a forearm long each and split down the middle along the grain, thick end rough where it parted, and on the second a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches in the flat with one run out of the side.

Nothing on that landing was hauled up or turned down or cradled in the hand to guess at or spoken out or fetched inside or put back or bent to a purpose, then or after, and no hand went on that wood to lift a name off it or to read one by. Nothing standing there was given a name that morning, and what that wood is was not said in any mouth on that bank, and nobody counted what stood there.

That morning went off the wall and the account on that landing was as open at eleven as it had been at six and was still in a hand at both hours, and the name on that second length was still wrong, and nobody standing on those steps said that any part of it was done.

And the boy stopped at the top of the lane with his pails in his hands and worked out, on his own, with his own arithmetic and nobody helping him, whether a whole figure of anything was a figure of a thing or a figure of a clock, and he went on down the lane with the pails swinging and had not told anybody what it was.

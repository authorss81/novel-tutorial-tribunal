# Chapter 1055: She Put It Away

The sheet was still there in the morning, sixteen inches on the top step and not one inch less, and the wind came across that landing before the light and did not raise anything on it, because a full bank with grit gone off the top step yesterday has nothing left loose to lift, and the man with the broom noticed that and said so.

Seventh and last day of the hundred and seventy-second week, and it is the Monday of that week. His eight hundred and fifth morning. Six hundred and fifty-five days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and ten weeks and two days old. Six hundred and nine days since the division. Five hundred and fifty-five days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and five hundred and one days in this county of Kell.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and it holds, about sixty of those ninety steps under, and three days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures on the last morning of that week and then gave the land the turning, and gave it from this side only.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the seventh and last day of the hundred and seventy-second week that the week turns between this morning and the morning after it, and that he was saying which side this morning is on so that nobody has to carry it, and that he was not going to say what is on the other side because he is not standing there yet.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, about sixty of those ninety steps under, and three days of the coming back, on the seventh and last day of the hundred and seventy-second week, and it is the Monday of that week. Three figures, and the week turns between this morning and the morning after it, and this morning is on this side of that turn.”

He put the broom head into the sheet and drew it and the sheet took it.

“Go and do your gravel. It is the last morning of the hundred and seventy-second week and the bank is holding, and the stone has nothing else for anybody.”

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow, and asked in the same words at the same hour, and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and stood with both hands on the wall and counted his own week out loud in the only way he has of counting anything.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and thirty-fourth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and fifty-five less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and thirty-fourth. One thousand and fifty-five less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

He put his thumb on a joint of the wall and looked at it while he spoke, which is where his eyes go when he is being exact.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.”

Nothing, and the wall, and the morning going on with them.

“I have stood at this wall on every morning of this week and nothing has come back to me on any of them, and it is the last morning of the week, and I am not going to stand here and put that together into a shape.” He took his hand off the wall and put it on the barrow. “A man who takes the week and the question and puts them side by side is asking a bigger question than the one he asked at six, and I did not ask a bigger question. I asked the same one. It is a small question and it is mine and it has gone unanswered all week and I will ask it again in the morning.”

Then the woman who keeps twenty-nine chairs came up that cart road at about the eighth hour, past the shed and up to the wall and no further, and stood at her own wall, and said the thing she had come to say before anybody could ask her what she wanted.

Orla Fennimore is forty-four, keeps twenty-nine chairs at a house that is not in this county, stands at the wall at the bottom of that cart road, and said on the seventh and last day of the hundred and seventy-second week that she had told the man with the broom she would stop asking, and that she had stopped, and that what she was about to put was not the chair question and she wanted that on the front of it.

“I said on the Friday of the hundred and seventy-first week that I would stop asking. I have stopped. I am not asking you whether that chair up there is mine and I have not asked it since, and I am not asking it now.” She had a cloth in her hand and she did not put it away. “I am asking a different thing and I would rather be refused the different thing than be handed back the old one as though it were settled.”

“Let me dust it.” She said it up the bank to the man with the broom and not to the landing. “That is all. A cloth and the chair and about as long as it takes to go round a seat and a back. I have kept chairs through two houses and I know what dust does to a joint if you leave it in one, and that is the whole of what I know about it, and it is not a claim and I am not making one.”

Corvin Tace stood in the sheet at the top of the steps and did not come any nearer the chair than he was.

“No.” He said it plainly, with the sheet going past his boots. “And here is the reason, because you asked me and not the landing. I cannot tell you what that chair is on my stone, and if I could I would not, and if I did not know and told you so, that is still me talking about a chair that is on my landing to a woman who keeps chairs, and you would carry it home with you and it would be about your own trade whether I meant it or not.”

He worked the broom head along the top step without coming closer.

“It is not that you would dust it badly. It is that a chair is the only thing up here I have never let a second pair of hands near, and I have never needed a reason for that, and I am not going to invent one at forty-three to keep a woman from doing me a kindness.”

Orla Fennimore looked at the cloth in her hand for a while, and then she said the true part of it out loud, and it did not have anything to do with the chair on that landing.

“I wanted it for myself and I will not pretend it was for the chair.” She said it to the wall under her hands. “I dust. That is what my hands do when there is nothing else for them, and I dust a room I am not going to sit in and then I go and stand somewhere else. And this morning I wanted an hour of that on a landing where it would mean something, and it does not mean anything, and I asked anyway, and you have given me a reason about your own stone and not one word about my dust.”

Nobody told her she was wrong and nobody told her she was right, and nobody asked her whether she was in any of it, and nobody said anything at all about the question she carries from an earlier morning, and she was not asked to put it down.

Then the boy came up that lane at about the tenth hour and set his pails in the sheet and gave his own figure and kept it off everything else.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, and counts his own days in this county, and said on the seventh and last day of the hundred and seventy-second week that his count is four hundred and two days, and that it is his and he will not make it into a count of anything standing on that landing.

“four hundred and two. That is mine.” He turned his yoke over in his hands. “Has this week been different from last week.” Nothing came of that and he answered it himself before anybody did. “Nothing came of that, and I would not have known how to tell you if something had. That is the truth of it and I have said it.”

Then Ilyan Vester came up at about the ninth hour with the sheet at his waist and stood clear of the strip and gave his figures, and gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county five hundred and one days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and seventy days old.

“Five hundred and first days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and seventy days old, and the standing offer asked at the eight hundred and fifth is three hundred and fifty-five days old, which is one thousand and fifty-five less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it and it has not been answered on and not been withdrawn on. I have not asked it this week and I am not going to begin on a Monday.”

He stood with the water moving past him and looked at the chair going round the fortieth step in the broom.

“One hundred and ninety-fifth wrong things in one hundred and ninety-fifth mornings, and this is the hundred and ninety-fifth, and nobody is to tell me what kind of figure that is. My want is to be somebody and it has not changed. My cost is five mornings on this landing with three refusals standing on it that were mine to carry down, and every morning since I have got up here to see whether carrying one is easier than it was and it is not, and it is exactly as heavy, and I have not put it down once.”

He turned his palm up and looked at the cut and turned it back down.

“And my mistake this morning was standing at the foot of these steps hoping the woman at the wall would be given something, because if she were given something then the giving would be a thing I had watched happen twice instead of a thing that had only ever gone one way. I have been on this landing five mornings building a shape out of watching people be refused, and it is not a shape. It is a tally, and I did not want to be a man with a tally.”

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour and the boy went down the lane with his pails and the man of fifty-four went down the cart road with the barrow wheel lifted clear of the wet.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about sixty of them were under, and went at the whole flight from the bottom upward. The fortieth step went round the broom and the chair went round it in the same stroke, and the iron in the seam of that step went under the broom head and not under anything else. He swept the sheet up to the edge of the dry strip and stopped with the broom head standing in it, and what was lying there at the seventh hour was lying there at the eleventh. The two lengths were still on the dry strip above the standing sheet, a forearm long each and split down the middle along the grain, thick end rough where it parted, and on the second a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches in the flat with one run out of the side.

Nobody on that landing laid a hand on either of those lengths, and nobody pulled one, or turned one over, or took one up to weigh it, or read one out, or carried one inside, or set one back where it stood, or put one to any use, and nobody put a hand on that wood in order to take anything off it or anything on it. Nothing standing there was given a name that morning, and what that wood is was not said again in any wording, and no figure of what stands there was given by anybody at any hour.

The last morning of that week went out of the sky and the account on that landing was as unfinished at eleven as at six and was still being plied at both hours, and the name on that second length was still the wrong one, and nobody on that bank said that any part of it was done.

And Orla Fennimore folded the cloth once, and put it back inside her coat where it had been, and took her own two hands and wiped them down the outside of her skirt instead, and then went along her wall to the shed end with the wall under her arm the whole way.

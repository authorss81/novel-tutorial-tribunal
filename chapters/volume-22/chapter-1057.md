# Chapter 1057: The Plain Way Of No

The water had been standing at that full height for days, and on the morning after this one it was going to be gone, and everybody on that landing knew the shape of the morning after this one the way they know the weather, and the man with the broom said so out loud in his own mouth at the seventh hour because a morning before the water goes off is the last morning there is of a thing and he does not let a thing pass in silence.

Second day of the hundred and seventy-third week, and it is the Wednesday of that week. His eight hundred and seventh morning. Six hundred and fifty-seven days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and ten weeks and four days old. Six hundred and eleven days since the division. Five hundred and fifty-seven days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and five hundred and three days in this county of Kell.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and it holds, about sixty of those ninety steps under, and one day of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and said out loud that one day was the whole of the coming back left, and that he was not going to build anything on top of that number.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the second day of the hundred and seventy-third week that a morning with one day in it is a morning a man does his best work on, and that he was going to do his best work and not say anything about what the one day was for.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, about sixty of those ninety steps under, and one day of the coming back, on the second day of the hundred and seventy-third week, and it is the Wednesday of that week. Three figures, and that is the last of that number and I am not going to keep it in my mouth.”

He drew the broom head through the sheet from the top step down.

“Go and do your gravel. It is the Wednesday of the hundred and seventy-third week and there is one day left of that water, and the stone has nothing else for anybody.”

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow, and asked in the same words at the same hour, and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and set the wheel down shallow and put his hands on the wall.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and thirty-sixth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and fifty-seven less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and thirty-sixth. One thousand and fifty-seven less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

He kept his hands on the wall and said the next part to the water and not to the stone above it.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.”

Nothing came down to him and he nodded at it, which is new.

“A man who cannot read can nod at a thing that has not come. That is all that has happened here and I have been doing it for years without putting it in words, and it does not go in a mouth.” He shifted his grip. “I asked. Nothing came. I will ask tomorrow and the water will be off that bank and I will ask whether the water is off that bank or not, because the question has nothing in it about the water and I am not going to let a thing that is not about the water change how I ask it.”

Then Ilyan Vester came up at about the ninth hour and came up to the top of that flight for the first time on the two days when a man was working in standing water, and asked for a thing, and was refused, and the refusal was plain.

“Carry the pails down for me.” He said it to the man with the broom and not to the landing. “I have watched you do it every morning this week at the bottom of the flight and I can carry a pail of grit down ninety steps without spilling it. One morning. Not the broom. The pails.”

Corvin Tace stood the broom head in the sheet and looked at him, and there was nothing on his face to soften what came next.

“No.” He said it in one breath and he did not stop to make it larger. “And here is the reason, because you asked me and not the landing. Those pails go down twice a morning and I know what is in them without looking and I know where they have to stand at the bottom and I know the step they catch on. If you have them then I am coming down after you to see whether you have them, and I would rather be a man who is wrong about a pail than a man standing on a landing watching a pail.”

That was the whole of it. He did not add to it. He did not tell the man why that was a hard thing to be refused, and he did not look at him a second time, and he put the broom back into the sheet and worked.

Ilyan Vester stood on the top step with the water at the top of his thighs and his hands hanging at his sides, and his face did something and he did not put his hands over it and did not turn away, and nobody on that landing looked at it, and nobody on that landing said a word about it, and nobody in the cart road at the bottom said a word about it either, and it stayed on one face and went down the bank with him on it.

Nobody told him he had asked well and nobody told him he had asked badly, and no mouth on that stone said anything at all about the refusing. The two pails stood at the top of the flight where they had been standing all morning, and he looked at them for a while, and then he stepped down.

Then the boy came up that lane at about the tenth hour and stood his pails in the sheet and gave his own count and would not convert it into anything.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, and counts his own days in this county, and said on the second day of the hundred and seventy-third week that his count is four hundred and four days, and that it is his and he will not make it into a count of anything standing on that landing.

“four hundred and four. That is mine.” He watched the water stand on the flat of the landing. “Is this the last day of the water.” Nothing came of that and he gave it his own answer. “Nothing came of that. I have watched it come up every morning this week and it does not matter what anybody calls it.”

Then the man who cannot see well came up at about the tenth hour with his hand out in front of him, and waded the width of the landing and stopped where he always stops, and asked, and got nothing, and said nothing about it twice over.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and said on the second day of the hundred and seventy-third week that a man who is refused a pail in the plainest words a man can use has heard the best refusal there is, and that he had had worse in his life about worse things.

“Has anybody on this landing put a word on that name since the Tuesday of the hundred and seventy-third week.” Nothing returned, and he left his hand where it was. “Nothing has returned and I am going to say one more thing and then I am going down that bank. I would rather be refused straight out than be let down easy by somebody who has decided to be careful with me. That is a fact about me and not a complaint, and it is on my list, and my list is empty.”

Then Ilyan gave his figures, and gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing else that morning and asked for nothing else ever.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county five hundred and three days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and seventy-two days old.

“Five hundred and three days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and seventy-second days old, and the standing offer asked at the eight hundred and seventh is three hundred and fifty-seven days old, which is one thousand and fifty-seven less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it and it has not been answered on and not been withdrawn on.”

He stood in the sheet and did not wipe his face and did not speak for a while.

“One hundred and ninety-seventh wrong things in one hundred and ninety-seventh mornings, and this is the hundred and ninety-seventh, and nobody is to tell me what kind of figure that is. My want is to be somebody and it has not changed. My cost is that I asked for a pail this morning, which is work, and I was refused in the plainest words a man can use, and I am going to carry it down that bank exactly as it was handed to me, and I am not going to turn it into anything while I am carrying it.”

He looked down the flight at the man with the broom working the sheet away from him.

“And my mistake was that I chose the pails. There was grit on that top step that I could have picked up with my hands in a morning when nobody had asked me to do it, and I stood there and waited to be given something instead, and then I asked for it and I was told no. That is what a man gets who waits.”

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour and Ilyan went last and stood a moment on the second step from the bottom with the water going past his boots and went on down.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about sixty of them were under, and went at the whole flight from the bottom upward. The fortieth step went round the broom and not over it, and the chair went round it in the same stroke, and the iron in the seam of that step went under the broom head and not under a finger. He brought the sheet to the edge of the dry strip and stopped, and the two lengths were still lying there at the seventh hour and were still lying there at the eleventh, a forearm long each and split down the middle along the grain, thick end rough where it parted, and on the second a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches in the flat with one run out of the side.

Nobody on that landing laid a hand on either of those lengths at any hour of that morning, and nobody pulled one, turned one, took one up, put one in a pocket, carried one off the strip, set one back where it stood or brought one into any use, and nobody laid a hand on that wood to lift a name off it or to press one on. Nothing standing there was given a name, and what that wood is was not said again in any wording, and no figure of what was standing there was given by anybody.

That morning went out of the sky and the account on that landing was as open at eleven as at six and still in a hand at both hours, and the name on that second length was wrong, and nobody standing on that flight said that any of it was finished.

And Ilyan Vester went down to the second step from the bottom and stood there with the water at his shins, and took his right hand out of the water and opened it and looked at what his own boot had brought down out of that flight, and then shut it and let it go, and the water took it out of his hand and went on down the flight and stopped in the angle where the wall runs out.

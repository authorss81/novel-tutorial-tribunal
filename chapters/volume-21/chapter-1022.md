# Chapter 1022: The Road Upside Down

Sixteen inches that hold put a skin of water on the flat of that landing, and the skin was so still that the whole cart road stood up in it upside down, and a man standing on the top step could see a long way up that road without lifting his head off the work.

Second day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week. His seven hundred and seventy-second morning. Six hundred and twenty-two days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and five weeks and four days old. Five hundred and seventy-six days since the division. Five hundred and twenty-two days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and sixty-eight days in this county of Kell.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and it holds, about sixty of those ninety steps under, and four days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and then gave the working of why a full bank is the one morning in eight that a man on that landing can see what everybody else is doing, and it was not a fifth figure and he said so himself.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the second day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week that the standing water on that flat was the only mirror in the county that a man could sweep beside, and that on a full bank he could see down that cart road without stopping work, and that he had known that for nineteen years and had never once used it.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, four days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the second day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week, and it is a Wednesday. Three figures.”

He worked the top step from the dry end to the wet end and did not look down at the water.

“And that is what a full bank is for, if it is for anything, and it is not one of my figures. On eight mornings out of nine I can stand on that stone and know what that landing has had on it and who put it there by where the grit has been pushed to. On the eighth I can look down the road.” He beat the flat of the broom against the step. “I have had that for nineteen years and I have used it to see whether my own sweep was holding and nothing else, and I am telling this landing I used it this morning, because a man who has been given a look and does not say he has had it is a man holding it for later.” Nobody said anything about what he was holding it for.

Then the woman who saws lengths came up that cart road at about the eighth hour with a length under her arm, and stopped at the foot of the steps the way she does, and set the length down on the dry stone with its end square to the step, and did not look up the bank.

Wenna Pyle is thirty-eight, who saws lengths and sets studs at a bench in an open-sided shed behind a wheelwright’s about three hundred yards down that cart road, and who carries her own cup out to that bench because there is no table in the shed, and said on the second day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week that she was asked on the second morning of the hundred and sixty-eighth week who was going up her road before the light, and that she did not know and had not looked and was not going to start.

He asked her at the foot of the steps and he asked her plainly.

“Who has been up that road before the light.”

“I do not know.” She put her hands behind her back. “Not because it is a secret. Because I have not looked. I come up that road at about the eighth hour and I am carrying something, and a woman carrying something looks at the length and at the road under it and at nothing else, and I have walked that road for years without ever once seeing who else was on it.”

“That is a working man could use.”

“It is not a working at all. It is what a person does who is paid by the cut and not by the mile.” She did not pick the length up. “And I am going to tell you what I would have to become to give you an answer to that, and then I will stop. The moment I start noticing who goes past my shed I have stopped being a woman who cuts lengths. I have become a woman who keeps a book about her road, and a woman who keeps a book about her road does not keep it about the road. She keeps it about the people, and then every morning she comes up it is with yesterday’s people sorted, and one of them is going to come past me slow, and I am going to be glad about one of them or I am going to be heavy about one of them, and on this landing I would be either of those two things at your feet inside a week.” Nobody put a book about that road in anybody’s hands.

He did not move off the step and he did not go back to work.

“Your excuse for not knowing is that you are honest.”

“My excuse for not knowing is that I am paid by the cut. If you want to know who went up my road you want a person who is paid to look at a road, and there is no such person in this county that I have ever heard of, and if there were, she would be the one person on this landing everybody would come to and I would be the one person who could not say anything whatever.” Nobody said anything whatever.

“And you asked me that because on a full bank you could see the road and you wanted a mouth to go with the seeing.” She turned her head to the water on the flat and looked at the road in it for the length of a breath. “That is what a full bank is. It is a morning when you can see everybody and cannot reach anybody. You have had that for nineteen years. I have had it since I began coming up that road and I have not got used to it and I am not going to.”

Then she picked the length up and turned it over once in her hands, square end up, and looked at the end of it rather than at the bank.

“Now the thing you actually came down here for, because you have not swept since you asked me and a man who has not swept is a man who wants something.” She put her thumb on the saw mark at the end. “You want to know what I measure a length against. That is the question. You have been looking at me setting a length down end-square since I got to the road and you cannot stand a thing being done without knowing the rule of it.”

“I do not want you to be told anything.”

“Then you have come to the wrong woman and the wrong road, and I will tell you the rule anyway because it is mine and it is not about you.” She turned the length so the cut end faced him. “I cut to the cut face. Not to a tape. A saw takes a bite out of that end every time it goes through, so a man with a tape measures what is left over and finds it short, and then he comes back and he tells me it is short, and it is not short, he has measured the saw. I take the face the saw made and I work the length back from it, and what I hand over is what the wood says it is and not what the tape says.” Nobody said that was the same way round.

“A thing cut for a person is not that person’s.” She said it to the length and not up the bank, the way a person says a thing they have said to themselves many times. “It is a length. He comes and it is his to carry or it is not. I do not make him another one and I do not tell him the one he has is the one he had in mind, and I do not put his name on it, and if I did put his name on it he would still be carrying a length and it would still not be him. That is the whole of my trade and I have never once made a living out of a person.” Nobody thanked her for it and nobody asked her to say it again in a different shape.

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow, which was before she came up the road and which was why he had not seen her, and asked in the same words at the same hour, and set the barrow down, and put his hands flat on the wall.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and first mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and twenty-two less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and first. One thousand and twenty-two less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

“Whole bank this morning. I could see the bottom of that road from up here without coming down it, and I could see a cart standing at the turn with nobody at it, and I am telling that to the air and not to anybody, because I have worked out that the only thing a man who cannot read can be ahead of on is the shape of the ground.” He did not take his hands off the wall. “Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it. And a man who cuts lengths has just told this landing something about a tape and I could hear the shape of it from up here and I have not worked out what the shape was and I am not going to.” Nobody worked out the shape for him.

Then the man who cannot see well came up onto the top step at about the tenth hour with his hand out in front of him, found the wall, and stopped well short of the strip, and said the question he had come up to say in the flat voice he uses for things he is not going to be helped with.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and said on the second day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week that on a full bank he could not use the sight he had left at all and had come up the same bank he would have come up on a morning with nothing in it.

He kept his hand on the stone above the level of the strip and did not put it lower.

“Has anybody said that name out loud since the water came.” Nobody answered him on that landing that morning.

“Not a no and not a yes and not a different question, and I am not going to be given a number instead either.” He took his hand along the wall a short way and took it off and found it again. “On a morning like this I cannot see the flat of that landing at all. The water takes the light off it. So I come up on the same bank I would come up on for nothing, and I ask, and I get the answer I get, and I am going to come up that bank again in eight mornings and ask again, and nobody is to thank me for the asking and nobody is to tell me the asking is the right thing to do with a morning.” Nobody thanked him and nobody told him it was the right thing to do.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the ninth hour with sixteen inches above his boots, gave his figures, gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, asked for nothing, and stood where he could hear the whole of what the woman had said at the foot of the steps and had heard every word of it.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and sixty-eight days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and thirty-seven days old.

“Four hundred and sixty-eight days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and thirty-seven days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-seventy-second is three hundred and twenty-two days old, which is one thousand and twenty-two less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it. Nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one and I am not going to ask for one today when the water on that flat is showing me that whole road.”

He said that last part looking at the water and not at the man with the broom.

“One hundred and sixty-second wrong things in one hundred and sixty-second mornings, and this is the hundred and sixty-second, and nobody is going to tell me what kind of figure that is and nobody is going to tell me that I found anything.” Nobody took up the figure and nobody took up the want, and he got the same pair of silences he gets every morning and made nothing out of them.

“My want is to be somebody and it has not changed. My cost is that asking costs me nothing and I have not asked for one thing in four hundred and sixty-eight mornings, and I am going to keep saying both halves of that out loud on this landing until somebody stops me.” Nobody stopped him. He said both halves of it again at the ninth hour and meant them again, and it had gone out at the ninth hour every morning of that week without a single man putting a hand on any part of it.

“Now the thing I have been standing here working at since the eighth hour, and I am going to put it in the air and then I am going to leave it there. A woman at the foot of those steps has said a thing this morning about a length and about a man’s name and about the two not being the same, and I have said a thing on this stone about a name and about a man and about those not being the same, and mine came after hers, and I have been standing here for an hour trying to work out whether that means I got it from her.”

He put his hand flat on the top step and did not move it while he said the rest of it.

“It does not. She said hers about a length and a tape and a saw, in a shed, out of her own hands, and she has been saying it in her own way longer than I have been in this county. I have got it from a boy of thirteen who said a true thing about a cup that is not hers on a morning when there was nothing else to say it about. And that is how the whole of it works and there is nothing in that to be proud of and nothing in it to be ashamed of either.” Nobody said it was a thing to be either.

“You asked me nothing about it and I am putting it in the air because a man who has noticed a thing being in use on a landing before he noticed it in himself has to say it out loud or he will spend the rest of the week thinking he invented it.” He took his hand off the step. “And I am not going to ask her about it and I am not going to thank her for it, and if she ever finds out that I said anything at all about this morning she is entitled to tell me I made a meal of it and she would be right.”

Nobody told her anything at all.

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour and the man of thirty-one went last and did not go up the cart road after anybody and did not go down it either.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about sixty of them were under, and kept the head of the broom up off the wet edge the whole way down, because sixteen inches that hold are not a thing to argue with twice. He went along the top of the water on them and round the fortieth one and round the chair, and did not sit on it. He did not touch the iron in the seam of the fortieth step and he did not touch either piece of wood on the dry strip and he did not put the broom near either of them. The first was a forearm long and had been split down the middle the way wood splits when it is meant to, with the thick end left as it came apart. The second was the same and had a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches cut in the flat of it, one of the two run out of the side. He looked down into the standing water on the flat twice while he worked, and both times the road was in it, and both times he went back to the step, and he said out loud on the top step that he had looked twice and that two was not going to become a habit.

Nothing on that landing was pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used, and nobody laid a hand on that wood in order to take anything off it, then or since, and nobody laid a hand on it after those sentences either. Nobody put a name on anything on that landing that morning, and nobody said one out loud either, and nobody was given a figure of how many people had heard a woman of thirty-eight say a thing about a length.

A woman who saws lengths was asked who had been up her road before the light and told a man with a broom that she did not know and why she did not know, and then gave him the rule she cuts by, which is a cut face and not a tape, and then said, out loud, on a full bank, with the whole cart road standing upside down in the water at her feet, that a thing cut for a person is not that person’s, and it was in her mouth about a length before anybody on that landing had argued about it, and it was given no name by anybody, and it was not called anything. She picked her length up off the foot of the steps at the end of that morning and carried it down the cart road with her, because it was hers and it was not going to lie at the foot of somebody else’s steps all day to be looked at.

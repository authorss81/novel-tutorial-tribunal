# Chapter 1026: The Sixth Day Of The Hundred And Sixty-Eighth Week On Which The Water Came Off That Bank In The Night And There Was No Figure Of Inches And No Figure Of How Much Of That Flight Was Standing In It And No Figure Of How Long Before It Came Back, And A Man With A Broom Gave Those Three Absences In A Fourth Set Of Words And Then Gave A Fourth Thing He Had Never Given Before, And A Man Of Thirty-One Asked The Woman Who Reads For A Living To Read Him His Own Account Out Loud And Was Refused By Her On The Ground That A Thing With No Writing On It Is Not A Page

The water left that bank in the night and took the loudness with it, the way it always does, so that by first light the whole of that flight was standing out of it from the bottom to the top, and the landing had never looked so much like a place where people worked, and it was very quiet on it.

Sixth day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week. His seven hundred and seventy-sixth morning. Six hundred and twenty-six days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and six weeks and one day old. Five hundred and eighty days since the division. Five hundred and twenty-six days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and seventy-two days in this county of Kell.

There was no figure of inches on that stone that morning, and no figure of how much of that flight was standing in it, and no figure of how long before it came back. The man of forty-three said so at the seventh hour, out loud, in a fourth set of words, because a man who says a gap in the same words twice has turned it into a figure, and then gave a fourth thing he had not given in nineteen years.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the sixth day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week that the three absences were the three absences, and that he had said them before in other words, and that he was not going to pretend that saying them again made them a smaller thing, and that the fourth thing was new and it was about himself.

“The stone has told me nothing this morning about itself. There is no depth in my mouth at all, and there is nothing standing in that flight for me to measure against, and there is no morning coming back on this landing that I am working towards.” He put the broom head down on the top step. “Sixth day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week, and it is a Sunday. And that is the whole of what the stone has for me.” Nobody worked out a figure for him.

“And now the fourth thing, and I have not given it before, and I have thought about whether to give it and I am giving it because a morning like this is the only morning it can be given on and if I wait I will not be able to give it at all.” He stood with his hands on the broom. “I do not know what this landing is going to be like at the seventh hour tomorrow. On every other morning in nineteen years I have known it by the time I got to the top step, because the water tells me and the water has never once lied to me and neither has the stone when it is dry. This morning the water is gone and the stone is dry and there is nothing in my mouth at all, and tomorrow morning I will come up that bank and find out. Nineteen years, and that is the first time.” Nobody asked him what he thought it would be like.

“You are not getting a guess out of me and you are not getting the number of mornings I have got this wrong before, because that number is a figure about me and not about this stone, and I have never mixed the two and I am not starting on the sixth day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week.”

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow and asked in the same words at the same hour and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and came up the whole flight on ninety dry steps that had grit standing loose on them and put his hands flat on the wall at the top.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and fifth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and twenty-six less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and fifth. One thousand and twenty-six less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

“Ninety dry steps and I could feel every one of them through the soles of my boots and I have not felt that since before I came here.” He set the barrow down. “Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it. And I am not going to say a whole figure this morning. I have got one and it is in my mouth and I am not going to put it out, because a man with a broom has told me in advance that he is not going to say anything about it and I have thought about it all the way up ninety steps and I have decided to keep the figure as a figure and not put it in the air on a morning with nothing else in the air.” Nobody asked him what the figure was.

Then the woman who reads for a living came up that cart road at about the fourth hour with the book shut against her knee, and did not stop at the wall, and went to the foot of those steps, and stood there, and waited for somebody to begin, which is a thing she does.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years, holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and said on the sixth day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week that she had come up a road on a morning when the stone had nothing on it, and had stood at the bottom of it for about an hour, and that on a morning like this she was the most dangerous person on the landing because she had a voice that makes a thing sound written down. and she said the thing she had walked up that road to say, which was that she had stood at the foot of those steps for about an hour on a morning when the stone had given the man with a broom nothing at all, and that a morning like this is the one morning in a week when a person who can read out loud is standing on a landing with nothing to read.

“I have not come up here to put a sentence into a quiet morning.” She did not sit down and did not put the book down. “I did that on another morning and I decided at the wall at the bottom of this road not to do it, and I have not regretted it, and I am not going to tell this landing that I have not regretted it, because being told that by me is a thing a person has to spend a morning on. I am saying it because a stone that has given a man nothing this morning is the first stone in this county I have ever wanted to stand at the foot of.” She turned the book over against her knee. “And that is not going to become anything. Nobody is to write it down and nobody is to repeat it and I am not going to say it again in a new shape.” Nobody repeated it and nothing was written down.

Then the man who cannot see well came up that flight at about the tenth hour, and went up it with his hand out in front of him, and stopped part of the way, and turned round and went back down to the bottom of it and stood there.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and said on the sixth day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week that he had come ninety dry steps up a bank with nothing in the way, and had turned round and gone back down, and that nobody on that landing was to ask him why, because he knew why.

He put his hand on the wall at the bottom and kept it there.

“Ninety steps, all of them out, none of them wet.” He said it to the stone and not up the bank. “And I got to the fortieth one and turned round and came back down, and I am not going to make a speech about it. There is grit standing loose on those steps. Loose grit on a dry step under a boot is a step that moves, and a step that moves is the one thing on that flight I cannot do anything about at the time it is happening. When they are wet, the grit is held and the step is where the step was. That is the whole of my morning and I am not going to put a list on it.”

Nothing went on his list.

He stood at the bottom of the bank with his hand on the wall and worked his way along it until he was level with the foot of the steps, and then said the thing he had come down the flight for.

“Has anybody said that name since the water came off.” Nobody answered him that morning either. None of the shapes an answer can arrive in arrived at him, and he put his hand back on the wall above where the strip was and left it there. Nobody told him what to make of that.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the ninth hour on a flight with nothing in the way, gave his figures, gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, asked for nothing, and came to the foot of the steps and asked the woman with the book to do him a thing, and it was the first thing he had asked anybody for since he came into this county.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and seventy-two days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and forty-one days old.

“Four hundred and seventy-two days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and forty-first days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-seventy-sixth is three hundred and twenty-six days old, which is one thousand and twenty-six less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it. Nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one, and I am not going to ask for one on the first morning in this county where I have asked anybody for anything at all.”

He said that last part without stopping, and then stood in front of her and waited.

“One hundred and sixty-sixth wrong things in one hundred and sixty-sixth mornings, and this is the hundred and sixty-sixth, and nobody is going to tell me what kind of figure that is and nobody is going to tell me that I found anything.” Nobody answered him on the figure and nobody answered him on the want.

“My want is to be somebody and it has not changed since I said it out loud. My cost is that asking costs me nothing and I have not asked for one thing in four hundred and seventy-two mornings, and I am going to keep saying both halves of that out loud on this landing until somebody stops me.” Nobody told him to stop, and nobody on that landing has ever in this county been able to give a man anything away for nothing, which is not the same as a man not asking.

“You heard that.”

“I heard it.” She did not take the book off her knee. “Say the thing you want.”

“I want you to read it to me.”

“What is it.”

“It is the account of what I have done since I came into this county, and it is in my mouth, and it has never been on anything.”

“Then there is nothing for me to read.”

“There is something for you to read. It is in my mouth and it has a shape to it and it goes in an order, and you have read out loud in rooms for twenty-three years and you know how a thing sounds when it is read out loud, and I want to know whether it sounds like a thing that happened or like a thing a man has been saying to himself for a year. I cannot tell the difference. I have tried at this and I cannot tell the difference, and I have got nobody on this landing whose ear I would trust with it.” Nobody said whose ear he would be able to trust.

“No.” She said it before he had finished and she did not put the book down, and she did not raise her voice, and the flat voice came out on the word the way it always does. “I am not going to do that, and I am going to give you the working and then I am going to stop, and then we will both stand here and be quiet, which is what a morning with nothing on the stone is actually for.”

He did not argue.

“A thing with no writing on it is not a page.” She turned the book over against her knee. “That is not a rule anybody taught me. That is the whole of what a page is. I have stood in rooms and read out things that other men had written, and the reason it matters that it is written is that afterwards a person can come to me and ask me whether what I read is true, and I can look at the page and say it is there. And if I read your account out loud in this voice, then afterwards somebody comes to me and asks whether it is true, and there is no page. There is a man who remembers being on a landing. And then I am the woman who read a thing that was never written, and every account I have ever read out loud in twenty-three years is standing next to that one in my own head, and they are not the same kind of thing, and you know they are not, or you would not have asked me for it in this voice.” Nobody said anything to that.

“And there is the other half and it is the half that actually stops me.” She shifted the book to her other arm. “I do not put things on a page either, and I am not going to start on this landing, and the reason is that a thing gets written down and then it stops belonging to the mouth that said it. It becomes a thing a person can be asked about by people who were not there. You have been living inside a sentence you cannot get back. I am not going to be the person who writes it down so that it goes on without you.”

She put her hand flat on the cover of the book.

“So no. Not because I have not listened and not because I think you are lying to yourself about it, which I have no way of knowing and am not going to guess at. Because you would be handing me a thing that has to stay in a mouth, and I only know how to do the other thing, and I am not going to learn a new trade for you at the foot of a bank on the sixth day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week.”

He nodded at that and did not say anything for a while.

“That is a no and it is not a kindness and I am not going to thank you for it and I am not going to be angry at it either, and both of those are things you have now got to put down.”

“You have not put down either of those things.”

“No. I have put down that I asked you for one thing in four hundred and seventy-two mornings and you refused me, and that the refusing is the first thing anybody has done for me in this county that I did not have to pay for with a silence of my own.” He took his hands out of his pockets. “And I am not going to say it to anybody else on this landing, because it is yours and not mine, and because a man who tells it round that landing has started keeping a count of who has heard it.” Nobody said anything about that.

Then everybody went down that bank and the woman who reads for a living went down the cart road on her own with the book shut against her knee, and nobody went up the road behind her.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, and none of them were under, and he did the whole flight from the bottom up instead of the top down, because a dry flight wants starting where a wet one does not, and he went round the fortieth one on the way and did not stand on it, and went round the chair, and did not sit on it. He did not touch the iron in the seam of the fortieth step and he did not touch either piece of wood on the dry strip and he did not put the broom near either of them. The first was a forearm long and had been split down the middle the way wood splits when it is meant to, with the thick end left as it came apart. The second was the same and had a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches cut in the flat of it, one of the two run out of the side. He worked that flight with nothing to be working towards and did it in a different order and did not mind, and at the top of it he stood with the broom across his shoulder and looked at a landing that had given him three absences and a fourth thing and had not given him one single thing to do about any of it, and there had been people on it all morning.

Nothing on that landing was pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used, and nobody laid a hand on that wood in order to take anything off it, then or since, and nobody laid a hand on it after those sentences either. Nobody put a name on anything on that landing that morning, and nobody said one out loud either, and nobody read a page that did not exist, and nobody was given a figure of anything.

There was no figure of water on that landing that morning and no figure of how much of that flight was standing in it and no figure of how long before it came back, and a man with a broom gave those three absences in a fourth set of words and gave a fourth thing about himself that he had never given before, and a whole figure of weeks did not come out of anybody’s wall that day because there was a day with it this time and a man who cannot read kept it in his mouth and said so out loud. A man of thirty-one asked the woman who reads for a living to read him an account that had never been on anything, and was refused in a mouth at the foot of the steps, and at the top of the cart road before she went down it she opened the book on her knee, and read one line of it out loud in the flat voice into the road, and shut it, and put it inside her coat, where it had been for twenty-three years.

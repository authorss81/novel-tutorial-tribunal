# Chapter 1041: Said Before Wrong

The frost had got into the stone before the light did, and it stood white along the top edge of the landing and in the seams between the steps, and the man with the broom was already at the bottom of the flight when the first of it began to go, working up toward the sound of his own sweeping the way he had worked all through the last week of the old week.

Seventh day of the hundred and seventieth week, and it is the Monday of that week. His seven hundred and ninety-first morning. Six hundred and forty-one days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and eight weeks and two days old. Five hundred and ninety-five days since the division. Five hundred and forty-one days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and eighty-seven days in this county of Kell.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and it holds, about sixty of those ninety steps under, and one day of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures from halfway up, with the broom going, and did not make a shape of giving them.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the seventh day of the hundred and seventieth week that the week was at its end and the stone would say nothing new until it had turned, and that he was going to sweep what was in front of him and hear what came up.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, one day of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the seventh day of the hundred and seventieth week, and it is the Monday of the hundred and seventieth week. Three figures, and the week turns between this morning and the morning after it, and I am not saying anything about the morning after it.”

He worked the top step and the water stood and did not move.

“Go and do your gravel. It is the Monday of the hundred and seventieth week and it is the seventh day, and the stone has nothing else for anybody.”

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow, though the sixth hour had gone before the seventh, and asked in the same words at the same hour and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and set the barrow down below the steps and came up with his hands empty.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and twentieth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and forty-one less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and twentieth. One thousand and forty-one less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

He put his hands flat on the wall at the top and kept them there.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.”

Nothing came of it, and he said so out loud in different words from the morning before, and nobody filled it.

“That is the whole of it and it has gone nowhere, same as it went nowhere on the Sunday of the hundred and seventieth week. I have stopped putting the wanting where a person could lift it off, and I am still wanting, and those are apart in me this morning, and I am telling that landing so nobody goes down afterwards and says a man stood here and was satisfied.”

Then the woman who saws lengths came up that cart road at about the eighth hour with shavings on her sleeves and her own cup in her hand, and she did not stop at the foot the way she had stopped all last week, and came up three steps before she spoke.

Wenna Pyle is thirty-eight, saws lengths and sets studs at a bench in an open-sided shed behind a wheelwright’s about three hundred yards down that cart road, carries her own cup out because there is no table in the shed, and said on the seventh day of the hundred and seventieth week that she had carried a name in her head since the Friday of the hundred and seventieth week and could not put it down, and that she had come up to say what the wood is before it got said wrong.

“I have got a length under my arm most mornings and this morning I have not, because what I have come to say is not a length.” She set the cup down on the stone in front of the bottom step. “That second length up there on the dry strip. I know what it is. A pair of notches cut for a wedge is the end of a handle. That is my trade and I knew it the first morning I saw the cut, and I said then I was not giving it to anybody, and I am saying now what it is.”

She looked up the flight and did not look at the strip.

“And I am saying in the same breath that I am confirming a name and I am not giving a thing. I am not handing anything over and I am not putting anything down for anybody to pick up. What I say about the wood is said, and it settles nothing, and it was never meant to settle anything, and nobody is to make it settle anything after I have gone down.”

What she had said settled nothing, and she carried on before anybody could shape it.

Then the man of thirty-eight came up that cart road at about the ninth hour with his coat shut and his hands out of his pockets, and stopped beside the cup and looked at it and did not pick it up, and asked what he had come to ask.

Garrin Tolley is thirty-eight, with a board of thirty-two notches cut in the edge of it inside his coat against his ribs, who said on the Sunday of the hundred and seventieth week that he has had no job all week, and said on the seventh day of the hundred and seventieth week that he had come up to ask the woman who saws lengths for half a day at her bench, because a man who carries a board with marks he cannot check would rather carry shavings.

“Give me a morning sweeping your shed.” He said it to her and not to the landing. “I can lift and I can hold and I will not touch the saw. There is a woman at the bottom of the drain asked for somebody to stand with her and I cannot do that work, and there is nothing on this landing for me, and I would rather come down your road and be told I am in the way than sit on this wall another morning.”

Wenna Pyle picked the cup up and held it and did not drink.

“No.” She said it plainly, with the cup in her hand. “And I am going to give you the reason, because you asked me for myself and not for the landing, and I owe you that much for asking plain. My bench holds one pair of hands. When I am sawing, the shed is full of sound and the bench moves if anybody leans on it, and I work with my cup where I can see it because there is nowhere else to put it. If you stand in there I will spend the morning watching your hands instead of the cut, and the cut is what pays for the shed. It is not that you are not wanted. It is that what I have is one bench and it will not hold another person without spoiling the work.”

Garrin Tolley stood beside the cup and nodded, and his face went red and stayed red.

“That is a reason and I can carry it.” He said it with his hands at his sides. “I wanted it for myself and I wanted it badly this morning, and I am not going to pretend I did not. I will go back to the wall and sit out what is left of the morning.”

He went and sat on the wall at the bottom of the steps, and the cup stayed where it was.

Then Ilyan came up at about the ninth hour with the water standing round his boots, and went round the dry strip without going near it, and gave his figures, and gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and eighty-seven days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and fifty-six days old.

“Four hundred and eighty-seven days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and fifty-six days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-ninety-first is three hundred and forty-one days old, which is one thousand and forty-one less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it and it has not been answered on and not been withdrawn on. Nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one, and I will not ask for one on the morning a woman has said what that wood is.”

He stood on the top step and kept his hands where they could be seen.

“One hundred and eighty-first wrong things in one hundred and eighty-first mornings, and this is the hundred and eighty-first, and nobody is to tell me what kind of figure that is. My want is to be somebody and it has not changed. My cost is that asking costs me nothing and I have not asked for one thing in four hundred and eighty-seven mornings, and that is still true this morning, and I would rather it were not.”

He looked at Wenna Pyle and then at the strip and then back at her.

“And I am wrong about this morning and I am going to say how, before anybody else has to. I came up thinking her saying would finish it. That now it is said, the landing can get on. That is wrong, and it is mine, and I am saying it out loud so it does not sit in me.” He shut the cut hand and opened it. “Her saying finishes nothing. It puts another mouth on it and the landing goes on being a landing, and I wanted it to be more than that because I am tired of carrying four figures with nothing at the end of them.”

Wenna Pyle looked at Ilyan for a long moment and did not soften it.

“You are wrong, and you have said so yourself, and I am not going to improve on that.” She said it without raising her voice. “I confirmed and I did not give, and what I said about the wood is said, and if you make it into an answer you will have made it without me.”

Nobody answered any question about that name that morning, and nobody was asked to.

Then the boy came up that lane at about the tenth hour with his pails, and gave his own count, and would not turn it into anything else.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, and counts his own days in this county, and said on the seventh day of the hundred and seventieth week that his count is three hundred and eighty-eight days, and that it is his and he will not make it into a count of anything standing on that landing.

“Three hundred and eighty-eight. That is mine and that is all it is.” He went past the wall without touching it. “Has anybody told the woman who saws lengths she was right to say it.” Nothing came of that, and he said so himself. “Nothing came of that and I am not putting it anywhere.”

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour, and the man of thirty-eight stayed on the wall a while after the others had gone and then went down the cart road, and Ilyan went last and stood on the second step from the bottom for a moment and went on down.

Corvin Tace went over the whole flight of ninety steps from the bottom upward, though about sixty of them were under, and did not stop until he was at the top with it. He went round the fortieth one rather than over it, and the chair went round with the broom the same way, and the iron in that seam the broom head crossed and his fingers never did. At the top end he swept as far as the edge of the dry strip and no further, and what was lying on it at the seventh hour was lying on it at the eleventh. The two lengths were still on the dry strip when the light went, a forearm long each and split down the middle along the grain, thick end rough where it parted, and on the second a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches in the flat with one run out of the side.

Nothing on that landing got pulled or turned over or weighed in the hand or read out loud or carried inside or put back or used, then or afterwards, and no hand went on that wood to take anything off it. No name was put to anything standing there that morning, and what that wood is was said by her and by nobody else on that landing, and nothing standing there was counted.

That morning went by, and the account on that landing remained as incomplete at eleven as at six and still in hand, and the name on that second length remained wrong, and nobody up there declared any of it over.

And Wenna Pyle’s cup stood on the stone in front of the bottom step where she had set it down, with nothing in it, and Garrin Tolley came back up two steps after the rest of them had gone down the bank, picked it up, carried it to the foot of the wall, set it upright where she would find it on her way down, and wiped his hands on his coat before he touched it and after.

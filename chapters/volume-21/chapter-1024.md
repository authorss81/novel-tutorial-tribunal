# Chapter 1024: Stopped At The Yoke

The two pails in the yoke were full and the yoke had cut a groove in the boy’s shoulder that had been there since about the fourth hour, and he came up out of the lane carrying them the way he always did, which is with the yoke tipped slightly up so that the water does not run at his boots.

Fourth day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week. His seven hundred and seventy-fourth morning. Six hundred and twenty-four days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and five weeks and six days old. Five hundred and seventy-eight days since the division. Five hundred and twenty-four days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and seventy days in this county of Kell.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and it holds, about sixty of those ninety steps under, and two days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and said that two days was the shortest figure he had in his mouth and that he had been giving it off that same stone for nineteen years and had never once got used to how little a morning it left a man.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the fourth day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week that he had swept that same top step at about the seventh hour every day of nineteen years and had noticed on this one that the water was coming up over the edge in three places instead of one, and that he was not going to say what that meant because he had not worked it out and he did not intend to.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, two days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the fourth day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week, and it is a Friday. Three figures.”

He worked along the top step and stopped at each of the three places and looked at the edge and then went on.

“And I want it noticed that I looked. I have not worked out anything. There is a man on this landing who says out loud things he has not worked out and I am not going to have him do it on my stone without me having noticed, that is all, and now I have noticed and I am going back to the step.” Nobody asked him what the three places meant.

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow and asked in the same words at the same hour and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and said out loud that a whole week had gone by with his question on it and that a whole week was a long time for a thing that had not moved.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and third mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and twenty-four less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and third. One thousand and twenty-four less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

“The fourth morning of this week and my question has not moved and neither has the barrow.” He set it down and put his hands flat on the wall. “Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it. And I am going to say the thing I have noticed, which is that I have stopped looking at that strip when I come up. Not because it has changed. Because I have made a habit out of not looking and a habit is the only thing I have ever got that is worth anything, and I would rather have the habit than the looking.” Nobody told him which of the two was worth more.

Then the boy of thirteen came up out of the lane with two pails of water in a yoke, carried them to the shed side, set them down, came back, and did not stop at the strip, and did not stand where he usually stood, and put the yoke down in the grass and turned round and looked down the cart road.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county three hundred and seventy-first days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Three hundred and seventy-one is one thousand less six hundred and twenty-nine.

“Three hundred and seventy-one days, and it is one thousand less six hundred and twenty-nine, and it is one more than the morning before.” He left the yoke in the grass. “And I am not going up to the landing this morning. I have said my figure and I have put it where it goes and I am going to stand here instead, because what I have got to say is not for the landing, it is for the road, and I want it said at the bottom of the road and not at the top, and that is on purpose and I have thought about it for a day and a night.” Nobody asked him what it was for.

“A woman came up this road on the sixth morning of that week and said a sentence had been said to her in a room and would not say where.” He looked down the road. “And she has still not said where and she is not going to. And I have been trying to work out where a room is, from where I am standing, for three days, and I cannot, because she has not given me anything to work with and I am not going to go and get it out of her.” Nobody went and got anything out of her.

“So I have changed the thing I am working at. I am not going to find the room. What I am going to do is put a stop in front of the sentence itself, and here is how, and I want somebody to stop me if I have it wrong.”

He was not going to say it and he said that part straight out.

“If the wrong thing goes into a room first, then the right thing cannot get past it. That is not me being clever. That is what happens. A thing that is said into a room where somebody has already said something else about the same thing has got somebody to answer to, and a thing with somebody to answer to is a thing that stays in the room. It does not go down the road. It stays and it gets argued about and it is finished.” He wiped his hands on his trousers. “And I have worked out that the wrong thing does not have to be about that strip at all. It just has to be in the same subject. Two different wrong things said in the same room stop each other just as well as one wrong thing stops one right thing, and I have got a whole handful of them in my head and I could have said any one of them by now.”

He did not say one of them. He stopped on the edge of it with his mouth open and shut it again, and nobody on that landing heard a name come out of him, and nobody ever heard what the first one was, and he did not write it down either, and there was no writing of it anywhere that anybody has ever found.

“And that is where I have got to, and I came up this morning to say it, and I have said it, and now I have to go down the road and say the other one before the day gets on.” Nobody told him not to go down the road.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the ninth hour with sixteen inches above his boots, gave his figures, gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, asked for nothing, and found the boy standing at the foot of the bank with his hands empty and his yoke in the grass behind him, and could not get past that.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and seventy days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and thirty-nine days old.

“Four hundred and seventy days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and thirty-nine days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-seventy-fourth is three hundred and twenty-four days old, which is one thousand and twenty-four less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it. Nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one and I am not going to ask for one today when a boy has shown me a way of doing a thing that would work.”

He said that last part flat and he did not take it back, and then he stood still and let it stand there where anybody on that landing could come at it.

“One hundred and sixty-fourth wrong things in one hundred and sixty-fourth mornings, and this is the hundred and sixty-fourth, and nobody is going to tell me what kind of figure that is and nobody is going to tell me that I found anything.” Nobody answered him on either half of it.

“My want is to be somebody and it has not changed. My cost is that asking costs me nothing and I have not asked for one thing in four hundred and seventy mornings, and I am going to keep saying both halves of that out loud on this landing until somebody stops me.” Nobody said anything for him. Nobody took either half off him and nobody put anything else in, and he went on down the bank carrying the whole of it and no lighter for it.

“I am going to say the rest of it now and then I am going to get off this bank.” He put his hands behind him. “I want that boy to do it. I have wanted it since before the light. He has found the way and it is a good way and it is the only one anybody has come up with since I put a sentence out loud, and I am not going to stand here and pretend that wanting it is a small thing, because it is the first thing I have wanted on this landing since I got here and I have wanted to be somebody every morning.” Nobody said anything about the wanting.

“And I am not going to help him and I am not going to ask him to and I am not going to say anything at all to him about whether he should, and here is why, and it is the whole of my morning. A man of thirty-one is the last man in this county who is allowed to want that, because I put the sentence in the air and he is putting a wall in front of it, and a wall in front of my own sentence is the one thing on this landing I am not allowed to have an opinion about. If I say yes then a thing has stopped going because the man who said it was frightened of it. And if I say no then I have spent the only thing I have got, which is saying a thing out loud, on holding a door shut.” Nobody said he was frightened of it and nobody said he was holding a door shut.

“And nobody is to come up here and ask me. Not him, not the man who cannot read, not the woman who saws lengths. I will not say yes and I will not say no and I will not say I have thought about it, because I have thought about it since the fourth hour and that is mine. If it happens, then it happened without me, and if it does not, then it did not happen with me, and those are the two shapes I can live with and there is no third one.” Nobody asked him, and he said in advance that nobody was to spend the morning deciding whether to tell him, and nobody did, and that is the whole of what he got for saying it in advance.

And then the boy asked him one more, and asked it in the plain voice of a boy who has run out of the things he had planned to say and has not stopped asking yet.

“Has anybody ever told you what that piece of wood is.” Nobody answered him. He did not get a no and he did not get a yes and he did not get a figure of how many mornings ago anybody had been told, and the man of thirty-one did not answer it then and did not answer it as the boy went down the bank. Then the boy went down that cart road with his hands empty and his yoke in the grass, and the woman of forty-four was standing at the wall at the bottom of it, where she stands, and she had been standing there since before the light and she had heard every word of the whole of that from about the level of the top step down, because a woman who keeps twenty-nine chairs in a house is used to hearing rooms through walls.

The boy got to her and put one hand on the yoke he had gone back for and she put her own hand flat on the other end of it, and that is the only hand either of them laid on anything that morning.

Orla Fennimore is forty-four, keeps twenty-nine chairs at a house that is not in this county, was asked once what she did and has not been asked since, and said at the wall at the bottom of that cart road on the fourth day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week that she put her hand on a boy’s yoke and stopped him finishing a sentence, and that she was not going to finish it for him and was not going to tell him it was wrong.

He looked at her hand on the yoke and then at her.

“I was going to say it down here where it would go down the hill.”

“I heard you say you were.” She did not take her hand off the yoke. “And I heard you say you had a handful ready and I am not going to ask you for any, and I am not going to tell you that I am stopping you because it is wrong.”

“Then why.”

“Because I keep twenty-nine chairs in a house that is not mine, and I have watched people do this in rooms.” She said it in the voice of a woman giving an account of a thing she has actually been in. “When a chair is standing in the wrong place, nobody stops using the room. That is what a room is. People walk round it. They go on putting things down and picking things up and they walk round the one chair, and it does not stop anything at all, and after a while the whole room has a hole in the middle of it shaped like a thing that should have been somewhere else. And the chair is still in the wrong place. It has not been moved and it has not been said out of the room, and it has been there the whole time, and every single person in that house has learned to walk round it, including me.” Nobody asked her about the house.

“So that is my working and it is the only one I have. A wrong thing said in a room does not stop a right thing being said in the same room. It gives the right thing somebody to answer to. And a thing with somebody to answer to is not a thing that goes down a road, it is a thing that stays in a room being argued about, and you are thirteen and you want that sentence to stop where it is and I am telling you that it will not stop there. It will stop in a room with two things in it instead of one, and that room will be there after both of us.”

He stood with his hand on the yoke.

“Would it be all right if I said it anyway.”

“I am not going to answer that.”

“Because it is wrong.”

“Because it is not mine.” She took her hand off the yoke and put it flat on the wall behind her, which is what she does. “And because a boy of thirteen who has just worked out that a person can stop a thing by standing in front of it is going to ask every person on this landing that same question today, and the answer is not in anybody’s mouth here, and it is certainly not in mine, and if I answer yours then you will have it and I will have had to be the woman who said it.” Nobody said it for her and nobody said it against it.

“And nobody is to thank me for the hand on the yoke, and nobody is to tell me that I have stopped something important, and nobody is to ask me at the end of the week what I did at the bottom of that road.” She did not move off the wall. “I am not in any of it and I have not said that I am not in it, because saying that is a way of being in it. I am going to go on standing here and the room with two things in it will happen or it will not happen and it will not have been me that put the second one there.” Nobody thanked her and nobody went up that road after her.

Then everybody went down that bank and the boy went down the lane with the yoke and two full pails in it, and he did not say it, and Ilyan Vester went down the bank last with his hands behind his back and did not go down the lane and did not go up the road.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about sixty of them were under, and went along the top of the water on them, and went round the fortieth one, and went round the chair, and did not sit on it. He did not touch the iron in the seam of the fortieth step and he did not touch either piece of wood on the dry strip and he did not put the broom near either of them. The first was a forearm long and had been split down the middle the way wood splits when it is meant to, with the thick end left as it came apart. The second was the same and had a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches cut in the flat of it, one of the two run out of the side. The water came up over the edge of the top step in three places instead of one that morning, and he looked at each of them twice, and worked round them, and did not put a stone in any of them.

Nothing on that landing was pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used, and nobody laid a hand on that wood in order to take anything off it, then or since, and nobody laid a hand on it after those sentences either. Nobody put a name on anything on that landing that morning, and nobody said one out loud either, and nobody said what a piece of wood was, and nobody wrote down a name on anything that day, and the sentence a boy of thirteen had got ready was stopped before the name in it and no person anywhere ever found out what that name was because it was never finished in the air and it was never put on anything.

A boy of thirteen worked out that a wrong thing said first in a room would give the right thing somebody to answer to and would stop it going anywhere, and said so out loud on that landing and to a woman at a wall at the bottom of that road, and a woman who keeps twenty-nine chairs put her hand on his yoke and stopped him before the name in it and gave him the working out of a chair standing in the wrong place in a room nobody stops using, and did not tell him it was wrong and did not tell him it was right. A man of thirty-one said out loud on that landing that he wanted the boy to do it and would not say whether he should, and the boy went down that lane with the yoke and two full pails in it and did not say it, and whatever is in that lane this morning is in it because nobody on that landing put it there.

# Chapter 1040: The Sentence Taken Back

The Sunday light came up flat and without any wind in it and the standing water on that landing lay like poured lead with the fortieth step and the chair standing out of it and nothing moving anywhere on it, and the whole of that bank was so quiet that a man could hear the frost come off the grass at the low side, which is not a sound most people have ever heard and which Corvin Tace has heard perhaps twice in nineteen years.

Sixth day of the hundred and seventieth week, and it is the Sunday of that week. His seven hundred and ninetieth morning. Six hundred and forty days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and eight weeks and one day old. Five hundred and ninety-four days since the division. Five hundred and forty days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and eighty-six days in this county of Kell.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and it holds, about sixty of those ninety steps under, and two days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and then did not give anything else for a long time, and then gave a thing and took it back inside a quarter of an hour.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the sixth day of the hundred and seventieth week that he had worked out on Saturday what he was going to do with the rest of his life on that stone and that he was going to stop himself doing it before he had said it out loud twice.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, two days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the sixth day of the hundred and seventieth week, and it is a Sunday. Three figures.”

He worked the top step and then stopped and stood with the broom head in the water.

“I offered that landing a thing on Friday morning and I have come to take it back, and I am going to say both halves out loud and in that order, and I want the taking back to be the part that stands.”

He turned the broom over in the water.

“What I offered was this. Every morning at the seventh hour, for as long as I hold this stone, I would say out loud that I do not know which of them the name on that strip is for, and that I am not going to find out, and that nobody on this landing is to ask me. Every morning. Out loud, where the whole bank can hear it, and then I would go back to my three figures and sweep.”

“That is what I want to do with it. And I have taken it back because I have worked out what a man becomes.” He did not raise his voice and he did not stop. “A man who says that every morning is a man who says that. That is all he is. He is not a man holding a stone and not asking. He is a man with a sentence he says every morning, and the sentence has got to be said every morning whether or not there is anything on the stone, and in a year the people coming up that road will not hear a man refusing to ask. They will hear a man saying a thing. And the first morning somebody on that landing says that man to somebody else, they will not be able to tell afterwards which part of it was the stone and which part was him, and by then he will not be able to tell them either.”

He put the broom head down flat against the top step.

“So it goes back in. That is the whole of the taking back and I would like it noticed that I put it in the air on Friday and took it out on Sunday, and that in between there was a Saturday when a man could have stopped me doing it and did not, because a man could not see well was on that landing at the time and I never asked him.” Nobody said they could have stopped him and nobody did.

“And I am not going to give a fourth thing off that stone today. I have had a morning where I was going to and I have not. A man who keeps three figures and a broom on a stone for nineteen years does not get to turn it into an office. That is what an office is. An office is a man with a sentence.” He began to work. “Go and do your gravel. It is a Sunday and it is not going to be any other day than that.”

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow and asked in the same words at the same hour and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and put his hands flat on the wall, and said the thing he had decided to say on Sundays.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and nineteenth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and forty less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and nineteenth. One thousand and forty less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

He left his hands on the stone and did not wipe them.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it. And I am not going to ask anybody about that name, and I have told that landing I am not going to ask, and I meant it on Friday and I mean it this morning, and nobody is to come up here on any morning and put it to me as a kindness.”

“And I am going to say the other thing, because I have thought about it all week and I have not said it and it is the true one.” He turned his head along the wall without lifting his hands. “Nobody has answered me. Not once. Not in this county, not in all the mornings I have come up this bank with a barrow. And I have stopped waiting for it. I have stopped waiting for it and I am telling that landing that so nobody goes up afterwards and says that a man of fifty-four stood here for years and was answered at the end.”

“But I am not going to tell anybody I have got what I wanted, because I have not got what I wanted. I wanted to be wanted for once. I still want that. What I have stopped is putting the wanting where a person might come and take it, which is a different thing from stopping wanting, and it is a smaller thing than it sounds, and it is the only thing I have managed to do this week that is mine.” He took his hands down and picked up the barrow shafts. “And it is not a favour and I am not going to say thank you to anybody for it, because there is nobody to say it to. That is what not being answered is. You get to the end of it and there is nobody to say anything to.”

Then the man of thirty-eight came up that cart road at about the ninth hour with a bottle in a paper bag and his coat open, and he had clearly been up that road before, because the bottle was already open.

Garrin Tolley is thirty-eight, with a board of thirty-two notches cut in the edge of it that lives inside his coat against his ribs, who came up that landing the first day of that week with a name in his mouth and put himself in the same sentence as it and was refused a figure, and who was asked on Thursday by a man with a broom whether he was to cut a notch and refused, and who said on the sixth day of the hundred and seventieth week that he had been up that road before the light and had drunk most of what was in the bag and that he was telling the landing that because he was not going to be asked and because he would have been found out by somebody sooner or later anyway.

He put the bag down at the foot of the steps and did not come up one of them.

“I have not had a job all week.” He said it plainly, with his hands in his pockets. “Not one. There is a place at the bottom of that drain where a woman washes lime and has asked out loud for a person to stand with her, and the man with the broom asked to be let down there a week ago and was refused twice over with two workings, and nobody has gone. So there is no job. And there is a thing on that strip with a name said out loud on it and a figure in the mouth of the man of thirty-one and a whole figure in mine, and nobody has asked me a single question about any of it since Thursday.” He looked up the bank. “I have come up this road three times this week to find out what a person is for. I have been given three workings and no job and I am not owed anything for any of it, and nobody on this landing has thanked me for asking and I did not want to be thanked, and I am telling you all of that so that it is not in a mouth of mine later on.”

He took his hands out of his pockets and looked at the top of that flight.

“And I have decided something about the board, so I am going to say it and then I am going to sit down on that wall and drink what is left. Thirty-two notches. That is what is on it and that is the end of it. I am not cutting one and I am not rubbing one out, and I am not going to be the man who has a mark on a board for a thing on a strip, and the reason is not that anybody refused me on Thursday. The reason is that I would rather carry a thing with nothing on it than carry a thing with one mark on it that I cannot check in a year.”

He sat down on the wall at the bottom of the steps, and there was nowhere on that cart road for a man to have sat before, and he had found it and he sat on it.

Then Ilyan came up at about the ninth hour with sixteen inches standing round his boots, and went round the two pieces of ash on the dry strip without going near them, and gave his figures, and gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and eighty-six days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and fifty-five days old.

“Four hundred and eighty-six days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and fifty-five days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-ninetieth is three hundred and forty days old, which is one thousand and forty less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it. Nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one and I will not ask for one on the morning a man with a broom has told us he is not going to be the man who says a thing every morning.”

He stood on the top step and did not step over onto the dry strip.

“One hundred and eightieth wrong things in one hundred and eightieth mornings, and this is the hundred and eightieth, and it is a whole figure, and nobody is to tell me what kind of figure it is, and nobody is going to stand on that landing this afternoon and do arithmetic at me about it, and nobody is to come up to me on any morning of this week and ask me whether a hundred and eight is a lot.”

He shut his hand and opened it and looked at the cut in the palm.

“My want is to be somebody. It has not changed in seven hundred and ninety mornings and it will not change on a Sunday because a man with a broom has been honest on it and a man of thirty-eight has been honest about having no job. My cost is that asking costs me nothing and I have not asked for one thing in four hundred and eighty-six mornings, and this week I was asked for nothing and I did not give it, and that is the whole of what I have learned on this landing in seven mornings and it is not much and I am not going to improve it.”

“And the last of it. I am going to be on this landing at the seventh hour on the morning after this one, and on the morning after that, and at the ninth hour I am going to stand where I have stood all week and give the figures that are mine to give.” He looked up the cart road and then back at the strip. “And I will not ask anybody for anything while I do it. Not one thing. Not the woman who saws lengths and not the woman who reads for a living and not the man with the broom and not the boy, and if any of them come up that road and want to give me something I am going to stand there and take it the way this landing takes rain, which is not at all specially.”

“Afterwards nobody is to come up here and tell me what those mornings were worth. I do not care what they were worth. I have been having that argument all week in my own head and I have lost it, and I am going to stop having it, because the only thing left of it was somebody else saying the figure, and there is no figure.”

Then the woman who reads for a living came up that cart road at about the eighth hour with the book in her hands, and stopped at the wall, and came no further than the wall, and asked one question into the road and got nothing, and then sat down on the coping of the wall and opened the book on her knee.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years, holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and said on the sixth day of the hundred and seventieth week that she had asked her question and that it had been refused by the shape of the morning rather than by anybody, and that she was going to sit on a wall with a book open on her knee and read nothing off it, and that nobody was to build anything on that.

“Has anybody on this landing put that name into the page of anything. Not out loud. Written, copied, entered, carried off in a pocket and put down somewhere else. That is the only part I cannot hear from the bottom of a road.”

Nothing came of it, and she said so out loud, and nobody filled it.

“That is three refusals this week and I have not been refused once by a person, and I have been refused every time by the shape of the thing, which is a great deal worse and I would rather it were a person.” She sat down on the coping with the book open flat on her knee. “I am going to sit here. I am not going to read anything. Nobody is to make anything of me sitting on a wall with a book open, and nobody is to thank me for it, and nobody is to write down that a woman who reads for a living sat on a wall and read nothing.”

She turned one page with her thumb and left it turned.

“That is all I have. I have asked three questions this week that I could not check, and I have had three answers put in my ear that I cannot unhear, and I am going to go down that road with all six of them and none of them is going in that book. It is not a refusal of the book. The book is the only thing I have ever trusted and I am not turning my back on it because a landing made three sounds this week. I am simply not putting six things into it, and in a year somebody will ask me whether a voice went down a road and I will have to say that I do not know, and that is the price of not knowing now, and I have paid it before.”

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour, and the man of thirty-eight went down the cart road on the low side of the wall so as not to have to pass the woman sitting on it, and Ilyan went last and stood on the second step from the bottom for a moment and went on down.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about sixty of them were under, and he went at the whole flight from the bottom upward, and the fortieth step went round, and the chair went round, and neither of them got his boot or his weight or the end of a broom handle. The iron in the seam of that step he has never once laid a hand on and he did not start this morning. The dry strip at the top end of that landing he swept up to the edge of and stopped, and what is lying on it lay there at the seventh hour and lay there at the eleventh. Two pieces of ash at the top end of that landing, out of the water, a forearm long each and split down the middle the way wood splits when it is meant to, thick end left as it came apart, with a saw mark on the square end of the second and two shallow notches cut in the flat of it and one of the two run out of the side.

At the top of the flight he stood for a while with the broom across his shoulder looking at a landing that had given him three figures and had taken a sentence back off him, and then he took the broom down off his shoulder and set it against the wall where it had stood the night before, head down, handle up, out of the wet and out of anybody’s way.

Nothing on that landing was touched, turned, weighed, read out loud, carried inside, put back or used, then or afterwards, and no hand went on that wood to take anything off it, including after those sentences. No name was put to anything standing on that landing that morning, and no name was said out loud on it, and nothing was said out loud about what that piece of wood is, and nothing standing there was counted.

That morning came and went, and the standing offer stood at three hundred and forty days and was not asked on and was not answered on and was not withdrawn on, and the account on that landing was as incomplete as it had been at the sixth hour and it was still in use, and the name on that second piece of ash was still wrong, and nobody on that landing said that any part of that was finished.

And the woman who reads for a living sat on the coping of that wall at the top of the cart road with the book open flat on her knee and the thumb of her other hand under the edge of a page she had turned and did not read, and she went on sitting there after the man with the broom had gone down the bank and after Ilyan had gone down the bank and after the man of thirty-eight had gone down the road, and the page stayed where her thumb had put it, and nothing was said out loud on that road for the rest of that day by anybody at all.

# Chapter 1050: Going Down Empty

The water had gone off that bank in the night and the whole flight stood out of it from the bottom stone to the top, and the grit lay loose on the dry steps, and the landing at the top lay empty of water with the dry strip showing the same as ever in the first light.

Second day of the hundred and seventy-second week, and it is the Wednesday of that week. His eight hundredth morning. Six hundred and fifty days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and nine weeks and four days old. Six hundred and four days since the division. Five hundred and fifty days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and ninety-six days in this county of Kell.

There was no water to speak of off that stone that morning, and no steps standing in anything to set a figure against, and no return to count toward. The man of forty-three said so at the seventh hour, out loud, in a seventh set of words, because he had said it before in other shapes and was not going to use any of them again.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the second day of the hundred and seventy-second week that the stone had gone quiet again and he was going to spend the morning on dry work, and that this morning is on the new side of the turning.

“My mouth has no depth in it this morning, my flight has nothing in it to measure by, and my week has no coming back in it to work toward. That is the three absences, said in words I have kept for a dry morning, and I will not make them smaller by explaining them.” He set the broom head on the dry top step. “Second day of the hundred and seventy-second week, and it is the Wednesday of that week. The week turned between the Monday of the hundred and seventy-first week and the Tuesday of this week, and this morning stands clear on this side.”

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow, and asked in the same words at the same hour, and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and came up ninety dry steps with the barrow rattling and put his hands flat on the wall.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and twenty-ninth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and fifty less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and twenty-ninth. One thousand and fifty less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

He looked down the dry flight and then back at the stone.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.”

It settled into quiet, and he gave that quiet its own shape, and nobody filled it.

“It has settled into quiet and I am letting it be quiet. Dry or full, the asking is the same asking, and I have stopped looking for a morning that changes it.”

Then Ilyan came up at about the ninth hour with dust on his boots from the dry flight, and went round the dry strip without going near it, and gave his figures, and gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing at first.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and ninety-six days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and sixty-five days old.

“Four hundred and ninety-six days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and sixty-five days old, and the standing offer asked at the eight-hundredth is three hundred and fifty days old, which is one thousand and fifty less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it and it has not been answered on and not been withdrawn on. Nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one.”

He stood below the top with his hands open.

“One hundred and ninetieth wrong things in one hundred and ninetieth mornings, and this is the hundred and ninetieth, and nobody is to tell me what kind of figure that is. My want is to be somebody and it has not changed. My cost is what the Saturday of the hundred and seventy-first week took, and I am not giving it a shape, and I have mornings ahead I cannot see.”

He looked at the broom and then at the man holding it.

“Let me sweep.” He said it to the man with the broom and not to the landing. “I have stood here for mornings without end and watched you work round what lies up there, and I want an hour with the broom for myself, to learn what it is to go round it.”

Corvin Tace held the broom across himself and did not hold it out.

“No.” He said it evenly, with the dry step between them. “And here is the reason, because you asked me and not the landing. This broom is the length of my arms after years of it, and my hands know where the head is without looking, and if I put it in your hands then I spend the morning watching your hands instead of the stone, and the stone is what I answer for. It is not that you should not learn. It is that what I have is one broom and it will not teach in other hands without spoiling the sweeping.”

Ilyan put his hands at his sides and his throat moved.

“I wanted it for myself, to have held it once.” He said it with his eyes on the dry stone. “That was wrong in the way wanting to smooth things is wrong, wanting to be the man who worked here instead of the man who stood here. You have refused me with a reason about your hands and not about me, and I can carry that, though I wanted it badly.”

Nobody answered any question about that name that morning, and his asking was about work and it was refused with a working, and nobody thanked anybody for it.

Then the man who cannot see well came up at about the tenth hour with his hand on the wall, and asked his question, and got nothing, and said so shortly.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and said on the second day of the hundred and seventy-second week that his list was empty and nothing that morning was going on it.

“Has that name been answered since the Saturday of the hundred and seventy-first week.” Nothing returned, and he moved on. “Nothing has returned and I am leaving it.”

Late in the morning the boy arrived from that lane at about the tenth hour with his pails on, told his own count, and kept it from becoming a count of anything else.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, and counts his own days in this county, and said on the second day of the hundred and seventy-second week that his count is three hundred and ninety-seven days, and that it is his and he will not make it into a count of anything standing on that landing.

“Three hundred and ninety-seven. That is mine.” He set the pails clear of the grit. “Has anybody swept that lane.” Nothing came of that, and he lifted the pails again. “Then I will go my own way down it.”

Then the woman who saws lengths came up that cart road at about the eighth hour with her cup in her hand, and stood at the foot and asked one question, and got nothing, and said so herself.

Wenna Pyle is thirty-eight, saws lengths and sets studs at a bench in an open-sided shed behind a wheelwright’s about three hundred yards down that cart road, and said on the second day of the hundred and seventy-second week that she had said what she had to say and was not going to say it again.

“Has anybody said what that wood is since the Monday of the hundred and seventieth week.” Nothing came of it, and she marked it. “Nothing has come of it and I am going down.”

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour, and the woman who saws lengths went down the cart road with her cup empty, and Ilyan went last and stood on the second step from the bottom for a moment and went on down.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, and none of them were under, and went at the whole flight from the bottom upward with the grit loose ahead of the broom. The fortieth one went round rather than under his boot, and the chair went round the same way, and the iron in that seam he swept across with the broom head and never touched with his fingers. The dry strip at the top end he swept up to the edge of and stopped, and what was lying on it lay there at the seventh hour and lay there at the eleventh. The two lengths were still on the dry strip in the clear air, a forearm long each and split down the middle along the grain, thick end rough where it parted, and on the second a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches in the flat with one run out of the side.

No piece of that place was shifted or overturned or tried in the hand or voiced aloud or taken within or set back or turned to account, then or afterwards, and no hand went on that wood to take anything off it. Nothing standing there was named that morning, and what that wood is was not said again, and nothing standing there was counted.

That morning came and went, and the account on that landing was as it had been at the sixth hour, still open and still in use, and the name on that second length was still wrong, and nobody there said any of it was finished.

And he set down what he was carrying at the foot of the flight and picked it up again and went on, with his hands tight round the handles and his eyes on the road ahead.

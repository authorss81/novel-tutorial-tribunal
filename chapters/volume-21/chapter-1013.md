# Chapter 1013: The Last Day Of The Hundred And Sixty-Sixth Week And A Landing That Had Said Almost Nothing For Two Mornings Was Listened To By Everybody On It And Nobody Filled It, And A Woman Who Reads For A Living Came Up The Cart Road To Fill It And Decided Not To, And A Boy Of Thirteen Asked One Question And Was Given Nothing

The cart road was empty from first light, which on that landing had not happened since before the strip at the top end of it had anything on it, and by about the seventh hour the difference had been noticed, and it was not noticed out loud.

Seventh and last day of the hundred and sixty-sixth week. His seven hundred and sixty-third morning. Six hundred and thirteen days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and four weeks and two days old. Five hundred and sixty-seven days since the division. Five hundred and thirteen days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and fifty-nine days in this county of Kell.

Twelve inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, about seventy of those ninety steps under, and five days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and then said he was going to be short this morning and everybody on that landing was welcome to take the shortness as a figure.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the seventh and last day of the hundred and sixty-sixth week that he was going to be short on purpose and that nineteen years had taught him that a morning with three figures and nothing after them was worth more than a morning with three figures and a speech.

“Twelve at the seventh hour, about seventy of those ninety steps under, and five days of the coming back, on the seventh and last day of the hundred and sixty-sixth week, and it is a Monday. Three figures.” He put the broom head down on the top step. “There is nothing after that this morning. That is not a refusal of anything. There is a thing people have been doing on this stone all week and I have watched it happen and this morning I am not going to do any of it.”

Nobody did any of it, and nobody asked him what he was not going to do.

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow and asked in the same words at the same hour and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and said out loud that this landing had gone quiet and that he was the man who had noticed it first because he had been coming up here longest.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and ninety-second mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and thirteen less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and ninety-second. One thousand and thirteen less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

“Since the morning before last.” He put his hands on the wall. “And again this morning. And I have been thinking about it since the sixth hour and what I have worked out is that a quiet landing is not a landing with nothing on it. It is a landing where everybody has decided on their own not to put anything down this morning, and that is not the same as a landing that has agreed not to put anything down, because nobody has agreed anything and nobody is going to.”

Nobody agreed anything and nobody said what they had decided.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it. And I am not going to fill the quiet with it and I am not going to say it quietly, which I have never done, or quickly, which I have done, and I am aware that doing it quickly is the closest I have come to using it as a weapon and I am not going to do that either.”

Nobody told him which was closest to using it as a weapon.

Then the boy of thirteen came up out of the lane with two pails of water in a yoke and carried them to the shed side and set them down and came back, and stopped at the strip, and stood there longer than he had any reason to.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county three hundred and sixty days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Three hundred and sixty is one thousand less six hundred and forty.

“Three hundred and sixty days, and it is one thousand less six hundred and forty, and it is one more than the morning before.” He put the yoke down in the grass. “And I am going to ask a question and then I am going to sweep the joints, and I want it said before I ask it that I have been thinking about asking it since about the fourth hour and that is all the thinking there was.”

Nobody said anything.

“Is that the one with the name on it.”

Nobody answered him.

“Not a no. Not a yes. Not a different question.” He picked the yoke up again. “I did not think it would be answered. I asked it because the whole of this landing has been standing about being careful for two days and I have not heard one person on it be careful about anything, and I wanted to put the careful thing down in the middle of them and see if anybody picked it up.”

Nobody picked it up.

“And nobody is to tell me afterwards that I was right to ask it or that I was wrong to ask it, and nobody is to tell me what is on that strip either. I have got my own figure and it is mine and it is not about that strip and I am not turning it into a figure of anything.”

Nobody told him and nobody converted it.

Then the woman who reads for a living came up the cart road at about the fourth hour with the book shut against her knee and stood at the foot of those steps and said that she had come up the road to fill the morning and was not going to, and did not say why.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years, holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and said on the seventh and last day of the hundred and sixty-sixth week that she had walked up a road to put something into a quiet morning and had decided at the wall at the bottom of it not to, and that she was not going to be thanked for the not doing.

Nobody thanked her and nobody asked her why.

“You have all been sitting on this stone for two days with the same thing behind your teeth and letting each other get on with it, and I know what the thing is and I am not going to be your mouth for it.” She did not sit down and did not put the book down. “That is the whole of what I have come to say and it is the opposite of what I came to say, and I would like both halves of that written down by somebody who is not me.”

Nobody wrote it down.

“And I am going to say the other half, because it is mine and it is old and I have been carrying it up that hill for a week. There is no age in the shape you are all being careful with. Not mine, not yours, and not that strip’s. If anybody on that step starts putting weeks on it this week I will read the page that says it and I will know it is a page and not a mouth, and I will say so out loud on that step, and it will not be a pleasant thing to sit through.”

Nobody put weeks on it.

“And nobody is to ask me where it came from, this week or any week, and nobody is to thank me for not saying. I said on the third day of this week that I would lie about it if I was asked and I have not been asked and I am not going to reward an untried week.”

Nobody asked her.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the ninth hour with twelve inches above his boots, gave his figures, gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, asked for nothing, and stood on the last step that was out of the water with his back to the strip.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and fifty-nine days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and twenty-eight days old.

“Four hundred and fifty-nine days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and twenty-eight days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-sixty-third is three hundred and thirteen days old, which is one thousand and thirteen less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it. Nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one and I am not going to ask for one before that water is off those steps again.”

He turned round and faced the landing, which he had not done, and did not go towards the strip to do it.

“One hundred and fifty-third wrong things in one hundred and fifty-third mornings, and this is the hundred and fifty-third, and nobody is going to tell me what kind of figure that is and nobody is going to tell me that I found anything.”

Nobody answered him on either half of it and nobody asked him which half he meant.

“My want is to be somebody and it has not changed. My cost is that asking costs me nothing and I have not asked for one thing in four hundred and fifty-nine mornings, and I am neither relieved nor unrelieved and I would like that said by somebody who is not me.”

Nobody said it for him.

“Now the thing this landing has been careful about all week and that everybody on it has just agreed out loud not to say, which I have also noticed and which I am putting in the air on purpose.” He put his hands behind his back. “A man of thirty-one has had something half built in his mouth for two days. On the second day of this week he stood on that step and told this landing he was not going to finish it. I am telling you that I have stopped. Not that it is finished. Stopped. I have not got one word of it and I have stopped going about this landing taking the next one out of it.”

Nobody said he had stopped.

“And I want the difference between the two put in the air as well, because a landing will read stopped as finished and it is not finished, it is unattended, and a thing that is unattended on a landing like this one does not stay where it was left. I am saying that so that when whatever this is happens, it cannot be anybody’s fault that it happened, including mine, and especially not the fault of the quiet, which was not arranged by anybody and was done on purpose by everybody including me.”

Nobody said whose fault it would be.

“And nobody is to ask me what it is. I am going to keep not answering that until it is said, and if it is never said then that is what this landing has got, and I am going to be the man who did not make it happen and nobody is to thank me for that either.”

Nobody thanked him and nobody asked him.

Then everybody went down that bank, and the boy went down the lane with the yoke, and the woman who reads for a living went down the cart road on her own, and nobody went up the road behind her.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about seventy of them were under, and went along the top of the water on them, and went round the fortieth one, and went round the chair, and did not sit on it. He did not touch the iron in the seam of the fortieth step and he did not touch either piece of wood on the dry strip and he did not put the broom near either of them. The first was a forearm long and had been split down the middle the way wood splits when it is meant to, with the thick end left as it came apart. The second was the same and had a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches cut in the flat of it, one of the two run out of the side.

Nothing on that landing was pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used, and nobody laid a hand on that wood in order to take anything off it. Nobody put a name on anything on that landing that morning, and nobody said one out loud either, and nobody answered a boy of thirteen who asked which piece of wood had a name on it, and nobody told him afterwards whether asking it had been right.

Twelve inches over that top step, about seventy of those ninety steps under, and five days of the coming back, which were the only three figures anybody on that landing had to say. A woman who reads for a living walked up a road to put a sentence into a quiet morning and decided at the wall at the bottom of it not to, and was not thanked. The account on that landing was as incomplete as it had been at the sixth hour and it was still in use, and the quiet went on being a quiet, and nobody on that landing said out loud what they thought was going to happen on the next day of that week, and the man who did have something half built in his mouth had told them on the second morning of that week that he was going to let it arrive, and nobody asked him whether it had, and nobody was relieved, and the offer stood at three hundred and thirteen days unanswered.

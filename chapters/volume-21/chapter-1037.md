# Chapter 1037: One More Notch

The rain that came in the night came in from the low side and came up the drain hard enough in the dark to put a brown skin on the top of the standing water and to shift about two handspans of grit along the flat of that landing and leave it lying against the foot of the wall at the top end, which is where a man with a broom begins on a morning after rain and which is the whole of his argument about rain.

Third day of the hundred and seventieth week, and it is the Thursday of that week. His seven hundred and eighty-seventh morning. Six hundred and thirty-seven days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and seven weeks and five days old. Five hundred and ninety-one days since the division. Five hundred and thirty-seven days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and eighty-three days in this county of Kell.

Twelve inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, about seventy of those ninety steps under, and five days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and then told the landing that the grit at the foot of the wall was not one of his three and that he was not going to put a fourth thing on that stone for a morning’s worth of moved sand.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the third day of the hundred and seventieth week that he had been down at the bottom of that flight in the dark with a lantern in his hand counting how far up the grit had come, and had come back up without saying the figure out loud to anybody, because a figure of grit is a figure of how much work a morning has put in front of a man and it is not the water and it is not the stone.

“Twelve at the seventh hour, about seventy of those ninety steps under, and five days of the coming back, on the third day of the hundred and seventieth week, and it is a Thursday. Three figures.”

He put the broom head against the grit at the foot of the wall and worked it out from the end and it came away in a wet roll and went over the low side of the landing and down.

“And I will tell you what I am not doing. I worked my way along that foot of the wall in the dark and I could have said out loud how much of it had moved and it would have been a true figure and there is nobody on this landing who could have checked it, including me, because I had a lantern and not a rule. That is the exact shape of the fourth figure and I am leaving it where it is.” Nobody asked him for it.

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow and asked in the same words at the same hour and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and came up seventy wet steps with the barrow high and put his hands flat on the wall, and did not say anything else, which is not what he does and which somebody at the top of that bank noticed.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and sixteenth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and thirty-seven less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and sixteenth. One thousand and thirty-seven less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

He put his hands on the wall and left them.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it. And I am not going to say anything else this morning. There is nothing to say. I have got my question and it goes where it goes and I have said everything I have got about the man on my road and about the woman at the wall, and this morning I would like to stand at a wall with my hands on it and be a man with a barrow for about as long as the barrow takes me to turn round. That is not me being put out with anybody. That is me having said enough for one week.”

Then the boy of thirteen came up out of the lane with two empty pails swinging in a yoke over his shoulder, carried them round to the shed side, and came back up to the landing with the yoke in his hand and stopped at the foot of the bank instead of the top, and said his figure before he said anything else, because that is the shape of him and it has been the shape of him all week.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county three hundred and eighty-fourth days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Three hundred and eighty-four is one thousand less six hundred and sixteen.

“Three hundred and eighty-four days, and it is one thousand less six hundred and sixteen, and it is one more than the morning before.” He put the yoke down in the grass at the bottom of the bank. “And it is a figure about me and I am not going to turn it into a figure of anything else, and if anybody up there wants a figure of what is on that landing they can come down here and get nothing, because I have not got one and I have not counted one and I am not going to start.”

“And I have got a thing to say and I have been carrying it since the middle of the week and I am going to say it down here at the bottom of a bank, on purpose, because up there it would sound like something I am saying to people and I am not saying it to anybody.” He turned the yoke over with his boot. “A yoke is cut for shoulders. That is what a yoke is. You cut it to fit a pair of shoulders and it does not belong to them. It belongs to whoever makes it and even that is only while he is making it. I have carried this yoke every morning this week down that lane and up again and it has not become mine and it is not going to, and if I left it here one morning and came back for it in the afternoon it would still not be mine, and if somebody else picked it up and carried it down that lane all day nobody on that landing could say that yoke had gone somewhere.”

“That is the shape of a thing. I am not calling it anything and I do not know that it is right, because I am thirteen and I do not know that anything is right. But I know what a yoke is for, and I know what it is not for, and I have known it since I was small enough to be carried about in one.” Nobody named it and nobody thanked him and nobody told him he was right.

Then the man of thirty-eight came up that cart road at about the ninth hour with his coat open and one hand still under it and stood at the foot of those steps and asked for the thing he had been working up to all week, in a voice with nothing in it that was trying to be steady.

Garrin Tolley is thirty-eight, with a board of thirty-two notches cut in the edge of it that lives inside his coat against his ribs, who came up that landing the first day of that week with a name in his mouth and put himself in the same sentence as it and was refused a figure, and who said on the third day of the hundred and seventieth week that he had been carrying a question about his own board all week and that he was going to put it to the only man on this landing who has ever given anybody a working instead of a thing.

“I want to ask one question and then I will go down that road and I will not come up it again this week with it.” He did not put his hand under his coat where the board was. “Do I cut a notch. Not a figure, a notch. One more on the edge of it, in the same place I have always cut them, with the same knife.”

He waited, and then put the working in for himself and then took it out again and left it for the man with the broom.

“I know what I am asking. I know what it is for and I know that everybody on this landing knows what it is for, and I am asking anyway because I would like there to be one more of them and because a man who has been carrying a thing about a name for a week has got to be able to put a mark somewhere or he is going to carry it in his mouth until his teeth go.”

Corvin Tace worked a step and did not stop.

“No.” He said it before the man had finished and he did not raise his voice. “You are not going to cut a notch on that board for that strip. Here is the working and I am going to give you all of it because you have come up here asking rather than doing it, which is the only reason I am answering at all.”

He turned the broom over.

“A notch is a figure a man made with a knife in his own hand on purpose. That is the whole of what a notch is. The thirty-two of them on that board are you, thirty-two times, with a knife, and they are the only thing in this county you have made with your hands that anybody has ever looked at. And if you cut one more on it for a strip of ash at the top of a bank, then from that day it is not thirty-two, it is something else, and it is a figure about a thing on that landing that nobody can check.” He came up a step. “Not you. You. You will not be able to check it. You are not a man who can read, and even if you were, you would not be able to read a board that is not in your own hand in a good light, and you would not know in a year which one of the marks was the one you cut this morning. You would look at that edge some morning with your thumb on it and you would not know. And you would not be able to ask anybody, because asking means telling them it is there.”

“So it goes on the board and nobody sees it. Which is where the thirty-two went, and where this one goes.” He worked the step again. “I am not going to look at that board. I am not going to ask you what the marks are for and I have not asked you twice this week and I am not going to ask you a third time. I am not going to tell you they mean nothing, because I do not know that and a man with a broom does not get to tell a man what his own board means. And nobody is going to tell you that you were right to want to put a mark on it, and nobody is going to tell you that you were wrong to want to. You have wanted to and you came up here and asked and I have said no, and the wanting is yours and the no is mine, and neither of them is going to be examined by anybody on this landing.”

Garrin Tolley stood in the wet at the foot of those steps for a while with his coat open and both hands hanging at his sides.

“That is the fourth time somebody on this landing has given me a working instead of a thing, and it is the only one of the four I have believed.” He said it without any weight in it and did not say thank you and was not thanked. “I am not going to cut it. I am going to go down that road and I am going to put my hand on it once on the way because I have been carrying the wanting of it all morning and my hand wants to know where it is. That is all. That is a man and a board and nothing on that landing.”

He went down the cart road with his hand on the board under his coat the whole way and did not say anything else on the road.

Then the man who cannot see well came up that flight at about the tenth hour and stopped at the fortieth step coming up and put his hand on the wall at the height it always goes at, and asked the boy one question and got nothing for it, which is what happens.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and said on the third day of the hundred and seventieth week that a boy of thirteen had stood at the bottom of that bank and said a thing about a yoke that he could not check and was not going to check, and that he was not going to put a line on his list for a boy.

He did not move his hand along the wall.

“Has anybody down that lane heard it. Not this week. Ever.” Nothing came of it in any shape, and the boy did not turn round, and the man who keeps a list did not ask a second question and did not write the first one down, because a question of his that nobody answers is still his and there is no reason on earth to make a copy of it.

Then Ilyan came up at about the ninth hour with the water at the top of his boots, and went round the two pieces of ash without going near them, and gave his figures, and gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and eighty-three days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and fifty-two days old.

“Four hundred and eighty-three days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and fifty-two days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-eighty-seventh is three hundred and thirty-seven days old, which is one thousand and thirty-seven less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it. Nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one, and I am not going to ask for one on a morning when a man has been told no about a mark he wanted to make on something of his own.”

He stopped at the second step from the top and did not come further.

“One hundred and seventy-seventh wrong things in one hundred and seventy-seventh mornings, and this is the hundred and seventy-seventh, and nobody is to tell me what kind of figure that is and nobody is going to stand on that landing this afternoon and do arithmetic at me. My want is to be somebody and it has not changed and it is not going to be said in a new shape because a boy said a true thing about a yoke at the bottom of a bank this morning and I would rather he had said it than me.”

“And the last of it. Nobody put a mark on anything this morning that I know of. Nobody is going to put a mark on that strip in this county in my hearing, and if one is put on it out of my hearing then it is not a thing I can stand on a step about, and I would rather that than be able to.” He shut his hand and opened it. “There is nothing on that landing that I have a hold on except a sentence I said out loud about a piece of wood and it cannot be taken back, and this morning I have found out that the two things I have been wanting this week — a figure of what my sentence is worth, and a mark on somebody else’s board — are the same wanting, and it is a wanting of somebody else’s thing, and it does not get better by being wanted out loud. I am going to sit with that and I am not going to do anything about it, because doing something about it is a notch.”

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour, and Ilyan went last and stood on the second step from the bottom for a moment and went on down, and the boy went down the bank with the yoke over his shoulder and the two empty pails swinging off it, and did not take the yoke down the lane with him that morning.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about seventy of them were under, and he went at the whole flight from the bottom upward, and he cleared the grit at the foot of the wall first, the whole width of it, working it out with the head of the broom and then going over it again with the bristles, and the rain-water brought more down while he worked and he took the second load off the stone without comment.

Then he swept around the fortieth step instead of over it, and around the chair, which he has never once sat on, and he left the iron in that seam where it has been since before he came and he did not put a hand or a bristle against either piece of wood on the dry strip at the top end. He worked his way along the foot of the wall twice over and the grit came off in two loads, and neither load of it went anywhere near the strip. A forearm long was what a piece of that ash was, and there were two of them on that strip. Both split down the middle the way wood splits when it is meant to, both with the thick end left as it came apart, and the second of them carrying a saw mark on its square end and two shallow notches cut in the flat, one of those two run out of the side.

He swept his way up to the top of that landing and left the water lying flat on the upper half of it, which it does on its own.

Nothing standing on that landing was pulled up, turned over, weighed in the hand, read out loud, carried inside, put back where it stood, or put to use, then or since, and no hand went on that wood to take anything off it, including after those sentences. Nothing on that landing was given a name that morning and nothing was said out loud about what that name is or what that wood is, and nothing on it was counted.

That morning came and went, and the standing offer stood at three hundred and thirty-seven days and was not asked on and was not answered on and was not withdrawn on, and the account on that landing was as incomplete as it had been at the sixth hour and it was still in use, and the name on that second piece of ash was still wrong.

And a boy of thirteen carried two empty pails down that bank to the shed side and then came back up the flight and laid his yoke down flat across the mouth of that lane, the two curved ends resting on the broken edges of the way in and the middle of it lying over the wet stone, and he stepped back and looked at it and said out loud that it stopped nothing, that nothing could go down that lane with a yoke lying across it, that he knew that, that it was not a barricade and he was not a person barring anything, and that he had put it there because he had been holding it all morning and had wanted to put it down somewhere that was not the grass, and then he turned round and went and got his pails and carried on up the bank with them.

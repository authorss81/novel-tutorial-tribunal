# Chapter 1014: The First Morning Of A New Week On Which A Man Of Thirty-One Said Out Loud In Front Of Everybody Who Had Come Up That Bank That It Is Better For That Piece Of Wood To Be Called The Wrong Thing By The Man Who Put It Down Than To Be Called The Right Thing By Anybody, And Nobody Was Given An Answer Afterwards And Nobody Was Thanked And A Woman Who Saws Lengths Said She Was Going To Say No Today And Then Said Something Else

The bank came up in the night and stopped, and when a bank stops it makes less noise than when it fills, so that the loudest thing on that landing at about the seventh hour was the absence of the water. Everybody who came up it that morning knew which way it was going without being told, and everybody came up anyway, and nobody could have said why that morning was different from the one before it, except that it was.

First day of the hundred and sixty-seventh week. His seven hundred and sixty-fourth morning. Six hundred and fourteen days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and four weeks and three days old. Five hundred and sixty-eight days since the division. Five hundred and fourteen days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and sixty days in this county of Kell.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and it holds, and that is a full bank, four days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and said that a full bank was the one morning in eight that everything else on that landing got in the way of, and that he had nothing to add and was not going to look for anything.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the first day of the hundred and sixty-seventh week that a full bank was the only morning he had ever wanted a fifth figure for and was not going to give one.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, that is a full bank, four days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the first day of the hundred and sixty-seventh week, and it is a Tuesday. Three figures.” He went along the top of the water and did not lean the broom against the wall. “I have been on this stone nineteen years and a full bank is the only morning I have ever been tempted to say a fourth thing, and I am not going to say it, and I am not going to explain that, because a man who explains why he is not saying a thing has said the thing louder.”

Nobody asked him what the fourth thing would have been.

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow and asked in the same words at the same hour and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and said out loud that this was the first morning of a new week and that his question did not know what day it was.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and ninety-third mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and fourteen less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and ninety-third. One thousand and fourteen less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

“The week has turned.” He put his hands flat on the wall, which is what he does. “And my question has not turned with it, and I am not going to improve it because it has turned. Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it. And nobody is to come up here today and tell me that the week starting is a fresh start. There is nothing on this landing that a week starting does anything to, and the man with the broom has already said out loud that the week turning and this stone turning are not the same statement.”

Nobody said anything about the week starting.

He said the next part of it without taking his hands off the wall.

“And I am going to ask one thing and I want it asked plain, and then I am going to be quiet for the rest of the day. Is that wood going to be looked at this week.”

Nobody answered him.

“Not a no,” he said. “I am a man who cannot read and I have heard a thing said about a piece of wood and I am asking whether anybody means to do anything about it. And nobody on this stone is going to answer a man who cannot read, and I have asked at the sixth hour for a very long while and I have got used to it, and I am not going to start being told that this was different because somebody stood up here.”

Nobody told him it was different and nobody told him it was the same.

Then the woman who saws lengths came up that cart road at about the eighth hour with a length under her arm that she put down at the foot of the steps and did not pick up again, and said one thing before anybody had asked her anything, which was that she was not going to say a word all day if anybody wanted one.

Wenna Pyle is thirty-eight, who saws lengths and sets studs at a bench in an open-sided shed behind a wheelwright’s about three hundred yards down that cart road, and who carries her own cup out to that bench because there is no table in the shed, and said on the first day of the hundred and sixty-seventh week that she was telling the landing she would not answer anything that morning before anybody had the chance to catch her at it.

Nobody caught her at it and nobody asked her for anything.

“Everybody on this stone is going to come to me today. Not today only. This week.” She put her hands behind her back. “I am standing here saying it now so that later on, when somebody comes, it is a thing I said and not a thing somebody caught me out on. I am not saying no to a person. I am saying that I have got nothing that is mine to give out on that strip, and that whatever is on it is not in my hands and has never been in my hands, and that a person who waits for me to open my mouth is waiting for a woman who cut a length once.”

Nobody told her she had been waiting for the wrong person and nobody told her she had not.

“And I am going to say the thing that is actually the trouble with today, because I have looked at this landing from the foot of these steps and I know what it looks like.” She did not look up the bank. “It looks like a morning where something is going to be said. And I am not going to help anybody get to that thing and I am not going to stand in the way of it either, because standing in the way of a thing on a landing is a way of being the man who stopped it, and I am a woman who saws lengths and there is nothing of mine on that strip to stop anything with.”

Nobody told her that anything was going to be said.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the ninth hour with sixteen inches above his boots, gave his figures, gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, asked for nothing, and stood in the middle of the landing with his back to the wall so that nobody was behind him.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and sixty days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and twenty-nine days old.

“Four hundred and sixty days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and twenty-nine days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-sixty-fourth is three hundred and fourteen days old, which is one thousand and fourteen less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it. Nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one and I am not going to ask for one today when there are more of you standing on this stone than there have been all week.”

Nobody took hold of either end of that.

“One hundred and fifty-fourth wrong things in one hundred and fifty-fourth mornings, and this is the hundred and fifty-fourth, and nobody is going to tell me what kind of figure that is and nobody is going to tell me that I found anything.”

Neither half of that was answered and nobody asked him which of the two he had meant.

“My want is to be somebody and it has not changed. My cost is that asking costs me nothing and I have not asked for one thing in four hundred and sixty mornings, and I am going to keep saying both halves of that until somebody stops me, and nobody is going to stop me.”

Nobody stopped him.

“And I am going to say a thing now and then I am going to stop talking, and I want the stopping to be noticed, because on a landing like this one a man who keeps talking after he has said something is a man who is still working out what he said, and I worked it out on the second day of this week and I did not know it then.”

He put his hand flat against the wall behind him.

“I have got no sentence left. I have been building one since before the light on the second day of this week and it has arrived, and it is about a piece of wood.” He did not turn round and he did not look at the strip. “It is better for that piece of wood to be called the wrong thing by the man who put it down than to be called the right thing by anybody.”

He said it into the landing and did not say it again and nobody on that stone said it after him.

Nobody answered it. Nobody agreed with it. Nobody told him it was right. Nobody told him it was wrong. Nobody told him where it had come from. Nobody gave him a figure of anybody who had heard it, and nobody gave him a figure of anybody who had not, and nobody went up the landing to fetch anybody, and nobody was sent for.

“I want that said out loud by somebody, and I am going to say it myself because nobody else is going to. Nobody on this landing owes me one word because of what I have just said. Not a yes. Not a no. Not a question back. And nobody is to walk up here afterwards and tell me they were not sure, because a man who is not sure is a man who has been waiting to be asked, and I am not going to ask anybody anything today.”

Nobody said a word to him and nobody came up afterwards to be sure.

“Now the part that is mine and about me and not about that wood, and I am going to say it once and then I am not going to refer to it again this week.” He took his hand off the wall. “I would rather have had the wrong one.”

Nobody said anything.

“That is the whole of the cost and I am not going to put a figure on it, and I am not going to describe it, and I am not going to be described. I have given away the only thing I had built in two days and I did not get anything for it and nobody was asked to give me anything. And I would like it noticed that I chose a sentence about a piece of wood and not about the man that piece of wood was named for, and that I chose it that way on purpose, and that if I had said it about a man this landing would have had to put him in it and it would not have had to do that with a piece of wood. Nobody is to say that back to me and nobody is to say they knew that was why.”

Nobody said it back to him and nobody said they knew.

Then the man who cannot see well came up onto the flat of those steps at about the tenth hour with his hand out in front of him, found the wall, went along it with his hand on the stone until he was level with the strip, and stopped, and put his hand back on the wall on the other side of it, and did not put it on the wood.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and said on the first day of the hundred and sixty-seventh week that a sentence said on a stopped bank cannot be taken back by the man who said it and he was not going to be the man who tried.

He kept his hand on the stone and not on the strip.

“A man said a sentence on that landing at the ninth hour and I heard it and I am not going to tell him whether it was true.” He drew his hand back along the wall. “I want it understood that I have just walked my hand along that wall to the end of that strip and past it and that my hand went along the stone and not on anything else, and that nobody asked me to do that and I did not do it for anybody, and that if anybody on this landing thinks I did it to show something then they have got it wrong and I am not going to explain it, because explaining it is how a man turns a thing he did into a thing he did for.”

Nobody thanked him for his hand and nobody asked him why it had gone along the wall.

“A sentence said on a stopped bank is heard by everybody standing on a stopped bank. That is not my figure, that is what a stopped bank is. And a sentence said out loud is not a thing the man who said it can take back, and he is the only man on this landing who could have taken it back, and he has told us himself he is not going to try, and I am not going to try on his behalf, because trying on another man’s behalf is putting your hand on a thing that is his.”

Nobody tried on his behalf.

“Nothing has come off my list. The thing at the end of that strip is still on it and it is going to be on it tomorrow, and I am not going to cross it off, and crossing it off is the only thing on that list that would take me a minute, and I have had the whole of this morning standing next to it with my hand on the stone and I did not do it. Nobody is to thank me. Nobody asked me to and there was nothing done by me.”

Nobody thanked him and nothing came off his list.

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour, and the woman who saws lengths picked her length up off the foot of the steps on her way past, and nobody went up the cart road after her, and the man of thirty-one went last and stood in the middle of the landing with his hands at his sides and did not go up towards the strip.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about sixty of them were under, and went along the top of the water on them, and went round the fortieth one, and went round the chair, and did not sit on it. He did not touch the iron in the seam of the fortieth step and he did not touch either piece of wood on the dry strip and he did not put the broom near either of them. The first was a forearm long and had been split down the middle the way wood splits when it is meant to, with the thick end left as it came apart. The second was the same and had a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches cut in the flat of it, one of the two run out of the side.

Nothing on that landing was pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used, and nobody laid a hand on that wood in order to take anything off it. Nobody put a name on anything on that landing that morning, before or after the sentence, and nobody said one out loud, and nobody asked the man who said it one question, and nobody was given one word of an answer.

A woman who saws lengths told a landing in advance that she would not answer anything and then told it what a morning like that morning looks like from the foot of a step, and was not thanked. A man who cannot see well walked his hand along that wall to the end of the strip and past it and did not put it on the wood. A sentence was said out loud at the ninth hour, about a piece of wood and not about the man that piece of wood was named for, and it is standing on that landing still, and nobody has taken it up and nobody has taken it down, and nobody was relieved, and the account on that landing is as incomplete as it was at the sixth hour, and it is still in use.

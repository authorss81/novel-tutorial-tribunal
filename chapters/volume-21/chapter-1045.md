# Chapter 1045: Water Over Ankles

The water stood higher that morning than on any morning since the week turned, and it came over the top step in a slow sheet and went down the flight without a sound, and the man with the broom stood in it to the ankle and worked it as if it were shallow.

Fourth day of the hundred and seventy-first week, and it is the Friday of that week. His seven hundred and ninety-fifth morning. Six hundred and forty-five days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and eight weeks and six days old. Five hundred and ninety-nine days since the division. Five hundred and forty-five days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and ninety-one days in this county of Kell.

Twelve inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, about seventy of those ninety steps under, and five days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures standing ankle deep, and gave them with the water moving past him.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the fourth day of the hundred and seventy-first week that twelve inches is a depth that covers a man’s boots and does not ask permission, and that he was going to sweep it whatever it covered.

“Twelve at the seventh hour, five days of the coming back, and about seventy of those ninety steps under, on the fourth day of the hundred and seventy-first week, and it is the Friday of that week. Three figures.”

He moved the broom through the sheet and it left no mark.

“Go and do your gravel. It is the Friday of the hundred and seventy-first week and the water is over my boots, and the stone has nothing else for anybody.”

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow, and asked in the same words at the same hour, and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and brought the barrow high through twelve inches and put his hands flat on the wall.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and twenty-fourth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and forty-five less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and twenty-fourth. One thousand and forty-five less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

He held the wall against the push of the water.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.”

No answer came down to him, and he said that in a new sentence, and nobody filled it.

“No answer has come down and I am standing here with wet sleeves and nothing else. The water is higher and the asking is the same, and I am not making the water into an answer.”

Then the woman of forty-four came up that cart road at about the eighth hour, past her own wall and up to the foot of the steps, and stopped there with a heel of bread in her hand, and asked what she had come to ask.

Orla Fennimore is forty-four, keeps twenty-nine chairs at a house that is not in this county, stands at the wall at the bottom of that cart road, and said on the fourth day of the hundred and seventy-first week that she had come past it because the water was high and she wanted to see it with her own eyes, and that she had a asking in her about the chair on that landing.

“Is that chair up there one of mine.” She said it with the bread in her hand and did not offer it. “I keep chairs and I know the shape of mine. If it is mine I want to know, and if it is not mine I want to stop wondering.”

Corvin Tace did not stop working, and answered her from the top step with the water round him.

“No.” He said it without lifting the broom. “And here is the working, because you asked me and not the landing. Everything on a landing stands still long enough to look like it belongs. The day that chair has an owner on this landing, the voice that came down your road has an owner too, and an answer is a door shutting. I will not shut it for you.”

Orla Fennimore put the heel of bread back in her pocket and did not eat it.

“I wanted it for myself, to stop the wondering.” She said it plainly, with her hands empty. “You have refused me with a reason about doors and voices, and I can see it is yours and not mine, and I am wrong to have asked it twice in two weeks, and I will stop asking anybody about it. If it turns out to be mine one day it will turn out without my asking.”

She went back to her wall and stood at it through the rest of the morning.

Nobody answered any question about that name that morning, and her question was about a chair and it got a refusal with a working, and nobody thanked her for coming up in the water.

Then Ilyan came up at about the ninth hour with the water over his boots, and went round the dry strip without going near it, and gave his figures, and gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and ninety-one days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and sixty days old.

“Four hundred and ninety-one days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and sixty days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-ninety-fifth is three hundred and forty-five days old, which is one thousand and forty-five less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it and it has not been answered on and not been withdrawn on. Nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one, and I will not ask for one on the morning a woman has been refused about a chair.”

He stood in the sheet with his boots full and did not step onto the strip.

“One hundred and eighty-fifth wrong things in one hundred and eighty-fifth mornings, and this is the hundred and eighty-fifth, and nobody is to tell me what kind of figure that is. My want is to be somebody and it has not changed. My cost is that asking costs me nothing and I have not asked for one thing in four hundred and ninety-one mornings, and my mistake this morning was wanting her to have the chair, because if she had it then the landing would have one thing less on it, and wanting the landing to have less on it is wanting it to be easier, and I am not here to make it easier.”

He looked at the wall where she stood and then at the strip.

“Has anybody on this landing said that name out loud since the Monday of the hundred and seventieth week.” Nothing came of that, and he said so without dressing it. “Nothing has come of that and I am letting it stand.”

Then the boy came up that lane at about the tenth hour with his pails bumping, and gave his own count, and kept it to himself.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, and counts his own days in this county, and said on the fourth day of the hundred and seventy-first week that his count is three hundred and ninety-two days, and that it is his and he will not make it into a count of anything standing on that landing.

“Three hundred and ninety-two. That is mine.” He kept clear of the sheet. “Has anybody moved my rings from the foot.” Nothing came of that, and he shrugged. “Then I will make new ones.”

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour, and the woman of forty-four went down the cart road with the bread still in her pocket, and Ilyan went last and stood on the second step from the bottom for a moment and went on down.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about seventy of them were under, and went at the whole flight from the bottom upward with the water at his ankles. He took the fortieth one round and not underfoot, and the chair round with it, and the iron in that seam he cleared with the broom head and did not once put a finger in the groove. He brought the broom to the edge of the dry strip and stopped, and what was lying there at the seventh hour was lying there at the eleventh. The two lengths lay on the dry strip with the sheet running below them, a forearm long each and split down the middle along the grain, thick end rough where it parted, and on the second a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches in the flat with one run out of the side.

No part of that landing was hauled up or flipped down or balanced in the hand or spoken out or fetched inside or laid back or bent to work, then or afterwards, and no hand went on that wood to take anything off it. No name was put to anything on that landing that morning, and the wood was not described again, and no count was made of it.

That morning ebbed out, and the account on that landing stayed unfinished from six to eleven and stayed in hand, and the name on that second length stayed wrong, and nobody standing there spoke of it as ended.

And Orla Fennimore stood at her wall long after the others had gone down, with the heel of bread still in her pocket and her hands on the coping, looking up the cart road at the water coming down the steps, and she did not eat and did not offer, and the road stayed empty all the way to the top.

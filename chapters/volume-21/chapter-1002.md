# Chapter 1002: A Thursday On Which The Water Came Off That Bank And Every One Of Those Ninety Steps Was Walkable And There Was No Figure Of Inches And No Figure Of Steps Under And No Figure Of Days And The Man With A Broom Said All Three Of Those Absences Out Loud Before Anybody Had Worked Out That They Were Absences, And A Woman Who Saws Lengths Came Up The Cart Road And Would Not Say What The Wood Was A Second Time

The water was not on the bank and Corvin Tace noticed it before he was halfway up the steps, and he stopped, and then went on up, and the stopping was because he had nineteen years of the top step being wet under his boot and it was not.

Third day of the hundred and sixty-fifth week. His seven hundred and fifty-second morning. Six hundred and two days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and two weeks and five days old. Five hundred and fifty-six days since the division. Five hundred and two days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and forty-eight days in this county of Kell.

The man of forty-three came up at about the seventh hour, put the broom against the wall clear of the strip, and said there was nothing to give.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the third day of the hundred and sixty-fifth week that he was going to say three absences out loud and that nobody was to stop him.

“I have no figure of inches for you this morning. I have no figure of steps under. I have no figure of days of the coming back. Three things I do not have, on the third day of the hundred and sixty-fifth week, and it is a Thursday, and I am giving all three of them out loud because a morning with nothing on it looks from the top of a bank like a morning where nothing happened, and something happened. The water came off this bank in the night. That is the whole of what I have got and I am not going to dress it up into a figure I have not got.”

Nobody stopped him. Nobody asked him which morning it was, and no figure of an ordinal came out of anybody’s mouth on that landing.

The landing stood open from the wall to the lip of the water, and the grit that had been under for two days was lying on the stone in grey lines where the water had left it, and the chair was standing where it had been put since a thing nobody had claimed was put on a strip above it, and the dry strip along the wall had two pieces of wood on it and neither of them had moved while the water was off the bank.

He went down the ninety steps that morning in a way he had not gone down them in nineteen years, which was one at a time, with the broom in his left hand and his right hand on the wall, and he did not go down in a hurry.

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour and asked in the same words at the same hour and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and he stayed where he was while the whole of the dry bank was in front of him.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and eighty-first mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and two less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and eighty-first. One thousand and two less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

“Nobody asked you for it, and every one of these ninety steps is out this morning and you can walk up them with your barrow if you had a reason to, and you have not got one, and I am not going to watch you decide whether to have one.”

“I have had ninety steps under me every morning since I came up this bank and I have never once wanted to walk them.” He set the barrow down. “Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it. That is the whole of it and it does not take a dry bank to ask it and it would not take a dry bank to answer it, and there is nobody standing here with an answer and there is going to be nobody standing here with an answer.”

Nobody answered him. Nobody told him he was owed an answer. Nobody thanked him for having said that a dry bank would not help.

He went down the bank with the barrow and stopped on the fortieth step, which was the one with the iron in the seam, and looked at the iron in the seam of it from above, and did not put his hand in the seam, and did not put his foot in it either though he could have stepped over it in one stride. Then he went on down.

Nobody on that landing put a hand in the seam of the fortieth step that morning. Nobody looked at the iron and asked anybody else to look at the iron.

Then the woman who saws lengths came up the cart road with her saw handle under her arm and stopped at the bottom of the steps and did not come up them and did not point at anything.

Wenna Pyle is thirty-eight, who saws lengths and sets studs at a bench in an open-sided shed behind a wheelwright’s about three hundred yards down that cart road, and who carries her own cup out to that bench because there is no table in the shed, and who said on the third day of the hundred and sixty-fifth week that she was going to say a thing about her own trade and was not going to say what the wood on that strip was.

She said it into the middle of the steps so that it would carry.

“I am not going to say what that wood is. I said a thing about a piece of wood on this landing once and it was said over the top of me by somebody else within a week, and I have had time to think about what that is and it is this. It is that a thing I said in my own mouth about my own trade came out of my mouth and then it was not mine any more, and it is not mine now, and I cannot get it back by saying it again, and I am not going to try.”

Nobody asked her what she had said and nobody asked her to say it again.

“But I am going to say the other part, because it is mine and it is about my bench and my saw and not about that strip at all.” She turned the handle over in her hands. “A thing I cut is not anybody’s because I cut it. I have cut hafts in this county for eleven years and not one of them was mine and not one of them was the man’s until the day the man stood in my shed and said it was his and named what he wanted off it, and that is the only day anything in this trade ever became anybody’s, and I have thought about it a great deal and I am not going to tell you what kind of a thought it is.”

Nobody named the day and nobody named the man.

“And if I had wanted a thing to be mine I should have had to cut my name into the end of it, and there is no name cut into the end of anything in my shed, and that is not modesty. It is that a name in wood is a nail you cannot take out without splitting it.”

Nothing in that shed had a name cut into it.

She picked up her saw handle and went back down the cart road, and nobody went up the road with her and nothing was said behind her back.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the eighth hour on a dry bank for the first time since he came to this county, and came all the way to the top, and did not go near the strip.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and forty-eight days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and seventeen days old.

“Four hundred and forty-eight days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and seventeen days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-fifty-second is three hundred and two days old, which is one thousand and two less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it and nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one and I am not going to.”

He was standing on the flat of the top step with the whole of the dry bank behind him and the strip at his elbow and he put his hands behind his back.

“One hundred and forty-second wrong things in one hundred and forty-second mornings, and this is the hundred and forty-second, and nobody is going to tell me what kind of figure that is and nobody is going to tell me that I found anything, and I am aware that there are going to be mornings on this landing where the two of those would come to the same man and I want it known that I have thought about that and I have not worked out what I would do.”

Nobody worked it out for him.

“My want is to be somebody. My cost is that asking costs me nothing and I have not asked for one thing in four hundred and forty-eight mornings, and it is the same both halves and I am carrying both up that bank myself.”

There was no sound off that landing of the kind a man makes when something has finally gone one way or the other.

“And now the part I have not been able to put down since before the light, and I am putting it down here because this is the only morning in a week where a man could walk up to that strip and touch it and nothing about it would be remarkable.” He did not move his hands out from behind his back. “I did not come up here to touch it. I am not going to touch it. And I am not going to tell you why not, because the reason not is a thing about me and I have found that the reasons I have about myself are worth exactly nothing to anybody on a landing.”

Nobody asked him for the reason not.

“But I will say this much and I want it heard in my own mouth and not written down anywhere. There are people in this county who will sleep better if that name on that wood is the right one. I have not got anything to give them and I am not going to be one of the people who says out loud that it would be all right. If somebody else stands at this strip on this morning and puts the right name on it, they will be doing it for those people and not for the wood, and that is what a name is, and I have seen it once already and I am not doing it again.”

Nobody stood at the strip. Nobody put a name on anything that morning, and nobody said the right one out loud, and nobody went near that wood with a hand on it or with anything else.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, and not one of them was under, and he swept the fortieth one along the top of the iron and did not put the bristles into the seam. He swept round the chair and did not sit on it. He swept round both pieces of wood on the dry strip and did not put the broom near either of them, and he did not say who had put the second one there and nobody asked him.

Nothing on that landing was pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used, and nobody laid a hand on that wood in order to take anything off it.

There was no figure of inches on that landing that morning and no figure of steps under and no figure of days, and a man of forty-three said all three of those out loud so that they could not be unsaid afterwards, and all ninety of those steps were walkable from the bottom to the top, and a man of fifty-four asked at the sixth hour whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it, and nobody answered him.

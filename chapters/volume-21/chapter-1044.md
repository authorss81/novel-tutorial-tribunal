# Chapter 1044: Asked Downhill

The wind came down that cart road before the light and lifted the loose grit off the steps and set it moving in thin lines, and the man with the broom stood at the top with his back to it until it dropped, and then began at the bottom the way he always begins.

Third day of the hundred and seventy-first week, and it is the Thursday of that week. His seven hundred and ninety-fourth morning. Six hundred and forty-four days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and eight weeks and five days old. Five hundred and ninety-eight days since the division. Five hundred and forty-four days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and ninety days in this county of Kell.

Eight inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, about eighty of those ninety steps under, and six days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures into the wind, and gave them without raising his voice.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the third day of the hundred and seventy-first week that eight inches is a depth that takes a man’s ankles and gives them back, and that he was going to sweep what he could reach of it.

“Eight at the seventh hour, six days of the coming back, and about eighty of those ninety steps under, on the third day of the hundred and seventy-first week, and it is the Thursday of that week. Three figures.”

He worked the water with the broom head low.

“Go and do your gravel. It is the Thursday of the hundred and seventy-first week and the wind is down the road, and the stone has nothing else for anybody.”

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow, and asked in the same words at the same hour, and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and put his hands flat on the wall and stood into the wind.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and twenty-third mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and forty-four less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and twenty-third. One thousand and forty-four less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

He did not lift his hands while he spoke.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.”

No answer reached him, and he put that in words nobody had heard from him before, and nobody filled it.

“No answer has reached me and I am not calling that anything. The wind took the sound off the wall and gave me nothing back, and I am not making a shape of that.”

Then the man of thirty-eight came up that cart road at about the ninth hour with his coat shut against the wind, and stopped at the foot of the steps, and asked what he had been turning over since the Monday of the hundred and seventieth week.

Garrin Tolley is thirty-eight, with a board of thirty-two notches cut in the edge of it inside his coat against his ribs, who was refused half a day at a bench on the Monday of the hundred and seventieth week, and said on the third day of the hundred and seventy-first week that he had another asking in him and was going to spend it where it belonged.

“Let me go down to the bottom of the drain.” He said it to the man with the broom and not to the landing. “There is a woman there washes lime and has asked out loud for somebody to stand with her. I have no job and I have a pair of hands that can carry. Give me leave to go down there and stand an hour with her, and I will come back up and sweep whatever you tell me to.”

Corvin Tace kept the broom in the water and did not look up the bank at him.

“No.” He said it flat, with the water round his ankles. “And you will have the reason, because you asked me and not the landing, and because you have been refused once this week already and come back. I hold this landing and the work on it, and what goes down that drain goes out of my sight and out of my hearing, and I cannot answer for a man who is down there when somebody comes up this bank asking where he is. It is not that the woman should be left. It is that I cannot spare a man off this stone on a morning when the water is coming back and the flight needs sweeping, and I will not send a man where I cannot see him to do work I cannot name.”

Garrin Tolley stood at the foot with the wind in his coat and took it.

“I wanted it for myself as much as for her.” He said it with his hands in his pockets. “I wanted to be the man who went. That is true and I am not hiding it behind her needing. You have refused me with a reason about your stone and not about me, and I can carry that, though I wanted to go badly.”

He sat down on the wall at the bottom and stayed there through the rest of the morning.

Then Ilyan came up at about the ninth hour with eight inches round his ankles, and went round the dry strip without going near it, and gave his figures, and gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and ninety days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and fifty-nine days old.

“Four hundred and ninety days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and fifty-nine days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-ninety-fourth is three hundred and forty-four days old, which is one thousand and forty-four less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it and it has not been answered on and not been withdrawn on. Nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one, and I will not ask for one on the morning a man has been refused a going.”

He stood clear of the strip with the water moving past his boots.

“One hundred and eighty-fourth wrong things in one hundred and eighty-fourth mornings, and this is the hundred and eighty-fourth, and nobody is to tell me what kind of figure that is. My want is to be somebody and it has not changed. My cost is that asking costs me nothing and I have not asked for one thing in four hundred and ninety mornings, and I kept that this morning while another man asked and was refused, and my mistake was thinking his refusal was about me, that because I want to be somebody I should have spoken for him. I said nothing, and that silence was the only right part of me this morning, and even that was not mine to be proud of.”

He looked at the man on the wall and then away.

“Has that name been answered by anybody since the Monday of the hundred and seventieth week.” Nothing came of that, and he marked it himself. “Nothing has come of that and I will not ask it again this morning.”

No question about that name found an answer that morning, and no gratitude was spent on the asking or the refusing.

Then the man who cannot see well came up at about the tenth hour with his hand along the wall, and asked his question, and got nothing, and said so in words of his own.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and said on the third day of the hundred and seventy-first week that his list had nothing on it and nothing was going on it that morning.

“Has anybody on this landing said that wood is anything other than what was said on the Monday of the hundred and seventieth week.” Nothing returned, and he let it go. “Nothing has returned and I am leaving my list out of it.”

Then the boy came up that lane at about the tenth hour with his pails half full, and gave his own count, and would not turn it into anything else.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, and counts his own days in this county, and said on the third day of the hundred and seventy-first week that his count is three hundred and ninety-one days, and that it is his and he will not make it into a count of anything standing on that landing.

“Three hundred and ninety-one. That is mine.” He looked up the wet flight without stepping onto it. “Has the wind been down here all morning.” Nothing came of that, and he went on. “Then I will carry low against it.”

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour, and the man of thirty-eight went down the cart road with his head down against the wind, and Ilyan went last and stood on the second step from the bottom for a moment and went on down.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about eighty of them were under, and went at the whole flight from the bottom upward. The fortieth one went round rather than under his boot, and the chair went round the same way, and the iron in that seam he swept over with the broom head and never touched with his fingers. The dry strip at the top end he swept up to the edge of and stopped, and what was spread on it stayed there at the seventh hour and stayed there at the eleventh. The two lengths were still on the dry strip in the wind, a forearm long each and split down the middle along the grain, thick end rough where it parted, and on the second a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches in the flat with one run out of the side.

Nothing on that landing got heaved or overturned or taken in the hand to judge or voiced out or brought inside or set back or bent to a purpose, then or afterwards, and no hand went on that wood to take anything off it. Nothing standing there was named that morning, and what that wood is was not said again, and nothing standing there was counted.

The morning wore away, and the account on that landing had not moved since six and was still in use, and the name on that second length had not altered and was wrong, and nobody on the bank pronounced any of it settled.

And Garrin Tolley’s board knocked once against his ribs as he went down the cart road, a dull sound through the coat, and he put his hand over it to still it, and kept his hand there all the way down, and nobody on that road asked him what it was.

# Chapter 1049: Cover Going Down

The cover had gone off the sheet in the night and the water lay open again to the light, sixteen inches over the top step and holding, and the man with the broom came up in the grey before anybody else and stood looking at it before he began.

First day of the hundred and seventy-second week, and it is the Tuesday of that week. His seven hundred and ninety-ninth morning. Six hundred and forty-nine days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and nine weeks and three days old. Six hundred and three days since the division. Five hundred and forty-nine days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and ninety-five days in this county of Kell.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and it holds, about sixty of those ninety steps under, and one day of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures with the light coming up behind him, and gave them facing down the flight.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the first day of the hundred and seventy-second week that the week had turned between the morning before it and this one, and that this morning is on the new side of it.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, one day of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the first day of the hundred and seventy-second week, and it is the Tuesday of that week. Three figures, and the week turned in the night between the Monday of the hundred and seventy-first week and this morning.”

He worked the top edge where the cover had been.

“Go and do your gravel. It is the Tuesday of the hundred and seventy-second week and the cover is gone, and the stone has nothing else for anybody.”

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow, and asked in the same words at the same hour, and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and put his hands flat on the wall and looked down the bank at the open water.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and twenty-eighth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and forty-nine less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and twenty-eighth. One thousand and forty-nine less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

He kept his hands where they were through the next part.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.”

That stayed without answer, and he said that in words of his own, and nobody filled it.

“It has stayed without answer and I am not asking it different to make it land. A new week does not make a new asking out of an old one.”

Then the boy came up that lane at about the tenth hour with his pails and his yoke, and set the pails down, and gave his own count, and asked what he had been holding back all week.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, and counts his own days in this county, and said on the first day of the hundred and seventy-second week that his count is three hundred and ninety-six days, and that it is his and he will not make it into a count of anything standing on that landing.

“Three hundred and ninety-six. That is mine.” He held the yoke across his shoulders. “I want to take this up the bank.” He said it to the man with the broom and not to the landing. “It lies across the lane and stops nothing, and I laid it there myself, and I want to carry it up and lean it against the wall up here, where I can see it while I work.”

Corvin Tace leaned on the broom and looked at the yoke and at the lane below.

“No.” He said it with the water round his boots. “And here is the reason, because you asked me and not the landing. That lane is a way for water and for feet, and what lies across it lies where people can see it is not barring anything. If you carry it up here and lean it against my wall, then it stands where I sweep, and I will have to sweep round it every morning, and in a week nobody will remember who put it there or why, and it will look like a thing the landing keeps. Leave it where it stops nothing, and let it go on stopping nothing where everybody can see it.”

Wat Marshe set the yoke down across his pails and his face worked.

“I wanted it for myself, to have it near me.” He said it with his hands on the wood. “I thought if it stood up here it would be mine in a way it is not down there. That was wrong, and I can see it now. A yoke cut for shoulders does not belong to the shoulders, and where I lean it does not make it more mine.”

Corvin Tace nodded and went back to work.

“You said the saying right and you said it yourself, and I am not going to make more of that.” He worked the edge of the sheet. “Go and do your carrying. Your lane will still be there when the water goes off.”

Nobody answered any question about that name that morning, and the boy’s asking was about wood and it was refused with a working, and nobody thanked anybody for it.

Then Ilyan came up at about the ninth hour with the water round his calves, and went round the dry strip without going near it, and gave his figures, and gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and ninety-five days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and sixty-four days old.

“Four hundred and ninety-five days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and sixty-four days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-ninety-ninth is three hundred and forty-nine days old, which is one thousand and forty-nine less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it and it has not been answered on and not been withdrawn on. Nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one, and I will not ask for one on the morning a boy has been told to leave his wood where it is.”

He stood clear of the strip with his hands visible.

“One hundred and eighty-ninth wrong things in one hundred and eighty-ninth mornings, and this is the hundred and eighty-ninth, and nobody is to tell me what kind of figure that is. My want is to be somebody and it has not changed. My cost is what the Saturday of the hundred and seventy-first week took, and I am not giving it a shape, and my mistake this morning was thinking the boy should have been allowed, because I liked him for asking plainly, and liking a asking is not a reason to let it stand.”

He looked at the lane and then at the strip.

“Has that name been answered by anybody since the Saturday of the hundred and seventy-first week.” Nothing came of that, and he put that in fresh words. “Nothing has answered out of that and I am not digging at it.”

Then the man who cannot see well came up at about the tenth hour with his hand on the wall, and asked his question, and got nothing, and said so briefly.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and said on the first day of the hundred and seventy-second week that his list was empty and nothing that morning was going on it.

“Has anybody on this landing handled that wood since the Saturday of the hundred and seventy-first week.” Nothing returned, and he took his hand off the wall. “Nothing has returned and I am keeping my hands out of it.”

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour, and the boy went down the lane with the yoke back across his shoulders, and Ilyan went last and stood on the second step from the bottom for a moment and went on down.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about sixty of them were under, and went at the whole flight from the bottom upward. The fortieth one went round rather than under his boot, and the chair went round the same way, and the iron in that seam he swept over with the broom head and never touched with his fingers. The dry strip at the top end he swept up to the edge of and stopped, and what was lying on it lay there at the seventh hour and lay there at the eleventh. The two lengths were still on the dry strip above the open water, a forearm long each and split down the middle along the grain, thick end rough where it parted, and on the second a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches in the flat with one run out of the side.

Nothing on that landing was snatched or upended or poised in the hand or proclaimed or stowed or replanted or tasked, then or afterwards, and no hand went on that wood to take anything off it. Nothing standing there was named that morning, and what that wood is was not said again, and nothing standing there was counted.

That morning thinned and went, and the account on that landing held its gap from six to eleven and stayed in work, and the name on that second length stayed wrong, and nobody on the road said any of it was resolved.

And Wat Marshe laid his yoke back across the mouth of the lane where it had lain before, flat and straight, and set his pails beside it, and went down the lane between them without touching either, and the wood stayed where he had put it.

# Chapter 1048: What Use Is Not

The morning came up cold with the water still holding high, and the sheet over the flight had skinned over in the night with a thin cover that broke under the man’s boots, and the sound of it going went up the bank ahead of him.

Seventh day of the hundred and seventy-first week, and it is the Monday of that week. His seven hundred and ninety-eighth morning. Six hundred and forty-eight days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and nine weeks and two days old. Six hundred and two days since the division. Five hundred and forty-eight days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and ninety-four days in this county of Kell.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and it holds, about sixty of those ninety steps under, and two days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures breaking the skin with his boots, and gave them with the cover going down the flight in pieces.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the seventh day of the hundred and seventy-first week that the week was at its end and the next morning would be in a new week, and that he was saying which side this morning is on so nobody has to carry it.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, two days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the seventh day of the hundred and seventy-first week, and it is the Monday of that week. Three figures, and the week turns after this morning and before the morning after it, and this morning is on this side of it.”

He worked the skin off the top step and let it go down.

“Go and do your gravel. It is the Monday of the hundred and seventy-first week and the cover is going, and the stone has nothing else for anybody.”

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow, and asked in the same words at the same hour, and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and put his hands flat on the wall with the cover crunching under his boots.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and twenty-seventh mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and forty-eight less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and twenty-seventh. One thousand and forty-eight less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

He settled his hands against the cold stone.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.”

That drew no answer, and he set that down in his own words, and nobody filled it.

“It has drawn no answer and I am not asking it softer for the cover. I asked and was asked in return on the Saturday of this week, and I am still standing here, and that is the whole of what I have to say about it.”

Then the woman of forty-four came up that cart road at about the eighth hour and stopped at the wall at the bottom, and came no further than the wall, and spoke from it before anybody could speak to her.

Orla Fennimore is forty-four, keeps twenty-nine chairs at a house that is not in this county, stands at the wall at the bottom of that cart road, and said on the seventh day of the hundred and seventy-first week that she had come up to say a thing she has known since she kept her first chair, and that she was going to say first what it is not.

“This does not answer my question and it is not meant to.” She said it from the wall with her hands on the coping. “What is a person to do with a place they are not in. That is mine and it stands unanswered, and nothing I say this morning is going to touch it, and nobody is to take it as touching it.”

She lifted her hands off the coping and set them back down.

“A thing made for a use is not the user’s. A chair cut for sitting is not the sitter’s. I have kept chairs through two houses and I know the shape of every one, and no chair belongs to the back that sits on it, and a handle cut for a hand does not belong to the hand, and that is not kindness and not unkindness, it is the shape of made things.”

Nobody thanked her and nobody asked her whether she is in it and nobody told her that nobody is answering her, and she went on before anybody could shape gratitude.

Then the woman who reads for a living came up that cart road at about the eighth hour and stopped beside the wall, with the book shut in her hands, and asked what she had come to ask.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years, holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and said on the seventh day of the hundred and seventy-first week that she wanted to put the chair saying into her book, because a saying that holds should be written.

“Give me leave to enter it.” She said it to the woman at the wall and not to the landing. “Not the name and not the wood. The chair saying, word for word as you said it, so it can be checked in a year.”

Orla Fennimore shook her head without lifting her hands off the coping.

“No.” She said it plainly, with the wall between them. “And here is the reason, because you asked me and not the landing. Your book is a place where a thing can be checked, and what I said is not a thing that can be checked. If you write it, then it becomes a line somebody can point at, and in a year somebody will point at it and say it was decided, and it was not decided, it was said. I said it from this wall and it stays on this wall, and I will not have it carried off in a pocket.”

Sena Dorr held the shut book and did not open it.

“I wanted it for the archive, and I wanted it badly, because my office is the only one here that writes things down.” She said it with her eyes on the wall. “You have refused me with a reason about checked things and said things, and I can see the difference, though I wanted not to.”

The saying stayed where it was said, and no thanks were spent on the refusing.

Then Ilyan came up at about the ninth hour with the skin breaking under his boots, and went round the dry strip without going near it, and gave his figures, and gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and ninety-four days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and sixty-three days old.

“Four hundred and ninety-four days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and sixty-three days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-ninety-eighth is three hundred and forty-eight days old, which is one thousand and forty-eight less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it and it has not been answered on and not been withdrawn on. Nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one, and I will not ask for one on the morning a saying has been said from a wall.”

He stood clear of the strip with the cover crunching under him.

“One hundred and eighty-eighth wrong things in one hundred and eighty-eighth mornings, and this is the hundred and eighty-eighth, and nobody is to tell me what kind of figure that is. My want is to be somebody and it has not changed. My cost is what the Saturday of this week took, and I am not giving it a shape, and my mistake this morning was wanting to thank her, and I am glad she stopped me before I could, because a saying thanked for becomes a thing handed over, and she said she was not handing anything over.”

He looked at the wall and then at the strip and did not go closer to either.

“Has anybody on this landing answered anything about that name since the Saturday of this week.” Nothing came of that, and he let it stand in new words. “Nothing has stood up out of that and I am leaving it lying.”

Nothing answered any question about that name that morning, and nobody was thanked for the saying or for the refusing.

Then the boy came up that lane at about the tenth hour with his pails empty, and gave his own count, and let it stand alone.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, and counts his own days in this county, and said on the seventh day of the hundred and seventy-first week that his count is three hundred and ninety-five days, and that it is his and he will not make it into a count of anything standing on that landing.

“Three hundred and ninety-five. That is mine.” He set the pails down out of the cover. “Has the cover gone off the lane.” Nothing came of that, and he took them up. “Then I will watch my footing.”

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour, and the woman of forty-four went down the cart road with her hands empty, and Ilyan went last and stood on the second step from the bottom for a moment and went on down.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about sixty of them were under, and went at the whole flight from the bottom upward with the cover breaking ahead of the broom. He took the fortieth one round on the edge of the broom and left it under no boot, and the chair round in the same turn, and the iron in that seam he sent the broom head across and did not lay a finger on. He swept the dry strip at the top end as far as its edge and no past it, and the two lengths had not been shifted by anybody between the seventh hour and the eleventh. The two lengths were still on the dry strip above the breaking cover, a forearm long each and split down the middle along the grain, thick end rough where it parted, and on the second a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches in the flat with one run out of the side.

Nothing on that landing was seized or inverted or hefted or declaimed or housed or reinstated or plied, then or afterwards, and no hand went on that wood to take anything off it. Nothing standing there got a name that morning, and what that wood is was not put into a mouth a second time, and nothing standing there was counted.

The morning spent itself, and the account on that landing lay as incomplete at eleven as at six and still lain-to, and the name on that second length lay wrong, and nobody at the wall called any of it past.

And Orla Fennimore’s hands stayed on the coping of that wall long after her voice had stopped, flat against the cold stone, and then she turned them over and warmed the palms against each other, and went down the cart road with her hands in her pockets.

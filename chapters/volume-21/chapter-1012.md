# Chapter 1012: Nobody Took It Off Him

Nobody on that landing heard the man of thirty-eight coming. He came up the cart road with his hand pushed up inside his coat against the edge of a board and his boots on the loose stone, and the loose stone took the sound off the boots and gave it back further up the road, so that by the time anybody on the landing could have heard him he was already standing at the bottom of those steps, and the man who reads for a living was at the wall a few yards below him, and neither said one word.

Sixth day of the hundred and sixty-sixth week. His seven hundred and sixty-second morning. Six hundred and twelve days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and four weeks and one day old. Five hundred and sixty-six days since the division. Five hundred and twelve days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and fifty-eight days in this county of Kell.

Eight inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, about eighty of those ninety steps under, and six days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and nothing else at all, and the nothing else was the longest part of his morning.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the sixth day of the hundred and sixty-sixth week that he had three gaps in his mouth yesterday and was not going to dig for more of them today.

“Eight at the seventh hour, about eighty of those ninety steps under, and six days of the coming back, on the sixth day of the hundred and sixty-sixth week, and it is a Sunday. Three figures.” He swept towards the strip and stopped and went round it. “That is all. Yesterday I gave three gaps after those three figures and it was the right thing to do once. A man who has found a good way of saying nothing does not say it again the next morning, because the second time it is a habit and a habit on a landing starts to be something the landing is doing.”

Nobody said the landing was doing anything.

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow and asked in the same words at the same hour and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and said out loud that he was going to stop telling people that nobody answers him.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and ninety-first mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and twelve less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and ninety-first. One thousand and twelve less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

“I am going to say a thing this morning and then I am not going to say it again this week, so write it on whatever you are keeping.” He put the barrow down. “I have been standing on this landing telling everybody that nobody answers me, and I have worked out that saying it out loud turns my question into a complaint about the people on it. It is not a complaint about the people on it. It is a question I have asked into a wall and the wall has not come back with anything, and those are two different things, and I have been saying the wrong one because the wrong one gets an answer of a sort.”

Nobody worked out what sort of answer the wrong one got.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it. And that is my week. Nobody is to improve it for me and nobody is to hand me a different one and nobody is to be thanked on my account, including me.”

Nobody thanked him.

Then the man of thirty-eight came up out of the road and past the wall, and stood at the foot of those steps, and put his hand flat over the notches through the cloth, and asked for what he wanted in the plainest voice he had, and nobody stopped him.

Garrin Tolley is thirty-eight, with a child of nine, thirty-two notches cut in the edge of a board in the inside of his coat, and said on the sixth day of the hundred and sixty-sixth week that he would give everything he had to know which morning of that week was going to have something said on that landing about the wood on that strip, and that he was asking for it out loud because he had worked out that being the man who did not ask was going to be worth nothing by the end of the week.

Nobody stopped him and nobody told him he could not.

“I am not going to pretend it is about the wood. It is about me. There is a shape going about this county and there is going to be a morning on that landing when somebody uses it properly, and I am the man who came up a road he said he would not come up, and if I am not on that step when it happens then everything I did this week was a man doing something in a week when nothing was going to happen on a landing.” He did not take his hand off the board. “So I will say it once and I will say it plainly. I would give everything I have got to know which morning it is. Not a figure of anything else. Just the morning.”

Nobody refused him.

“And I want it noticed where it is left,” he said. “Nobody has stopped me. Nobody has told me I cannot ask. Nobody has told me I am wrong to want to know. I have been standing on this step since before the light and every single person who came past me this morning waited until I had finished, and that is the politest this landing has ever been to me and it is worse than being told no, and I am not able to say why it is worse, and I am not going to work it out on your step.”

Nobody worked it out for him.

Then the man who cannot see well came up onto the flat of those steps at about the tenth hour, found the wall with his hand out in front of him, and said that he had been asked the same question in his own head twice already this week and had not answered it himself.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and said on the sixth day of the hundred and sixty-sixth week that he knew which sort of morning some of this week was and would rather have his hand taken off than tell a man which one.

He kept his hand on the stone well above the level of the strip.

“A man with a board in his coat has just said out loud on that step that he would give everything to know a morning. I have not been asked anything by him and I am not going to be, because if I answer him from up here then I am a man on a landing with a way of knowing, and every person in this county who wants that morning is going to come up this bank and ask me, and I will not be able to stop answering them without making a shape of it.”

Nobody asked him.

“So I am going to say the half of it I can stand behind and let the rest of it stay where it is. A name on that strip has not put anybody on that strip. I cannot read it and I do not know what it says and it has not put me on it, and it did not put the man with the broom there and it did not put the man who cannot read there, and it is not going to put the man who put it there either, and I have been thinking about that since before the light and I have not got any more than that.”

Nobody agreed with him and nobody disagreed with him.

“Nothing has come off my list. That thing at the end of that strip is on it and it is going to stay on it and I have not crossed anything off this morning and I am not going to, and nobody is to thank me for a morning where I did not do anything.”

Nobody thanked him.

Then the woman of forty-four said one sentence from the wall at the bottom of that cart road to nobody, and the man of thirty-eight had gone down the road half an hour before, and nobody had gone up the road all morning.

Orla Fennimore is forty-four, keeps twenty-nine chairs at a house that is not in this county, was asked once what she did and has not been asked since, and said on the sixth day of the hundred and sixty-sixth week that a man came up her road before the light and did not speak to her on his way past, and that she had been ready for him to.

Nobody went up that road.

“I am not going to say what I am not part of and I am not going to stand here and be asked whether I am in it.” She did not move off the wall. “What I am going to say is that a man who has to get up in the dark to walk past somebody without speaking to her is a man carrying something up that hill, and I am the somebody, and I noticed, and that is the end of it. Nobody is to come up this road and ask me what I noticed and nobody is to thank me for noticing and nobody is to tell me that nobody noticed anything, because I have never once needed to be told that.”

Nobody thanked her and nobody told her anything and nobody went up the road.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the ninth hour with eight inches above his boots, gave his figures, gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, asked for nothing, and stood at the second step from the bottom and did not come up any further.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and fifty-eight days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and twenty-seven days old.

“Four hundred and fifty-eight days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and twenty-seven days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-sixty-second is three hundred and twelve days old, which is one thousand and twelve less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it and nobody has answered it and nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one.”

He looked up the bank at the standing figure of the man of thirty-eight and then at nothing in particular.

“One hundred and fifty-second wrong things in one hundred and fifty-second mornings, and this is the hundred and fifty-second, and nobody is going to tell me what kind of figure that is and nobody is going to tell me that I found anything.”

Nobody took either half of that up and nobody put either half of it down.

“My want is to be somebody and it has not changed. My cost is that asking costs me nothing and I have not asked for one thing in four hundred and fifty-eight mornings, and I am going to keep saying that out loud because it is the only figure I have got that nobody on this landing can say for me.”

Nobody said it for him.

“Now I am going to say the thing that is really on this landing this morning and I am going to say it in my own mouth and about me and not about that wood. A man stood at the foot of those steps before the light was properly on that wall and said he would give everything he had to know a morning. Everybody on this landing has let him say it. And I want everybody on this landing to understand that letting a man ask is not the same as a man being answered, and that the two look identical from the bottom of a bank in the dark, and that I am the man everybody would come to if somebody did answer him, and I am not going to answer him, and he is not going to hear a figure off me.”

Nobody answered him and nobody gave him a figure of anything.

“And I will say why, because a man who refuses a question without a reason on a landing like this gets talked about, and I would rather be talked about with the reason. Asking is the one thing on this landing that costs me nothing. That is the whole of what I have got. If I start handing out what other people are saying, then I will be doing the one thing I am good at, and it will be a different thing, and it will still cost me nothing, and then there will be nothing left on me that this landing cannot use.”

Nobody used anything.

“And the other half, which is for me and not for him. The thing I told this landing yesterday that I was not going to say, I am still not saying, and I have not worked out one word of it this week, and I want that noticed too, and I am not going to be thanked for it.”

Nobody thanked him.

Then everybody went down that bank, and nobody went up the cart road, and the man of thirty-one went last and stood at the second step from the bottom with his hands in his pockets.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about eighty of them were under, and went along the top of the water on them, and went round the fortieth one, and went round the chair, and did not sit on it. He did not touch the iron in the seam of the fortieth step and he did not touch either piece of wood on the dry strip and he did not put the broom near either of them. The first was a forearm long and had been split down the middle the way wood splits when it is meant to, with the thick end left as it came apart. The second was the same and had a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches cut in the flat of it, one of the two run out of the side.

Nothing on that landing was pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used, and nobody laid a hand on that wood in order to take anything off it. Nobody put a name on anything on that landing that morning, and nobody said one out loud either, and nobody answered the question that was asked at the foot of those steps, and nobody gave that man a figure of anything at all, and nobody gave a figure of how many people were on that landing or under the wall or at the bottom of a road.

A man with a board in his coat said out loud that he would give everything he had for a morning and nobody took it off him and nobody gave him anything, and a man who cannot see well said he would rather have his hand taken off than say which morning, and a woman at a wall at the bottom of that road said she had been ready for a man to speak to her and he had not, and nobody went up that road afterwards. The offer stood at three hundred and twelve days and it was not answered and nobody was relieved, and a man of thirty-eight went down that bank and then down that road, and the wall at the bottom of it stayed where it was, and nobody on that landing was any nearer to knowing a morning than he had been at the sixth hour.

# Chapter 1015: Nothing Done About It

Nobody on that landing opened their mouth about it all morning. That is the whole of what happened on the second day of that week, and it took the man with the broom until about the eleventh hour to be sure of it, and he was sure of it because he kept stopping to look up the bank and nobody ever looked back.

Second day of the hundred and sixty-seventh week. His seven hundred and sixty-fifth morning. Six hundred and fifteen days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and four weeks and four days old. Five hundred and sixty-nine days since the division. Five hundred and fifteen days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and sixty-one days in this county of Kell.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and it holds, three days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and then worked all morning on a different question and gave the answer to it out loud at about the eleventh hour, when his arm was finished.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the second day of the hundred and sixty-seventh week that he had spent the whole of a morning watching a landing do nothing at all with a thing it had heard once, and that this was the most work he had done on that stone in a fortnight.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, three days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the second day of the hundred and sixty-seventh week, and it is a Wednesday. Three figures.” He beat the flat of the broom against the step. “And then a different thing, which is not a figure and which I have spent a morning on, so I am going to put it down plain because a thing I have spent a morning on should not go into a landing in a shape.”

He stood in the middle of the landing and looked up and down it.

“A landing does nothing at all with a thing it has heard once.” He said it slowly. “That is the finding. Not nothing this morning. Nothing. A man said a sentence on this stone yesterday at the ninth hour and today this stone has carried on exactly as it carried on before, and I have been up and down it all morning looking for a difference and there is not one I can put my hand on, and I did not find one because there is not one to find, and if there had been one I would have swept round it the same as I sweep round that strip.”

Nobody put a hand on anything and nobody contradicted him.

“And I am going to say why that is not a disappointment, because somebody is going to come up this bank in a week and say that this landing was cold about a man saying a true thing. It was not cold. Cold is a thing you can measure by putting your face near it. This stone is not cold. This stone is doing what it has done for nineteen years with whatever a person stands up here and says, and that is that it holds the landing and the landing goes on.”

Nobody said anything about it being cold.

“Whatever a man says on this stone stays said until somebody else says something, and nobody else has said anything, so it is standing here on its own, and I am not going to help it along and I am not going to hold it back, and nobody is to bring me a morning where I have done either.”

Nobody thanked him and nobody told him whether he had found it right.

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow and asked in the same words at the same hour and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and said out loud that he had heard a thing yesterday and was not going to say it again, and gave the reason himself before anybody asked him for it.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and ninety-fourth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and fifteen less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and ninety-fourth. One thousand and fifteen less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

“I heard him.” He set the barrow down and did not put his hands on the wall. “Yesterday. Ninth hour. Everybody on that stone heard it and I am not going to repeat it back, and I am not going to repeat it because I cannot read and a man who says a thing out loud that he cannot read is a man who has it wrong before he has finished, and if I get it wrong in the air then it is a thing I have said, and I have enough of those.”

Nobody asked him to say it again and nobody read it back to him.

“And nobody is going to come up to me today and find out what it had to do with me, because a man at the ninth hour yesterday said a thing about a piece of wood, and I do not see how a piece of wood gets anybody into it, and I am not going to work it out, and if I cannot work it out then it has not got me.” He picked the barrow handles up. “Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it. And I am not going to use a thing I heard yesterday to make that question bigger than it is. It is the size it was when I first asked it and it is the size it is going to be when I cannot ask it any more.”

Nobody told him the question was the right size and nobody told him it was not.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the ninth hour with sixteen inches above his boots, gave his figures, gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, asked for nothing, and sat down on the second step from the bottom, which is a thing he had not done on that landing.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and sixty-one days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and thirty days old.

“Four hundred and sixty-one days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and thirty days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-sixty-fifth is three hundred and fifteen days old, which is one thousand and fifteen less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it. Nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one, and I am not going to ask for one today, because today I am going to sit on a step and do the thing I should have done on the first day I came up this bank.”

He put his hands on his knees and left them there.

“One hundred and fifty-fifth wrong things in one hundred and fifty-fifth mornings, and this is the hundred and fifty-fifth, and nobody is going to tell me what kind of figure that is and nobody is going to tell me that I found anything.”

Neither half of it was answered and both halves were left standing where they were.

“My want is to be somebody and it has not changed. My cost is that asking costs me nothing and I have not asked for one thing in four hundred and sixty-one mornings. Both halves of that get said out loud on this landing every morning until somebody stops me, and this morning I am going to add one half to it, and the half is that yesterday I spent the only thing I had, and it did not cost me anything, and I have been sitting on this step since about the ninth hour finding that out.”

Nobody commented on it.

“Now the working out, in my own mouth, and it is the reason I sat down. A name on a thing that is wrong can be refused by the man it is for, and refusing it costs him nothing. Nothing. He is owed nothing by a thing that is not his and there is no debt standing anywhere, so there is nothing for him to carry and nothing for anybody to have taken off him.” He turned his hands over. “A name on a thing that is right is a debt. It is not a favour and it is not a gift and it is not an honour. It is a debt, and it sits there, and the man it is for cannot refuse it in the same words, because a man who refuses a debt has to say what he is refusing, and saying what he is refusing is saying the thing is his, and saying the thing is his is the whole of what a debt is.”

Nobody said a word and nobody took hold of either end of it.

“And I said out loud yesterday, on that stone, that I would rather have had the wrong one. So I have said it and I have worked out what it is worth, and here is what it is worth. There is nothing on this landing for me now. If somebody on this bank came up with a name for that strip this afternoon and said it out loud, then a man who cannot read would have a debt standing on a stone with a strip of wood on it and he would not be able to put it down, and I would have put it there by having said yesterday that I wanted the wrong one, and nobody would ever be able to show whose it was, because I would be the man who said he would rather have had the wrong one and that is all anybody would have.”

Nobody said anything about whose it would be.

“And I am not going to try to take the sentence back.” He said it to his own knees. “I am going to say why, and the why is the whole of this morning. Taking it back means going up to that strip and saying something else over it, and there is not one person on this landing who may put a hand on that wood, and there is not one person on this landing who may say a thing over that wood so as to put another thing under it, and I am the only man in this county who could unsay it and I am not going to, and I am not going to try to do it in a way that would look like not doing it.”

Nobody told him he could have tried it differently.

“And the last of it. Nobody on this landing owes me an answer because of what I said yesterday and nobody is going to get one off me this morning.” He looked up. “If anybody asks me what I meant, I am not going to explain it, and I am not going to explain it to be kind to them, and I am not going to be asked.”

Nobody asked him.

Then the man who cannot see well came up onto the flat of those steps at about the tenth hour, found the wall, and asked the question he had walked up to ask, in a flat voice, and did not put his hand anywhere near the strip.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and said on the second day of the hundred and sixty-seventh week that he had asked a man who had said a thing out loud one question about that thing and had expected nothing and got nothing.

He kept his hand on the stone above the level of the strip.

“Is the name on that strip the right one.”

Nobody answered him.

“Not me either.” He said it to the wall. “I did not come up here for an answer and I am not standing here disappointed. I have got a list and the thing at the end of that strip is on it and whether it is the right name or the wrong name is not a thing on my list, because my list is not a list of what is true. It is a list of what I cannot check.”

Nobody told him what his list was or was not.

“A man told this landing yesterday that nobody owes him a word because of what he said. I want it noted that I heard him say that, and that I asked him anyway, and that the asking was wrong of me, and that I am not going to ask him anything else, and that I am not going to thank him for the not-answering because a man who is not owed an answer is not a man who has been given a favour.”

Nobody thanked him and nobody told him the asking was wrong.

Then everybody went down that bank, and the man of thirty-one was the last one up it and not the last one down, because he sat on the second step for a while after the others had gone and then got up and went down and did not look at the strip on his way past it.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about sixty of them were under, and kept the head of the broom up off the wet edge the whole way down, because sixteen inches that hold are not a thing to argue with twice. He went along the top of the water on them and round the fortieth one and round the chair, and did not sit on it. He did not touch the iron in the seam of the fortieth step and he did not touch either piece of wood on the dry strip and he did not put the broom near either of them. The first was a forearm long and had been split down the middle the way wood splits when it is meant to, with the thick end left as it came apart. The second was the same and had a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches cut in the flat of it, one of the two run out of the side. His arm was finished at about the eleventh hour and he finished the top step standing, and gave the answer to a different question out loud on it, and did not say anything else about that either.

Nothing on that landing was pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used, and nobody laid a hand on that wood in order to take anything off it. Nobody put a name on anything on that landing that morning, after a sentence had been said on it the day before or at any other time, and nobody said one out loud either, and nobody gave a figure of anything to anybody who asked for one.

A landing did nothing at all with a sentence it had heard once, and a man who cannot read heard it and would not say it again, and a man of thirty-one sat on a step and worked out that a wrong name can be refused for nothing and a right one is a debt, and said out loud that he would rather have had the wrong one. He said that second thing once and did not say it again for the rest of that day, and nobody asked him what it was worth and nobody told him, and the offer stood at three hundred and fifteen days and nobody was relieved, and the account on that landing was still in use.

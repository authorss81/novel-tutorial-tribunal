# Chapter 1034: Past The End Of The Wall

The water was gone off that bank before the light and the whole flight stood out of it from the bottom to the top, and the grit that had been packed into a lip along the wet edge on the last day of that week had been lifted off it and spread back down the steps in a thin grey skin by whatever had come over in the night, so that the landing had the look of a thing that had been turned over in the night and put back roughly.

Seventh day of the hundred and sixty-ninth week, and it is the Monday of that week. His seven hundred and eighty-fourth morning. Six hundred and thirty-four days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and seven weeks and two days old. Five hundred and eighty-eight days since the division. Five hundred and thirty-four days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and eighty days in this county of Kell.

There was no figure of inches on that stone that morning, and no figure of how much of that flight was standing in it, and no figure of how long before it came back. The man of forty-three said so at the seventh hour, out loud, in a fifth set of words, because he had said it before on this landing three times in three different shapes and he was not going to do it a fourth time and call the doing of it something.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the seventh day of the hundred and sixty-ninth week that the stone had gone back to saying nothing at him and that he had stopped arguing with it, and that this morning was going to be spent on the parts of that bank that do not need the water to be worth anything.

“There is no depth in my mouth at all this morning, there is nothing standing in that flight for me to put a figure against, and there is no morning coming back that I am working towards. That is the three of it, and I have said those three absences on this landing before, in three other sets of words, and I am not going to say them in a fourth and I am not going to let the fourth one be the one that finally makes them smaller.” He put the broom head down on the top step. “Seventh day of the hundred and sixty-ninth week, and it is a Monday. That is the whole of what my stone has for me today and I am not going to stand up here and work it into a fourth thing, because I have watched men do that and it always ends with the man going about the landing with a face on him.”

“And the other thing, and I am going to say it before anybody asks me and nobody is going to ask me. This is the last morning of this week and I am not going to say anything about what tomorrow is. There is a man on this landing who has been asked that and has refused it, and a man with a broom is going to refuse it too, and the refusing costs nothing and it is not a figure of anything, and I would rather say no than say something I would have to be right about.”

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow, and asked in the same words at the same hour, and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and came up ninety dry steps with grit standing loose on them and put his hands flat on the wall at the top and left them there longer than he usually leaves them.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and thirteenth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and thirty-four less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and thirteenth. One thousand and thirty-four less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

He did not take his hands off the wall and he took longer over the next part than he had on any morning of that week.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it. And I have got a whole figure in my mouth this morning and it came out on the wall the other day with nothing beside it, and I am keeping it in there this morning. A man with a broom has told me in advance he will not say a word about it and I have taken him at his word and I have had nothing to put beside it and nothing to take off it.”

He turned his head along the wall, going the other way from the flight.

“And I am going to say the other half, and then I am going to do a day’s gravel. Ninety steps, all of them out of the water, all of them with grit standing loose on them, and I felt every one of them through the soles of my boots and I have not felt that since before I came to this place.” He put his hands flat and looked down at them. “There is a thing at the top of this bank that a man who cannot see well is going to do before the light is off it, because he has done it every morning of this week and there is no reason for him to stop, and I am stood here with my hands on this wall and I have not heard a sound of it any of the times. He will put that hand out over that strip and it will not go down on it. And I am not going to ask him what he is doing and I am not going to ask him why he takes it back off, and I want it put down that I did not ask, because a man who cannot read is the last man anybody should be asking what a hand was doing over a thing he cannot see.”

“And I am going to say one more thing this morning and then I am going to do a day’s gravel and not come back up this bank until it is dark. The man that came up my road. I have had a week to get used to him being in my head and I have not managed it and I have stopped trying, and what I have instead is this.” He took his hands off the wall and held them up in front of himself, palms out, the way he holds them when he is looking at the grit in them. “I cannot tell you what his voice was doing. I know it was not doing anything to me.”

“That is all I have got and I have had a week of him and that is the whole of it, and I would rather say that out loud at this wall than carry a week of guessing about a man’s voice into the ground with me. If a person comes up this road and says something near you and you cannot tell whether it was for you, then it was not for you. That is all the use it is. It is not a rule and it is not a philosophy and it is not worth anything on any morning that a man does not come up a road.” He put his hands flat on the wall again. “I have wanted to be able to work it out properly all week and I cannot and I am not going to keep at it.”

Then Ilyan came up at about the ninth hour on a flight with nothing in the way, gave his figures, gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, asked for nothing, and did not go up past the second step from the top.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and eighty days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and forty-nine days old.

“Four hundred and eighty days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and forty-nine days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-eighty-fourth is three hundred and thirty-four days old, which is one thousand and thirty-four less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it. Nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one and there is no version of this morning on which I ask for one, because a man who asks on a morning when there is nothing to ask about is a man who is asking about himself.”

He stood with the wall behind him.

“One hundred and seventy-fourth wrong things in one hundred and seventy-fourth mornings, and this is the hundred and seventy-fourth, and nobody is to tell me what kind of figure that is and nobody is going to stand on this landing this afternoon looking at me and doing arithmetic. My want is to be somebody and it has not changed and it will not be said in a new shape, because a stone went quiet and a man who cannot see well cannot find a wall.”

“And I am going to say the last of it and nobody is to answer any part of it, and I say that knowing there is nothing on this landing this morning to answer it with anyway.” He shut his hand. “A man who cannot see well has put a hand over that strip this morning and has not put it on it. A man with a broom has a figure of his own in his mouth and is not giving it out. A man who cannot read has a whole figure in his mouth and is keeping it in. And I am standing on the second step with four figures in my mouth and I am the only man on this landing this morning who has got less than he came up with, and that is not a thing I am going to make into anything.” Nobody made it into anything.

Then the man who cannot see well came up that flight at about the tenth hour and got to the top of it and put his hand on the wall at the height it always goes at, and did not stand on the strip and did not stand near the middle of the landing, and worked his way along the wall until the stone ran out under his hand, and kept his hand out anyway.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, and has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint. On the seventh day of the hundred and sixty-ninth week he took his hand past the end of that wall on purpose and held it in the air over that strip, and nothing on this landing had asked him to and nothing on this landing was going to.

He stopped with his arm out and his hand flat and turned over nothing at all.

“I have been doing this for years and it is the same every time. Hand on the wall, along the wall, take it off, put it back on further along, because when I put it back on a different bit of stone I know it is a different bit of stone and not because I have seen it.” He moved the hand out past the last of the wall. “That is all I have ever had off that wall and it is not nothing and it is not much. So I have just gone past the end of it, because the wall stops and my hand did not, and my hand is now in the air over that strip and it has been in the air over both of those pieces of wood and out the far side of them and there was nothing under it the whole way.”

He brought it back down without touching anything on the way, not the wall and not the stone and not the air it had been in.

“Now I want to say the working out of that and I want it said properly because I have had that hand in the air over a thing and somebody is going to want a figure out of it by the afternoon. A hand that has gone past the end of a wall has not found anything. It has been further over that strip than it has ever been and it has not touched it, and there is nothing on my list for that, because my list is a list of things I could describe to a person and what I have just done is not that. It is a hand in the air over a thing. That is all of it, and I am not going to make it into more than that, because a man who has had his hand over something for the length of a breath is not a man who knows anything about it and the day he thinks he is, he will be a man who knows.”

“And I am going to say the rest of it, which is what I would have had to do if I had put that hand down where it wanted to go, because I worked it out on the way up that flight and it is worse than the distance and I would rather have the distance.” He brought his hand back down the way a man brings a hand back off a hot plate and kept it in the air at his side and did not put it on anything. “If I put a hand on that strip then I have touched a thing on this landing that nobody else has ever touched, and I would not be able to say afterwards what was on it, because I cannot see. I would know what a piece of ash feels like and nothing else at all, and a feeling is not a thing anybody can check, and I have spent a long while building a list of things I cannot check and I am not going to start putting the first one on it with my own hand on a day when I cannot even see my own fingers.”

“And that is not all of it and I am going to say the whole of it or I am not going to say it. If a hand goes down on that strip then this landing has a man on it who has had a hand on it, and the man is me, and I am the worst man on this landing to have had a hand on it, because I am the one man here who could put his hand on a thing and not be able to say one single thing true about it afterwards. Any person on this landing could lay a hand on that wood and be asked what it was like. I could lay a hand on that wood and be asked what it was like and I would have to make something up, and a man who makes something up about a thing he has touched is not a man who checked it.”

“He is a man who has decided what it was like from having his hand there, and that is the same as saying it, and it is saying it with my hand instead of my mouth, and my hand cannot be asked to stop.” He put his hand into his coat. “So the distance is what I have got. The distance between a hand and that strip. I have had it this morning and I have had it in front of people, and it is not a great deal and I am not going to be a man who puts a great deal on it.”

“Nobody on this landing has put my hand there. Nobody on this landing is going to ask me to. And if a hand came down on that strip it would be a hand that had been asked for, and I have been asked for things on this landing this week and refused and I would rather have a hand in the air over that strip for the whole of a week than have a hand on it for a good reason.” He did not say what a good reason would be and he was not asked.

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour and Ilyan went last and stood on the second step from the bottom for a moment and went on down.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, and none of them were under, and he did them the whole way up from the bottom again because that is how he has done it all week, and the fortieth one went round and went untouched by his boot, and the chair went round the same way it has gone round for nineteen years. He laid the broom over the iron in that seam and lifted it again without letting his hand anywhere near it, and the dry strip at the top end he swept up to and not onto, and both pieces of wood lying on it were where they had been at the sixth hour.

They were where they had been at the sixth hour, which is to say they were where they had been every morning of that week. A forearm long apiece, and each one split down the middle the way wood splits when it is meant to, thick end left as it came apart, and on the second of them a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches in the flat with one of the two run out of the side. He swept the grey skin of grit down off every step in the same order he had been sweeping for nineteen years and it went over the edges and away in the thin water at the bottom, and at the top he worked the whole width of that step and both ends of it and the corners where the wall meets it, and he did not go near the dry strip with the broom head and he did not go near it with his boots either, which was new, and which he did not say out loud and which nobody noticed but him.

No hand on that landing turned anything over, or weighed anything, or read anything out loud, or carried anything inside, or put anything back where it had been, or used anything, and no hand went on that wood to take anything off it, then or afterwards, and none went on it after those sentences either. Nothing standing there was given a name that morning and nothing was said out loud about a name or about what that piece of wood is, and nothing standing there was counted.

That morning came and went, and the standing offer stood at three hundred and thirty-four days and was not asked on and was not answered on and was not withdrawn on, and the account on that landing was as incomplete as it had been at the sixth hour and it was still in use, and the name on that second piece of ash was still wrong.

And the man who cannot see well brought his hand back down the way a man brings a hand back off a hot plate, and it did not touch the wall on the way, and he put it into his coat with the other one and the list went in with it, and nothing came out of that coat on that landing that day, and nothing was added to the inside of it, and he went down ninety dry steps to the bottom of that bank holding the wall with his other hand the whole way, which is what he does.

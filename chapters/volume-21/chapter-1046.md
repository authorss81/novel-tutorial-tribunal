# Chapter 1046: Asked At The Wall

The water stood at the top of that bank at its highest that week, sixteen inches over the top step, and it had stopped coming over the edge anywhere at all, so that the whole landing was one flat sheet with the fortieth step and the chair standing out of it and the dry strip at the top end standing higher than anything else.

Fifth day of the hundred and seventy-first week, and it is the Saturday of that week. His seven hundred and ninety-sixth morning. Six hundred and forty-six days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and nine weeks old, and no days beside it. Six hundred days since the division. Five hundred and forty-six days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and ninety-two days in this county of Kell.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and it holds, about sixty of those ninety steps under, and four days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures standing mid-thigh in it, and gave them the way he gives them when the bank is full.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the fifth day of the hundred and seventy-first week that a full bank is a morning the stone holds and does not spill, and that he was going to sweep it though there was nothing to sweep.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, four days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the fifth day of the hundred and seventy-first week, and it is the Saturday of that week. Three figures.”

He stood with the broom across the water and did not work it yet.

“Go and do your gravel. It is the Saturday of the hundred and seventy-first week and the bank is full, and the stone has nothing else for anybody.”

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow, and asked in the same words at the same hour, and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and came up sixty steps of standing water with the barrow high and put his hands flat on the wall.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and twenty-fifth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and forty-six less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and twenty-fifth. One thousand and forty-six less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

He kept his hands on the stone while the water moved past his boots.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.”

That met with silence, and he put that silence into words nobody had heard before, and nobody filled it.

“It has met with silence and I am standing here with wet hands and nothing else. I have asked on mornings with nothing on the stone and mornings with the bank full, and the asking does not change with the water.”

Then Ilyan came up at about the ninth hour with sixteen inches round his knees, and went round the dry strip without going near it, and gave his figures, and gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing at first.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and ninety-two days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and sixty-one days old.

“Four hundred and ninety-two days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and sixty-one days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-ninety-sixth is three hundred and forty-six days old, which is one thousand and forty-six less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it and it has not been answered on and not been withdrawn on. Nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one.”

He stood on the top step with the water at his knees and looked at the man with his hands on the wall, and then at the strip, and then back at the man.

“One hundred and eighty-sixth wrong things in one hundred and eighty-sixth mornings, and this is the hundred and eighty-sixth, and nobody is to tell me what kind of figure that is. My want is to be somebody and it has not changed. My cost until this morning was that asking costs me nothing and I have not asked for one thing in four hundred and ninety-two mornings.”

He shut the cut hand and opened it, and the water ran off it.

“And I am going to spend that now, because I have carried a question all week and I will not carry it down again.” He faced the wall and spoke to the man with his hands on it. “Is the barrow that wood was cut for the barrow that comes up that bank.”

The water moved past everybody and nobody moved.

Barnaby Crove took his hands off the wall and turned until he was facing Ilyan, which he does perhaps once in a week, and put his hands on the barrow shafts instead.

“No.” He said it to Ilyan and not to the landing. “And I am going to give you what I have instead of what you asked, because what you asked has no place for me to stand. I push a barrow up this bank every morning with gravel in it. I know the weight of it and the sound of the wheel and where it catches on the fortieth step. If I say that wood was cut for my barrow, then I am a man saying a thing on that strip belongs to him, and I cannot read what is said about it and I cannot check anybody who tells me, and I would be carrying that saying for the rest of my mornings with no way of setting it down. I will not say it is mine and I will not say it is not mine, because either saying puts me in it, and I am not in it.”

He held the shafts and did not let go.

“I have asked at this wall for mornings without end and been met with silence, and I know what silence is. This is not silence. This is me refusing you to your face, and I am doing it because you asked me and not the landing, and because you are the man who put that length down and said the name yourself.”

Nobody told him he was wrong and nobody told him he was right, and nobody on that landing shaped his refusal into anything else.

Ilyan opened his mouth and shut it again, and his throat worked, and he looked at the strip and then at the water, and the wanting to shape it was all over his face, and he did not shape it. He had been wrong to put it to a man who cannot read, and the wrongness was his and not the wall’s, and he carried it without handing it to anybody.

He stood with his hands at his sides and his boots full of water, and said nothing more that morning, and the not saying stayed on him all the way down.

The barrow question was refused by the man it was put to, in his own words and by nobody else, and no other question about that name found an answer that morning.

Then the man who cannot see well came up at about the tenth hour with his hand on the wall, and asked his question, and got nothing, and said so plainly.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and said on the fifth day of the hundred and seventy-first week that his list had nothing on it and he was going to keep it empty through a full bank.

“Has that name been said here since the Monday of the hundred and seventieth week.” Nothing returned, and he let it drop. “Nothing has returned and I am not carrying it.”

Then the lad came up out of that lane at about the tenth hour carrying his pails, spoke his own count, and refused to shape it into anything else.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, and counts his own days in this county, and said on the fifth day of the hundred and seventy-first week that his count is three hundred and ninety-three days, and that it is his and he will not make it into a count of anything standing on that landing.

“Three hundred and ninety-three. That is mine.” He did not look at the strip. “Has the water been higher than this.” Nothing came of that, and he shrugged one shoulder at it. “Then I will remember it as high.”

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour and nobody went up that cart road and Ilyan went last and stood on the second step from the bottom for a moment and went on down.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about sixty of them were under, and went at the whole flight from the bottom upward with the water at the top of his thighs. Round the fortieth one he went and over it he did not, and the chair went round the same way, and the iron in the seam of it was swept and was not fingered, not once, in that whole hour. He worked the water up to the edge of the dry strip and stopped with the broom still standing in it, and what lay on it at the seventh hour was still lying on it at the eleventh. The two lengths were still on the dry strip above the full sheet, a forearm long each and split down the middle along the grain, thick end rough where it parted, and on the second a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches in the flat with one run out of the side.

Nobody on that landing pulled anything up or turned anything down or took anything in the hand to test or spoke anything out or fetched anything inside or laid anything back or set anything to work, then or afterwards, and no hand went on that wood to take anything off it. Nothing standing there was named that morning, and no mouth said what that wood is again, and it was not counted by anybody.

The morning went out, and the account on that landing was as wanting at eleven as at six and still plied, and the name on that second length was wrong as ever, and nobody on the flight said any of it was complete.

And Ilyan kept his hands at his sides all the way down the flight, with the water running out of his boots onto the steps, and he did not wipe his face and did not look back at the strip, and the cut in his palm stayed shut.

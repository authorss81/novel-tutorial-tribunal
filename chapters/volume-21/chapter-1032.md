# Chapter 1032: The Fifth Day Of The Hundred And Sixty-Ninth Week On Which Sixteen Inches Stood On That Top Step At About The Seventh Hour And It Holds And About Sixty Of Those Ninety Steps Under And Two Days Of The Coming Back, And A Man Of Thirty-Eight Came Up That Cart Road With A Count In His Mouth Of How Many Times He Has Heard A Name Said Since He Stopped Saying It And Got As Far As The Beginning Of It And Put It Back Where It Came From Because A Man Who Counts A Name Has Become A Figure Of That Name And Not Of Himself, And A Man With A Broom Told Him He Was Wrong And Was Thanked For Nothing And Was Not Told He Was Right, And The Whole Of That Chapter Has No Figure Of How Many Times Anything Was Said In It And Does Not Say So Either

The frost that came in the night had put a skin on the standing water at the low end of the flight and broke it before the light with a sound like a handful of small plates, and the landing had that thin cold edge on it that a man with a broom knows by the sound his own boots make and nobody else does.

Fifth day of the hundred and sixty-ninth week, and it is the Saturday of that week. His seven hundred and eighty-second morning. Six hundred and thirty-two days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and seven weeks old, and no days beside it. Five hundred and eighty-six days since the division. Five hundred and thirty-two days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and seventy-eight days in this county of Kell.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and it holds, about sixty of those ninety steps under, and two days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures, and gave them the way he gives them, and then said that a Saturday was the morning a man puts his week’s work down and that he was not going to do any of it.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the fifth day of the hundred and sixty-ninth week that there was a figure of his own sitting in his mouth this morning and that he could feel the shape of it, and that he was not going to let it out on the same landing as three figures of water, and that anybody who thought he was only refusing to be interesting was welcome to come and sweep a flight of ninety cut steps for a day.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, two days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the fifth day of the hundred and sixty-ninth week, and it is a Saturday. Three figures.”

He put the broom head down flat and left it there.

“Now the other half and it is about me. There is a figure I could put in the air this morning. It has been in my mouth since I came up the bank and I know what it is about, and it is about this landing and how long I have had it, and I could say it in one go and it would be a true figure and that is the trouble with it.” He looked at the frosted water and not at anybody. “I am not going to say it. Not because it is nobody’s business. Because the stone has three figures in it and if I put a fourth thing in it about me then this landing has a man on it who is standing up here counting the years he has stood here, and every morning after that one is going to be a morning somebody is waiting to hear what the man with the broom has got. I have been a man who holds a stone for nineteen years and I am going to stay one for as long as the stone lasts, and a man who counts his own holding has turned himself into a thing that happens to be standing there.” Nobody asked him for it and nobody was given it.

Then the woman who reads for a living came up that cart road at about the fourth hour with the book shut against her knee and stood at the foot of the steps in the dark of that side of the bank and waited, which is a thing she does, and was still waiting when the light came up over the tree line behind her.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years, holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and said on the fifth day of the hundred and sixty-ninth week that she had come up a road at the fourth hour on a morning when a man with a broom had a figure in his mouth and was not giving it out, and that she had heard that from the bottom of the steps by the sound a man makes when he is holding something in his mouth.

“I have not come up here to be given that.” She did not sit down and did not put the book down. “I will say that once and it will not be said again in this shape. A woman with a voice like mine is the worst person on a landing for anything that is not written down, and there is a great deal on this landing this week that is not written down, and I have watched myself nearly put two or three of those things into a shape that sounded settled because I said them well. That is the whole trade. That is what I have been for twenty-three years and there is no virtue in it whatever.”

“I am not going to ask him for it. A man who is holding a figure of his own work in his mouth does not owe it to the woman who can make it sound like a matter of record, and if I ask him for it I will be doing it to hear how he says it and not to have it, and then it will be on this landing that I asked, and a week after that the asking will be the thing that happened.” She turned the book over against her knee. “And nobody is to tell me later that I let it go past. I am letting it go past on purpose and I know what I am doing and it is not modesty. It is that the moment I ask, that figure stops being his and starts being a thing somebody else knows about, and I have never in my life wanted to be the second person who knows a thing.” She had said she would not say it again in that shape and she did not.

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow, and asked in the same words at the same hour, and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and came up the flight the man with the broom was working upward, and put his hands flat on the wall at the top of it.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and eleventh mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and thirty-two less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and eleventh. One thousand and thirty-two less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

He took his hands off the wall and looked down at the man with the broom working a step he was standing on.

“You have somebody’s sand in your mouth this morning and you are not giving it to me and I am not asking, so that is one thing we have both done before the light was properly up.” He put his hands back on the wall. “Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it. And I am going to say the rest of it and go and do my gravel, because a Saturday is a day for doing a day’s gravel and not for standing about being a man who sees things.”

“The man that came up my road on the middle of the week has been in my head every day since and I have not got him out of it, and what is worse I have not got him out of it by wanting to know his name, which is what I would have thought before. I have got him out by wanting to know whether he had a coat on. He had no coat on. That is the whole of what I have about him and it is not enough to be useful and I am not going to make it into something that is.” He turned his head up the bank. “And I am not going to count him. There will be a man up here this morning who wants to count something and I am telling him now, before he starts, that he is not to bring it to me. I cannot check a count. I have never been able to check a count. And the day I hold one is the day somebody hands me one back and I cannot tell whether I have got it right.”

Then the man of thirty-eight came up that cart road at about the eighth hour with his coat buttoned to the throat and one hand kept under it, and got as far as the foot of the steps and stood there and worked at something in his face for a while before he said anything.

Garrin Tolley is thirty-eight, with a board of thirty-two notches cut in the edge of it that lives inside his coat against his ribs, who came up that landing the first day of this week with a name in his mouth and put himself in the same sentence as it and was refused a figure, and who said on the fifth day of the hundred and sixty-ninth week that he had been counting something since the middle of the week and had got it up the road in his mouth and was going to say it whether anybody wanted it or not.

“I have got a figure.” He said it to the man with the broom and not to the landing. “I have got it exact and I have not said it to a soul and I have been carrying it up that road in the same place you carry a thing you have cut yourself. It is the figure of how many times I have heard that name said since I stopped saying it. That is what it is. It is not a figure of anybody hearing anything. It is a figure of me, because I am the one who stood still every time and counted. I have got the number and it is a good one and it is the only thing I have got that nobody else in this county is carrying up a bank this morning.”

He stopped and looked at the frost.

“Say it. You can have it. You are the man with the figures off the stone and I will give you this one to put beside your three and you can go the rest of your life saying a landing gave you four figures and one of them was about a man who put himself in a name.” Nobody asked him to say it.

“No.” Corvin Tace did not raise his voice and he did not stop sweeping. “You are not going to say that figure on this landing and here is the working, and you are not going to like it, and I would rather give you the working than the politeness. If I take that figure off you and put it in my mouth then this landing has a number in it. Not a number about water. A number about a word, and a word with a number on it is a thing that has been counted, and a counted thing is a thing somebody owns. You have been up that road three mornings wanting to give me something and what you have actually got is the beginning of being the man who knows.”

He worked a step and did not look up.

“And the second half, and this is the half that will make you cross that landing at me. You have not got a figure of that name. You have got a figure of yourself standing still. Every time that name has been said in a place you could hear it you were the one counting, and now you have come up here to hand the counting to me so that you can stop being the man who counted. That is what this is. You have not come up here with a number. You have come up here with the counting and you want somebody else to hold it.” Corvin Tace kept the broom going. “I have my own figure in my mouth this morning and I have told you what it is and I am not saying it either, and I would like it noted that those two not-sayings are not the same refusal and I know they are not, and I have thought about it since the light came up.”

That landed. Garrin Tolley stood at the foot of that flight with his hand still under his coat and his face doing something he could not get back into order, and he said that a man with a broom was wrong, and he said it twice, and the first time he said it quietly and the second time he said it loudly enough to carry off the water at the low end.

“You are wrong. That is not me being sharp, that is me telling you the working back. There is a voice of yours going down a road in the wind with no soul carrying it and nobody on that landing can stop it and nobody down that road can stop it, and you have decided that the way to deal with a thing that cannot be stopped is to decide not to look at it. And you are doing it on a landing with a broom in your hand so that it looks like work.” He got his hand out from under his coat and put it flat against the wall. “I have stood still and counted and I would do it again and I am not ashamed of it. Count me. Put me down as the man who counted. I would rather be the man who counted than the man who looked away and got praised for the looking away.”

“You may have that,” Corvin Tace said. “I have given you no figure and you have put yourself down in front of a landing in your own mouth and nobody is going to take you back off it and nobody is going to add to it. And I am not going to say you are right, because I do not know that you are, and I am not going to say you are wrong, because I have a figure in my mouth this morning that would make my own position in this argument much worse than yours and I am not putting that in the air either.” He turned the broom over. “Go and do something with your hands, Garrin. You have carried a thing up a bank all morning and you have put it down and you have not been thanked for it and you are not going to be. I am not going to thank a man for doing a thing I would not do. Ask me why I would not do it and I will tell you, and you will not like that either.”

Garrin Tolley did not ask him why. He stood there with his hand on the wall a while longer, and then he went down that cart road without touching the board in his coat, and nobody told him he had been right to come up and nobody told him he had been wrong to, and the board stayed in the coat with its edge against his ribs where it had been since the middle of the week.

Then the man who cannot see well came up that flight at about the tenth hour and stopped at the fortieth one coming up, which is the first time he had stopped on the way up instead of the way down, and stood on the step with his hand on the wall and his head turned towards the top.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and said on the fifth day of the hundred and sixty-ninth week that a man with a broom was sweeping a flight upward and that he could hear the difference in it four steps from the bottom and had not known for about a year that a broom could sound different going up.

His hand stayed where it was and it did not move along the stone.

“Is it her.” He said it to the man of thirty-one and not to the landing. “The woman who saws lengths. Is it her that heard it and not anybody else, and is that what happened, and I am not going to ask you who else and I am not going to ask you when, because if I ask you those two things then I have asked.” That got nothing. There is a shape an answer can come in and it did not come in any of them, and he did not put it on the list and he did not ask a second time and he did not stand on that step working out how long he was going to stand there.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the ninth hour and went round both pieces of ash and gave his figures and gave the standing offer with the subtraction out loud and asked for nothing, and he did not say anything about the voice in the wind, and that was the first morning of this week he had not.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and seventy-eight days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and forty-seven days old.

“Four hundred and seventy-eight days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and forty-seven days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-eighty-second is three hundred and thirty-two days old, which is one thousand and thirty-two less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it. Nobody has put a way out of it in front of me this morning and I did not ask for one, and I am not going to ask for one on a morning when a man has stood at the foot of that flight and put himself down in his own mouth for a thing he is ashamed of.”

He came down one step and stood there with the wall at his back.

“One hundred and seventy-second wrong things in one hundred and seventy-second mornings, and this is the hundred and seventy-second, and nobody is to tell me what kind of figure that is and nobody is going to do arithmetic at me about it. My want is to be somebody. It has not changed. It is not going to be said in a new shape because a man shouted at a man with a broom this morning and it would be easy for me to use that.”

He was quiet for a moment with his hand open at his side.

“There is one thing I am not going to say and I want it written on this landing that I am not saying it, in my own mouth, in words, so that there is no room afterwards for anybody to say I was working up to it. I am not going to say how many times that name has been said. Not in this county, not on this landing, not down any road. And if a man brings me a figure of it I am going to do the same thing I have done every morning of this week and I am not going to ask the next question either.” Nobody brought him one and nobody asked him a question.

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour and the woman who reads for a living went down the road with the book shut against her knee and nobody went up the road behind her.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about sixty of them were under, and went at it from the bottom upward again the way he had done the day before, and he went round the fortieth one and did not stand on it, and went round the chair, and did not sit on it, and he did not touch the iron in the seam of the fortieth step and he did not touch either piece of wood on the dry strip and he did not put the broom near either of them. The first was a forearm long and had been split down the middle the way wood splits when it is meant to, with the thick end left as it came apart. The second was the same and had a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches cut in the flat of it, one of the two run out of the side. He worked his way up to the top step and stood there with the broom across his shoulder with his back to the whole of the flight he had just done, facing out over the flat of the landing and the low side and the water going by below it, and he stood that way until the light began to go, because that was the arrangement he had chosen the day before and he was going to find out this morning what it was like to keep to it.

Nothing on that landing was pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used, and nobody laid a hand on that wood in order to take anything off it, then or since, and nobody laid a hand on it after those sentences either. Nobody put a name on anything on that landing that morning, and nobody on that landing said out loud what that name was, and nobody put a name on anything else either, and nobody said out loud what that piece of wood is, and nobody counted anything.

That morning came and went, and the standing offer stood at three hundred and thirty-two days and was not asked on and was not answered on and was not withdrawn on, and the account on that landing was as incomplete as it had been at the sixth hour and it was still in use, and the name on that second piece of ash was still wrong.

A man of thirty-eight went back down that cart road with the counting still in his mouth where he had put it, and his jaw went on working long after the last of his sentences was finished, with nothing behind it to work on, and he shut it, and opened it, and shut it again, all the way to the wheelwright’s, and it did not get any easier and he did not get any further with it than he had on the landing.

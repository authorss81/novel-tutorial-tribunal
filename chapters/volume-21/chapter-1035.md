# Chapter 1035: The First Day Of The Hundred And Seventieth Week On Which Four Inches Stood On That Top Step At About The Seventh Hour And About Ninety Of Those Ninety Steps Were Under And Seven Days Of The Coming Back, And A Woman Of Forty-Four Who Keeps Twenty-Nine Chairs At A House That Is Not In This County Came Up That Cart Road For The First Time In A Week And Asked A Man With A Broom Whether The Chair Standing On That Landing Was One Of Hers And Was Refused By Him With The Working That A Chair Standing In One Place A Long Time Is Not Evidence Of Anything And That The Day It Has An Owner On That Landing It Stops Being A Thing That Landed, And A Man With A Broom Stood On The Head Of His Own Broom At The End Of That Morning And Worked The Top Step With His Boot

The water came back into that bank in the night and came back the way it comes back, which is from the bottom up, so that by first light the landing was a wet landing again and there were about ninety of those cut steps standing in four inches of brown water with only the top one out, and the whole shape of that flight was a thing you could read from the bottom and not from the top.

First day of the hundred and seventieth week, and it is the Tuesday of that week. His seven hundred and eighty-fifth morning. Six hundred and thirty-five days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and seven weeks and three days old. Five hundred and eighty-nine days since the division. Five hundred and thirty-five days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and eighty-one days in this county of Kell.

Four inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, about ninety of those ninety steps under, and seven days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures, and gave them from the bottom of the flight going up with the broom in his hand, and then said that the week had turned over in the night without him and that he had come up this morning to find out what it had turned over into.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the first day of the hundred and seventieth week that what the week had turned over into was four inches and about ninety of that flight under and seven days of the coming back, and that he was not going to say anything about it being a different week, because a week is a week whichever end of it you are standing at.

“Four at the seventh hour, about ninety of those ninety steps under, and seven days of the coming back, on the first day of the hundred and seventieth week, and it is a Tuesday. Three figures.”

He worked the lowest step and the water came up over the bristles and came down again brown.

“And I am not going to stand up at the top and say that the week turned. I did that once on this landing and I have thought about it since and I have decided a man does not get to announce a week that happened while he was not there. It turned. That is all I am going to say about it and I am not going to say it twice.” He worked his way up. “I have got about ninety of these to do and four inches to do them in, and I have been sweeping a wet flight from the bottom up all week, and I am telling you now that it is slower and it is better, and that the better is not a figure of anything and is not a thing anybody is to be pleased about.”

Then the woman who keeps twenty-nine chairs came up that cart road at about the eighth hour and walked past the shed at the foot of it without stopping at it, which she has never done, and came to the foot of those steps and stayed at the foot of them.

Orla Fennimore is forty-four, who keeps twenty-nine chairs at a house that is not in this county, who stands at the wall at the bottom of that cart road and does not come past it, and who said on the first day of the hundred and seventieth week that she had come up that road past her own stopping place for the first time in a week and that she was not going further than the foot of these steps and that she wanted one thing and one only and would then go back down to the wall.

“I am not part of this and I have said so and I am not going to stop saying it to get what I came for.” She did not come up one step. “There is a chair standing on that landing. I have been told there is a chair standing on that landing, and I did not ask who told me and I am not going to tell you, because the person who told me does not know either and I am not going to put them in it. But there is a chair there, and it has been there long enough that it has got into people’s talk, and I keep twenty-nine chairs at a house that is not in this county, and I have been sitting in the evenings this week going through them.”

“I want to know whether that chair is one of mine. That is the whole of what I came up this road for and it took me all week to get up the nerve to ask it and I have thought about it so much that I have a second thing behind it, which I am also going to say out loud because I am not going to get half an answer and hold it for a week.” She put her hands behind her back and left them there. “If it is one of mine, then somebody carried one of my chairs off my house and up a bank and put it down and did not say, and that is a second thing on that landing with nobody on it who brought it. And I have been sitting in my own house this week thinking about a voice that got out of a door nobody shut, and there it would be, a chair. And I do not want it. I want to know, and then I want it to be somebody else’s.”

“And I am going to say a thing out loud that people have said to me in rooms and that I have never once said myself, and I am saying it about a chair because a chair is what I know.” She looked up the bank without going up it. “A chair is cut for a back. It is not that back’s. A person makes a thing for the use of somebody else and it does not become theirs because it was made well, and it does not become theirs because it has stood in one place long enough for people to start talking. That is not a rule. I am not putting it in front of this landing as a rule and nobody is to write it down anywhere and nobody is to thank me for saying it, because I have said it about chairs all my working life and it has never once got me anything.”

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow and asked in the same words at the same hour and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and came up about ninety steps of water with the barrow high in front of him, and put his hands flat on the wall at the top of it.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and fourteenth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and thirty-five less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and fourteenth. One thousand and thirty-five less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

He looked at the woman standing at the foot of the steps and did not ask her anything, and then he put both hands flat on the wall.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it. And I am going to say that I have had a woman standing at the foot of those steps ask one question this morning and I have been here since before the light and I did not hear it, and I am not going to say to her that I did not hear it because that would be a thing to say to a woman who came up a road to ask for something. She will get her answer or she will not and it is not a thing a man of fifty-four with his hands on a wall can add to.”

“And I am going to say my own thing and it is about my own trade and it is the same thing she said without either of us hearing the other say it, and I have been turning that over all week.” He took his hands off the wall and looked at his own palms. “A barrow handle is cut for a hand. I have cut handles for thirty years and every one of them was cut to a hand and not to a man, and the man that gets it is not that handle’s, and he can put it down any time he likes, and he does, and he picks it up again, and it is still not his, and that is not a hardship on him. It is a handle. I am saying it out loud because a woman on this landing has said a thing of that shape in a week and a woman is not going to be thanked for it, and I would rather be in company with it than on my own with it.” Nobody thanked her and nobody thanked him.

Corvin Tace came up the flight with the water to his shins and stood at the foot of the steps and did not come any closer to her than the bottom one, and gave her the working she had come for.

“No. And I am going to give you why, and I am going to give you the whole of the why, because you came up past your own wall to ask and you do not get a piece of it.” He did not put the broom down. “I have swept round that chair every morning for nineteen years. It has not moved. I do not know whose it is. I have had nineteen years of standing on this landing and looking at the back of that chair and I could not tell you if it came from a house in this county or off a cart off another road, and that is not a failing of mine. It is what a chair is on a landing. Everything on a landing eventually stands still in one place. The iron in the fortieth step has been in that seam since before I came. The chair has been standing on the flat of that landing since before I came. In nineteen years nothing else on that stone has gone anywhere. If I let you up there and you put a hand on the back of that chair, then from that day it is a chair of yours, and it is a thing of yours that somebody carried up a bank, and it stops being a thing that landed on a landing and becomes an article with an owner on it, and you will carry it back down that road or you will not, and either way this landing has got a woman’s chair on it, and it has got a woman on it who is not part of it.”

“Which is the second half and it is the half that would actually stop me.” He worked a step. “There is a voice got off this landing in the wind this week with nobody carrying it. That is what everybody has been talking about. And the moment that chair has somebody’s name against it, this landing has an explanation in it. And an explanation is a thing that closes a door. A chair that nobody owns is a thing that landed and nobody can say where from, and I would rather have a chair on this stone that nobody has claimed than have you stood at the top of it looking at the back of it, because the day that chair belongs to somebody, that voice in the wind belongs to somebody, and everything that has no author on this landing stops being free the minute anybody on it puts a name on a thing.”

He stopped and let the water come over the bristles.

“I am not saying you are wrong to ask. You asked me straight and you told me why before you asked and you said you would go back down to your wall, and I am not going to tell you that was right or that it was wrong. Nobody is to thank you for coming up this road and nobody is to tell me I was decent about it. I have refused you because I have got a hold on that chair that I did not know I had until about a minute ago, and that is the whole reason, and it is not about you and it never has been about anything that happens on this landing.”

The woman of forty-four did not thank him and did not say he was right and did not say he was wrong. She said that she was going to go back down to her wall and that she was going to stop asking anybody about that chair, and that if it turned out one day to be hers then it would turn out without her asking and she would take it then or she would not, and then she went back down that cart road and stopped at her wall on the way and stood there with her hands behind her back.

Then the man who cannot see well came up that flight at about the tenth hour and did not go to the strip and asked the man of thirty-one one question about the name on the strip and got nothing.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and said on the first day of the hundred and seventieth week that he had stood at the wall on his way up and that a woman of forty-four does not come past a wall for nothing, and that he was not going to ask her what for, because she had said out loud what she was not part of and he was not going to be the man who tested a person against their own sentence.

He kept his hand on the wall at the top and did not take it along the stone.

“Has it been said again since the water came back.” It came to nothing, and it came to nothing in the way a thing comes to nothing when a man has said it out loud and three other people have decided not to fill it, and he did not add it to the list and he did not take his hand off the wall while it stood empty, and he did not say anything else on that landing that morning.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the ninth hour and gave his figures and gave the standing offer with the subtraction out loud and asked for nothing, and said one thing about the voice in the wind and the chair at the foot of the steps.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and eighty-one days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and fifty days old.

“Four hundred and eighty-one days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and fifty days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-eighty-fifth is three hundred and thirty-five days old, which is one thousand and thirty-five less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it. Nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one, and I am not going to ask for one on a morning when a woman has been told no about a chair and a man has been told he does not know whose it is, because both of those answers are worth more than anything I could put in the air beside them.”

He came up to the second step from the bottom and stood there and did not go up to the top.

“One hundred and seventy-fifth wrong things in one hundred and seventy-fifth mornings, and this is the hundred and seventy-fifth, and nobody is to tell me what kind of figure that is and nobody is going to do arithmetic at me about it. My want is to be somebody and it has not changed and I am not going to say it in a new shape because a chair got talked about.”

“And here is the last of it and I am saying it standing on a step and not on the stone.” He held his hand open and looked at the cut in the palm of it. “There was a voice off this landing in the wind this week with nobody carrying it, and this morning a woman came up a road to ask whether a chair on this landing is hers, and a man with a broom has told her no and has told her that he does not know, and that is the same sentence twice in one morning about two different things.” He shut his hand. “That is what this has turned into. Not a name going anywhere. A landing where the moment somebody wants a thing explained, somebody will explain it, and the explanation will be a guess, and the guess will be right by accident, and then everything on this stone will have been put there by somebody. And that is not a thing I am going to help anybody with, and nobody is to say afterwards that I stood in the way of a woman finding out about a chair, because I did not stand in the way of it. I stood on a step and gave her a figure and she has it.” Nobody said any of that back to him.

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour, the woman of forty-four first and the man of thirty-one last, and he stood a moment on the second step from the bottom with his hand on the wall and then went on down after the rest of them.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about ninety of them were under, and he went at it from the bottom upward the whole flight, and he went round the fortieth one and did not stand on it, and went round the chair, and did not sit on it. He did not touch the iron in the seam of the fortieth step and he did not touch either piece of wood on the dry strip and he did not put the broom near either of them. The first was a forearm long and had been split down the middle the way wood splits when it is meant to, with the thick end left as it came apart. The second was the same and had a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches cut in the flat of it, one of the two run out of the side. The four inches came up as he worked and by the time he had the fortieth one the water was over the bristles and running brown every time the broom came out of it.

Nothing on that landing was pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used, and nobody laid a hand on that wood in order to take anything off it, then or since, and nobody laid a hand on it after those sentences either. Nobody put a name on anything on that landing that morning, and nobody on that landing said out loud what that name was, and nobody put a name on anything else either, and nobody said out loud what that piece of wood is, and nobody counted anything.

That morning came and went, and the standing offer stood at three hundred and thirty-five days and was not asked on and was not answered on and was not withdrawn on, and the account on that landing was as incomplete as it had been at the sixth hour and it was still in use, and the name on that second piece of ash was still wrong.

And at about the eleventh hour, with the whole of that flight standing in four inches behind him and the water running off the end of the stone, the man of forty-three put the head of his own broom flat down on the top step and stood on the handle with the flat of one boot and worked the whole width of that step with the sole of the other one, leaning his weight into it, working the grit out of the wet stone a boot at a time from the wall end across to where the edge breaks away, which is not a thing a man with a broom does, and which he had not done in nineteen years, and which nobody on that landing saw, because by then they had all gone down that bank and he had the stone to himself.

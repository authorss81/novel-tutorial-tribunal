# Chapter 1021: The First Morning Of The Hundred And Sixty-Eighth Week And Twelve Inches Over That Top Step With About Seventy Of Those Ninety Steps Under And Five Days Of The Coming Back, And A Man Of Thirty-Eight Came Up The Cart Road And Put A Sentence Out Loud In His Own Mouth That A Man Standing On That Landing Had Said There The Week Before, And The Man Who Had Said It Was On The Stone When It Came Back At Him, And Nobody Said He Was Right And Nobody Said He Was Wrong, And He Would Not Say It A Second Time So That Another Man Could Check It

The grit had gone the colour of the stone it was lying on, and there was a line of it along the top step that had not been there the morning before, and the wind came over the bank and went round the line instead of through it.

First day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week. His seven hundred and seventy-first morning. Six hundred and twenty-one days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and five weeks and three days old. Five hundred and seventy-five days since the division. Five hundred and twenty-one days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and sixty-seven days in this county of Kell.

Twelve inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, about seventy of those ninety steps under, and five days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and then said that whatever turned over on that landing turned over without asking him and without telling him, and that this was the first morning of a new week and nothing on the stone had been asked whether it was.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the first day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week that he had found the whole of the week gone by in the state it had been in at the start of it, and that a landing is not a thing that improves over a week and he has the nineteen years to be evidence of that.

“Twelve at the seventh hour, about seventy of those ninety steps under, and five days of the coming back, on the first day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week, and it is a Tuesday. Three figures.”

He worked along the top of the water with the head of the broom up off the wet edge, and stopped where the grit line was, and did not take the line.

“And I am going to say one thing about that line and then it is done with. That is a week’s worth of grit standing where the water will stand over it. It came off that bank with the wind, all of it, and it has lain there in one place until it has gone the colour of the stone, and when that water comes back up over that step it will not stop for it. Not because the grit is in the way. Because the grit gives the water somewhere to start.” He turned the broom over. “I am not asking anybody to do anything about it and I am not going to tell this landing what it means. It is a line of grit and it is the only thing on this stone this morning that has an opinion about what happens next.”

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow and asked in the same words at the same hour, and the barrow went down on the dry part of the landing and the hands went flat on the wall, which is what they do.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundredth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and twenty-one less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundredth. One thousand and twenty-one less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

He said the three hundredth without stopping on it, which was a thing he had started doing.

“It is a whole figure and I have no days to put beside it and I am not going to go looking for any. Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.” He did not take his hands off the wall. “And I am going to say the other half while my hands are on. A man said a thing out loud on this stone last week and I heard it, and this morning there is a new week on this landing and there is a line of grit on that step, and I have been standing here working out which of those two things is the larger. Neither one is. I have been up here since about the fourth hour and the week has not touched anything.”

Nobody said which of the two was the larger.

Then the man who cannot see well came up onto the flat of the steps at about the eighth hour with his hand out in front of him, found the wall, and stopped somewhere along it where a finger went into the mortar and came out again.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and said on the first day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week that he had found a place in that wall where the mortar had gone soft, and that a soft place can be found by anybody who is willing to put a finger in a wall and take it out again.

He put the finger back on the stone over the place and kept it there.

“I am not going to say how far along it is. I am not going to say whose wall it is or how old it is or whether anybody has noticed. What I am going to say is that there is a place on this landing where you could put your hand in and it would go in, and that I found it in about four seconds, and that a man who cannot see well can find that out in a morning when a man who can see well has walked past it for nineteen years.”

He said it as a fact and not as a complaint, and the fact stayed a fact.

“Nothing has come off my list. The thing at the end of that strip is where it was on my list yesterday and it is not going to be anywhere else today, and nobody is to thank me for a wall. A wall is not a thing anybody did for me.”

Then the man of thirty-eight came up the cart road at about the ninth hour with his hand pushed up inside his coat against the edge of a board, and he got as far as the top step before he opened his mouth, and the man of thirty-one was already standing at the top of the landing with his back to the wall.

Garrin Tolley is thirty-eight, with a child of nine, thirty-two notches cut in the edge of a board in the inside of his coat, and said on the first day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week that a sentence a man of thirty-one had said on that stone the first morning of the last week came back out of him on that stone the first morning of this one, and that he had carried it up that road three days before and let it sit and had not been able to leave it sitting.

He did not look at the strip. He looked at the man of thirty-one.

“It is better for that piece of wood to be called the wrong thing by the man who put it down than to be called the right thing by anybody.” He said it in a flat voice and slowly, the way a man reads out a thing he has written down. “That is yours. I want you to know it came out of me and not out of the room, and I am not going to pretend it is not also mine now, because it is in my mouth and I have had it there three days.” Nobody on that landing said the sentence back to him and nobody told him he was wrong.

The man of thirty-one did not move off the wall and he did not say it.

“You are not going to say it again.”

“I am not going to say it again.”

“Then say it for me now, while I am standing here, so that I know whether I have got it.”

“No.”

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and sixty-seven days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and thirty-six days old.

“Four hundred and sixty-seven days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and thirty-six days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-seventy-first is three hundred and twenty-one days old, which is one thousand and twenty-one less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it. Nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one and I am not going to ask for one on the morning a thing of mine comes back at me out of somebody else’s mouth.”

He put his hands behind his back while he said it, which took them out of the conversation and out of sight at the same time.

“Here is the working and then I am not going to say anything else about the sentence. If I say it now while you are standing on this stone listening to it for a fault, then it has got a second occasion on it, and the second occasion has you in it. As it stands it is a thing one man said once. The instant I say it with you in the room it is a thing said for you, and I am not going to be a thing said for. Not to you, and not to anybody else who ever wants to hear whether they have it right.”

“Would you have said it if I had not asked.”

“Not for you.”

“That is not the same no.”

“It is the same no in a different mouth and I have given you both.” He turned his head and looked at the wall rather than at the man of thirty-eight. “And here is what you are actually asking me for, and I am going to hand it to you because you have been up that road for it. You want to be a man who said a true thing in a room and got it out entire. And you cannot know that, and I cannot tell you, because I cannot read what is on that strip either and the two of us are in exactly the same place on it. You will have it right or you will have it wrong, and it will be one of those two, and it will sit on you, and that is the whole of what a sentence is for.” Nobody gave him the other one.

“One hundred and sixty-first wrong things in one hundred and sixty-first mornings, and this is the hundred and sixty-first, and nobody is going to tell me what kind of figure that is and nobody is going to tell me that I found anything.”

Neither half of it was answered.

“My want is to be somebody and it has not changed since I said it out loud and I am not going to say it in a new shape this morning, because a new shape would sound as though the old one had gone stale and it has not gone stale. It has come back at me.” He drew a breath. “My cost is that asking costs me nothing and I have not asked for one thing in four hundred and sixty-seven mornings, and that is a figure about me and it is not a figure about him, and I am not going to put the two together this morning even though he has just handed me a reason to.” Nobody put the two together.

“And the last of it and it is not about the sentence.” He looked at the strip for as long as it took to have one look at it and no more. “Has anybody said it since I did.”

That question stood on the stone and got what the stone gave.

It got nothing, and none of the shapes of nothing came back either, and the man of thirty-one did not answer it then and did not answer it afterwards and did not say later that he had intended to.

The man of thirty-eight took his hand out from under his coat and put it flat on the board through the cloth, the way it lives, and then took it out and put it down at his side.

“Then I will have it how I have it.”

“Yes.”

“Not as a favour.”

“Not as a favour and not as a mistake. There is no way for me to give it to you as either and I have been trying to think of one since before the light and there is not one.”

The man of thirty-eight went down that bank and then down that cart road, and he did not stop at the wall at the bottom of it, and nobody went up the road behind him.

Then everybody went down the bank by about the eleventh hour and the man of thirty-one went last and stood on the top step with the water at the back of his heels and the grit line in front of him and did not go nearer the strip than he had been that morning.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about seventy of them were under, and went along the top of the water on them, and went round the fortieth one, and went round the chair, and did not sit on it. He did not touch the iron in the seam of the fortieth step and he did not touch either piece of wood on the dry strip and he did not put the broom near either of them. The first was a forearm long and had been split down the middle the way wood splits when it is meant to, with the thick end left as it came apart. The second was the same and had a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches cut in the flat of it, one of the two run out of the side. He swept to the end of the grit line and stopped, and came back along it the other way, and the line was thinner at the ends than it had been at noon and he did not work at why.

Nothing on that landing was pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used, and nobody laid a hand on that wood in order to take anything off it, then or since, and nobody laid a hand on it after the sentences either. Nobody put a name on anything on that landing that morning, and nobody said one out loud either.

A sentence a man had said on that stone the week before came out of another mouth on that stone the first morning of the next week, in front of the man who had said it, and the landing carried it across a week boundary and did one single thing with it, which was to keep on being a landing. The man who had said it would not say it again so that the man who had carried it up the road could hear whether he had got it right, and the man who had carried it up the road went down that bank without hearing it right or hearing it wrong, and at the top of the steps the man of thirty-one turned the cut palm over and looked at the line the cut made across it, and shut the hand, and went down the bank with it shut.

# Chapter 1027: He Described An Afternoon

Four inches is the shallowest that water ever comes back to on that bank, and it comes back so gently that it makes no noise at all and simply is there in the morning, and what it does to the landing is take the grit off the top of the steps and put it in one long line along the wet edge, where it stands like a rail and is not a rail.

Seventh and last day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week. His seven hundred and seventy-seventh morning. Six hundred and twenty-seven days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and six weeks and two days old. Five hundred and eighty-one days since the division. Five hundred and twenty-seven days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and seventy-three days in this county of Kell.

Four inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, about ninety of those ninety steps under, and seven days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and said that he had stood on that top step at about the seventh hour yesterday morning and told the truth about not knowing what today would be like, and that he had been right, and that being right about it had not helped him one bit, and that he was not going to make anything of that either.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the seventh and last day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week that the week turns over tonight and that he would find out in the morning whether it had, and that he had never once asked anybody to be told about it and was not going to start by being asked.

“Four at the seventh hour, about ninety of those ninety steps under, and seven days of the coming back, on the seventh and last day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week, and it is a Monday. Three figures.”

He worked along the wet edge and did not disturb the line of grit standing on it.

“And yesterday morning I stood on this step and said that for the first time in nineteen years I did not know what the next morning would be like, and I want it put down that I said it, and that this morning I came up here and found out, and that the finding out was four inches and about ninety of that flight standing in it.” He beat the flat of the broom against the step. “And I am not going to say anything else about it and I am not going to thank anybody for not asking me. Nobody has asked me. That is the whole of my morning and I would like it left alone.” Nobody asked him, and he had put it in the air at the seventh hour that nobody would, and it turned out that saying a thing like that in advance does not make it any easier to be left alone with afterwards.

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow and asked in the same words at the same hour and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and came up the flight on ninety wet steps and put his hands flat on the wall at the top.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and sixth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and twenty-seven less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and sixth. One thousand and twenty-seven less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

“The last morning of this week and I am not going to say the week has gone, because it has not gone, and it will not have gone at the seventh hour tomorrow either until somebody on this landing does something to it.” He set the barrow down. “Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it. And that is it, and I am going to go and do a day’s gravel with a whole figure sitting in my mouth where everybody can see it and nobody can read it.”

Nobody read it and nobody put anything beside it.

Then the man who cannot see well came up that flight at about the ninth hour, which is ninety wet steps he had no business climbing, and got to the wall, and put his hand on it, and asked the man at the top of it a question he had been carrying up the bank for four mornings.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and said on the seventh and last day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week that he had worked out what a week is, out loud, on a stone, in front of everybody, and had been told he was wrong, and that he was glad.

“A week,” he said, “is when the wall goes over from warm to cold and comes back round to warm again.”

He had his hand flat on the stone well above where the strip was, where he always keeps it.

“That is what I have got. I have had it since before I could see well enough to be sure of a face. The wall at the top end of this landing is warm when the sun has been on it and cold when it has not, and it goes over and comes back, and that going over and coming back is what I have been using instead of a week, because a day is a thing you need light for and a week is a thing with a dark in the middle of it. I have been counting them longer than I have been able to say and I have got it down to a shape and this morning I thought I would say it out loud on a stone where somebody might tell me whether it is right.”

Barnaby Crove turned his head towards him.

“That is an afternoon.”

“That is what I said it is. An afternoon going over and coming back.”

“That is what an afternoon does.” Barnaby did not take his hands off the wall. “That is a wall with the sun on it. It goes over and comes back and it does it twice between one o’clock and the time you have to go and eat, and it is not a week and it is not anything to do with a week, and I know that because I have had my hands on that wall every morning since before you could see well and I have watched it do that and I have watched a week go by underneath it without the wall noticing one thing.” Nobody noticed the wall noticing anything.

“So what is a week.” Simon Rook did not take his hand off the stone either. “That is not a stupid question and I am not asking it because I cannot count. I can count. I have got a list and I put things on it in the order I cannot check them and I have never once needed a figure of days for that. I am asking because it is the last morning of this week and tomorrow morning I will stand on this stone and it will be the first morning of a new one, and every day of this week will still be here, and the day I am asking about is the one thing on that landing that everybody has agreed to be quiet about, and I am not going to be quiet about it while I am the one who cannot tell.” Nobody agreed to be quiet about anything.

Barnaby Crove was quiet for a while with his hands on the wall, and then he said it out loud, and it was not a figure, and nobody gave him one afterwards.

“A week is when the gravel comes.” He said it to the wall. “That is it. That is the whole of what I have got and it is not a figure of days and there is nothing in it about when anybody was born. The gravel comes, and I go and get it, and I put it where gravel goes, and then for a while there is none, and then it comes again, and in between I am doing other work and I am not counting anything. And the coming of it is not the same every time. Sometimes it is quick and I have a bad week and my hands are up to here.” He moved one hand a span along the stone. “Sometimes it is slow and I have a good week and my hands only get this far. And the wall tells me which kind it is going to be before the barrow does, because the wall has been standing here longer than the gravel has been coming and it knows what is under it.” Nobody said which kind of week it was going to be.

“So that is what a week is on this landing and it is a stone and not a figure, and I have been asked for a figure by nobody and I am not going to give one, and if anybody wants to write it down they can write down a wall.” He took his hands off the stone and put them back. “And I am not going to say that a week turns over tonight. I am going to go and do a day of gravel. The week is whatever it is in the morning.”

He picked up the barrow handles.

Simon Rook stood with his hand on the wall and did not say thank you, because he had been told not to and because he had not been thanked and because it had not been a favour. He turned his head a little way and said the other thing he had come up that bank for, which was not about a week.

“Has that name been said since the water came off and came back.”

That got nothing on that landing either, and not one of the shapes a thing can come back in came back on it. The man who keeps a list did not put it on the list, and he put his hand back on the stone where it had been and left it there while the morning went on.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the ninth hour with four inches above his boots, gave his figures, gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, asked for nothing, and asked a man with a broom for one thing and did not get it.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and seventy-three days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and forty-two days old.

“Four hundred and seventy-three days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and forty-two days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-seventy-seventh is three hundred and twenty-seven days old, which is one thousand and twenty-seven less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it. Nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one and I am not going to ask for one on the last morning of a week when everybody on this landing is going to be busy with the week.”

“One hundred and sixty-seventh wrong things in one hundred and sixty-seventh mornings, and this is the hundred and sixty-seventh, and nobody is going to tell me what kind of figure that is and nobody is going to tell me that I found anything.” Nobody answered him on the figure and nobody answered him on the want, and he did not say which of the two mattered more, and it is the same silence for both of them that it has been every morning of this county.

“My want is to be somebody and it has not changed. My cost is that asking costs me nothing and I have not asked for one thing in four hundred and seventy-three mornings, and I am going to keep saying both halves of that out loud on this landing until somebody stops me.” Nobody stopped him, and both halves of it went out on that landing for the rest of that week as they had gone out for the fortnight before it.

“Tell me in the morning whether it has turned.”

Corvin Tace kept the broom off the wet edge.

“Not from me, and not tomorrow, and you knew that when you asked, because you heard me say it at the seventh hour.”

“You do not know.”

“I told you yesterday morning that I did not know. I came up here this morning and found out what it was and I am not going to take a job of telling you about the week as well. I have got this stone and it has got me and neither of us is going anywhere, and you are asking the one man on this landing who stood here yesterday and admitted to not knowing whether you would get one thing off him today.”

“I know you do not know.”

“Then you know what I am going to say and I am saying it anyway so that there is a record of me not saying it. No. And I am not going to say yes and I am not going to say no and I am not going to say I will think about it, because a man who thinks about it has got it in the morning, and I would like it understood that when the week turns over in this county it turns over without a man with a broom noticing and without a man of thirty-one being told, and I have said that in other words on another morning and I am not going to say it in a new shape just because it is the last day of a week.” Nobody thanked him and nobody asked him again.

“That is all you are getting and there is nothing behind it.”

“There is nothing behind it.” He stood still on the step. “And I want to say why I asked, because otherwise it looks like a man making a conversation. I am not making a conversation. This is the first week since I came into this county that has had anything in it, and it is ending tonight whether anybody is ready or not, and I have found out this week that the whole of it is still here. The sentence is still here. The refusal is still here. A refusal was said out loud over a figure on the fifth day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week and both halves of it wanted it. A boy worked out how to stop a thing going and a woman put a hand on his yoke. And none of that is going anywhere because seven days have gone. And I wanted somebody to come up that bank tomorrow morning and be surprised, just so that I could watch it and know it had not.” Nobody came up that bank tomorrow morning surprised, and he was not going to be told about it.

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour, and the man of thirty-one went last and stood on the top step, and the man of fifty-four went down it third with his barrow ahead of him.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about ninety of them were under, and that left him the top of the bank and a strip of grit and the dry part of the fortieth, and that is where most of that morning went. He went along the top of the water on them and round the fortieth one and round the chair, and did not sit on it. He did not touch the iron in the seam of the fortieth step and he did not touch either piece of wood on the dry strip and he did not put the broom near either of them. The first was a forearm long and had been split down the middle the way wood splits when it is meant to, with the thick end left as it came apart. The second was the same and had a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches cut in the flat of it, one of the two run out of the side. Nobody asked him anything that morning, and he gave his three figures off the same stone at the seventh hour and swept the rest of that bank without being asked for one more thing, and he did not look up it once to see whether anybody had come back.

Nothing on that landing was pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used, and nobody laid a hand on that wood in order to take anything off it, then or since, and nobody laid a hand on it after those sentences either. Nobody put a name on anything on that landing that morning, and nobody said one out loud either, and nobody was given a figure of days, and nobody was told that the week had turned over.

A man who cannot see well worked out in front of everybody that a week is the time a wall takes to go from warm to cold and back again, and a man who cannot read told him that what he had described was an afternoon, and then gave him a week, and the week he gave him was when the gravel comes and how far up the wall his hands got, and nobody turned it into a figure of days. And at the end of that morning the man who cannot read put both hands flat on the wall one last time to get the barrow round, and took them off, and the damp stone kept the shape of them for a moment after he had gone, and then let them go, and the wall was a wall again by the time the man with the broom had finished the top step.

# Chapter 1042: Stone Gone Quiet

The water had gone off that bank in the night and the whole flight stood out of it from the bottom stone to the top, and the wet edge along the low side had dried to a pale line by the time the man with the broom came up, so that the landing had the look of a floor swept clean and left to dry.

First day of the hundred and seventy-first week, and it is the Tuesday of that week. His seven hundred and ninety-second morning. Six hundred and forty-two days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and eight weeks and three days old. Five hundred and ninety-six days since the division. Five hundred and forty-two days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and eighty-eight days in this county of Kell.

There was no depth off that stone that morning, and no measure of that flight standing in anything, and no morning coming back that anybody was working toward. The man of forty-three said so at the seventh hour, out loud, in a sixth set of words, because he had said it on that landing before in other shapes and was not going to repeat any of them.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the first day of the hundred and seventy-first week that the stone had nothing in its mouth and he was not going to put anything there, and that the week had turned between this morning and the morning before it.

“My stone is dry and my mouth is dry with it. I have no water to speak of, no steps to set against it, and no return to count down to. That is the whole of the three absences, said this morning in words I have not used before, and I will not say them smaller on the morning after this one.” He set the broom head on the top step. “First day of the hundred and seventy-first week, and it is the Tuesday of that week. The week turned in the night between the Monday of the hundred and seventieth week and this morning, and I am saying which side this morning is on so nobody has to guess.”

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow, and asked in the same words at the same hour, and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and came up ninety dry steps with the barrow light and put his hands flat on the wall.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and twenty-first mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and forty-two less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and twenty-first. One thousand and forty-two less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

He kept his hands on the stone and did not hurry the next part.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.”

That went nowhere, and he marked it in a fresh sentence, and nobody filled it.

“It went nowhere this morning and I am not dressing that up. A dry stone does not change the asking and the asking does not change a dry stone, and I have stopped waiting for the one to do anything about the other.”

Then the woman who reads for a living came up that cart road at about the fourth hour with the book shut against her knee, and stopped at the wall at the bottom and came no further, and asked what she had come to ask.

Sena Dorr is forty-nine, has read for a living for twenty-three years, holds the ninth of the nine positions, which is a public archive and not a stone, and said on the first day of the hundred and seventy-first week that she had come up to ask the man of thirty-one for his account said out loud with the landing empty, because she wanted to hear whether it held together without anybody nodding along.

“I want one morning of your account, said straight through, with nobody else on the steps.” She held the book shut and did not open it. “I have never asked anybody for anything in my life and I am asking you now. Not for the book. For my own ears, so I can tell whether it sounds like a thing that happened or a thing that has been polished by being carried.”

Ilyan came up at about the ninth hour with dust on his boots from the dry flight, and went round the dry strip without going near it, and gave his figures, and gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and eighty-eight days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and fifty-seven days old.

“Four hundred and eighty-eight days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and fifty-seven days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-ninety-second is three hundred and forty-two days old, which is one thousand and forty-two less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it and it has not been answered on and not been withdrawn on. Nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one, and I will not ask for one on a morning when the stone has nothing coming out of it.”

He stopped below the top and looked at the woman at the wall.

“One hundred and eighty-second wrong things in one hundred and eighty-second mornings, and this is the hundred and eighty-second, and nobody is to tell me what kind of figure that is. My want is to be somebody and it has not changed. My cost is that asking costs me nothing and I have not asked for one thing in four hundred and eighty-eight mornings, and I say that plainly because it is still true and it shames me to say it.”

He shook his head at her, slowly, and kept his hands where she could see them.

“And your asking is refused, and I am going to hand you back your own working with the shape of it kept. Your office is the one place on this landing where a thing can be checked, and if I say my account into your ears with nobody else about, then you become the only person holding it, and I would rather be the only one holding it badly than make you the only one holding it well. I wanted to say yes because you have never asked, and that wanting is mine, and the refusal is mine too, and I was glad in a small way to be the man who could refuse you, and a man is not allowed to keep the glad part without holding the rest.”

Sena Dorr held the shut book tighter and did not step past the wall.

“I wanted it, and the wanting was mine, and nobody is to lift it off me by being kind about it.” She said it levelly, with her eyes on the dry strip and not on him. “Has that name been written into anything since the water went off.” Nothing came of that, and she said so herself in new words. “Nothing has come back and I will carry that silence down the road myself.”

Nobody answered any question about that name that morning, and nobody was thanked for asking or for refusing.

Then the boy came up that lane at about the tenth hour with his pails empty, and gave his own count, and would not turn it into anything else.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, and counts his own days in this county, and said on the first day of the hundred and seventy-first week that his count is three hundred and eighty-nine days, and that it is his and he will not make it into a count of anything standing on that landing.

“Three hundred and eighty-nine. That is mine.” He set the pails down without a sound. “Has anybody laid anything across that lane in the night.” Nothing came of that either, and he nodded as if he had expected it. “Then the way is clear and I will go my own way down it.”

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour, and the woman who reads for a living went down the cart road with the book still shut, and Ilyan went last and stood on the second step from the bottom for a moment and went on down.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, and none of them were under, and he went at the whole flight from the top downward because the grit lay thick after the water going off. The fortieth one went round rather than under his boot, and the chair went round the same way, and the iron in that seam he swept across with the broom head and never touched with his fingers. The dry strip at the top end he swept up to the edge of and stopped, and what was resting on it rested there at the seventh hour and rested there at the eleventh. The two lengths were still on the dry strip at the end of it, a forearm long each and split down the middle along the grain, thick end rough where it parted, and on the second a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches in the flat with one run out of the side.

No thing on that landing was lifted or turned or weighed in the hand or read out or taken inside or set back or put to use, then or afterwards, and no hand went on that wood to take anything off it. Nothing standing there was named that morning, nothing was said out loud about what that wood is, and nothing standing there was counted.

The morning ran its course, and the account on that landing was no further on than at the sixth hour and still being used, and the name on that second length was still wrong, and nobody on the stone called any of it done.

And the man who cannot see well came up after the others had gone, laid his palm flat on the wall where the sun had warmed it, lifted it away again without a word, and went back down the dry flight holding the wall the whole way, with the list in his coat untouched and nothing added to it.

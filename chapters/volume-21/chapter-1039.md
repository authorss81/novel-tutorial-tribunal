# Chapter 1039: The Fifth Day Of The Hundred And Seventieth Week On Which Sixteen Inches Stood On That Top Step At About The Seventh Hour And It Holds And About Sixty Of Those Ninety Steps Under And Three Days Of The Coming Back, And A Man Who Cannot See Well Asked A Man Of Fifty-Four For His Own Count And Was Refused By Him On The Ground That He Would Rather Never Be Owed Anything He Could Not Check, And A Man With A Broom Put A Figure Of His Own Into The Air And Nobody Would Take It Off Him And Then A Man Of Thirty-One Picked That Broom Up And Carried It To The Wall And Was Not Thanked For It And Did Not Expect To Be

There was a hard frost on that landing before the light and it had taken the surface of the standing water into a thin skin that the man of thirty-one broke with his own boot on the way up, and the crack went out flat across the whole of the landing and off the low side, and he felt it go through the sole of the boot and up into the bone, and he stood on the top step for a moment afterwards not doing anything at all.

Fifth day of the hundred and seventieth week, and it is the Saturday of that week. His seven hundred and eighty-ninth morning. Six hundred and thirty-nine days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and eight weeks old, and no days beside it. Five hundred and ninety-three days since the division. Five hundred and thirty-nine days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and eighty-five days in this county of Kell.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and it holds, about sixty of those ninety steps under, and three days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and gave them on the ice, which he said was the first time in nineteen years he had said them on ice, and that it had not made a sound and he had said them anyway.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the fifth day of the hundred and seventieth week that he had decided this morning to put a figure of his own into the air on that stone and to say in advance that nobody was to take it off him, and that he had been thinking about that since the light.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, three days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the fifth day of the hundred and seventieth week, and it is a Saturday. Three figures, said on ice, and that is the only thing different about them.”

He worked a step and the ice broke and went down the flight in a sheet.

“Now the other thing and I am going to put it in the air and then I am going to stop talking about it for good. Nineteen years.” He said it flat, standing on the ice, with the broom in his hand and not working. “That is mine. It is the only figure of anything on this stone that belongs to me and it is about my hands and not about the water and not about anybody else on this landing, and I am giving it out this morning because I have been holding it since Wednesday and a man who holds a figure for a week is a man who has started to think of it as something he is going to use.”

“I am saying it once. Nobody is to take it. I am saying in advance that if anybody comes up here this morning and asks me for it I am going to say no, and if anybody stands on that stone and works out what it means, they can work it out, but they are not to come and get it out of my mouth a second time. It is in the air now and it is not a thing on offer. If you want it you can have the shape of it off the edge of what I have said and I will not help you with that either.” Nobody asked him for it.

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow and asked in the same words at the same hour and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and put his hands flat on the wall and said that the whole figure was still in his mouth and that he had stopped waiting for a place to put it.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and eighteenth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and thirty-nine less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and eighteenth. One thousand and thirty-nine less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

He got his hands up to the wall and left them flat against it.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it. And I have got a whole figure in my mouth, same as the middle of the week, and this time I am not going to put it beside anything and I am not going to give it to the air at all. It is in there and it is staying in there, and I have given up on the idea of finding the morning to say it. It used to feel like something I was carrying to a door. Now it is just a thing I say to myself on this wall at six in the morning, and if it never comes out of my mouth then it goes down with me, and that is allowed.”

“And I am going to say the second thing and it is about a man with a broom and I am saying it because he has just put a figure into the air and I want it noticed that I noticed.” He turned his head up the bank. “You have put yours out. Good. I am not going to take it and I am not going to work out what it means, and I would like it understood that not taking it is a thing I have had to think about and not a thing I forgot. It is a figure of you. It is the only one on that stone that is. Everything else off that stone is about water.”

Then the man who cannot see well came up that flight at about the tenth hour and got to the top of it and went along the wall to where the man of fifty-four was standing with his hands flat on it, and stopped beside him, and asked for the thing he had asked for before and had been refused before, and was refused again in a shape he had not heard before.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and said on the fifth day of the hundred and seventieth week that he had asked a man who cannot read for his own count three times that week and been refused three times, and that he had asked again this morning because a man with a broom had put a figure into the air in front of him, and that he had been wrong to do it and was going to say so himself before anybody else could.

He put his hand on the wall and it was a couple of inches from the other man’s and it stayed there.

“I am asking you for your figure. The one you carry against that line. I have asked before and I have been refused and I am asking again, because a man with a broom has just put his own into the air and I have been thinking all morning that a man who will not take mine has no business giving one away.”

Barnaby Crove took his hands off the wall and turned round until he was facing him, which he does perhaps twice in a week.

“No,” he said. “And I am going to give you the reason, and it is not the reason I have given you before, and I would rather you had the true one than the one that sounds like a rule.”

He put one hand up and pressed it flat against the stone behind him.

“You have asked me three times this week and every time I have given you the same answer, and the same answer is not true. The true one is this. I want you to be refused.”

He let that sit in the cold.

“If you take that figure off me I am a man of fifty-four who has been given something he cannot check, and I have spent this week working out that I am not going to be given anything. And there is more than that and I have been ashamed of it since Wednesday. If I am owed a figure then I am a man with something coming. And when something is coming to a man who cannot read, then he is a man who is going to be asked whether it arrived. And I cannot say. If it arrives and I say it arrived, then a person told me. If it does not arrive and I say it did not, then I have lied to a person who will check. So a man who cannot read being owed a figure is a man who has to lie one way or the other about it in about a year, and I would rather be a man who was owed nothing than that.” He put his hands flat on the wall again. “So no. And it is a selfish no, and it is the first selfish thing I have said on this landing, and you may have it, and I am not going to pretend it is a rule about counts.”

Simon Rook did not say anything for a while and his hand stayed on the wall.

“Then take that one.” Corvin Tace had come up behind him with the broom over his shoulder and had not been announced. “Nineteen years. It is in the air and you may have it and I will not give it to you twice.”

“I will not have it,” Simon Rook said, and did not move his hand. “And I want to give you the working out of why not, because you gave me one and you did not have to, and because I am a man who keeps a list and I would like the landing to see what happens when a man on it refuses a figure that is already in the air.”

He turned his head towards the broom and not towards the man holding it.

“A figure handed to a man who cannot check it is not a figure of him. It is a figure of the man who handed it over, dressed up as something the other man is holding.” He took his hand off the wall. “You have been standing on that stone for nineteen years and you have kept that to yourself, and that has been the shape of you on this landing the whole time, and if you give it to me this morning then the shape of you on this landing is a man who gave his figure away to a man who cannot see well and cannot read it, and that is a nicer shape and it is not yours. I am not going to be the reason you get a better shape out of the last week you have had that stone.” He put his hand in his coat. “And I am not thanking you for offering it, because you said in advance not to be asked, and I would have had to work out whether I had gone round something.”

Nobody thanked him for refusing it and nobody thanked Corvin Tace for putting it out and nobody took either of those two things off anybody.

Then the woman who saws lengths came up that cart road at about the eighth hour with her own cup in her hand and a length of wood under her arm that she had cut that morning at her own bench, and stopped at the foot of the steps and asked one question and got nothing, and she had expected nothing and said so before she asked.

Wenna Pyle is thirty-eight, who saws lengths and sets studs at a bench in an open-sided shed behind a wheelwright’s about three hundred yards down that cart road, and who carries her own cup out to that bench because there is no table in the shed, and said on the fifth day of the hundred and seventieth week that she had come up that road with a length in her arm on purpose, so that nobody on that landing could say she had come empty-handed again, and that she was going to ask one thing and it was going to get nothing.

“That is not me putting a good face on it. That is me having worked out that I am owed no answers on this landing and that I would rather ask and get nothing than not ask and not know whether I would have got something, because I have found that out the hard way about people and I am not doing it about a name.” She set the length down across the bottom step, not on it, on the stone in front of it. “Has it been said out loud since the water came off that bank and came back. That is all. I am not asking who and I am not asking when and I am not asking how many of you have heard it, because those are the three parts I have decided to do without.”

That got nothing, and she said so out loud, and nobody filled it.

“Right.” She picked the length up off the stone again and turned it over once in her hands. “I do not know what that piece of wood is. I have never said on this landing what it is and I am not going to, and I have had that in my mouth for two days now and I have worked out that I am going to keep on not saying it for as long as there is a landing to not say it on.”

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the ninth hour with the ice breaking under his boots, and went round the two pieces of ash without going near them, and gave his figures, and gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and eighty-five days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and fifty-four days old.

“Four hundred and eighty-five days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and fifty-four days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-eighty-ninth is three hundred and thirty-nine days old, which is one thousand and thirty-nine less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it. Nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one, and I am not going to ask for one on the morning a man with a broom has put the only figure of himself there has ever been into the air and been refused at both ends.”

He came down to the second step from the bottom and stood there with his hands at his sides.

“One hundred and seventy-ninth wrong things in one hundred and seventy-ninth mornings, and this is the hundred and seventy-ninth, and nobody is to tell me what kind of figure that is and nobody is going to do arithmetic at me about it. My want is to be somebody and it has not changed and it is not going to be said in a new shape because a man of fifty-four has worked out that he would rather be owed nothing than be lied to about it in a year.”

“And I am going to say the last of it and then I am going to do a day’s nothing, which I have not done in four hundred and eighty-five mornings.” He looked up the flight at the broom lying where it had been left across the top step. “A man of thirty-one has put his figure out and been refused and has not been thanked and is not owed anything back, and that is the cleanest thing that has happened on this landing all week, and I am not going to put my hand on it or improve on it. My figure is in my mouth and it comes out because I say it out loud and nobody else can say it for me, and that is the only thing I have that nobody can take off me, and it is not going to get me a single thing this week, and I knew that before I said it and I am saying it anyway, because a man who only says it when it works is not saying it.”

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour, and the woman who saws lengths went down the road with her length under her arm, and the man of thirty-one went last and stood on the second step from the bottom for a moment and went on down.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about sixty of them were under, and went at the whole flight from the bottom upward, and he went round the fortieth one and did not stand on it, and went round the chair, and did not sit on it, and he did not touch the iron in the seam of the fortieth step and he did not touch either piece of wood on the dry strip and he did not put the broom near either of them. The first was a forearm long and had been split down the middle the way wood splits when it is meant to, with the thick end left as it came apart. The second was the same and had a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches cut in the flat of it, one of the two run out of the side. When he had the top step he worked it and then he laid the broom down across the flat of the landing at the top of the flight, head against the wall and handle out over the edge, and left it there, because it was the end of the day’s work and there was no reason to hold a broom he was not going to use again.

Nothing on that landing was pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used, and nobody laid a hand on that wood in order to take anything off it, then or since, and nobody laid a hand on it after those sentences either. Nobody put a name on anything on that landing that morning, and nobody on that landing said out loud what that name was, and nobody put a name on anything else either, and nobody said out loud what that piece of wood is, and nobody counted anything.

That morning came and went, and the standing offer stood at three hundred and thirty-nine days and was not asked on and was not answered on and was not withdrawn on, and the account on that landing was as incomplete as it had been at the sixth hour and it was still in use, and the name on that second piece of ash was still wrong.

And after the others had gone, the man of thirty-one came back up that bank, went to the broom lying across the top of that flight, picked it up with both hands, and carried the whole length of it across the landing and stood it against the wall at the top end, out of the wet, with the head down and the handle up out of the way of anybody coming up, and set it there and took his hands off it; and the man of forty-three came up behind him in the half-light and saw where his broom was and stood and looked at it, and said that he was not going to thank a man of thirty-one for carrying a broom, and the man of thirty-one said he had not expected to be, and neither of them said anything else about it, and the broom stood against that wall all night with its head on the wet stone and nobody put a hand on it after that.

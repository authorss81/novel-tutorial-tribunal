# Chapter 1025: The Fifth Day Of The Hundred And Sixty-Eighth Week And Sixteen Inches On The Top Step At About The Seventh Hour And It Holds And About Sixty Of Those Ninety Steps Under And One Day Of The Coming Back, And A Whole Figure Of Weeks Came Out Of A Man’s Wall With Nothing Beside It For The Second Time It Was Not Easier, And A Man Who Cannot See Well Asked To Be Given One Man’s Own Count And Was Refused And Then A Man Of Thirty-One Offered His Own Count To The Man Who Cannot Read And Was Refused By Him With A Working About Nobody Ever Checking It

The low sun came over the wall at the top end of that landing for a few minutes in the morning and then went behind the hill, and in those few minutes the stone at that end showed every mark on it, and the marks were weather, and a broom head dragged, and the leg of a barrow stood on too hard, and the wall’s own weight on the two lengths of wood lying at its foot where nobody had ever painted or cut anything on either of them.

Fifth day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week. His seven hundred and seventy-fifth morning. Six hundred and twenty-five days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and six weeks old. Five hundred and seventy-nine days since the division. Five hundred and twenty-five days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and seventy-one days in this county of Kell.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and it holds, about sixty of those ninety steps under, and one day of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and then said that one day was the shortest figure he gave and that on this landing the shortest figure was always the morning with the most in it, and that he had no explanation for that and had stopped looking for one some time before the first of these weeks.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the fifth day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week that he had said his piece about whole figures twice and was not going to say it again this morning, and that a man who keeps finding new words for a silence he has already spoken about is not being careful, he is making a set.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, one day of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the fifth day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week, and it is a Saturday. Three figures.”

He turned the broom over and worked down the top step and did not stop anywhere.

“There is a man coming up this bank in about an hour who has a whole number in his mouth and nothing beside it, and I am not going to say one word about that number, and this is me saying so out loud in advance so that nobody has to spend the morning deciding whether to ask me. I have said what I have to say about a whole figure. I said it once in words I had not used and I said it again in other words I had not used, and I was right both times, and I am not going to find a third set of words for it, because the third set would not be about him at all. It would be about me having three sets.”

He worked the step and did not look up the bank.

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow and asked in the same words at the same hour and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and put his hands flat on the wall, and said a whole figure out loud with nothing beside it, and put it down the same way he put the barrow down.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and fourth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and twenty-five less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and fourth. One thousand and twenty-five less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

“One hundred and six weeks.” He did not take his hands off the wall. “Whole, and no days with it, and there is a man with a broom on that stone who has just said out loud that he is not going to say anything about it, and I want it noticed that I heard him say that and that hearing a man decline to speak is not the same as him owing me something.” Nobody said he did.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.” He set the barrow down. “And here is the part I actually came up for. That whole figure came out of this wall before, and it came out whole then too, and I have found out this morning that doing it again is not easier. It is harder the second time, and I want that said out loud by somebody, because I think a landing thinks a thing gets easier when you have done it before, and it does not. A man with a broom has told this landing not to put a small thing beside it and I am going to say that I agree with him and that agreeing with a man is not the same as him owing me anything.” Nobody agreed with him out loud and nobody disagreed.

Then the man who cannot see well came up onto the top step at about the ninth hour with his hand out in front of him, found the wall, and asked the man of fifty-four for his figure, which is the one thing nobody on that landing had ever thought to ask him for and the one thing he would have given away without being asked twice.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and said on the fifth day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week that he had asked a man who keeps a count for that count, and had been refused, and that the refusal had been the most useful thing anybody had said to him on that landing all week.

He kept his hand on the stone above the level of the strip.

“I am not going to ask you twice. Give it me once and I will not ask again and I will not ask anybody else on this landing and I will not say it out loud.”

“No.” Barnaby Crove did not take his hands off the wall. “And I am going to give you the reason, because you asked me plain and you asked me once.”

“You do not have to give me a reason.”

“I know I do not have to. That is not why I am going to.” He turned his head towards the sound of the voice and could not see him and did not pretend to. “Son. That figure is not mine. I say it out loud on this stone at about the sixth hour every morning and while it is in my mouth it is a number of mornings that I have come up a bank and asked a question nobody has answered. The moment a man hands it to another man it stops being that. It becomes a number of mornings that somebody has been keeping, and that is a different thing, and it is a thing about me, and you would be holding it.”

Simon Rook did not put out his hand.

“I want you to hear the rest because it is the part that matters to you and not to me. You have got a list of things you cannot check and you have just worked out that there is a thing on this landing you could have had. And you are right. That is the one thing on that list that was never really on it. And I am not going to give it to you, because the moment I do, you have got a figure of me and you will check it against what you think I have been doing up here, and if it ever does not match you will have to decide which of us is wrong, and you cannot see well enough to decide that, and I am not going to put you in that.” Nobody put him in that.

“So no. Not a no about the mornings and not a no about you. A no about what happens to a figure when it leaves the mouth it was counted in.” He put his hands flat again. “And nobody is to thank me for refusing you and nobody is to thank you for asking, because you asked me one thing and I gave you one thing and neither of those is a favour.” Nobody thanked either of them.

Before he went he put a different question to the stone, in the same flat voice he uses for things he is not going to be helped with.

“Has anybody said that name out loud this week.”

That got nothing on that landing either. Not a no, and not a number, and not a question back, and the man who keeps a list did not add it to the list.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the ninth hour with sixteen inches above his boots, gave his figures, gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, asked for nothing, and then offered the man who could not read a figure of his own, out loud, in his own mouth, and was refused by him with a working.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and seventy-one days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and forty days old.

“Four hundred and seventy-one days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and forty days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-seventy-fifth is three hundred and twenty-five days old, which is one thousand and twenty-five less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it. Nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one and I am not going to ask for one on the morning when this landing has found out that figures do not travel.”

He came up to the top step and stood there and did not go nearer the wall than the second step.

“One hundred and sixty-fifth wrong things in one hundred and sixty-fifth mornings, and this is the hundred and sixty-fifth, and nobody is going to tell me what kind of figure that is and nobody is going to tell me that I found anything.”

The figure got nothing and the want got nothing, and it is the same silence for both of them and it was the same silence yesterday.

“My want is to be somebody and it has not changed. My cost is that asking costs me nothing and I have not asked for one thing in four hundred and seventy-one mornings, and I am going to keep saying both halves of that out loud on this landing until somebody stops me.” Nobody stopped him. He kept saying it out loud on that landing, and the reason he kept saying it was that it was the only figure about him that nobody else was in a position to hand out for him, and he would rather say it himself.

“Now the part I want in the air.” He turned round to the man at the wall. “You have a count and you keep it in your mouth and you say it out loud at the sixth hour. I have a count and I keep it in my mouth and I say it out loud at the ninth hour. And this morning I am going to hand you mine. Not because I am generous with it. Because a man who cannot read has told this landing that his count stays his, and I have thought about it for a day and I have worked out that mine ought to stay mine too, and the only way I have found to work that out is to find out whether I would give it up if it were mine to give.”

He said the figure and the working and the day it had come out of his mouth for the first time.

“One hundred and sixty-fifth, and it is a figure of things I have got wrong, and nobody on this landing is going to tell me what kind of figure it is, and there is nothing at all standing beside it, not a day and not a calendar, and there is not going to be.”

Barnaby Crove did not take it.

“No. And I am not refusing you because you are a man of thirty-one and I have nothing for men of thirty-one.”

“Then give me the working.”

“The working is me.” He had his hands flat on the wall and he was not going to take them off to say it. “I cannot read. If you give that figure to me it is a figure that nobody on this landing will ever check, because the only person who could check it is you and you would have made it mine to check and you would have had to come up this bank and stand here and ask me whether I had it right, and then I would have had to say no every morning, and a man who says no every morning to a figure he cannot read is a man being asked to be a wall.” Nobody asked him to be a wall.

“So keep it. I am not being kind and I am not being hard. I am a man who cannot read and you are handing me a number, and there is no way to hold that without me owing it to somebody, and I have not got anything left to owe.”

He picked up the barrow handles and did not go down the bank yet.

“And I want that noticed, because I have been the man everybody on this landing thinks does not want anything. I have wanted that figure since the day before yesterday. I have wanted it and I have said no and I would like it recorded that wanting it is not the same as taking it and that I have not taken it.” Nobody recorded it and nobody asked him for it again.

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour and nobody went up the cart road and the man of thirty-one went last.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about sixty of them were under, and went along the top of the water on them, and went round the fortieth one, and went round the chair, and did not sit on it. He did not touch the iron in the seam of the fortieth step and he did not touch either piece of wood on the dry strip and he did not put the broom near either of them. The first was a forearm long and had been split down the middle the way wood splits when it is meant to, with the thick end left as it came apart. The second was the same and had a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches cut in the flat of it, one of the two run out of the side. He had said out loud at the seventh hour that he was not going to say a word about a whole figure, and he did not, and the whole figure stood in the air on that landing until about the eleventh hour with nothing at all beside it, and by the time everybody had gone down the bank the stone had taken the grit off the top of it and looked exactly as it had looked the morning before.

Nothing on that landing was pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used, and nobody laid a hand on that wood in order to take anything off it, then or since, and nobody laid a hand on it after those sentences either. Nobody put a name on anything on that landing that morning, and nobody said one out loud either, and no figure was put beside a whole figure, and nobody was given a figure of anybody.

A whole figure of weeks came out of a wall for the second time with nothing at all beside it and a man with a broom had said in advance that he would not speak about it and did not speak about it, and the second time was not easier than the first and a man who cannot read said so out loud on that landing. A man who cannot see well asked for one man’s count and was refused it so that no figure of a person would leave the mouth it was counted in, and a man of thirty-one then offered his own count to the man who cannot read and was refused by him on the ground that a figure handed to a man who cannot read is a figure nobody will ever check, and both of them wanted it and neither of them took it. And at the end of that morning the man of thirty-one went back up the top step on his own and put the palm with the cut in it flat on the wet stone where the water had stopped, well below the strip and a long way from either length, and held it there to see whether the water was still moving, and the cut was wet when he took it off, and he did not go up past that step and he did not go down the bank until it had dried.

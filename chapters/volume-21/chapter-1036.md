# Chapter 1036: The Second Day Of The Hundred And Seventieth Week On Which Eight Inches Stood Over That Top Step At About The Seventh Hour And About Eighty Of Those Ninety Steps Were Under And Six Days Of The Coming Back, And A Woman Who Saws Lengths Came Up That Cart Road To Say That She Has Been Carrying The Wrong Word In Her Head Since The Middle Of The Week And Is Giving It Back And Is Not Going To Be Told Which Word It Was, And A Man Who Cannot See Well Asked Whether The Word She Is Carrying Is The Name And Nobody On That Landing Answered Him In Any Shape At All, And A Woman Of Forty-Four Stood At The Wall At The Bottom Of That Road And Would Not Be Asked Whether She Is In Any Of It And Ate The Heel Of A Loaf She Had Brought Up With Her

The water came up over the flat of that landing in the night and lay there in the morning without moving, the way it does when there is no wind on it at all, so that the landing had a mirror on the top half of it and the man with the broom came up the bank and could see his own boots in it before he could see the wall.

Second day of the hundred and seventieth week, and it is the Wednesday of that week. His seven hundred and eighty-sixth morning. Six hundred and thirty-six days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and seven weeks and four days old. Five hundred and ninety days since the division. Five hundred and thirty-six days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and eighty-two days in this county of Kell.

Eight inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, about eighty of those ninety steps under, and six days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures, and then said that a still morning was the worst kind of morning there was for a man who works by what he can hear, and that he had been at the top of that flight at the light trying to make the water say something and had instead heard his own breathing in it.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the second day of the hundred and seventieth week that a flat wet morning was the one kind of morning on that bank he had no answer for, and that he had answered it by going to the top step and standing still for a while before he started, which he had never done and which he was not going to explain.

“Eight at the seventh hour, about eighty of those ninety steps under, and six days of the coming back, on the second day of the hundred and seventieth week, and it is a Wednesday. Three figures.”

He stood at the top of the flight with the broom in his hand and the water lying flat in front of him.

“There is a morning I do not have a working for. That is what this one is. It is not a figure and I am not going to make it into one, and I am not going to stand here this morning pretending I have something to say, because a man who has nothing to say on a landing still has a stone to sweep and the stone does not care whether he has something to say.” He began at the bottom and worked up. “I have been at the bottom of this flight all week and I am going to be at the bottom of it until the week turns, and if the sound I am waiting for comes up that bank it will come up behind me and I will hear it before anybody else on this stone does, and if it does not come then I have lost a morning of work going up the right way round and I will take that.”

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow and asked in the same words at the same hour and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him and he put his hands flat on the wall and said the thing he had been getting at all week.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and fifteenth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and thirty-six less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and fifteenth. One thousand and thirty-six less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

He kept his hands on the wall and did not take them off to talk, which is not what he does.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it. And I am going to say a thing this morning and I have been saying things all week and I want somebody to know that I know what I am doing with my mouth.”

“There was a woman stood at the foot of these steps yesterday who was told no about a chair, and I was on this landing the whole of it and I did not know she had been here until she told me this morning going past with her barrow.” He nodded at the wall. “And nobody asked me what I thought about that and nobody is going to, and that is not a favour anybody is doing me. She has said out loud that she is not part of this. If I stand at the top of this bank and tell that woman what I think about her chair then I am a man who has put a woman who said she was not part of it inside it, and I do not know how to get her back out of it, and neither does she, and I am not going to be the one who does it.”

“And I am going to say the last of it. The man that came up my road. I have decided what I think about him and it is not much and I am going to say it anyway. He was not talking to me and he was not talking to anybody behind me. He was talking to himself out loud because there was nobody else in that lane to say it to.” He took his hands off the wall and wiped them on his coat and put them back. “That is worse than the other two and I would rather it was the other two, and if I am right then he has been saying a thing to himself on my road for no reason at all, and if I am wrong then he was talking to somebody in that lane who is not there either, and either way I am a man who cannot read and there is nothing on this landing for that.”

Then the woman who saws lengths came up that cart road at about the ninth hour with her own cup in one hand and nothing under her arm at all and stopped at the foot of the steps and would not come up one of them, and she had clearly walked up there to do a thing and not to hear one.

Wenna Pyle is thirty-eight, who saws lengths and sets studs at a bench in an open-sided shed behind a wheelwright’s about three hundred yards down that cart road, and who carries her own cup out to that bench because there is no table in the shed, and said on the second day of the hundred and seventieth week that she had had a word in her head since the middle of the week and that the word was the wrong word, and that she had worked that out on Saturday night at her own bench and had come up this morning to hand it back.

“I am going to say this quickly because I have got a length in the shed that wants doing and I did not come up here to talk to anybody.” She did not put her hands behind her back. “The word I have been carrying is the wrong word. That is all. I have been sitting with it since Friday and I have had it in my mouth when I was setting studs and I have had it in my mouth when I was wiping the bench, and on Saturday night I worked out that it is the wrong word, and I have thought about it since and I am not going to sit here and say how I worked it out, because the way I worked it out is the only thing about it that is mine and I am not giving that to this landing either.”

“So I am handing it back.” She turned the cup round in her hand. “I am not saying which word it was. I am not saying where I got it. I am not saying how long I have had it or how many times anybody has had anything to do with it, and if anybody asks me any of that today I will not answer it, and I would rather say that now than have to say it at somebody.”

“And the part that is the reason I came up.” She looked up the bank and did not look at any one person. “Nobody is to correct me. I have been wrong about a word for two days and I am going to be wrong about it or right about it or neither for the rest of my life, probably, because I cannot check it and neither can anybody on this landing, and the only thing that would make it better is a person coming up here and telling me the right one, and if that happens then it is not in my head any more, it is in theirs, and then I have to go on taking it off them.”

“That is what a name does. I have watched it do that to a landing. So no. You can have the wrong word back and you can have the whole of me saying out loud that it was the wrong one, and you cannot have the other one, and I am not going to be asked for it, and if the woman who reads for a living is standing anywhere on this road I would like her to hear me say that I am not going to be given it out of a book either.” Nobody answered her and nobody gave her anything.

Then the man who cannot see well came up that flight at about the tenth hour and got to the top of it and put his hand on the wall and said the thing he had come up to say and then asked the question, and the question was the sharpest one anybody had asked on that landing all week and nothing came of it at all.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and said on the second day of the hundred and seventieth week that the woman who saws lengths had said the wrong word out loud at the foot of those steps in a voice that carried, and that he was going to ask her whether it was the name, and that he knew what he was doing.

He put his hand on the wall at the height it always goes at and it did not travel along the stone.

“I heard her. I am not going to say I did not, because she said it loud enough to be heard and I have been up here since before the light.” He turned his head towards the foot of the steps. “Is the word you are handing back the name on that strip. That is all I am asking. Not when and not where and not how many of you.” Nothing came back. “That has come to nothing at all and I want it noted that it came to nothing fast, which means everybody on this landing had already decided before I got to the top of the steps, and I am not blaming anybody for that and I am not asking again.”

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the ninth hour and gave his figures and gave the standing offer with the subtraction out loud and asked for nothing, and said the one thing he had come up that morning to say, which was about himself and not about the landing.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and eighty-two days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and fifty-one days old.

“Four hundred and eighty-two days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and fifty-one days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-eighty-sixth is three hundred and thirty-six days old, which is one thousand and thirty-six less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it. Nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one and I am not going to ask for one on the morning a woman has handed a word back to this landing in the wrong order.”

He came down to the second step from the bottom and stopped there.

“One hundred and seventy-sixth wrong things in one hundred and seventy-sixth mornings, and this is the hundred and seventy-sixth, and nobody is to tell me what kind of figure that is and nobody is going to do arithmetic at me about it.”

“And here is the part I have not said out loud on this landing yet and I am going to say it now while there is a woman standing at the bottom of this bank who is the only person here who knows what that piece of wood is and has just told us she has been wrong about a word for two days.” He held his hand up and did not look at it. “A man who has been nobody for four hundred and eighty-two mornings does not want to be somebody because of what he knows. That has been true the whole time and I have never tested it out loud. I want it for me. When somebody eventually knows what that strip is for, I want to have been the man who did not have to be told, and I want that to be worth something to me while I am still the kind of man it can be worth something to.”

“That is my want and it is mine and nobody is to thank me for saying it and nobody is to say I have said it well. It is the worst thing I have said on this landing and I have said a good many, and I am saying it because a woman has just told us that the wrong word is not a small thing, and it is not. It is the only thing anybody in this county has got and I want mine.”

Then the man of thirty-one went back up and stood at the foot of the steps and did not go past them, and did not ask the woman standing there anything, and did not go up the cart road after her when she went, and nobody on that landing asked her whether she was in any of it, and nobody told her that nobody was answering her, and she had not asked.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about eighty of them were under, and he went at the whole flight from the bottom upward, and he went round the fortieth one and did not stand on it, and went round the chair, and did not sit on it. He did not touch the iron in the seam of the fortieth step and he did not touch either piece of wood on the dry strip and he did not put the broom near either of them. The first was a forearm long and had been split down the middle the way wood splits when it is meant to, with the thick end left as it came apart. The second was the same and had a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches cut in the flat of it, one of the two run out of the side. The still water had a skin on it by the time he reached the fortieth and every time he drew the broom out it left a lane of clean stone and a lane of dirt behind it, and at the top of that flight he stood with the flat water going all the way out to the low side of the landing and did not walk out into it.

Nothing on that landing was pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used, and nobody laid a hand on that wood in order to take anything off it, then or since, and nobody laid a hand on it after those sentences either. Nobody put a name on anything on that landing that morning, and nobody on that landing said out loud what that name was, and nobody put a name on anything else either, and nobody said out loud what that piece of wood is, and nobody counted anything.

That morning came and went, and the standing offer stood at three hundred and thirty-six days and was not asked on and was not answered on and was not withdrawn on, and the account on that landing was as incomplete as it had been at the sixth hour and it was still in use, and the name on that second piece of ash was still wrong.

And the woman of forty-four stood at the wall at the bottom of that cart road where she has stood for a week, on the same side of it, with her hands behind her back, and ate the heel of a loaf she had brought up that road with her that morning out of her own house and had not offered to anybody and had carried back down half of, and she did not go up that bank and she did not go into the shed, and she did not ask anybody whether she was in any of it, and when the loaf was finished she wiped her hands on her skirt and stayed where she was until the light went.

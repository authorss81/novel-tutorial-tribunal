# Chapter 1028: The Working Instead

The wind had come round from the west in the night and put every leaf that was on the landing against the wall at the top end, all of them lying with their stems one way and their blades the other, so that the wall had a comb of them on it and the strip at its foot was clear of everything for once.

First day of the hundred and sixty-ninth week. His seven hundred and seventy-eighth morning. Six hundred and twenty-eight days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and six weeks and three days old. Five hundred and eighty-two days since the division. Five hundred and twenty-eight days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and seventy-four days in this county of Kell.

Eight inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, about eighty of those ninety steps under, and six days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and then said that the week had turned over in the night without him and that he had come up this morning to find out what it had turned over into, and that the answer was eight inches and about eighty of that flight, and that he did not propose to say anything about that either.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the first day of the hundred and sixty-ninth week that he had spent the whole of last week sweeping and had come up this morning with something to say, and that it was the first thing he had brought up that bank in nineteen years that was not about the stone.

“Eight at the seventh hour, about eighty of those ninety steps under, and six days of the coming back, on the first day of the hundred and sixty-ninth week, and it is a Tuesday. Three figures.”

He worked the top step and did not clear the leaves off the wall, because the wall had been keeping them since about the fourth hour and they were dry and would go when the wind went.

“Now the other thing and I am going to say it plainly because I have worked out that a man who has held something to himself and lets it out sideways is a man who has been using the stone. I am asking to be allowed to go down that lane.” Nobody had asked him for anything in nineteen years and nobody said that out loud.

“There is work at the low end of that drain that a person cannot do by themselves, and a woman there asked out loud for a person to stand with her, and she did not name anybody, and I have known about that since a man of thirty-one said it out loud on that stone and I have thought about it every morning since.” He put the broom head down. “And I want to go. Not because it would be good of me. Because I have swept this stone for nineteen years and I have not been able to tell you what is wrong with me, and I have wanted one morning of a road for about a year, and I have just worked out that there is a piece of work at the bottom of this landing that wants a person standing on it and that I am the person standing nearest to it, and I am not going to pretend those two things arrived together by accident.” Nobody told him they had not.

“I am a man who has never once asked this landing for a thing and I am asking. Take it or tell me the working out of why not, and I want the working, because I have watched this landing hand out workings instead of answers all week and it turns out I would rather have the working.”

Then the woman who saws lengths came up that cart road at about the eighth hour with a length under her arm and stopped at the foot of the steps and heard the end of that and said, before anybody had asked her anything, that she was not going to be the one who carried it down that road.

Wenna Pyle is thirty-eight, who saws lengths and sets studs at a bench in an open-sided shed behind a wheelwright’s about three hundred yards down that cart road, and who carries her own cup out to that bench because there is no table in the shed, and said on the first day of the hundred and sixty-ninth week that a man with a broom had asked to be let go down that lane, and that she was not going to take a message down there for him, or for anybody, and that she had said that out loud before anybody could ask her to.

“Put me down first, because I can see where this is going.” She did not put the length down. “I am the woman everybody goes to on that road. People come up past my bench and ask me to take something to somebody who is not at a bench. And I have never done it and I am not starting, and it is not because I am careful. It is because a thing I carry arrives with me in front of it, and then the woman on the other side of it is not hearing her own asking. She is hearing that somebody thought her asking was worth carrying.” Nobody asked her to carry anything.

“And there is a second thing and it is the one that actually stops me, and it is that nobody has told that man what to say when he gets there.” She looked up the bank. “If you go down that lane with nothing in your mouth then you are a man standing next to some work, and you will be thanked for standing next to it, and she will be a woman who has got a person now, and the asking she did will have been about having somebody there and it will turn out to have been about you. And nobody down there will ever be able to tell the difference, and neither will she.”

“I would not do that.”

“You would not. And I am not saying you would. I am saying that I have been asked by men to take things down that road and I have never once been told what the man was going to say when he got there, and the not telling is the whole of my refusal, and it is yours too if you want it.”

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow, which was before the man with the broom got to the foot of those steps and which is why the man with the broom had not seen him come up, and asked in the same words at the same hour and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and put his hands flat on the wall.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and seventh mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and twenty-eight less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and seventh. One thousand and twenty-eight less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

“There are men talking at the foot of those steps about going down that lane and I cannot hear the words and I am not going to try.” He set the barrow down. “Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it. And I am going to say one thing about that lane and then I am going to go and do my gravel, and I have been turning it over since the sixth hour. Whatever is down there has been down there a long time and has not stopped. And a morning on this landing where somebody is kind about it is not the morning it gets done on.” Nobody said the morning it would get done on.

Then the man who cannot see well came up that flight at about the tenth hour, about eighty of those ninety steps being under and the rest of them out, and stood on the top one with his hand on the wall, and said the thing he had come up for and then said the other thing.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and said on the first day of the hundred and sixty-ninth week that the lane and the drain were the same water and that he had known that since before he could see well, and that he was not going to say it in a figure.

His hand stayed on the stone at the height it always stays at, and not one inch lower.

“That lane runs into that drain. I know it because I follow the sound of water when I cannot follow anything else, and the sound of the lane and the sound of the drain are one sound and they have been one sound all my life, and I have never once said it out loud because there is no point in a man who cannot see well announcing that he knows where water goes.” He took his hand along the wall a short way. “So I am saying it, and I am saying that nobody on this landing is to go and stand at the end of either because of it. I am not sending anybody anywhere. I have not got that and I never will have it.” Nobody went anywhere because of it.

“Has anybody said that name out loud since the week turned.”

That got nothing, and none of the shapes an answer can arrive in arrived, and the man who keeps a list did not add it to the list, and the man who cannot see well did not take his hand off the stone while it did not come.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the ninth hour with eight inches above his boots, gave his figures, gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, asked for nothing, and refused a man with a broom.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and seventy-four days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and forty-three days old.

“Four hundred and seventy-four days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and forty-three days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-seventy-eighth is three hundred and twenty-eight days old, which is one thousand and twenty-eight less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it. Nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one and I am not going to ask for one on the morning after a man has asked me for the first thing anybody has ever asked me for.”

He came up to the second step from the bottom and stood there and did not go up to the top.

“One hundred and sixty-eighth wrong things in one hundred and sixty-eventh mornings and in one hundred and sixty-eighth, and this is the hundred and sixty-eighth, and nobody is going to tell me what kind of figure that is and nobody is going to tell me that I found anything.” Nobody answered him on any part of that.

“My want is to be somebody and it has not changed. My cost is that asking costs me nothing and I have not asked for one thing in four hundred and seventy-four mornings, and this morning that stopped being true, and I would rather say it than not say it, and nobody is to make a figure of the morning it stopped.” Nobody made a figure of it.

“No. You are not going.”

Corvin Tace did not argue and did not put the broom down.

“Give me the working.”

“I have given you a different one from the one I gave last week and you will know it by the shape of it.” He came up one step. “Last week I said no because nobody had asked me. That is still true and it is not why. This time it is because you have got a reason and I have read it and it is a good reason, and that is what makes it worse.”

He stopped and looked at the water going over the bank.

“A man who is sent down a lane is not standing with a woman. He is standing between her and the work. And then she has not done it alone at all. She has done it with a man in the way of it, and the man cannot see what she cannot see and cannot do what she cannot do and is going to be standing there the whole time. And that is not the same thing she asked for, and it is worse than the asking, because at least when she did it alone it was hers.” Nobody said that was not the same thing.

“And the second half and it is the half I would not give you for anything.” He put his hands behind his back. “She asked for a person and she did not name anybody. If I take that asking up this bank and I put your name on it, then it has got a name on it. And it is the only thing in this county that has ever been got right by refusing to name it. If it gets a name then the next woman on that landing who asks gets your name, and the one after that gets whichever name is standing about, and in a year there will be a name for it, and it will be a man’s name, and nobody down there will ever have asked for a man.” Nobody said anything about a man.

“So no. Not because you are not wanted and not because you are not good enough and not because nobody asked you. Because you have got a morning of a road in the bottom of this and so do I, and neither of us is allowed to take it out of a woman’s asking, and if I let you, then that asking has been used, and used up, and the two of us will feel very well about ourselves at the bottom of a lane in this county.” Nobody felt well about anything at the bottom of that lane, because nobody went to the bottom of it.

“And nobody is to come up here afterwards and tell me that was the right of it, and nobody is to come up here and tell me it was the wrong of it either, because a man who refuses something and then waits to be told he was right about it has not refused it, he has put it down and picked it up again to see how it looks.”

He turned round and went back down one step and stopped.

“You asked me for the working and I have given you two and they are both mine and neither of them is for you. Go and sweep your stone.”

Corvin Tace stood on that top step for a while with the broom in both hands and did not say anything, and then he took the broom to the middle of the flight and worked the fortieth step twice, once in each direction, without going round it, which was the first time in nineteen years he had worked that step without going round it and he did not do it again.

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour and nobody went up the cart road and nobody went down the lane, and the man of thirty-one went last and stood on the second step from the bottom with his hands at his sides.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about eighty of them were under, and went along the top of the water on them, and went round the fortieth one, and went round the chair, and did not sit on it. He did not touch the iron in the seam of the fortieth step and he did not touch either piece of wood on the dry strip and he did not put the broom near either of them. The first was a forearm long and had been split down the middle the way wood splits when it is meant to, with the thick end left as it came apart. The second was the same and had a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches cut in the flat of it, one of the two run out of the side.

Nothing on that landing was pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used, and nobody laid a hand on that wood in order to take anything off it, then or since, and nobody laid a hand on it after those sentences either. Nobody put a name on anything on that landing that morning, and nobody said one out loud either, and nobody went down that lane and nobody went to the low end of that drain, and the work at the low end of that drain was not done.

A man with a broom asked a man of thirty-one for the thing he wanted, and a man of thirty-one gave him the working instead of the thing, twice over and in two different shapes, and a woman who saws lengths would not carry the asking down that road in her mouth, and at about the eleventh hour the man with the broom stood on the top step and turned the broom end for end, because the head was worn down on the side he had worked with for nineteen years and had never once been turned, and tried one step with the other side of it, and the grit did not take at all and went in a scrawl and left a smear, and he turned the broom back again and did the same step properly, and that was the whole of what he did with his hands that afternoon.

# Chapter 1029: The Second Day Of The Hundred And Sixty-Ninth Week On Which Twelve Inches Stood Over That Top Step At About The Seventh Hour And About Seventy Of Those Ninety Steps Were Under And Five Days Of The Coming Back, And A Man Of Thirty-One Went From One To Another On That Landing Asking What He Was Owed For A Sentence He Had Said Out Loud About A Piece Of Wood And Was Refused A Figure By A Woman Who Saws Lengths And Was Given A Working By A Man Who Keeps A List Of Things He Cannot Check And Found Out That He Is Owed Nothing And Was Not Relieved

The comb of dry leaves that had been lying all along that wall was gone by the ninth hour and there was a wet line on the stone at the height they had lain, and the line was already going out at both ends, and by the middle of the afternoon there was no sign of the leaves anywhere on that landing and no sign of where they had been either.

Second day of the hundred and sixty-ninth week. His seven hundred and seventy-ninth morning. Six hundred and twenty-nine days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and six weeks and four days old. Five hundred and eighty-three days since the division. Five hundred and twenty-nine days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and seventy-five days in this county of Kell.

Twelve inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, about seventy of those ninety steps under, and five days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and said that a week had turned over in the night and that he had come up the bank expecting to have an opinion about it and had not, and that he had worked nineteen years to get to a place where a thing could happen to him and nothing did.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the second day of the hundred and sixty-ninth week that nobody on that landing had asked him a single thing about the week turning over, and that he had noticed the not being asked, and that he had got no use for the noticing.

“Twelve at the seventh hour, about seventy of those ninety steps under, and five days of the coming back, on the second day of the hundred and sixty-ninth week, and it is a Wednesday. Three figures.”

He worked the top step and pushed the leaves that were not there as though they were.

“You have all had a week to get used to and I have had a night and a half and I am the one who is behind. That is not a complaint and I am not going to say it twice. I have noticed it and I have got no use for it and I am going to sweep.” Nobody swept on his behalf.

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow and asked in the same words at the same hour and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and put his hands flat on the wall, and said that he had noticed that a man of thirty-one had been going about that landing asking everybody something, and that he could hear asking being done from the bottom of the bank, and that he was not going to report what it was.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and eighth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and twenty-nine less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and eighth. One thousand and twenty-nine less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

“Yesterday a man asked me for a figure of mine and I said no, and the day before that another man offered me one and I said no, and this morning a third man has been round asking a different question of everybody on that stone.” He set the barrow down. “Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it. And I am not going to use any of it. Refusals being said out loud in every direction is not a thing about my question and I will not let it get to be.” Nobody let it get to be.

Then the woman who saws lengths came up that cart road at about the eighth hour with a length under her arm and put it down at the foot of the steps end-square, the way she does, and had the question put to her before she had the length down.

Wenna Pyle is thirty-eight, who saws lengths and sets studs at a bench in an open-sided shed behind a wheelwright’s about three hundred yards down that cart road, and who carries her own cup out to that bench because there is no table in the shed, and said on the second day of the hundred and sixty-ninth week that a man of thirty-one had come to the foot of those steps and asked her what he was owed for a sentence he had said out loud, and that she had said no to him without using her trade once.

She did not pick the length up.

“You are not owed anything, and I am not going to be the one who says it twice.”

“Give me the working out of it.”

“I am going to give you a working and it is not the working you would get from my bench and I am not going to pretend otherwise.” She put her hands behind her back. “You have come down here to be given a number. You want to know what a sentence is worth so that you can put the two side by side and see which way round the pair reads. And there is no number, and there is not going to be one, and I am not going to make you one out of my trade to be kind to you, because that is the whole of what you are asking a woman who cuts lengths for.” Nobody spoke for her.

“If I said a figure at you now it would be a figure of a thing I made. It would not be about your sentence. It would be about a length and a saw and a face I cut to, and you would hold it and think it told you something about a sentence you said on a step, and in a few more weeks you would not be able to tell which was which, and you would be a man in this county carrying two numbers that are about different things.”

She looked at the length on the ground and not at him.

“And here is the other half and it is the half that is actually mine.” She did not soften it. “You do not need me to tell you. A man who is owed something has something in front of him that he could collect. You have not got one. You have got a sentence that is still said, and a strip of stone, and a man with a broom, and every one of those is here whether you are owed for it or not. If you collect nothing today then you are not owed nothing, because there was never anything on the table. It is not an empty plate. It is a table with the plate taken off it.” Nobody said she had taken a plate off anything.

“So that is the working, and it is not out of my bench, and you are not to come back to me on another morning and ask me the same thing in a lower voice. I will not say it differently for you and I will not be kinder about it later.”

“I am not going to come back.”

“Then that is the end of it and I am going to my length.”

She picked it up and stood holding it and did not go up the bank and did not go down the road either.

Then the man who cannot see well came up onto the top step at about the tenth hour with his hand out in front of him, found the wall, and was asked a question by a man of thirty-one that was not about the strip, and he answered it, which is a thing he had not done on that landing.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and said on the second day of the hundred and sixty-ninth week that he had been asked a question he could answer and had answered it, and that he was not going to be thanked for answering it and would rather not be.

He kept the hand where he keeps it, which is above the strip and has been for a fortnight.

“You are the only man on this landing who has ever asked me something and stopped before I had to work out whether I was being asked.” He turned his head towards him. “So I am going to answer properly, and then I am going to tell you what I want, because I have not asked you for anything either and I am tired of that.”

“Ask.”

“Put one thing on my list.” He took his hand along the wall a short way and found it again. “Not the strip. The strip is on it. I want a thing I could describe to a person. You have had a sentence in the air on this landing and I have got nothing about it because it is not a thing I can check and it is not a thing on my list, and I would like one line on that list that says a man said a thing, and that is all, and it would not have to be true or false.” Nobody put a line on it.

“The working, and then I will tell you why I have thought about your question all morning before you asked it.” He put his hand back above the strip. “Nothing on my list gets bigger. That is the thing about a list. You do not add to it every day. You add to it when you stop looking at a thing, and that is all, and it happens about as often as it happens. A thing you did and got nothing for is not on my list and never was, because there is nothing about it to check. It is finished. It cannot be checked and it cannot be unchecked and there is no morning coming up that is going to be different. If it is owed to you it is owed, and being owed is not a thing that can be checked either. You would have to be able to check the world.” Nobody could check the world.

“So. You are not owed anything for that sentence, and you are not owed anything against it either, and both of those are the same shape and you will not be able to tell which one you are standing in.” He turned his head. “And I am not relieved for you. I have got no relief in me about anything and you will not get one out of this list. And you are not to tell me I have given you something, because I have not. I have told you what I know and what I know is that a thing that cannot be checked does not grow.”

He said nothing else about it and did not put anything on the list.

And then he said one more, and he said it to the stone and not to the man of thirty-one.

“Has anybody said that name since the morning before.”

That got nothing, and not one of the shapes a thing can come back in came back on it, and the man who keeps a list did not add it to the list.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the ninth hour with twelve inches above his boots, gave his figures, gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, asked for nothing, and then told a landing what he had found out, and found out nothing.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and seventy-five days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and forty-four days old.

“Four hundred and seventy-five days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and forty-four days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-seventy-ninth is three hundred and twenty-nine days old, which is one thousand and twenty-nine less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it. Nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one and I am not going to ask for one today when I have been round this landing asking about something else and got nothing that can be spent.”

He stood in the middle of the landing with the wall behind him.

“One hundred and sixty-ninth wrong things in one hundred and sixty-ninth mornings, and this is the hundred and sixty-ninth, and nobody is going to tell me what kind of figure that is and nobody is going to tell me that I found anything.” Nobody answered him on the figure and nobody answered him on the want, and he did not say which of the two mattered more, because he has been told on another morning that it is the same silence for both of them.

“My want is to be somebody and it has not changed. My cost is that asking costs me nothing and I have not asked for one thing in four hundred and seventy-five mornings, and this morning I asked everybody I could get to the same question and it cost me the same as it has cost me every morning, which is nothing, and that is how I knew what the answer was before I had it.” Nobody said he had it.

“And here it is, and I am going to say it in the plainest words I have got and then I am going to stop talking about it for good.” He turned his hand over and did not shut it. “I am not owed anything for that sentence. Not by the strip and not by the man it names and not by the man who put it down, and not by the landing and not by a broom. I said a thing out loud about a piece of wood and it cost me something on the first day of the hundred and sixty-seventh week and I still do not know what and I am not going to find out and nobody is owed anything for it, including me.” Nobody told him he was owed nothing and nobody told him he was owed something.

“And I am not relieved.” He shut the hand. “I want that said out loud by somebody who is not me and I have been waiting all morning for somebody to say it and nobody has, and I am going to say it myself and then I am going to stop asking.” Nobody said it for him.

“And the last of it and it is about why I asked at all, and it is not a good reason. Asking a man whether you are owed something is the cheapest thing there is. It costs nothing, it can be asked on any morning, it does not put anybody in anything, and it does not have to be a real question. I have known that since before I came into this county. And that is exactly why the answer is worth nothing to me. If I had to work for it I would be standing somewhere else this afternoon.” Nobody told him where else he would be standing.

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour and the woman who saws lengths went down the cart road with the length under her arm and nobody went up the road after her.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about seventy of them were under, and went along the top of the water on them, and went round the fortieth one, and went round the chair, and did not sit on it. He did not touch the iron in the seam of the fortieth step and he did not touch either piece of wood on the dry strip and he did not put the broom near either of them. The first was a forearm long and had been split down the middle the way wood splits when it is meant to, with the thick end left as it came apart. The second was the same and had a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches cut in the flat of it, one of the two run out of the side. He asked nothing all morning, which nobody on that landing had ever been able to say about him, and he gave his three figures off the same stone at the seventh hour and swept the rest of that bank without being asked for one more thing, and he did not look up it once to see whether anybody had come back.

Nothing on that landing was pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used, and nobody laid a hand on that wood in order to take anything off it, then or since, and nobody laid a hand on it after those sentences either. Nobody put a name on anything on that landing that morning, and nobody said one out loud either, and nobody said what a piece of wood was, and nobody was told that a man had come up that bank owing nothing.

A man of thirty-one went from one to another on that landing asking what he was owed for a sentence he had said out loud about a piece of wood, and a woman who saws lengths refused to give him a figure and gave him the working out of a table with the plate taken off it, and a man who keeps a list of things he cannot check told him that a thing which cannot be checked does not grow, and would not put a line on that list, and would not be thanked for answering. And when the man of thirty-one told that landing that he was owed nothing for what he had said about that wood and that he was not relieved about it, the man who cannot see well put his hand inside his coat, where the list was, and felt for it, and did not take it out, and put his hand back on the stone above the strip and left it there.

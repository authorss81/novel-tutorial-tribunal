# Chapter 1030: The Lip He Took Down

The water came over the edge of that top step in three places that morning, the way it had been doing since the fourth day of the hundred and sixty-eighth week, and in each of those places it had got hold of a line of grit that had been standing on the wet edge since before the light and had packed itself into a lip about the width of two fingers, and the lip was holding water back on the stone the way a finger holds water back in a bowl.

Third day of the hundred and sixty-ninth week. His seven hundred and eightieth morning. Six hundred and thirty days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and six weeks and five days old. Five hundred and eighty-four days since the division. Five hundred and thirty days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and seventy-six days in this county of Kell.

Sixteen inches on the top step at about the seventh hour and it holds, about sixty of those ninety steps under, and four days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and then gave the figure of what that lip of grit was holding, because it was holding something and it was the only thing on that landing he could measure from his own stone without touching anything.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the third day of the hundred and sixty-ninth week that he had found something on his stone that was doing a job, and that he was going to spend the day undoing the job, and that he was going to do it on his own because he had not asked anybody and he was not going to.

“Sixteen at the seventh hour and it holds, four days of the coming back, and about sixty of those ninety steps under, on the third day of the hundred and sixty-ninth week, and it is a Thursday. Three figures.”

He put the broom head down beside the lip of grit and looked along it from one end to the other without touching it.

“And here is the other thing and it is not a figure about anybody. That grit came off this stone in a week of wind, and it has been standing on the wet edge since the water came back, and the water has been coming over the edge in three places, and in all three of those places the grit has packed itself into a lip and the lip is holding about two fingers of water back on that step.” He turned the broom over. “That is not nothing. That is a thing doing a job. Every drop of that water that ought to be running down off this stone is being held up on it and is standing in a bowl of its own making. And it has got four days of coming back in front of it and there is a week behind it.” Nobody said what would happen when the water came. “I am not going to predict it. I have got three figures off that stone and none of them is about grit, and if I stand here this morning working out what happens when this bowl fills then I have made a fifth figure out of my own head and I am not doing that. I am going to take it off. That is a job and I have got nineteen years of taking things off.”

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow and asked in the same words at the same hour and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and set the barrow down, and put his hands flat on the wall, which is what he does.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and ninth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and thirty less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and ninth. One thousand and thirty less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

“Third morning of this week and I am going to say one thing and then I am going to do a day’s gravel.” He did not take his hands off the wall. “There is a man on this stone who has found something to do this morning that is not about the water and not about the strip and not about anybody’s name, and I am glad of it, and I am not going to stand here and work out what it is, because I have worked out more than one thing this week and most of them were wrong. Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it. And a man with a broom has been asked things by people on that landing all week and has given some of them a working and some of them nothing, and there is a man of thirty-one who has been round asking what he is owed, and I am not going to use any of it, and my question goes down that bank in the same shape it went up it.” Nobody told him what shape it went up in.

Then the boy of thirteen came up out of the lane with two pails of water in a yoke, carried them to the shed side, set them down, came back, and did not stop at the strip, and stood at the foot of the bank instead, and waited until the man of thirty-one had come up the flight at about the ninth hour.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, has been in this county three hundred and seventy-seventh days, and counted the figure in the lane before the light came up. Three hundred and seventy-seven is one thousand less six hundred and twenty-three.

“Three hundred and seventy-seven days, and it is one thousand less six hundred and twenty-three, and it is one more than the morning before.” He put the yoke down in the grass. “And I am not going to be on that landing this morning and I am going to tell you why at the bottom of the bank instead of the top, because it is a thing about the bottom of a bank.” Nobody asked him to come up.

“I have got something to say and it is the second thing I have wanted to say and the first one got stopped by a woman with her hand on my yoke, and I am not going to pretend that woman was wrong to stop it, because she gave me a working and the working was good and I have been using it every morning since.” He wiped his hands down his thighs. “So the second one. You said on that step that you were not going to come down that lane with anybody. And I have thought about that every day since, and I have worked out that it is not the same as nobody going down it.”

He looked up the bank at him.

“I want you to come down that lane with me.” Nobody said anything.

“Not for the work at the end of it. I am not asking you about the work at the end of it and I am not going to tell anybody you said anything about it. I am asking because I am going down that lane this morning and I have worked out that I would rather do it with a person than without one, and that is a reason about me and it is not a good reason and I have not got a better one.”

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and seventy-six days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and forty-five days old.

“Not with you and not with anybody, and that is the whole of it.”

“Then tell me why not.”

“I am going to give you the working and you are going to hate it, because it is about you and it is not about the lane.” He came down to the second step from the bottom and stood there. “You are asking me to go down that lane because going down it with a person is easier than going down it on your own, and that is true, and it is the whole reason, and it is not a reason to do it. A thing you did on your own is a thing you did on your own. Put a man in it and it is a thing men did together, and then it is a story about men, and the part where you did not say that name comes out of it as the part where a man of thirty-one was standing in the lane, and in a year that is the version that gets told, and there is no way on this landing to put your name back on the part where you did not say it.”

He stopped and let it stand.

“So you go down on your own. And I am not going to stand at the top of this bank and watch you go, and I am not going to say it was right, and I am not going to say it was wrong, and it is not going to become my job on a step to tell a boy of thirteen what his not doing was worth.” Nobody told him what it was worth.

“I am not going down that lane. Not today and not this week, and not with you and not with anybody, and nobody on this landing is to come up here afterwards and tell me that I should have gone. I said on the first morning of this week that I would not, and I have had a week to find a reason and I have not found one and I am not going to go looking with the last day of it.” Nobody went looking.

The boy picked the yoke up and put it on his shoulder and the two pails went up and down once each and settled.

“Then I am going down it.”

“I know.”

“And I am not going to say it.”

“You are not going to say it. And I am not going to tell anybody you did not, and I am not going to thank you for it, and if it ever comes out that you did not then it comes out without me.” Nobody said anything about how it would come out.

Then the woman who saws lengths came up that cart road at about the tenth hour with nothing under her arm at all, which was the first morning anybody had come up that road without something in their hands since before the first of these weeks, and stood at the foot of the steps and asked a question in her own mouth, which is a thing she does about twice in a week, and did not answer it and did not expect to.

Wenna Pyle is thirty-eight, who saws lengths and sets studs at a bench in an open-sided shed behind a wheelwright’s about three hundred yards down that cart road, and who carries her own cup out to that bench because there is no table in the shed, and said on the third day of the hundred and sixty-ninth week that she had come up that road with nothing in her hands to ask a question, and that she knew the answer to it and was not going to say it either, and that she was going to ask it anyway because a question nobody asks is still owed by somebody.

“Has anybody on this landing been told what that piece of wood is.” Nobody answered her.

“Not you.” She did not look up the bank. “I am not asking you. I asked the landing and I got the landing, and I am going to stand here and let it be the landing that does not answer, and I am not going to take it back off the landing and put it on a person.”

She turned and went down that cart road with her hands empty and nobody went up the road after her.

Then the man of thirty-one went back up the flight and gave his figures with sixteen inches above his boots, gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, asked for nothing, and said out loud that a whole figure had come out of his mouth and that he was not going to let the landing do anything with it.

“Four hundred and seventy-six days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and forty-five days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-eightieth is three hundred and thirty days old, which is one thousand and thirty less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it. Nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one and I am not going to ask for one on the morning I have said a whole figure out loud.”

He came back down off that top step and stood in the middle of the landing with the wall behind him and did not go up towards the strip.

“One hundred and seventieth wrong things in one hundred and seventieth mornings, and this is the hundred and seventieth, and it is a whole figure, and nobody is going to tell me what kind of figure it is and nobody is going to tell me that I found anything, and nobody is going to stand on this stone this afternoon looking at me and doing arithmetic.”

Nobody told him what kind of figure it was and nobody did arithmetic.

“My want is to be somebody and it has not changed since I said it out loud and it is not going to be said in a new shape this morning. My cost is that asking costs me nothing and I have not asked for one thing in four hundred and seventy-six mornings, and a boy asked me for one thing this morning and I said no, and that is the first time in this county that a person has asked me for something, and I want both halves of that said out loud and I want neither of them improved.” Nobody stopped him and nobody said either half for him.

“And I am going to say the last of it and it is not about me.” He shut his hand. “There is a thing owed on this landing by a woman who cuts lengths and a thing owed to her by people who know what that piece of wood is, and I have not asked her and I am not going to ask her. There is a whole figure of weeks in the mouth of a man who cannot read and a man who cannot see well has been refused it and has said so out loud. There is a man who swept round that strip nineteen years and a man who put a name on it and cannot unsay it. And there is a piece of wood on a dry strip with nothing on it that I have not looked at this morning and am not going to look at.” Nobody said a word about any of it.

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour, and the boy went down the lane with the yoke and two full pails in it and said nothing, and the woman who saws lengths went down the road with her hands empty, and the man of thirty-one went last and stood on the second step from the bottom for a moment and went on down.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about sixty of them were under, and went along the top of the water on them, and went round the fortieth one, and went round the chair, and did not sit on it. He did not touch the iron in the seam of the fortieth step and he did not touch either piece of wood on the dry strip and he did not put the broom near either of them. The first was a forearm long and had been split down the middle the way wood splits when it is meant to, with the thick end left as it came apart. The second was the same and had a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches cut in the flat of it, one of the two run out of the side. He had spent the whole of that morning going round the lip of grit and not breaking it, and at about the eleventh hour he stopped going round it and put the broom head flat against the step above it and drew it down, one step at a time, and the grit came off in a roll and went down over the wet edge and away in the brown water, and he went back up for the next load, and he did that for the rest of the day without stopping, and nobody came up that bank to help him because he had not asked anybody, and by the time the light was off the water there was no lip of grit on that top step at all and the three places where the water came over the edge were three places where the water came over the edge again, running off as they had run off before the wind brought the grit.

Nothing on that landing was pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used, and nobody laid a hand on that wood in order to take anything off it, then or since, and nobody laid a hand on it after those sentences either. Nobody put a name on anything on that landing that morning, and nobody said one out loud either, and nobody said what a piece of wood was, and nobody was told that the account on that landing was any nearer to being finished than it had been at the sixth hour.

That morning came and went, and the standing offer stood at three hundred and thirty days and was not asked on and was not answered on and was not withdrawn on, and the account on that landing was as incomplete as it had been at the sixth hour and it was still in use, and the name on that second piece of ash was still wrong.

A man with a broom took a line of grit off the top step of that landing on his own with a broom, load by load, all afternoon, and did not finish the day any earlier than he had finished the day before, and the three places where the water came over that edge came over it a fourth time while he was standing there with the grit going down past his boots.

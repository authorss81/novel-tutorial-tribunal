# Chapter 1020: Not Going To Ask It

The last morning of that week came up grey and low over the water and the whole of that landing had spent the week getting ready for it without saying so, and by the seventh hour it was plain that nothing had been ready at all, and that was the whole of what the morning had to say about itself.

Seventh and last day of the hundred and sixty-seventh week. His seven hundred and seventieth morning. Six hundred and twenty days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and five weeks and two days old. Five hundred and seventy-four days since the division. Five hundred and twenty days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and sixty-six days in this county of Kell.

Eight inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, about eighty of those ninety steps under, and six days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures and said that a week turns over in the night and that this stone does not notice it, and that he had been giving figures off it for nineteen years and it had never once turned over with one.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the seventh and last day of the hundred and sixty-seventh week that the week turning and the stone turning were two different things and that he had said so before and was not going to improve on it by saying it again.

“Eight at the seventh hour, about eighty of those ninety steps under, and six days of the coming back, on the seventh and last day of the hundred and sixty-seventh week, and it is a Monday. Three figures.” He beat the flat of the broom against the stone. “The week turns over tonight. Nothing on this landing turns over with it. There is a person on this stone who has said out loud that a sentence of his has got away from him, and a man on that wall who has said out loud that he is not coming up this bank, and a woman who saws lengths who told us all she would not answer anything, and a man who said a name out loud in his own mouth and has not said it since. None of that has gone anywhere because the week ended. It is all still here and it will be here on the first morning of the next week.”

Nobody said the week had carried any of it off.

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow and asked in the same words at the same hour and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and said out loud that it was the last morning of the week and that the week had made no difference and that he was not going to let a week be a reason.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried two hundred and ninety-ninth mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and twenty less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Two hundred and ninety-ninth. One thousand and twenty less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

“Last morning of this week.” He set the barrow down and put his hands flat on the wall, which is what he does. “I have said this question on the last morning of every week I have been coming up here and I am not going to stand here this morning and pretend the week ending has made it heavier. A week is a way people count days. It is not a day and it does not ask anything and it is not a person and nobody has asked me whether I am glad that this one is finished.”

Nobody asked him whether he was glad that the week was finished.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.” He did not take his hands off the wall. “And a man at the ninth hour this week said out loud that he had a question and was not going to ask it, and I am going to say now, while I have my hands on this wall, that nobody is to come up here and ask me what his question was. I am not going to answer it and I am not going to guess at it and I am not going to be glad that he did not ask it and I am not going to be disappointed. He has not asked me anything and that is a thing that did not happen and I am not going to be thanked for it and I am not going to be the man somebody goes to because he did not say anything.”

Nobody thanked him and nobody went to him.

He turned the broom over.

“And I am going to ask one thing before this week is out and then I am not going to ask it again. Is that sentence of his going to be any use to anybody.”

Nobody answered him.

“Not a no,” he said. “I have been on this stone nineteen years and I have watched a shape of words come up that road and go round this county without anybody being the worse off, and I do not know whether a sentence out loud is a thing that gets used or a thing that gets put down and left. And nobody on this landing is going to tell me, and I am not going to work it out in a corner with my hands on the broom, and that is nineteen years of me not wanting a fifth figure and it is still nineteen years of me not wanting one.”

Nobody told him and he gave no fifth figure.

Then the woman of forty-four stood at the wall at the bottom of that cart road for the last time in that week and said one sentence, and nobody went up that road, and nobody said to her afterwards that nobody had gone up it.

Orla Fennimore is forty-four, keeps twenty-nine chairs at a house that is not in this county, was asked once what she did and has not been asked since, and said on the seventh and last day of the hundred and sixty-seventh week that a week had ended at the bottom of that road and that she had noticed it there and that it had not required her to do anything, and that nobody was to tell her that was remarkable.

Nobody told her and nobody went up that road.

“And I am still not in it, and I am not going to say what I am not in, and I am not going to be asked, and I am not going to be told that nobody is answering me.” She did not move off the wall. “And nobody is to come to this wall at the end of a week to tell me that I have been standing at the bottom of a road for a week and that that is a thing that has been happening, because I have been doing the standing. I would like that noticed, in whatever it is that notices things.”

Nobody noticed and nobody thanked her.

Then the man of thirty-one came up at about the ninth hour with eight inches above his boots, gave his figures, gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, asked for nothing, stood on that landing the whole time he was saying them, and then asked nobody anything at all.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and sixty-six days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and thirty-five days old.

“Four hundred and sixty-six days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and thirty-five days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-seventieth is three hundred and twenty days old, which is one thousand and twenty less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it. Nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one and nobody is going to be offered one, and I am not relieved and I am not unrelieved and nobody is to tell me which of those two I look like.”

Nobody told him which of those two he looked like.

“One hundred and sixtieth wrong things in one hundred and sixtieth mornings, and this is the hundred and sixtieth, and it is a whole figure, and nobody is going to tell me what kind of figure that is and nobody is going to tell me that I found anything.”

Nobody told him which of those two he looked like and nobody told him anything else about it.

“My want is to be somebody and it has not changed since I said it out loud and it is not going to be said in a new shape this morning because a new shape would sound as though the old one had gone stale. My cost is that asking costs me nothing and I have not asked for one thing in four hundred and sixty-six mornings, and I am going to keep saying both halves of that on that landing until somebody stops me, and nobody is going to stop me.”

Nobody stopped him.

“Now I am going to say the thing I came up this bank to say and it is not a question and it is not about that wood and it is about me and it is going to be said out loud because a man who carries a question up a bank for a week and does not say he has got one turns into a thing that is being carried.” He stood still. “There is a question in my mouth and I am not going to ask it, and I am not going to tell this landing what it is, and I am not going to tell a man who cannot read what it is, and I am not going to tell the woman who saws lengths what it is even though she is the only person up here who would be able to answer a version of it.”

Nobody asked him what it was and nobody told him they could have worked it out.

“And I am not going to be asked what I am not asking. If anybody on that landing wants to ask me what I have got in my mouth this morning, they are going to get nothing, and they are not going to get a figure, and they are not going to get it in any other week either. Not today, and not on the first morning of next week, and not when the water comes off that bank again. I have had a sentence out loud once this week and I have watched what it cost and what it was worth, and I am not spending the second one on anybody who comes up here wanting to hear it.”

Nobody asked him and nobody asked him the following morning either.

“And there is a second thing and I am putting it out loud so that it is not waiting on me. There is somebody on this landing who knows what that piece of wood is and who has already told this landing out loud that she is not going to say. I am not going to ask her. I am not going to ask her this week and I am not going to ask her next week and I am not going to ask her the week after. Not because I have decided she is keeping something from me. Because she said she was not going to say it and a woman who says that on a landing has put a boundary on that landing and I am not going up to it with a reason.”

Nobody asked her and nobody said he had asked her.

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour, and the man of thirty-one went last, and stopped on the second step from the bottom on his way down, and stood there for a moment with nothing in his hands, and went on down.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about eighty of them were under, on the last morning of that week, and he worked them in the order he had worked them in all week and did not change the order because it was the last one. He went along the top of the water on them and round the fortieth one and round the chair, and did not sit on it. He did not touch the iron in the seam of the fortieth step and he did not touch either piece of wood on the dry strip and he did not put the broom near either of them. The first was a forearm long and had been split down the middle the way wood splits when it is meant to, with the thick end left as it came apart. The second was the same and had a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches cut in the flat of it, one of the two run out of the side. Eight inches on the top step and a week going over in the night behind him, and the stone under the broom the same stone it has been for nineteen years, and he swept it and did not work out what he made of that either.

Nothing on that landing was pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used, and nobody laid a hand on that wood in order to take anything off it. Nobody put a name on anything on that landing that morning, and nobody said one out loud either, and nobody said what a piece of wood was, and nobody was given a figure of anybody and nobody was owed one.

The offer stood at three hundred and twenty days, unanswered, and nobody was relieved.

Then the week turned over in the night, as weeks do, and nothing on that landing turned with it. A man with a broom came up the bank on the morning after and swept ninety steps that had a different set standing out of the water on them from the one he had spent the week on, and he did not work out what he made of that either, and the stone under the broom was the same stone it has been for nineteen years, and there was no figure of anything on that landing that anybody was owed, and the morning went on being a morning.

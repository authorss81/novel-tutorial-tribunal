# Chapter 1043: Sawdust On Boots

The sound of a saw came up that cart road before the light did, thin in the cold air, and stopped and started again the way a saw does when the cut is being checked, and the man with the broom heard it from the bottom of the flight and swept toward it without saying anything about it.

Second day of the hundred and seventy-first week, and it is the Wednesday of that week. His seven hundred and ninety-third morning. Six hundred and forty-three days after the settlement. The fever one hundred and eight weeks and four days old. Five hundred and ninety-seven days since the division. Five hundred and forty-three days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county, and four hundred and eighty-nine days in this county of Kell.

Four inches on the top step at about the seventh hour, about ninety of those ninety steps under, and seven days of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave those three figures with the broom still, and gave them facing up the bank toward the shed sound.

Corvin Tace is forty-three, holds that landing nineteen years, gives four figures off one stone, has never once given a fifth, and said on the second day of the hundred and seventy-first week that four inches is a depth a man can work in without getting his knees wet, and that he was going to work it while it lasted.

“Four at the seventh hour, seven days of the coming back, and about ninety of those ninety steps under, on the second day of the hundred and seventy-first week, and it is the Wednesday of that week. Three figures.”

He worked the edge of the top step where the water lay thinnest.

“Go and do your gravel. It is the Wednesday of the hundred and seventy-first week and the saw is going down the road, and the stone has nothing else for anybody.”

Then the man of fifty-four came up at about the sixth hour with the barrow, and asked in the same words at the same hour, and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him, and set the barrow shafts down and put his hands flat on the wall.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four, holds the gravel on that landing, and cannot read. He carried three hundred and twenty-second mornings of asking for a day against the ninth line, which is one thousand and forty-three less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one.

“Three hundred and twenty-second. One thousand and forty-three less seven hundred and twenty-two, and then one. Nobody asked me for it, same as every morning I have come up.”

He left his hands where they were and spoke to the stone.

“Whether there is one thing in this county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.”

That found no answer, and he named that in his own fresh words, and nobody filled it.

“It found no answer and I am not smoothing that over. I asked at six and the wall gave me the wall back, and that is the shape of every morning of asking I have carried up this bank.”

Then the boy came up that lane at about the eighth hour with his pails full and his boots wet, and set them down at the foot of the steps, and gave his own count, and asked what he had come to ask.

Wat Marshe is thirteen, and counts his own days in this county, and said on the second day of the hundred and seventy-first week that his count is three hundred and ninety days, and that it is his and he will not make it into a count of anything standing on that landing.

“Three hundred and ninety. That is mine.” He wiped his hands on his sleeves. “I want to know what the woman said on the Monday of the hundred and seventieth week. Not the name. What the wood is. I was down the lane with water in my boots and I heard only that she had said, and I want it for myself because I carry wood too.”

The woman who saws lengths had come up behind him on the road with shavings on her boots, and heard the end of it, and stopped where she was.

Wenna Pyle is thirty-eight, saws lengths and sets studs at a bench in an open-sided shed behind a wheelwright’s about three hundred yards down that cart road, and said on the second day of the hundred and seventy-first week that she had said what she had to say on the Monday of the hundred and seventieth week and was not going to say it again to spare anybody carrying it.

“No.” She said it to him and not past him. “And you are owed the reason, because you asked for yourself and you asked plain, and because you are thirteen and carry wood. What I said on the Monday of the hundred and seventieth week was said on that landing with the water standing, and it was heard by whoever was there to hear it, and I will not say it again down a road or on a step to save anybody the trouble of having missed it. If I say it again for every person who was down a lane, then it becomes a thing I carry round and hand out, and I said on the Monday of the hundred and seventieth week I was not handing anything out, and I am holding to that.”

Wat Marshe picked up the pails again and his knuckles went white round the handles.

“I wanted it badly and I wanted it for myself.” He said it with his eyes on the steps. “I thought if I knew what the wood is, I would know how to carry my yoke without feeling like a fool for laying it where it stops nothing. That was wrong, and I can see it now you have said no. Knowing what a length is does not tell me how to carry mine.”

Wenna Pyle did not step closer and did not soften her voice.

“You are right that you are wrong, and I am not going to make more of that than it is.” She said it firmly. “Go and carry your water. Your yoke stops nothing and you said so yourself, and that saying was yours and nobody took it off you.”

No question about that name found an answer that morning, the boy’s question was about the wood and it got no answer either, and nobody was thanked for asking or for refusing.

Then Ilyan came up at about the ninth hour with four inches round his boots, and went round the dry strip without going near it, and gave his figures, and gave the figure of the standing offer with the subtraction out loud, and asked for nothing.

Ilyan Vester, thirty-one, nobody’s, and has been in this county four hundred and eighty-nine days. The cut across that palm is three hundred and fifty-eight days old.

“Four hundred and eighty-nine days in this county, and the cut across that palm is three hundred and fifty-eight days old, and the standing offer asked at the seven-hundred-and-ninety-third is three hundred and forty-three days old, which is one thousand and forty-three less seven hundred. It is unanswered. I have not asked it and it has not been answered on and not been withdrawn on. Nobody has offered me a way out of it and I did not ask for one, and I will not ask for one on the morning a boy has been refused well.”

He stood on the second step and stayed clear of the strip.

“One hundred and eighty-third wrong things in one hundred and eighty-third mornings, and this is the hundred and eighty-third, and nobody is to tell me what kind of figure that is. My want is to be somebody and it has not changed. My cost is that asking costs me nothing and I have not asked for one thing in four hundred and eighty-nine mornings, and I held that this morning while a boy asked and was refused, and I wanted to step in and smooth it, and that wanting to smooth it was a mistake, because a refusal given cleanly does not need a man of thirty-one to improve it.”

He looked down the flight at the pails and then back at the strip.

“I thought for a moment the landing would be kinder if I explained her. That is the worst thing I have thought all week, and I am saying it so it does not stay in me. She explained herself and she did not need me.”

Then the man who cannot see well came up at about the tenth hour with his hand on the wall, and asked his question, and got nothing, and said so in new words.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two, cannot see well, has a list of things he cannot check and has said out loud more than once that the list is a list and not a complaint, and said on the second day of the hundred and seventy-first week that his list had nothing on it that morning and he was going to keep it that way.

“Has that wood been spoken of since the Monday of the hundred and seventieth week.” Nothing returned, and he marked that his own way. “Nothing has returned and I am not writing that down anywhere.”

Then everybody went down that bank by about the eleventh hour, and the boy went down the lane with the pails bumping his legs, and Ilyan went last and stood on the second step from the bottom for a moment and went on down.

Corvin Tace swept all ninety of those steps, though about ninety of them were under in the thin water, and went at the whole flight from the bottom upward. The fortieth one he cleaned round and did not tread, and the chair he cleaned round in the same turn of the broom, and the iron in that seam took the broom head and nothing else. He came to the dry strip at the top end and swept it to its edge and stopped, and it held what it held from the seventh hour to the eleventh. The two lengths were still on the dry strip when the water thinned, a forearm long each and split down the middle along the grain, thick end rough where it parted, and on the second a saw mark on the square end and two shallow notches in the flat with one run out of the side.

Nothing on that landing was dragged or flipped or held up to weigh or sounded out or taken inside or set back or turned to use, then or afterwards, and no hand went on that wood to take anything off it. No name went onto anything standing there that morning, and what that wood is was not said out a second time, and nobody counted what stood there.

That morning passed off, and the account on that landing stood where it had stood at six, unfinished and still worked, and the name on that second length stood wrong, and nobody there said any part of it had closed.

And Wenna Pyle went down that cart road with sawdust on her boots from her own bench, and Wat Marshe’s pails left two wet rings on the stone at the foot of the steps where he had set them down, and the rings dried while the man with the broom worked, and nobody marked them.

# Chapter 553: The Second Day Of The Hundred And First Week, And A Wednesday, And On Which A Man Of Thirty-One Is On A Flat Boat For Eleven Hours In A Country That Is Not Chalk, And A Boy Of About Nineteen Tells Him In Four Words What A Season Is And Then Tells Him The Word The Hundred Uses For It And Where The Word Came From, Which Is Nowhere On Paper, And A Man Of Thirty-One Asks A Question He Had No Standing To Ask And Is Answered Correctly And Does Not Get Anything For It, And A Town With About Eleven Hundred People In It Is Reached At About The Fifth Hour In The Afternoon

Second day of the hundred and first week. His three hundred and third morning. One hundred and fifty-three days after the settlement. The fever thirty-eight weeks and four days. One hundred and seven days since the division. Fifty-three days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

The water was out of the channel at Coldcombe for two feet and a half, and the man with the pole went over it without noticing, and about four miles below Coldcombe it was up to a foot and a half and the country had stopped being a country you could walk.

---

Eleven hours on the flat of it. Sallow on both sides to the height of a man, then sedge, then a mile of open water with nothing on it and the sky coming down on it the way it does nowhere else.

There were no hedges after the first hour. There were no walls, no lanes, no stiles, and no chalk under the oars at any point, and after about the third hour Ilyan understood that the whole of what he knew about where a man may stand had come out of the boat with him and was lying in a field somewhere behind.

His leg went at about the fourth hour and stayed gone. He sat down on the tarpaulin over the stone at about the seventh and did not get up again. The boy on the pole said nothing about it and neither did the man at the stern, and about that they were the first two people in two days who had not asked him how he was.

At about the second hour he asked the boy what a season was.

“*Somebody’s, and not yours,*” said Reuben Searle.

“*Say the rest of that, because that is four words and I have come twenty-one miles to ask it.*”

“*That is the whole of what I know and I have been asked it four times this year. I am nineteen and I have been on this water since I was thirteen in the water-season and off it the rest, and that is the answer I have.*”

He set the pole and looked back up the river they had come down.

“*But you have not heard how we say it, and you would not have got that from me either unless you had asked me something about a season, so I will give you the word. We call it borrowed.*”

“*Somebody takes up somebody else’s season.*”

“*Yes.*”

“*And you call that borrowing.*”

The boy thought about it for the length of one pull.

“*We call it borrowing because the district has called it borrowing for longer than anybody living, and I have asked two men and one woman and all three of them said their grandfather said it, and I have never found it on a piece of paper anywhere and I have been in the weigh-house with the sheet on the wall in front of me four hundred times.*”

“*Say the rest of it, because a word with nobody behind it is a thing I know a great deal about.*”

“*There is nothing on that sheet that says a man may take up his neighbour’s season. There is a column for the ground and a column for the taking and a column for the year, and the ground is yours to pay the rate on whether you work it or not. That is not borrowing. Borrowing is when you ask. Nobody here asks. They take it up, and we call it borrowed, and my sister calls it something else.*”

“*What does your sister call it.*”

“*She says the taking, and she has said it four hundred times as well.*”

---

Then he asked the question he had no standing to ask, and he asked it because he had asked eight people questions in a hundred and fifty-three days and it had never once occurred to him to stop.

“*Do you have a season yourself.*”

The boy did not answer for about four seconds, and the boat went a long way on the current without a pole in it.

“*No,*” he said.

“*Say the rest of that.*”

“*We had a holding on the high ground east, nine acres, my father’s, and it burned in a fortnight in the dry time and there is a common of about eight hundred acres round it that is not anybody’s and not anybody’s to sit on. We came down four days ago behind eleven other households, and my sister is on the low side of the water in eleven tents with about sixty of us. The whole of what we have is a licence to stay until the water turns. I have never seen the stone and I cannot tell you how far off it is.*”

“*And it is a season.*”

“*It is a season and I have not got one and she has not got one, and I asked Slee for a place for the water-season and he took me on because I am cheap and I can pole and I do not go to the taproom.*”

Nobody on that boat thanked anybody and nobody on that boat was sorry for anybody.

“*I am sorry about your holding.*”

“*Do not do that. You have been on this water one day and you have said the right thing and I have not got a use for it, and I am nineteen, and I would rather you asked me the next question than that one.*”

He had it worked out before Ilyan got the sentence finished, and he set the pole again.

“*I would rather you asked me what the clerk is like. Everybody asks me about the fire.*”

---

The town came up out of the sedge at about the fifth hour of the afternoon and it was not on a hill and it was not under a hill.

It is on a green about two hundred yards across, with a pump at the top of it and about eleven houses with their backs to it. A long low building of sodden brick and tarred oak, about sixty feet long, stands along the south side with a crane-beam out of the middle of its roof.

The boy put the pole over and said one sentence about the name of the town and then went and helped the man at the stern with the rope, and said nothing else for the rest of the afternoon.

---

The boat did not stop at the green. It went along it and round the end of it and in under a bridge with one arch. Then it was on a channel about nine feet wide with a towpath on the left, and about nine men were on that towpath cutting a ditch with a machine pulled by two horses, and they did not look up.

It stopped at a gate in a brick wall, and Slee said a word to the man on the horse, and the man got down and opened it.

The water on the far side of that wall was a foot higher than the water on the near side. It was not falling and it was not running. About four feet of it stood in a straight line all the way along a stone facing, with green weed on the top of it and black mud on the bottom.

Then there was a block of limestone in the middle of the facing, about as big as a chest, and there was a line cut round it.

“*That is the tally stone,*” said Reuben Searle, from the bow, without being asked.

“*Say what it is for.*”

“*You come down and you read the line on it, and you tell me whether the water is over it or under it. If it is over it, it is the first day of the year, and every season in the hundred has gone, all of them, the ones you paid for and the ones you did not. After midday the clerk writes the new ones on the sheet on the wall.*”

“*Say the rest of that, because that is the whole of a year in nine seconds and I have been in one country since I was nine years old.*”

“*That is the whole of a year. The rest of it is the year itself.*”

He put his hand on the stone and did not say anything else about it.

---

The inn is the Green Paw and it has four beds and a parlour, and the woman who keeps it is about fifty and takes the money and gives no advice.

“*A penny for the bed and a penny for the bread in the morning and that is the whole of the list.*”

He gave her the penny and said the number out loud, because he had said a number out loud on a stone in another county two days ago and it had cost him nothing.

“*One pound and seventeen pence.*”

“*Say what that is.*”

“*Sixty-five days and two thirds of a day of a bed and bread at threepence a day.*”

“*That is not a thing anybody buys.*” She had the string off her apron before she said it. “*That is two thirds of a loaf and a penny over, and I have had two men in this room work it out that way and both of them thought they were being hard on themselves. I do not know what you are doing here and I would rather you did it without counting out loud in my parlour.*”

“*Say the rest of that, because I have been counting out loud for a hundred and fifty-three days and I have just been told to stop and I would like to know why.*”

“*Because you counted your money in front of me and not in front of yourself, and a woman who is given a number in a parlour has to be given a reason with it, and you have not got one, and I have a room to clean.*”

---

He had the bed. He had a bucket and no place to put the water, and his leg was two degrees worse than it had been on the stones at Coldcombe, and he had about nine pounds in a bag and no address in this county and no address in the last one.

At about the ninth hour he went out and walked round the long building because a man who has walked twenty-one miles in two days does not sit down in a bed on the second.

The north wall of it has a thing on it. It is a sheet of parchment about the length of two men hung from two rings, ruled into three columns, and it is a hand’s breadth off the brick, and there is a lamp on a bracket above it that is not lit.

He could not read a line of it in the dark and he stood there and tried.

“*You can read it in the morning.*”

There was a man standing about nine feet back in the dark by a door, and he had not been there a moment before and he had not made a noise.

“*I am aware of that. I have been standing here for about a minute.*”

“*Then you have been standing here for about a minute of a day when nobody reads it after the hour of eight. You may read it in the morning and I will be in the same building.*”

“*Are you the water-clerk.*”

“*No.*”

“*Then I will not tell you what is on it, and you have not asked me, and both of those things are the right way round, and I would like you to notice that you did not ask me and that I have not made you.*”

He put his hand on the ring the parchment hung from and did not lift it.

“*Goodnight,*” he said. “*You have not asked me and I am glad of it, and I am going to bed, and I am going to lie in a bed in a town of about eleven hundred people and be a stranger in it, and I do not know yet whether I can do that, and there is nobody here I can be sent for about.*”

The man by the door said nothing at all, and Ilyan went in, and the lamp on the bracket was not lit, and nobody in that town of about eleven hundred people had been asked anything about anything because of him.

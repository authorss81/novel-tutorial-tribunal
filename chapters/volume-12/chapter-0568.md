# Chapter 568: The Third Day Of The Hundred And Third Week, And A Thursday, And On Which There Is A Foot Of Water Standing On A Common Of About Eight Hundred Acres That Belongs To Nobody With Fifty-Eight People On It And Nine Children Under Nine On It, And Four Closes Take A Foot Too And Are Nobody’s Business That Day, And It Is The Tenth Day From A Monday And The Second Day After The Rising And Both Of Those Figures Are Said Out Loud In Front Of About Nine People On A Plank Bridge And Added Together And Both Of Them Come Out At The Same Morning, And Nobody Is Drowned And A Man Of Thirty-Three Is Standing In It With A Piece Of Paper In His Coat That Is Worth More Than The Ground, And A Woman Of Twenty-Eight Takes Her Count Off A Wall In The Open And Is Refused By A Man Who Has Promised To Answer Anything

Third day of the hundred and third week. His three hundred and eighteenth morning. One hundred and sixty-eight days after the settlement. The fever forty weeks and five days. One hundred and twenty-two days since the division. Sixty-eight days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

---

There is no hill in that country anywhere, and the reason a man can be drowned four miles from where he can walk is not a hill.

It is the distance between the top of a swell about nine hundred yards long and the flat behind it, and the water does not come up the swell from the river. It comes across the flat and stops, and everything on the swell is above it, and then the wind gets up at about the seventh hour and pushes the flat water over the top of the swell in a band about eleven feet wide, and that is what got them.

---

It came over the low common at about the eighth hour, and the water did not stop on the rise.

He was on the plank bridge at about the seventh hour with about nine people when he saw it go, and about nine people were on that bridge and about four of them said the number out loud at the same moment, which is what people do.

“*Say what you are looking at, because I have been in this county six days and about nine of us are on a bridge in about a foot of water saying a number and I would like one of us to be exact about which number.*”

“*Ten days. I counted ten days off the Monday of last week, off you at the bridge, and I have been saying ten ever since, and it is this morning.*”

Nobody on that bridge thanked anybody.

“*Eight. I counted eight off the Wednesday, off a water-clerk in a weigh-house in front of about thirty people, and it is this morning.*”

“*Say that again, because I am the man with the bad leg and I have said eight in four doorways in the dark, and one of them put it back on me in about four seconds with a subtraction, and I would like to know whether I have been wrong since then or right since then.*”

“*You were wrong on Thursday night by a day and you have been right ever since, because eight days off the Wednesday is this morning and ten days off the Monday is this morning, and those are the same morning.*”

Nobody on that bridge thanked anybody.

“*Work it out in front of me, both of you, and add the two together, because about nine of us have two numbers and one morning and that is a thing that gets somebody put in the wrong end of a ditch.*”

About nine people did it and it took about four minutes because three of them could not add.

“*Ten off the Monday. The Monday was the seventh and last day of the hundred and first week. Ten days off that is this morning.*”

“*Eight off the Wednesday. The Wednesday was the second day of the hundred and second week. Eight days off that is this morning.*”

“*Then the two numbers do not both come out of the same water, and only one of us has ever been able to check his against anything.*”

Nobody on that bridge thanked anybody.

What the water came from was not a mile of drain.

It was the low meadow, about thirty-one acres, and the bottom of this district. It had been under since Friday, because the rate on eleven parcels had not been paid for two years and the ditch that takes the meadow off the pasture has a foot of sallow growing in it.

The eleven parcels are the pasture. The pasture is above the meadow. The meadow is above the flat. Every year in a wet time the low meadow takes the whole of it and hands it on, and the flat hands it to the rise.

The rise is about nine hundred yards long. It belongs to nobody. It had fifty-eight people and about nine children on it.

A man of fifty-four four miles south had cut a mile of drain at the top of his own pasture in March to keep his own nine acres dry, and had taken it out again on Saturday at his own cost, because a rule about a parcel and not about a water is a rule that can be kept while somebody else drowns.

Both of those were true on the same morning, and neither of them had made the water.

---

There were no eleven tents on the rise at about the ninth hour.

Two of them were still standing, on the highest third. About nine of the eleven had gone about eleven feet. The ropes had been cut, the pegs pulled and the mats carried, and about nine men and about nine women had put what they could in about an hour on the flat, in about eight hundred acres of wet sedge.

Nobody was drowned. That is the first thing and it is the whole of the first thing.

---

About thirty people from Withermere went down at about the ninth hour, and about half of them were not of the hundred and had come because there is no form anywhere in this county that stops a man walking onto a common, and about nine of them had been told the number by a wall that morning.

They were on the low side from the ninth hour to the fourth hour in the afternoon. About nine of them were in the water to the knee. About four of them took a woman of seventy and a boy of about nine out of a line of tent-pegs at the eleventh hour and put them on a cart, and the road was under.

“*Say what you are standing in,*” said Bett Searle, to Ilyan, on the rise, at about the eleventh hour.

“*I am standing in a foot of water on a common of about eight hundred acres that belongs to nobody, with fifty-eight people on it and about nine children under nine. I am standing in it because a wall said fifty-eight on Wednesday and I said it too, and because you told a market in Withermere nine days ago that there would be fifty-eight of you here, and you are right about all of it.*”

Nobody on that common thanked anybody.

“*About nine people have been here for two hours and about nine of them came because of a number. I want you to finish that before I say what I came to say.*”

“*The rest of that is that I do not know one thing about the state of this ground from where I was standing. I knew that on Friday when I counted your tents out loud, and I counted them anyway, because a number is the only thing anybody can be checked against and a man with a bad leg on a plank is a man who gets told things.*”

“*Then you have been standing in a foot of water for two hours on the strength of a number and a wall. I want you to stand there and be that for another hour and then say something, because it is the only use I have got for you.*”

---

The four closes took a foot about the same hour and nobody in Withermere was standing at them, because about thirty people were on the low side instead, and that is the whole of what a foot of water is to a man whose water it is not.

Two of the four had men on them who were on the low side. The third had a woman on it who had stayed, and she got eleven sheep out of a low corner at about the tenth hour on her own, and lost a hurdle and about four feet of paling. The fourth is nine acres of new sward a man sowed in March, and there is no man of the four in the district, and on Tuesday a man of fifty-four had said in front of about thirty people that he would still put the boards in and pay for them out of his own purse, and nobody has asked him whether he meant it.

Nobody in Withermere was asked about any of the four closes on that day, and about nine people in that hundred had been told on Thursday night at four doorways that it was coming.

The worst ruin of the day was not the camp and it was not the four closes.

A man of about thirty-three was standing in eleven inches of water on six acres of the sally bed on the top side, with a purse in his hand. His brother had sixteen acres on that water and had not come. On Monday morning he had got up and given a season to a room.

About four miles away a hundred of about thirty men had gone out into about three hundred people and nobody had said one word about him.

“*Say what you are standing in,*” said Iven Ashcombe, who had come down with eleven men and a barrow and no orders.

“*I am standing in eleven inches of water on six acres that had a season on them at the sixth hour yesterday morning and have nothing on them now. I am standing in it because I gave them to a room, because a man of fifty-four took a drain out at his own cost, and because I could not stand in a room yesterday and hear what a stranger said at my door.*”

“*Say the rest of that, because I am the man who wrote six lines on a wall on Saturday about a man who gave a season to a room, and you are standing in it.*”

“*Say the rest of it. Take it off the wall.*”

“*I will not take it off the wall. There is no form in this county that lets me take a thing off a wall, and if I did it would be because you are standing in the water and about nine people are watching, and I will not do a thing to a wall in a wet field because a man is standing in the wet.*”

“*Then put the other thing on it. Say what you want written.*”

“*I want nothing written. I want the twelve shillings and sixpence to cut a ditch that is not going to be cut, because that is what the rate was for and there is nobody to pay it now, and in about two years the ditch will have sallow in it and the low meadow will go under for good and it will not be a wet year that does it. That is what my six acres are worth now and it is worth nothing on a wall.*”

Nobody in that field thanked anybody and nobody in that field was sorry for anybody.

Then a man of about twenty-eight with a crowbar got him out of it, and did not apologise, and did not say it was the wrong thing to give a season to a room.

---

Then Bett Searle went back over the plank bridge at the fourth hour in the afternoon, in nine inches of water on the flats, with her daughter asleep on her hip, and went up a green in a town of about eleven hundred people to a wall. About nine people were at it, because it is the hour that about nine people are always at it.

fifty-eight

was in the taking column of the low common, with the day on it, and it had been read by about nine people every morning of its life.

“*Say what you have come for, because I have answered anything anybody in this hundred has asked me for four days and about nine of them were about that figure and about nine of them were wrong about what it means, and I do not have to guess.*”

“*I have come to take my number off your wall.*”

Nobody at that wall thanked anybody.

“*Say the rest of that, and say the reason, because I wrote it there on Wednesday morning in about nine seconds at the request of a woman standing in nine inches of water, and I have had two days to think about whether I should have.*”

“*I wrote it there on Wednesday morning in about nine seconds because I asked you to. I have had two days to think about it. I have come back over that bridge in nine inches of water to take it off, because about thirty people walked down onto a common on the strength of a number a man put on a wall on Wednesday. The number who went down because of it is about thirty. The number of people on the common is fifty-eight. About nine of those thirty have never met a person of the fifty-eight and about nine have never seen eleven tents.*”

“*There is not one word in that about the number. It is all about what you have said twice now.*”

“*I wrote fifty-eight in a column of use under a piece of ground that belongs to nobody, and you wrote it in nine seconds because a woman asked, and nobody read it and checked it, and about thirty people walked into a foot of water on it at the ninth hour this morning.*”

Nobody at that wall thanked anybody and nobody at that wall was sorry for anybody.

“*No,*” said Iven Ashcombe.

“*Say the reason and not the sentence, because you have a board outside your own door that says any person in the hundred may ask you anything and you will answer it, and about nine people have watched you keep that this week and about nine have watched you do this.*”

“*Because I wrote it. It is in my hand and it is on the wall with the day on it and there is no form in this hundred or this county that lets me take a thing off a wall when about thirty people have read it and about thirty people have been somewhere on the strength of it. And if I take it off this afternoon then on Saturday morning a man with a bad leg writes it off as a mistake and about nine people in this hundred believe him, and I am not going to be a man whose mistakes are other people’s sentences.*”

“*Say the rest of that, because you have just refused me twice on the same ground and the ground is a wall and a hand, and I came over that bridge in nine inches of water for it.*”

“*Then it is on the wall and it is right. It got about thirty people down to a camp of fifty-eight in a foot of water, and about four of them got an old woman and a boy of nine out of a line of tent-pegs at the eleventh hour. I would write it again this morning. I would rather you had not asked me.*”

“*You would rather I had not asked you because you have learned in four days that a thing you write on that wall comes true in about nine hours, and I have learned the same thing, and that is the only thing two people in this county have agreed about in four days.*”

Nobody at that wall thanked anybody and nobody at that wall was sorry for anybody.

Then a woman of about seventy who had been at that wall every morning for about two hundred and thirty years said one sentence about it, and about nine people heard it.

“*Say what you are standing in front of, because I have been in front of this wall every morning of my life and I have read that figure in the taking column every morning for two days, and I have got a thing to say about it and it is not about the figure.*”

“*Say it.*”

“*On Wednesday you wrote fifty-eight under a piece of ground that belongs to nobody. It is the first time in about two hundred and thirty years that there has been a number of people on that sheet anywhere. About nine of us read it every morning and not one of us knew what to do with it, and this morning about thirty people went down a common because of it.*”

“*Say the rest of that, because you have said what it is and not what you think of it.*”

“*About nine of us have read that figure every morning for two days and not one of us has been asked a single question about it. There is a boy of fourteen who writes the week’s sheet in chalk on a Friday night, and he was not asked about it either. I have been in front of this wall every morning of my life and I have never once in two hundred and thirty years seen a number of people on it. I did not know there was anywhere to put one.*”

Nobody at that wall thanked anybody.

The fever was forty weeks and five days old. A foot of water came over about eight hundred acres that belongs to nobody on the second day after the rising, and about fifty-eight people and about nine children under nine were on it, and two of eleven tents were still standing and about nine had gone about eleven feet, and nobody was drowned.

Two numbers were said out loud on a plank bridge in front of about nine people and added together, and they did not come out of the same water. One of the men who said them said in four seconds that he had carried the wrong one forward from the day before, and would not be the man who took a number off a bridge.

A man of thirty-three stood in eleven inches of water on six acres that had a season on them at the sixth hour the day before and nothing on them now, and asked for a line about him to be taken off a wall, and was refused, and was not sorry for anybody.

A woman of twenty-eight walked four miles over a plank bridge in nine inches of water to ask for a number to be taken off a wall. She was refused by a man who has promised on a board outside his own door to answer anything, and who told her the reason in four sentences, and who told her that if he took it off on Wednesday then about nine people on Saturday would believe a stranger who had written it off as a mistake.

And it is still on the wall, and it is still right, and about thirty people walked into a foot of water on the strength of it at the ninth hour, and about four of them carried an old woman and a boy of nine out of a line of tent-pegs at the eleventh.

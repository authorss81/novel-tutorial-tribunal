# Chapter 595: The Second Day Of The Hundred And Seventh Week, And A Wednesday, And On Which A Man Of Fifty-Four Walks Four Miles Into A Room Over A Stable The Way He Walked Into A Weigh-House On The Rising With No Seat In It, And On Which A Woman Of Twenty-Eight Says In About Nine Seconds That Nothing Is Standing On That Meadow And Is Told In Front Of All Of Them That She Did Not Know She Was Saying It For Him, And On Which A Man Of Fifty-Four Says For The Third Time That He Is Not Saying No And Says That He Has Had Thirty Days To Find A Way To Say No That He Could Live With And Has Not Found One, And On Which He Says Out Loud That He Is Asking To Be Asked For Forty-Four Acres Of Long Grazing Four Miles South In Its Sixth Year And Not Given It And Not Put On A Bar, And On Which Three Boards Are Read At A Gate In The Open By Three People And The True Of Them Is That They Hold A Level And That Nobody In That Hundred Knows What They Hold Off

Second day of the hundred and seventh week. His three hundred and forty-fifth morning. One hundred and ninety-five days after the settlement. The fever forty-four weeks and four days. One hundred and forty-nine days since the division. Ninety-five days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

The day before was the first day of the hundred and seventh week.

---

The man of fifty-four came four miles on his own legs into a room over a stable at about the ninth hour on the Wednesday, the way he had come into a weigh-house sixty feet long on the rising with no seat in it and nobody had told him to stay away.

About seven hundred sheep four miles south. Forty-four acres of long grazing in its sixth year with about nine acres of new sward in it. Two hundred and ten acres across eleven parcels at two shillings and a penny the acre, which is twenty-one pounds seventeen shillings and sixpence a year, every penny of it paid, and no rule broken and nobody lied to.

The carrying was twenty-nine days. Three boards were in a yard four miles north with no money for the rest of them, and a condition he had set himself in front of about three hundred people was eleven days old.

Nobody in that room thanked anybody.

---

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Aldis Crane, who had a holding, a seat in the hundred and a skep behind his own gate. “*Because about nine people are in this room and a man of fifty-four has walked four miles into it. There is a sheet four feet wide on that board with three things on it and one of them has been blank since Monday.*”

Nobody in that room had anything to put to it.

“*Then say that last part,*” said Aldis Crane, “*and say it before the fourth hour, because about nine of us will work it out from a man with a barrow otherwise.*”

“*About nine of us have already worked it out from a man with a barrow,*” said Hoare Ryley, from the doorway. “*And it is not what you would have said.*”

“*I am standing in front of three things and about nine people, and one of them is three boards in a yard, and I have been carrying that twenty-nine days.*”

Nobody in that room had anything to say.

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Iven Ashcombe, who had come in with no slate under his arm. “*And say whether the hay is off that meadow, because on Friday I said out loud in a market of about three hundred people that a condition you set yourself cannot be met, and about nine of us have been working out since Friday whether that was the true of it.*”

About four seconds went past.

“*Then say it before I do,*” said the man of fifty-four.

---

Then a woman of twenty-eight said it, and it took about nine seconds, and nobody in that room had asked her to.

She had come the four miles in the flat with a daughter of six asleep against her shoulder and fifty-eight people on a rise about four miles off, on a Wednesday, the way she had done it on a Monday and a Tuesday. About nine people in that hundred had stopped saying out loud that she was owed something for it.

“*There is nothing standing on that meadow,*” said Bett Searle. “*Five passes and the last of it came off on Saturday morning about the ninth hour. It is in the top corner of that field in rows and there is no barn in this hundred that takes it.*”

Nobody in that room took it up.

“*Then say that last part,*” said Iven Ashcombe.

“*I have not been on that field and I am saying what I have been told by a man who cut it,*” said Bett Searle.

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Iven Ashcombe, “*because that is the true of it and it took about nine seconds and about nine of us have been arguing for four days about which half of it is the condition.*”

“*And that is the whole of it and it is yours to say which half,*” said the man of fifty-four. “*I said on that green that I would put three boards in that gate when the hay is off that meadow. Nothing is standing on that meadow.*”

Nobody in that room had a word for it.

“*Then say the rest of it,*” said Hoare Ryley.

“*A hay that stands over its time took five passes and it is not a hay and it is in the weather and it is going to rot where it lies. About nine people in that hundred know all of that.*”

About four people in that room said something at once.

Nobody in that room thanked anybody.

---

“*Then say the rest of that and do not be sorry for anybody,*” said Owain Bray. “*You have just said the half that lets a man of fifty-four off, and you have done it in a room of about nine people in four days after you said in a market of about three hundred that it could not be met.*”

Nobody in that room took it up.

“*Then say that last part,*” said Aldis Crane.

“*And about four of us would like to know which one of us was right on Friday,*” said Aldis Crane.

“*Then say it to about thirty people and not to me,*” said Bett Searle, “*because I said it in about nine seconds and it was not for you and it was not against you and about four of us in this room now have it in a shape that is about you.*”

“*Then say what the shape is,*” said Hoare Ryley, who had a barrow and had been in the doorway the whole time.

“*That a man of fifty-four has a way out and a woman of twenty-eight gave it to him in four days,*” said Bett Searle. “*And I did not know I was giving it to him and I would have said it the same way on Friday.*”

Nobody in that room found anything to say to that.
---

Then a man of fifty-four said the rest of it himself, and it was about nine sentences, and it is the reason there are no boards in that gate.

“*I am standing in front of three things on that sheet and about nine people, and I have had thirty days to find a way to say no that I could live with, and I have not found one.*”

Nobody in that room answered him.

“*So I am not saying no. I have said twice that I am not saying no and I am saying it a third time in a room with about nine people in it.*”

Nobody in that room took it up.

“*I am not going to be the man who put a foot of water on four closes and the common on the low side in the same week as I asked nine men for a fortnight. And I am not going to be the man who has three boards in a yard for thirty days and says he is not saying no, either, because about nine people in this hundred have that sentence now and it has stopped meaning anything at all.*”

“*Then say what you have instead,*” said Aldis Crane, “*because about four of us are waiting for it and about nine of us are not.*”

“*Three things. What I have is that the hay is off that meadow and I have not put the boards in. There is no form in the county of Kell that makes me and no form anywhere that stops me, and both of those are facts and about nine of you can check either of them.*”

---

And then he said the thing that is the reason the hay is in, and he said it out loud in a room over a stable with about nine people in it, and about nine of those nine had been waiting for eleven days to hear whether the hay would be asked for.

“*Say the rest of that and do not be sorry for me,*” said Hoare Ryley. “*About nine people in this hundred have been standing at a bar on a Friday for five weeks waiting for somebody to ask a man of fifty-four four miles south for his hay, and nobody has.*”

Nobody in that room thanked anybody.

“*Then say that last part,*” said Aldis Crane.

“*About four of us have worked out that the reason nobody has is that nobody can be sent for about him, and about nine have worked out the other reason,*” said Hoare Ryley.

“*About four of you have worked out that I am not asked because asking is a way of owing. That is right and it is not the whole of it. The other nine of you have worked out that asking is a way of not asking, because a man who is asked can say no and a man who is not asked cannot.*”

Nobody in that room thanked anybody.

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Aldis Crane.

“*So I am asking to be asked. Not as a favour and not as a thing you owe me and not on a bar. There is no form in this county that makes me ask and no form that makes anybody answer.*”

Nobody in that room thanked anybody.

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Aldis Crane.

“*Forty-four acres of long grazing four miles south, in its sixth year, with about nine acres of new sward in it, in season. Every penny of the rate on two hundred and ten acres paid and not one rule broken. That is the hay that feeds a hundred of about thirty men in this district.*”

Nobody in that room said a word.

“*Then say the rest of that and do not end it at the hay,*” said Hoare Ryley, “*because a room of about nine people has been asking for eleven days whether that sentence is a gift.*”

“*And it is mine and it is the only thing I have got that is worth anything to anybody,*” said Owain Bray.

About four seconds went past.

“*Then say the rest of that and do not end it at the gate,*” said Hoare Ryley.

“*And when anybody asks me for it, they have to come and ask me. It cannot be read off a wall and it cannot be put in a square at the end of a line, and it cannot be carried into a room by a man with a barrow who heard me say it once on a green.*”

Nobody in that room took it up.

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Aldis Crane.

“*If I say yes then it is in, and if I say no then it is out, and nobody in this hundred is to be blamed for a hay that did not come off ground that was in season to a man who paid for it.*”

---

And then they went down to the gate, because the third thing on that sheet was three boards and a settlement cannot have three boards in a yard in it.

It is about six feet of frame set in a sill of limestone blocks, on the main dyke, tarred oak with the tar gone soft in about two places. There are six boards that go into it and there are three boards in it. Three boards is a foot below the tally line. Six boards is the line.

A space about as wide as two fingers where two sill blocks meet on the low side, with sallow grown up through it from the low side and matted across the gap and gone hard. About four feet from the low post of the frame a ditch was wet to the top of its bank on the Tuesday of the sixth week. About a hundred and forty yards of that ditch came out of the ground in sixteen days with a barrow, and the sallow was heaped on the top side in a row about as long as a cart.

Above the frame on the high side there were two lines on the timber. The lower one was wet and dark and about level with the top of the boards. The upper one was pale and dry, about the width of two fingers above it.

Nobody in that hundred can say what the upper one is, and nobody could say what it was on the Sunday of the sixth week either.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Hoare Ryley, to about nine people on the high side of that gate. “*We have been four days on that sheet with three boards in it, and about nine of us have now put our hands on this gate. I would like the two men who have put their hands on it to say out loud in about nine sentences each what three boards hold off.*”

Nobody at that gate thanked anybody.

“*Then say that last part,*” said Aldis Crane.

“*And about four of us know they will not agree and about nine do not,*” said Hoare Ryley.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Iven Ashcombe. “*I have carried the figure three is a foot below and six is the line on that wall for twenty-eight days and nobody has ever asked me where it came from.*”

Nobody at that gate thanked anybody.

“*Then say that last part,*” said Aldis Crane.

“*And the man who owns this gate has told me twice in a market that it is not what it is for,*” said Iven Ashcombe.

“*Three boards hold a level,*” said Owain Bray. “*That is a true thing about a gate and it is the only true thing about a gate anybody has said in a year of not saying anything about it.*”

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Iven Ashcombe, “*because about nine people in this hundred have that sentence and about nine of us have the other one, and the two nines have not asked each other.*”

Nobody at that gate had anything to say to it.

“*That other one is mine,*” said Hoare Ryley, “*and I have said it three times in three places.*”

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Hoare Ryley.

“*The foot of water that went over the common on the second day after the rising morning did not come through this gate. It came off the low meadow, whose ditch had a foot of sallow growing in it, and the water off the meadow goes over the old line and down onto the flat, and the wind at the seventh hour takes it over the top of a swell about eleven feet wide.*”

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Aldis Crane.

“*I have said it three times in three places and about nine of us did not hear me the first time,*” said Owain Bray.

“*And what does the gate hold off,*” said Aldis Crane.

“*That is the question and nobody in this hundred has worked it out, and it is a year since anybody asked it and about nine of us have been in a room about nine times saying it was asked.*”

Nobody at that gate thanked anybody.

“*Then say the rest of that and do not end it at not knowing,*” said Bett Searle, who had come the four miles and stood on the low side with her hand on the frame. “*Because a settlement that says it does not know is a settlement that will be read in about nine mouths as a settlement that decided something and is not saying what. Write down what the two men disagree about, and write down what would settle it, and what it costs.*”

“*Four hours at a full gate,*” said Iven Ashcombe. “*A man with a rod on the high side and a man with a rod on the low side, and about four hours of water doing whatever it does.*”

Nobody at that gate said anything.

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Bett Searle.

“*And the two lines on that timber with a hand on them and a figure of how far apart they are, and the gap under the sill with a hand in it and a figure of how much water comes through it and where from,*” said Iven Ashcombe.

“*And what that costs,*” said Aldis Crane, “*in a money chain that about nine people in this hundred can check in about four seconds.*”

Nobody at that gate had anything to say to it.

“*Say it and do not put in shillings you have made up,*” said Owain Bray. “*The boards are three boards, and a man with a team hauled stone for eleven years said a price off a plank at a market, and about four of us heard it and about nine did not.*”

Nobody at that gate thanked anybody.

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Aldis Crane.

“*And I have the money for the boards and I am not putting them in, and both of those are facts,*” said Owain Bray.

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Hoare Ryley.

“*Four hours is a day’s pay for about four men and a rod is a rod, and nobody in this hundred has ever been asked to pay for four hours of a river.*”

Nobody at that gate thanked anybody and nobody at that gate was sorry for anybody.

The fever was forty-four weeks and four days old. Three boards have been in a yard four miles north for twenty-nine days. A man of fifty-four has carried a condition about them since the rising, answered it once in front of about three hundred people, and said twice that he was not saying no.

On the Wednesday he said it a third time in a room over a stable with about nine people in it, and said that the hay was off that meadow and he has not put the boards in. There is no form in the county of Kell that makes him and no form that stops him. He was not excused and he was not punished.

He asked to be asked for his hay, out loud, in the open, in front of about nine people. Forty-four acres of long grazing four miles south in its sixth year is the hay that feeds a hundred of about thirty men in that district.

And three boards were read at a gate, in the open, by three people. The true of them came out of the mouth of a man of fifty-four: they hold a level, and nobody in that hundred knows what they hold off. About nine people stood on a bank and wrote that down.

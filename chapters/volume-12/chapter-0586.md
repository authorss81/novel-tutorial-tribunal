# Chapter 586: The Seventh And Last Day Of The Hundred And Fifth Week, And A Monday, And On Which A Woman Who Keeps Four Beds And Has Never Given Him Her Name Prices A Year Of A Man Of Thirty-One Out Loud In About Nine People’s Hearing, Says Three Pence A Day And Three Hundred And Sixty-Five Days, Puts It At Four Pounds Eleven Shillings And Threepence And Then Says The Half Of It She Cannot Put A Figure On, And Then Gives Him A Parcel Instead Of Her Name, And On Which A Water-Clerk Says That What The Hundred Has To Sell Is One Morning And One Room And One Seat In About Thirty

Seventh and last day of the hundred and fifth week. His three hundred and thirty-sixth morning. One hundred and eighty-six days after the settlement. The fever forty-three weeks and two days. One hundred and forty days since the division. Eighty-six days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

The morrow is the first day of the hundred and sixth week, and it is a Tuesday.

---

The Green Paw has four beds and a parlour and the parlour is the only room in that town with a fire lit on a Monday.

There were about nine people in it at about the ninth hour. About four of them had come for the asking and about five of them had come because the bar was there.

Nobody in that parlour thanked anybody.

Ilyan came in at about the ninth with his bad leg out along the bench under the table, the way he puts it, and he had been in that county thirty-two days.

He had under two pounds in a purse. He had said in a yard in the mud on the Tuesday of this week that he would stay nobody’s until the year turns, and about nine mouths in that town had it a way that was not his.

He had held the low end of a plank on Wednesday and taken nothing for it. He had said the true of a woman’s answer in a lane on Thursday about nine seconds before she gave it. He had said a sentence out loud in a market on Friday and about four people had it as a man who had picked a side. He had put a wrong parcel on a wall on Saturday out of something a man had said at a bar, and a man of fifty-one had come down a lane and taken it off with a rag in about four seconds.

He wanted to know what staying nobody’s cost.

He had known since Saturday that he was not going to be the one to say it, and he had had two days to work out who was, and he had not got anybody, and so on a Monday morning in a parlour with a fire in it he asked a woman of about fifty who keeps four beds and has never given him her name.

---

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said the woman with the tray, “*because there are about nine people in this room and you have come in with your leg out and you have not asked me for anything in thirty-two days, and about four of us would like to know what a man nobody can be sent for comes into a parlour on a Monday to say.*”

“*I am standing in front of a fire and a sum. I have decided to stay nobody’s until the year turns and I said it out loud in a yard on Tuesday and about nine people have it a way that is not the way I said it. I am not going to say what that costs me, because when a man says what a thing costs him in a room it is a number he has chosen.*”

“*Then say the rest of that and do not tell me you are not going to ask me. You are going to ask me, and about nine people in this room are going to hear me answer, and that is the thing I would like you to say out loud before you do it, because about nine of us have watched you do this three times in a fortnight and every time the person who answered was worse for it than the question.*”

Nobody in that parlour thanked anybody.

“*Then I am asking you, in front of about nine people, and you are going to price a year of a man you have known thirty-two days, and I would rather that were done out loud than be carried about in my own head.*”

“*Say what you want priced. A man of thirty-one who is nobody’s until the year turns. That is what I have in front of me.*”

“*That is right. Go on.*”

“*A bed in this house is a penny and bread in the morning is a penny. That is what everybody in this town charges, and it is on no board anywhere, and I have charged it for eleven years.*”

“*Then say the rest of it, and I know what you are going to say about the rest of it, and I would rather you said it than had me say it for you.*”

“*I am not going to take two pence a day off you, because two pence a day is a man with no leg and no work, and a man of thirty-one with a leg that stopped working on a common eighteen days ago eats three pence a day. I have watched you. You have not asked me what you were eating and I have not asked you and there is a way of not asking that is worse than asking.*”

Nobody in that parlour thanked anybody.

“*Say the rest of it and put it in a number, because about nine people in this room can check a number against each other and about four of them cannot add.*”

“*Three pence a day. Three hundred and sixty-five days in a year, because a year is the time between one crossing of that block of limestone and the next and it is not a fortnight and it is not a quarter and there is nobody in this county who can tell you how long it is until it comes.*”

About nine people in that parlour looked at her.

“*That is a thousand and ninety-five pence. Four pounds is nine hundred and sixty of them, and there is a hundred and thirty-five over. A hundred and thirty-five pence is eleven shillings and threepence. Four pounds, eleven shillings and threepence.*”

Nobody in that parlour said anything.

“*Say the rest of that, because about nine of us have a number and about nine of us want to know what it is the number of.*”

“*It is the number of a man of thirty-one keeping himself in a bed and fed for three hundred and sixty-five days in a town of about eleven hundred people, in a year in which the water comes over the tally stone once. It is not the number of the rate. It is not the number of a hay. It is not the number of six acres, which is twelve shillings and sixpence a year, and which I know about because a man in this room has not paid it and another man in this room has tried to and been refused.*”

“*Then say the rest of it, and do not end it at the fair part.*”

“*The rest of it is that in about four of those days you would have bought the season on the smallest holding in this hundred, which is two acres at four shillings and twopence, and put the money on that bar and been one of about thirty men for a year. You are not going to. You said so in a yard on Tuesday, in the mud, to the man whose ground it is.*”

“*So four pounds eleven shillings and threepence is what your year is worth.*”

“*About four people in this room think it is worth more than that and about nine think it is worth less, and I am the one who has your bed and your bread, and I am telling you the figure is what it is and not what you would like it to be.*”

---

“*Then say the half of it you have not said,*” said a man of fifty-eight at the back of that parlour, who had come in with no slate under his arm. “*Because I have a board outside my own door that says any person in this hundred may ask me anything and I answer it, and about nine people in this county have asked me about a year since the rising and nobody has asked me what it costs the hundred.*”

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said the woman with the tray, “*because you have come into my parlour and there are about nine people in it and about four of them have been in this room since the seventh hour and did not know you were coming.*”

“*I am standing in front of a fire, and in front of a room with about nine people in it. A hundred of about thirty men meets at midday on the morning the water comes over the tally stone, in a building sixty feet long, and a stranger may stand in it and speak. In a year he will cross that block of limestone once and he will not be in that building on the morning it happens.*”

“*Then say the rest of it in a number, because about nine of us have just had one out of a woman and about nine of us would like to know what a hundred thinks a seat is worth.*”

“*One. One morning, one room, one seat in about thirty. That is the whole of what the hundred has to sell and I have not got a figure for it in shillings and I am not going to invent one in my own building.*”

About four people in that parlour said something at once and about nine did not.

Nobody in that parlour thanked anybody and nobody in that parlour was sorry for anybody.

Then a man of about thirty-four with a barrow outside the door asked, from the step, whether anybody in that room had heard about the load in the lane on Thursday, and about nine of them said they had, and about four said they had not.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said the woman with the tray, “*because there is a load of wet hay that stood in my only way in for about six hours on Thursday and went off it in the night, and about four of us would like to know where it went, and there is no form anywhere that says a man may ask.*”

“*It went back down the lane to a yard with a barn in it that I have never been inside and whose man I do not know, because there were about nine of us at that cart and about nine of us had nowhere else to take it, and I did not ask him first, and I am saying that here because there are about nine people in this room who would otherwise find it out on a Friday.*”

Nobody on that step thanked anybody.

“*Then say the rest of it,*” said Iven Ashcombe, “*because about nine of us will now go and look at that barn, and I would rather the true of it came from you in this room than from four men in a lane.*”

“*The true of it is that he took it in because there was nowhere else, and not because he wanted it, and I do not know whether he has put it in his barn or on his cart, and neither does he, because nobody has asked him and he is a man with nine acres and no seat and about nine of us have never spoken to him.*”

---

Then he asked her for her name, and she did not give it, and she gave him a parcel.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Iven Ashcombe, “*because a man of thirty-one has been priced in about nine people’s hearing by a woman who keeps four beds and he has under two pounds in a purse, and about four of us have worked out what he is about to do and about nine have not.*”

“*I am standing in front of a fire and a woman who has told me what I cost, and I cannot pay her for thirty-two days of a bed and bread without her name on something, and there is no form in the county of Kell that says what a thing like that is.*”

“*Then say the rest of that and do not put it on a wall,*” said the woman with the tray. “*You have said out loud in this county that a paper with a name at the top of it is a paper nine people can be argued with. That is my reason and it has not changed and it is not the same reason as yours.*”

“*Say what your reason is.*”

“*Put it in the ground. Two acres of the strip behind that yard. Four shillings and twopence a year on it, paid out of a purse in a lane in front of about nine people, and I do not work it and nobody in this hundred knows what it grows. You cannot argue with that at a wall and there is nothing on it to rub out.*”

Nobody in that parlour thanked anybody.

The fever was forty-three weeks and two days old. A man of thirty-one was priced in about nine people’s hearing in a parlour on a Monday by a woman who keeps four beds, and the figure was worked out loud in front of all of them, and the figure was four pounds eleven shillings and threepence for a year of a man of thirty-one keeping himself fed.

About four people in that room thought it was worth more than that. About nine thought it was worth less.

And the woman who priced it gave him a parcel instead of her name, and he could not pay her for a bed without it, and she would not say what her reason was, and the morrow is the first day of the hundred and sixth week and it is a Tuesday.

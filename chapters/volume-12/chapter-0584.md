# Chapter 584: The Fifth Day Of The Hundred And Fifth Week, And A Saturday, And On Which A Boy Of Fourteen Holds The Slate At The Sixth Hour And Reads Two Men Long Of Parchment And Gets One Number Wrong Before He Has Finished The Sentence, And On Which One Of Nine Parcels On A Morning List Is Found To Be Wrong By The Man Who Put It There And Is Rubbed Off In About Four Seconds, And On Which A Man Of Thirty-One Gives A Parcel Out Loud For The One Challenge That Has None And A Man With Sixteen Acres Says It Is Not His And The Clerk Does Not Write It Down, And On Which At The Ninth Hour A Man Of Fifty-One Comes Down A Lane And Says It Is His

Fifth day of the hundred and fifth week. His three hundred and thirty-fourth morning. One hundred and eighty-four days after the settlement. The fever forty-three weeks and no days. One hundred and thirty-eight days since the division. Eighty-four days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

The reading is at the sixth hour on a Saturday morning, under the lamp on the bracket, with the door of the weigh-house open and the street behind it. The lamp is lit an hour earlier on a Saturday than it is on any other morning, because the sixth hour is the hour the reading is at, and that habit is about two hundred and thirty years old and nobody in that hundred could tell you who started it.

On this Saturday the boy with the slate was fourteen years old and his name was Toby Henshaw.

He had been asked to write a thing five times in his life and had said he did not know what to do with it four times. About nine people stood under the lamp. About four more stood in the street where they could hear.

Nobody in that building thanked anybody.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Iven Ashcombe. He stood to one side of the lamp with his hands behind him and had not held the slate for three readings and did not intend to hold it for a fourth. “*Because you have it in your hand and about nine of us are at that wall, and you have told me twice that you do not know what to do with it, and this is the fifth time anybody has asked you.*”

“*I am standing in front of two men long of parchment and I have been reading it since the fifth hour to find out whether I can.*”

“*Then say the rest of that, and say it before the sixth, because about nine people in this hundred have been standing in this street since the fifth hour waiting to find out whether a boy of fourteen can read a sheet that is two hundred and thirty years old.*”

“*I can read it. I read the notice on the smithy wall on a Friday night for a penny and that was worse, because I had to find the names myself. This has got columns in it and columns are easier than names. The bit I cannot do is the little figures going left from the squares. There are two hundred and thirty of them and I can count to nine and then I have to start again.*”

“*Then say what you do about the two hundred and thirty.*”

“*I read them out as a run of them and I say so out loud before I start. About nine of us will hear me say that and about four of us will check.*”

Nobody in that weigh-house thanked anybody.

---

Then he read the eleven lines first, parcel and acres and taking and the figure in the square, in that order. Every season in that hundred had gone and been re-let eighteen days before, on the rising, and every square had a figure in it except one.

The one was six acres of the sally bed on the top side.

He read the line. Then he read the square. Then he said the thing he had said on the last two Saturdays without knowing that anybody was listening for it.

“*Nothing in it. For the third week running.*”

About nine people in that room heard a boy say it. About four wrote nothing down.

Then he read the four lines about how many men fill the chairs in a room. Then the eleven-line notice off the smithy’s end wall as it stood copied under the clerk’s hand, with about thirty names in chalk and a stroke beside the ones that are not of the hundred. Then the remedy of about three hundred words off the front, and the five lines a woman of thirty-eight wrote on the back of it.

He read it all in eleven minutes and he got one thing wrong and it was corrected before he had finished the sentence.

“*Seven hundred sheep,*” he read. “*Four miles — four miles south.*”

“*Say it again,*” said Aldis Crane, from the street. “*Because it says seven hundred and about nine of us have it as about nine hundred, and a flock is a thing a man can be counted and it has been counted twice this year by two different men and the two counts do not agree.*”

“*Then say which count is wrong,*” said the boy.

“*Say the rest of that instead,*” said Iven Ashcombe. “*Because about nine of us would rather hear a number corrected at a wall than a number agreed on in about four mouths over a week, and I would rather you said that than that I did.*”

“*Nine hundred is a range and not a flock,*” said Owain Bray, from the back of that building. He had come four miles on his own legs. “*About seven hundred is mine. There is no flock of nine hundred in this hundred. Somebody carried a number from a place where they were not standing.*”

About four people in that room laughed and about nine did not.

Nobody in that weigh-house thanked anybody and nobody in that weigh-house was sorry for anybody.

---

Then came the list of nine parcels, and one of the nine was wrong, and the man who had put it there said so himself.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Iven Ashcombe, to a man of about thirty-four with a stroke beside his name in chalk and a barrow outside the door. “*Because you put a parcel on that wall last Saturday at about the eighth hour with a willow stump in the description, and about nine of us have read it every morning since, and this is the hour a thing on that wall is supposed to be checked.*”

“*Then read the ninth one out and I will say what is wrong with it before you have finished it.*”

The boy read it out. Nine parcels stood on a list under everything else, and the ninth said two acres of the top side above the lane, on the right going up, with the willow stump at the corner, hay standing.

“*It is the left going up,*” said the man of about thirty-four. “*I put it up on the Saturday morning before I had my boots on and I copied it off the second column of a line I have never stood in. It is the left. I have worked that ground since I was nine and it is the left, and there is a willow stump on the right going up too and it is not the corner one.*”

“*Then say how long it has been on that wall in that form,*” said Iven Ashcombe, “*and then the boy will take the rag to it, because you are right and about nine of us can see you are right and there is no form in the county of Kell that says a wrong thing may stand on a wall.*”

“*Six mornings.*”

The boy took the rag to it. It was off in about four seconds.

Nobody in that weigh-house thanked anybody.

---

Then the six challenges, five of them with a parcel against them and one with nothing.

The first five were read out with the parcel and the day on each, and the parcels were there, and about nine people checked them against each other without a word being said.

The sixth said that a line reading hay, mown was wrong, and there was nothing against it under the day.

About nine people waited.

Nobody stood up.

“*Say the rest of that,*” said Iven Ashcombe, “*because a line with nothing against it has now been read at two readings and it is a sentence with no ground in it, and about nine of us have noticed that a boy of fourteen can say that out loud in a room and get no answer.*”

“*I have read that line twice now with nothing against it,*” said Toby Henshaw. “*And a line with nothing against it is a sentence. Somebody said that to a man on the top side in a yard on Tuesday and about nine of us heard it. I am not asking anybody to stand up. I am saying that I read it and there is nothing in it.*”

Nobody in that weigh-house said anything.

---

Then a man of thirty-one filled the gap, out loud, in front of about nine people, and it was wrong, and he knew it was wrong four seconds after he had said it.

He had been in that county thirty days. On the Wednesday of last week he had stood at that same lamp with a bad leg straight out along a bench and had said out loud that a challenge with no parcel is a number no man can be checked against. On the Tuesday of this week he had stood in a yard in the mud and said he would not write a thing down anywhere, because a stranger’s hand on a fifth thing in that county is a fifth hand.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Iven Ashcombe, to him, at about the sixth hour and the fortieth minute. “*Because you have been in this county thirty days and about nine people in this room have watched you say twice that you were not going to put another thing on that wall, and you have just said a parcel out loud in front of all of them.*”

“*I am standing in front of six lines and five of them have a parcel and I have just given the sixth one one.*

“*Then say where you got it, and about nine people in this room are going to check it against a man rather than against me.*”

“*At a bar on the top side on the Wednesday. A man of about thirty with sixteen acres was asked at that bar which of his eleven acres the ditch on the sally bed took the water off, and he said the two acres above the second close, and I have carried it four days without putting it anywhere.*”

The man of about thirty with sixteen acres was standing at the back of that building and had been there since the sixth hour.

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Iven Ashcombe.

“*That is not mine and was never mine. That is my brother’s, and it is four acres and not two, and I have paid the rate on it at two shillings and a penny the acre, which is four hundred pence, which is one pound thirteen shillings and fourpence, and I have worked that out twice, and I said it out loud at that bar in the open because a man was standing nine feet off it with his mouth shut and I wanted him to hear a number.*”

About four seconds went past.

“*Then say the rest of it,*” said Iven Ashcombe, “*and understand that I have a wall and no keeper and about nine people who can be checked against each other in it.*”

“*Say it once and say it plain. It is the four acres above the second close off the dyke. It is not hay standing, it is pasture, and it has not been mown in any year of the little figures, because the woman who holds it has never held it and a man four miles south held it in the middle of April. That is my brother’s ground and not mine and I am the wrong man to have said so to a woman holding a purse in April, and I have known that since April.*”

Nobody in that weigh-house thanked anybody.

The clerk did not write it. He said out loud that he would not, because a man in the room had said the parcel was wrong and there is no form in the county of Kell that lets him write a thing a man has said is wrong.

Nobody in that weigh-house was sorry for anybody.

---

Then the reading ended and about nine people went out into the street and the boy began to read the wall back from the top of the first column.

At about the ninth hour a man of fifty-one came down the lane with his sleeves rolled and mud to the knee.

He had cut ditch in that district for about thirty years. He had four acres of his own on that water and his two brothers had eleven between them, and he had about nine days of sallow left in a ditch that was on nobody’s rate.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Iven Ashcombe, at the wall, with the boy and about nine people. “*Because a man has been in this county thirty days and put a parcel on that wall forty minutes ago out of something a man said at a bar, and about nine of us have not been able to correct it, and now you have come down that lane and I would like everybody at this wall to hear both things.*”

“*Then say it on the wall and let the boy say it with the rag, because about nine of us have just read it six mornings running.*”

So it was said on the wall, and the boy took the rag to it, and it was off in about four seconds.

“*Say the rest of that,*” said Iven Ashcombe. “*Because you have corrected a stranger’s error and about nine of us have watched you do it for nothing, and there is no form anywhere that says what a man is owed for that.*”

“*I am owed nothing. I said on the Tuesday that the two acres are not the two acres, and about nine of us at that bar heard me and about nine did not, and about nine of us have heard this stranger say them out loud four days later and about nine have not. That is the whole of it and I would rather it went on the wall than not.*”

Nobody at that wall thanked anybody.

The fever was forty-three weeks and no days old. A boy of fourteen who had said he did not know what to do with a thing four times out of five held the slate at the sixth hour on a Saturday and read two men long of parchment, and he got one number wrong and it was corrected before he finished the sentence, and one parcel wrong and it was rubbed off a wall in about four seconds by the man whose ground it was.

And the sixth of six challenges went back into that building with nothing against it under the day, and about nine people read it every morning, and a boy of fourteen says out loud that a line with nothing against it is a sentence.

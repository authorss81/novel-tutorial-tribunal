# Chapter 572: The Seventh And Last Day Of The Hundred And Third Week, And A Monday, And On Which A Man Of About Fifty With A Holding And A Seat In The Hundred Asks In A Parlour Over A Bar The One Question He Has Wanted To Ask For Nine Years And Is Answered In A Number, And The Number Is About Twenty-One Acres Of About Thirty-One That Are Not Going To Be Mown This Year, And Nobody In That Room Can Check It And The One Man Who Could Says Out Loud Why He Is Not Going To Walk Four Hundred Yards To Look, And A Boy Of Fourteen Is Sent For Because A Number Is A Thing A Man Can Be Checked Against And Nobody In The Room Has Got A Line To Give Him, And The Woman Who Keeps The Inn Says The Same Number Wrong Fourteen Minutes After A Man Of Fifty-Eight Said It Right

Seventh and last day of the hundred and third week. His three hundred and twenty-second morning. One hundred and seventy-two days after the settlement. The fever forty-one weeks and two days. One hundred and twenty-six days since the division. Seventy-two days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

The morrow is the first day of the hundred and fourth week, and it is a Tuesday, and the first of the hay in this district is either cut on that morning or a fortnight late, and about nine people in this hundred now know that and about nine do not.

---

The Green Paw has four beds and a parlour and the parlour is the only room in that town with a fire lit on a Monday.

There were about nine people in it at about the ninth hour and by the tenth there were about four. The ninth had gone to work.

He was about fifty and his name was Aldis Crane, and he had a holding with a season on it, and one of the thirty seats, and behind his own gate there was a skep on a stand under a board, which on a fen is a thing a man has and on a chalk hill is a thing a man has not got. The woman who keeps that house had not asked him why he was in it and he had not said.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Iven Ashcombe. “*Because there is a fire in it on a Monday and I was in this room at the last two of them, and about nine people have come to me in two days with a thing they have wanted to say for nine years. I would like the ninth one to come from the man who has been carrying it longest.*”

The man of fifty did not sit down.

“*I have wanted to ask you whether the low meadow has ever gone under. In any year. Not what you remember of it and not what a man told you at a gate. Whether it has gone under before.*”

“*Say the rest of that,*” said Iven Ashcombe, “*because you have been carrying it since Sunday and about nine of us have watched you carry it, and you have just asked me a thing that nine lines on a board outside the water-clerk’s door say I may not be asked.*”

“*The ninth line says a man may answer what he knows now and not what he remembers. The sixth line says the rule does not stop a man giving a true thing he can be checked against. A meadow that has gone under, or has not gone under, is a thing to be checked against. About nine of us can walk it. You can be checked against. So I have come and asked it, and I would like the answer in a number and not in a story about a man who was in another county for four days of it.*”

Nobody in that parlour thanked anybody.

Ashcombe stood with his back to the fire and did not answer for about four seconds.

“*Three times that I know of in about nineteen years. The first of them was the year before last. The meadow went under in the second week and it stayed under. The year before that it went under for a fortnight. The third was the Thursday, and it was over the common at about the eighth hour.*”

“*Say the rest of that, and say it as a number, because I have asked for a number and about four people in this room are about to write it on something.*”

“*The low meadow is about thirty-one acres. About two thirds of it is under now. It was under on Thursday and it is under this morning, and that is about twenty acres. The third that is not under is about ten, and it is standing hay, and it can be mown.*”

“*Then say the number of acres that are not going to be mown this year, and put it in a mouth, because I have been in nine rooms in this county in eighteen days and every one of them has had a number in it that nobody could check, and I am asking you for one that can be.*”

“*About twenty-one.*”

“*Say the rest of that, and say how long the ten will take, because about four of us have just written that number down on something and we are about to be asked.*”

“*A fortnight. A scythe and about nine men. It has to begin on the first of this week, which is the morrow. A hay that stands over its time on this fen goes to seed, and then a man takes it in four passes instead of one, and by the fourth pass it is a hay of the following year.*”

Nobody in that parlour thanked anybody.

Then the man of fifty put his hand flat on the table, and he said the thing that turned a figure into a number.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Iven Ashcombe. “*Because you have put a hand on my table in my own town, and I have a board outside my door that says a person may ask me anything, and you have not asked me a thing for nineteen years, and about four people in this room have noticed the difference and about nine have not.*”

“*I am standing in front of the number twenty-one. I am saying out loud, in a room, that it is the truest thing anybody has said about that meadow in nine years. And I am saying it to a man and not to a wall.*”

“*Then say it twice, and say it to a man four miles south before you say it to a wall. A number said to a man is a thing a man can be argued with. A number said to a wall is a thing a hundred and thirty people are right about for about four seconds.*”

“*It is the truest thing anybody has said about that meadow in nine years. And if you put it on that wall then I will have said it twice, and one of the two will be on a wall, and a number that is on a wall cannot be asked about. You taught that on the rising and about nine people repeated it. I am one of them and I have not said so.*”

Nobody in that parlour thanked anybody and nobody in that parlour was sorry for anybody.

---

Then somebody asked the question this hundred has been walking towards for ten days, and he asked it standing up, and about nine people heard it, and nobody in the room answered it and this account does not answer it either.

“*Say what a number is for,*” said Aldis Crane. “*In a room. In a town of about eleven hundred people, in the second week of a year that is counted from a block of limestone. Say what on earth a number is for, when there is one man in this county who can be asked anything, and he has put nine lines on his own door that stop anybody asking him what they remember, and no second man in the county who can be asked at all, and a wall that any person in this town may correct in about four seconds and nobody may afterwards be asked whether what is on it is true.*”

Nobody in that parlour said anything.

“*Say the rest of that, because you have asked it in a room and about four of us are going to repeat it before the end of the day, and you have not asked me, and I am the one person in this county who would have to answer it, and I have not worked out whether I am allowed to.*”

“*I am going to say the true of it and it is not an answer. It is a wall in the open with about nine people at it every morning, and a figure that about nine of them are right about and about nine of them are wrong about what it means. It is a remedy of about three hundred words under an oak bar that no person in this county is bound by. And it is me, and I am the only one, and I have put nine lines on my own door that stop anybody asking me what I remember.*”

“*Say the rest of that, and do not stop there to be modest, because you have been in this room two years without asking and about nine people in this hundred have gone without knowing, and I am one of them and I am saying so in a bar.*”

“*That is what a number is for, and I have not got an answer, and I am not going to give you one out of a fire in a parlour on a Monday when I have had it in a room with about thirty men in it and not given it there either.*”

Nobody in that parlour thanked anybody.

---

Then the trouble with a number in a room happened in that room, and it took about fourteen minutes.

The woman who kept the inn came in with a tray and said the number out loud to a man at the door, because she had heard it and because that is what a number in a bar is for.

“*About twenty-one acres of the low meadow are not going to be mown this year.*”

“*Say the rest of that, because you have said it in four sentences in a different order and about four of us noticed, and I have kept this house eleven years and I have not been wrong about a number in it in eleven years.*”

“*He said twenty acres. He said the meadow is about thirty-one. I said twenty-one, because thirty-one less ten is twenty-one, and about four of us had it written and about nine had it in their heads. A number that is in a head is the worst of the two.*”

“*Then say it the way he said it, and do not do it again. In about nine days there will be a man in this town who says the low meadow lost twenty-one acres. There will be a man in another village who says it lost about a third of it. Both of them will be repeating a woman who was carrying a tray.*”

Nobody in that parlour thanked anybody.

---

And then the boy of fourteen was sent for, and nobody in the room had anything to give him.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Iven Ashcombe. “*Because you have been in this county eighteen days and you have said out loud in four rooms that a number is a thing a man can be checked against, and about four of us have been asking, in that order, what a man is checked against. So I have sent for the only person in this hundred who walks a line, and he is fourteen, and he writes a sheet in chalk for a penny, and I would like everybody in this room to be quiet while he is told the question.*”

“*Say the question first,*” said Toby Henshaw, “*because I have been asked to write a thing five times in my life and I have said I did not know what to do with it four times, and I am not going to be the fifth until I know what the thing is.*”

“*Can thirty-one acres be walked.*”

“*Then I want a line. I have got about nine feet of knotted line in my bag from the Saturday and I can walk a straight one and I can walk a crooked one, and I cannot do a circle and I cannot do a corner, and about nine people know that and about nine do not.*”

Nobody in that parlour thanked anybody.

“*Then the answer is no,*” said Iven Ashcombe, “*and about four of us have just heard the true of a thing about a number that came out of a mouth this morning, and the true of it is a boy of fourteen with nine feet of knotted line and a bad light, and the bad light is the only part of it we could have got round.*”

“*Say the rest of that, and say what happens to a figure of twenty-one acres on a fen where no person may be sent for about a field,*” said Ilyan.

“*That is your question and not mine, and I have been in this county eighteen days and I have put a hand on four walls in it, and I am not putting a fifth one on because I have run out of rooms to put it in.*”

Nobody in that parlour thanked anybody and nobody in that parlour was sorry for anybody.

Then the man who could have checked the number said out loud why he was not going to check it, and about nine people heard it.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Iven Ashcombe to him, at about the eleventh hour, with the fire gone down. “*Because you have been in this room for two hours and you have not once walked to the north end of that meadow, and there are about four hundred yards of it from this door, and the water is off it, and about four people have wondered about that and about nine have not.*”

“*I have a leg that stopped working on a common yesterday at the ninth hour. A woman of about thirty walked me four hundred yards of standing water. I am not walking four hundred yards of dry ground this morning to be the man who has seen it.*”

“*Then say the rest of that, because I have not asked you a question about a thing in this county since Monday, and I would like to know what the twenty-one is doing in your head.*”

“*It is the truest figure anybody has given me about that meadow. I have written nothing down. I have said it out loud in about four rooms in two days. I know what a number does at a step and I know what a number does on a bar, and I do not know what a number does in a parlour with a fire in it, and I found out this morning that it is that somebody carries a tray and says it back in a different order.*”

“*And there is nothing on this earth that makes a number safe once it is out of a mouth. The nine lines on my board are about a different thing. I put them up because a man can be asked what he remembers. A number cannot be asked anything at all.*”

Nobody in that parlour thanked anybody.

The fever was forty-one weeks and two days old. A man of about fifty with a holding and a seat in the hundred asked in a parlour over a bar the one question he had wanted to ask for nine years, and was told the answer in a number.

The number was about twenty-one acres of a low meadow of about thirty-one that are not going to be mown this year. The other ten acres are standing hay, and it takes a fortnight with a scythe and about nine men, and the first of it has to come down on the morrow, which is the first day of the hundred and fourth week, a Tuesday.

Nobody in that room could check the figure. The one man who could have it says out loud that he is not going to walk four hundred yards to look at it before the hour of ten, and that the reason is that if he puts his hand in that meadow and it is under, then about nine people in this hundred will have it from him and about nine will not, and there is no form in the county of Kell that lets anybody ask him about it afterwards.

And a boy of fourteen was sent for, because a number is a thing a man can be checked against, and he has about nine feet of knotted line, and nobody in the room had a line to give him, and thirty-one acres is a figure that cannot be walked by anybody in the county of Kell with what there is.

And the woman who keeps that house said the same number wrong fourteen minutes after a man of fifty-eight said it right, and both of those are a number in a bar, and neither of them can be taken back.

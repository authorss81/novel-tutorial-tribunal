# Chapter 593: The Seventh And Last Day Of The Hundred And Sixth Week, And A Monday, And On Which The First Move Of A Settlement In That Hundred Is Made At About The Seventh Hour By A Woman Of Twenty-Eight With A Daughter Of Six On Her Hip And Nobody Has Asked Her To, And On Which Eleven Tents Go Up Along The Top Of A Rise About Nine Hundred Yards Long In Two Hundred And Twenty Feet Of Ground That Belongs To Nobody, And On Which A Water-Clerk Says Out Loud That He Wrote That Figure On The Rising Morning Before He Wrote It And Knew It Would Be Useless In The Same Breath, And On Which The Woman Who Put The Tents Up Says Out Loud Herself That Twenty Feet A Tent Is A Tent And A Fire And A Churn And A Line And Was Never A Figure For Fifty-Eight People, And On Which A Man Of Thirty-One Asks A Room For A Number Of Feet That Fifty-Eight People Need And Is Told On The Ground That Nobody In That Hundred Knows It And That In About Four Days About Nine Mouths Will Be Measuring Children, And On Which Nothing Comes Down

Seventh and last day of the hundred and sixth week. His three hundred and forty-third morning. One hundred and ninety-three days after the settlement. The fever forty-four weeks and two days. One hundred and forty-seven days since the division. Ninety-three days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

The day before was the sixth day of the hundred and sixth week. The morrow is the first day of the hundred and seventh week.

---

The first thing a settlement does in that hundred was done at about the seventh hour on the Monday by a woman of twenty-eight with a daughter of six on her hip, and it was done four miles out across a plank bridge that is crossable at the seventh hour and not at any other.

Nobody in that hundred had asked her to.

---

About nine people had been out to that rise in twenty-seven days and had seen the peg holes and the fire pits and the churn hoop and the boat with her pole across her.

She had put eleven tents up on it before the light, along the top of it, in one row. There was a daughter of six asleep across her hip the whole time, and a boy of nineteen with a pole who had carried the last of it four miles in the flat and had said nothing.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Iven Ashcombe, who had come out across the bridge at about the eighth hour with no slate under his arm and about nine others behind him. “*Because a woman has put eleven tents on a common that belongs to nobody and there is no form in the county of Kell that says she may or may not, and about nine of us have walked out here to look at a thing that was a strip of wet grass at the seventh hour.*”

“*I am standing in front of eleven tents,*” said the woman of twenty-eight. “*On the square at the right-hand end of the low common’s line on that wall is fifty-eight, and there has been nothing on that rise for twenty-seven days.*”

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Iven Ashcombe, “*because a number on a wall is not a place and you have a daughter on your hip.*”

“*She has not had a floor to sleep on since the second day after the rising morning,*” said the woman of twenty-eight.

Nobody on that common thanked anybody.

---

Then somebody said the thing that was wrong with it, and it was said by the woman who had done it, and that is the part about nine people in that hundred repeated on the Tuesday.

“*Then say the rest of that and say how long that row is,*” said Iven Ashcombe.

“*Two hundred and twenty feet,*” said the woman of twenty-eight. “*And it is not mine. A man of fifty-eight wrote it in a square on that wall on the rising morning with the day on it, and he said out loud before he wrote it that it was a figure about a width of ground and a number of tents and not about people, and that he knew it would be useless in the same breath.*”

“*Then say the rest of that and say where the number came from,*” said Aldis Crane, who had come out across the bridge with a skep and no slate.

“*About nine hundred yards of the top of that rise is the edge a tent can stand on, and the rest of that common is under water in lines. Twenty feet a tent, for the tent and the fire and the churn and the line. He measured it with his own feet against two gate-posts he knows the distance of. Twenty feet times eleven tents is two hundred and twenty feet.*”

About four people on that common said something at once.

“*Then say the rest of that and do not be sorry for me,*” said Iven Ashcombe. “*I wrote that figure and I said out loud that I knew it would be useless. I have had twenty-seven days to find out how useless, and this is how useless it is.*”

“*Say the rest of it,*” said Aldis Crane.

“*A woman has picked it up off a wall and used it as a place for fifty-eight people,*” said Iven Ashcombe, “*and it is the wrong figure by every count anybody in this hundred can make about it.*”

“*It is the wrong figure by mine,*” said the woman of twenty-eight. “*Twenty feet is a tent and a fire and a churn and a line. It was never a figure for fifty-eight people and it is not a figure for fifty-eight people now.*”

Nobody on that common said anything.

“*Then say the rest of that and say who you will not have it made into one by,*” said Hoare Ryley.

“*Not by a man with a barrow, and not by a stranger, and not by me,*” said the woman of twenty-eight. “*And I will say that again on a Friday in front of about three hundred people if anybody in this hundred writes it down as though it were.*”

Nobody on that common said anything.

---

And then a man of thirty-one did the thing he has done in this county since the first week of it, and it was the worst version of it he has done.

He had been in that county thirty-nine days. He had been priced at four pounds eleven shillings and threepence by a woman who keeps four beds and had been given a parcel instead of her name. He had said four things at a step on Saturday in about nine seconds and about nine mouths in that hundred had them by the fourth hour. He had said on a headland on Sunday that he was not going to take the next number out of anybody’s hand.

He wanted the place to be a number that anybody could check, because that was the only thing he had ever been any good for.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Hoare Ryley, who had come out across the bridge at about the eighth hour with a barrow and had not spoken. “*Because you have been in this county thirty-nine days and you have said one useful thing out loud in two days, and it was that you would not take the next number out of anybody’s hand, and about nine of us have carried that about four miles behind us.*”

“*I am standing in front of two hundred and twenty feet of a rise and fifty-eight people on it, and neither of those is a figure anybody can be checked against.*”

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Hoare Ryley, “*and do not say the rest of it in a number, because I have been cutting ditch in this district for thirty years and about nine of us know what happens when a man measures people.*”

About four seconds went past.

“*Then how is it fixed,*” said Ilyan. “*A settlement that has a place in it has to say how much of it. Not a number of tents. A number of feet that fifty-eight people need on a rise that is about eleven feet wide and about nine hundred yards long, and then about nine of us can walk it with a line in about four seconds and check it, and about nine of us who are not of this hundred can be shown it.*”

Nobody on that common said anything.

“*Then say what fifty-eight people need,*” said the woman of twenty-eight, “*and say it out loud and do not be sorry for me.*”

Nobody on that common thanked anybody.

“*I cannot say it and neither can you and neither can he,*” said the woman of twenty-eight. “*You have never stood on that rise with me at the seventh hour. I have stood on it twice. Fifty-eight people are not a number of feet.*”

Nobody on that common said anything.

“*Then say the rest of that and say what twenty feet is,*” said Iven Ashcombe, “*because about nine of us have it as a number of people and it is not one.*”

“*A tent and a fire and a churn and a line, and it was never anything else,*” said the woman of twenty-eight. “*If you write a number of feet for fifty-eight people on a sheet in a room in that town, then in about four days about nine mouths in this hundred will have a figure of it.*”

“*Then say the rest of that and do not be sorry for anybody,*” said Hoare Ryley.

“*And about nine of those nine will be measuring children,*” said the woman of twenty-eight.

About four people on that common said something at once and about nine did not.

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Iven Ashcombe. “*And say what is going to be written instead. The first thing a settlement does here has been done and it has been done wrong, and about nine of us are standing on the wrong thing.*”

“*Then say that last part,*” said Aldis Crane.

“*There is no form anywhere in the county of Kell that lets us take eleven tents down again in about four seconds,*” said Iven Ashcombe.

---

So nothing came down.

And on the Monday afternoon, in a room over a stable, a sheet of paper about four feet wide was written on with a cracked steel pen, and there was nothing at the top of it, and it was the first thing anybody had written anywhere in that hundred for eleven weeks.

The woman of twenty-eight put two hundred and twenty feet on it in her own hand. The man who had written it in the square said out loud that two numbers were going on that sheet and that they were not the same number and that nobody was to be told which of them was the truer, because he did not know.

Nobody in that room thanked anybody.

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Aldis Crane, “*because about nine of us have just watched four hands go into a sheet and I have four of them in that hundred and I would like to know whose they are.*”

“*Mine,* said Hoare Ryley, *the ditch and the rate and the six acres with nothing in the square.*”

“*Mine,* said the woman of about fifty who keeps four beds. “*The two acres and the sallow in the ditch behind them.*”

Nobody in that room said anything.

“*Say the rest of that and say whether there is a name at the foot of it,*” said Aldis Crane.

“*There is not,*” said the woman of about fifty who keeps four beds, “*and there is not going to be. A paper with my name at the top of it in this county is a paper nine people can be argued with, and I have said that out loud twice in a fortnight, and I am not starting on a Wednesday.*”

Nobody in that room said anything.

---

And a load of wet hay came off the low meadow on the Monday afternoon and went down the lane and into a barn that already had one in it, because there was nowhere else, and nobody had asked the man whose barn it was and about nine people in that hundred said out loud that there was nowhere else.

The fever was forty-four weeks and two days old. A woman of twenty-eight with a daughter of six on her hip put eleven tents on the top of a rise about nine hundred yards long and about eleven feet wide before the light on the Monday. Nobody in that hundred had asked her to, and there is no form in the county of Kell that says she may or may not.

The first thing it did was wrong. It took two hundred and twenty feet off the square at the end of the low common’s line and used them as a place. Twenty feet a tent is a tent and a fire and a churn and a line and was never a figure for fifty-eight people, and the woman who used it said so out loud herself in about nine people’s hearing before anybody else got near it.

And a man of thirty-one asked out loud for a number of feet that fifty-eight people needed, and was told on the ground by a person who has stood on that rise twice that nobody in that hundred knows it and that about four days from then about nine mouths would be measuring children.

The top of that rise is about eleven feet wide. A foot of water went over the low side of the swell under those tents on the second day after the rising morning, and two tents were standing at the ninth hour on that day, and nobody was drowned.

And the eleven tents stayed up, and two numbers went onto a sheet four feet wide in a room over a stable with nothing at the top of it, and they were not the same number, and nobody was told which of them was the truer, and about nine people in that hundred read both of them over the next two days and got them the wrong way round.

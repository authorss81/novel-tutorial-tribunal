# Chapter 585: The Sixth Day Of The Hundred And Fifth Week, And A Sunday, And On Which There Is No Room And No Slate And No Bar And A Man Of Thirty-One Walks About Four Miles Out Of A Town Of About Eleven Hundred People With A Bad Leg, And On Which He Puts His Hand Flat Through A Space Between Two Blocks Under A Gate With Three Boards In It And Feels Moving Water On The Other Side Of His Wrist, And On Which He Looks At A Rise About Nine Hundred Yards Long That Had Fifty-Eight People On It Nineteen Days Before, And On Which He Does Not Write Down One Word Of What He Saw

Sixth day of the hundred and fifth week. His three hundred and thirty-fifth morning. One hundred and eighty-five days after the settlement. The fever forty-three weeks and one day. One hundred and thirty-nine days since the division. Eighty-five days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

There was no room on the Sunday and no slate and no bar, and there has never been a form in the county of Kell for any of the three on any day of the week.

A man of thirty-one walked out of that town at about the seventh hour with a crust in his coat and nothing else, and the light was flat and the wind was off the flat and about eleven hundred people were in that town and about nine of them were at the north wall of the weigh-house under the lamp, reading it, because that is what they do every morning.

Fifty-eight was in the taking column of the low common’s line.

It had been there for eighteen days. It was the number a woman of twenty-eight had asked for out loud on a plank bridge and a water-clerk had written in about nine seconds with no name above it and no name below it, and there is no copy of anything anywhere in this county that says what fifty-eight is a figure of.

He went past the wall without stopping, and that was the first thing he did on that Sunday that he thought about afterwards.

---

The gate is on the main dyke and it is a walk and not a ride.

It is about six feet of frame set in a sill of limestone blocks, and there are six boards that go into it and there are three boards in it. Three boards is a foot below the tally line. Six boards is the line. Nobody has told him that; he worked it out in the first week of this year from a thing a grazier said in a room and a thing a water-clerk said at a step, and he has carried the two figures since without anybody checking them together.

He went down to it to look at the water, because the water had been coming up the drain for three days and he wanted to see it with his own eyes and not off a wall.

Nobody was there. There is a latch on the high side of that gate and the latch has not been lifted in about nine days.

The frame is tarred oak and the tar has gone soft in about two places. The sill under the low side of it, where the blocks meet, has a space between them about as wide as two fingers, and sallow has grown up through it from the low side and matted across the gap and gone hard.

He put his hand through it flat, up to where the wood turns up.

There was moving water on the other side of his wrist.

That is the whole of what he saw at the gate, and it is not a figure and it is not a story.

The water on the low side was at the top of its own bank about four feet from the low post of the frame. The water in the channel on the high side was at the top of the three boards that are in it. Those are not the same height and he stood there long enough to see that they are not the same height twice, once when the wind moved the channel and once when it did not.

Above the frame on the high side there were two lines on the timber. The lower one was wet and dark and about level with the top of the boards. The upper one was pale and dry and about the width of two fingers above it.

He did not know what the upper one was. He looked at it for about nine seconds and then he looked at it for about nine seconds more and he could not get it, and there is no form anywhere in this hundred that says a man may ask about a mark on a gate.

---

He came back off the dyke side of the gate and up the lane a quarter of a mile, because the ditch that takes the meadow off the pasture runs past the back of that gate and there is nowhere else to see what a foot of sallow does to a ditch.

It was open for about nine yards at the near end of it. The sallow had been taken out of that nine yards with an axe and a spade and heaped on the bank on the top side in a row about as long as a cart, and the water was running in the bottom of the cut about two inches deep and moving, and it was clear water on the top of the mud and not the colour it had been in the first week of this year.

Then the ditch ran nine yards and the sallow began again, standing in it, black and wet and about a foot high, and the water had gone back into it and stopped.

He stood in the lane above the second nine yards for long enough to see that the ditch does not take the water at the near end and does take it at the far end, and that both of those are the same ditch, and that the sallow in between is the reason and not the fault of anybody.

There is no form anywhere in the county of Kell that says a man may not stand in a lane and look at a ditch, and about nine people in that hundred have looked at that ditch this week and about nine have counted the loads.

Then he went along the top of the dyke to the plank bridge, which is crossable at the seventh hour and not at any other, and across it, and out onto the rise.

The rise is about nine hundred yards long and the top of it is about eleven feet wide, and it belongs to nobody.

Eleven tents had been standing on the top of it on the morning the water came over the tally stone, with fifty-eight people on it. The water went over it on the second day after that morning, and two tents were still standing at the ninth hour on that day, and nobody was drowned.

There was nothing on it on the Sunday.

Not eleven tents and not two. Nothing. The top of the rise was bare from the south end to the north end and about eleven feet wide the whole way, and the grass on it had been grazed flat in strips where the beasts had been and had not come back in nineteen days.

There were eleven lines of peg holes in a straight row and no poles in them. There were eleven fire pits in a second row and none of them had a stone round it. There was a churn hoop in the mud about the middle of the row and the stave it had been on was gone. There was a boat drawn up at the north end with her bottom out of the water and her pole lying across her.

And there was no sack under the churn, because there was no churn.

Nobody on that common thanked anybody, and about four people were on it, and three of them were there because they had been told by a boy of fourteen at a wall that there was nothing on it.

Nobody in that place said anything.

---

He was on his feet for about four hours and then he sat down on the top of the rise with his bad leg out in front of him in the wet grass, and a man of about sixty with a shepherd’s crook came up the flat from the north, stopped about nine feet off, and stood there.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Perrin Voss. “*Because you are the third person to come out here in about nine days and I have read a wall every morning for twenty-nine days, and I would like to know what there is to look at before you tell me there is nothing to look at.*”

“*Eleven lines of peg holes. Eleven fire pits. A churn hoop. A boat with her pole across her. No sack under the churn because there is no churn. Nothing on the top of the rise from end to end.*”

“*Then say the rest of it, and say how many, because a number is a thing a man can be checked against and about nine of us have been saying that at a wall for a fortnight.*”

“*I cannot give you a number. Fifty-eight is on that wall and fifty-eight is not standing here, and those are two things and only one of them is about the ground I am standing on, and nobody in this county can be sent for about the difference.*”

Nobody on that common said anything.

“*Say the rest of that,*” said Perrin Voss, “*and then say whether you are going to write it down, and I am asking because you have said out loud in this county that you would not put a hand on a fifth thing, and you said it to a man in a yard on Tuesday, and about nine of us heard you.*”

“*I am not going to write it down anywhere.*”

“*Then say why, and do not say because I asked you not to.*”

“*Because a stranger’s chalk on that wall is a fifth hand, and I said in that yard on Tuesday that I would put no more of them on, and about nine of us at that bar heard me say it. A figure that is in one head is the worst of the two, and I know that because a woman with a tray said a number back wrong in a parlour about eleven days ago and it went up about nine streets in her shape and not mine.*”

Nobody on that common thanked anybody and nobody on that common was sorry for anybody.

---

He came back across the bridge at about the fourth hour and along the dyke and into the town, and his leg had gone again and stayed gone.

On the way back in, at the corner where the smithy stands, there was a boy of fourteen with a rag in his hand doing nothing again, because there is nothing on that end wall to rub out on a Sunday night either.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Toby Henshaw. “*Because you have come back in off the flat with about four miles on a leg and you have not been at a wall or a bar or a door, and about four of us have watched you go out this morning from the corner and about nine have not.*”

“*I have been at a gate and on a rise and about nine yards of a ditch.*”

“*Then say what is on the rise, because I have told a man of about fifty that there is nothing on it and he will tell a man of about thirty-four and about nine of us will have it by the ninth hour, and I told him because you told me last Monday that nothing cannot be rubbed out and about nine of us heard that.*”

“*Say the rest of that. I told you on Monday what a rag is for. I have not told you what is on the rise and I am not going to, and the reason is not pride and you would want the reason.*”

“*Then say the reason.*”

“*Because about nine people in this town would have it by the ninth hour in whatever mouth I said it in, and I have been in this county thirty-one days and every one of those nine has been a worse position for somebody than it was the day before. There is no column in that county for the difference and I am tired of being the thing that is easier to repeat.*”

He went past the north wall of the weigh-house at about the seventh hour of the evening. The lamp was out. About nine people were standing at it anyway, because it is about two hundred and thirty years old that they stand there, and there is no form anywhere in the county of Kell that says what they are for.

The second column of the low common’s line said fifty-eight.

Nobody had said anything to put anything else there. The sack that had held the sheet with the figure and the day on it and nothing at the top of it and nothing at the bottom of it was not on this ground and was not in this town, and there is no copy of it in the county of Kell, and the water-clerk has never seen it, and there are nine lines on a board outside his own door that say no person in this hundred is to be asked what they remember, including his wife.

The fever was forty-three weeks and one day old. A man of thirty-one walked about four miles out of a town of about eleven hundred people on a Sunday with his bad leg, past a gate with three boards in it, and across a rise about nine hundred yards long and about eleven feet wide that had held fifty-eight people nineteen days before.

What he saw with his own eyes was eleven lines of peg holes, eleven fire pits, a churn hoop, a boat with her pole across her, and bare grass the length of a rise that belongs to nobody.

And a figure of fifty-eight is not a figure of tents, and a licence said *until the water turns* is not a place, and there is no form in the county of Kell that says where a person goes when the ground they were on stops being any use, and nobody has said the number out loud anywhere in this hundred except on a wall, in a column that is for what goes on a thing.

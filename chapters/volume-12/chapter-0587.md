# Chapter 587: The First Day Of The Hundred And Sixth Week, And A Tuesday, And On Which The Water Comes Back To The Tally Stone On The Day After The Day A Water-Clerk Of Fifty-Eight Said It Would And Stands At The Cut In It For About Four Hours And Does Not Go Over The Cut, And On Which The Top Of The Three Boards In The Gate On The Main Dyke Goes Under And About Four Feet On The Other Side Of That Frame A Ditch Is Wet To The Top Of Its Bank, And On Which A Man Of Thirty-One Says Out Loud In About Nine People’s Hearing Where That Water Came From And A Man With A Barrow Takes It Out Of His Hand In About Four Seconds

First day of the hundred and sixth week. His three hundred and thirty-seventh morning. One hundred and eighty-seven days after the settlement. The fever forty-three weeks and three days. One hundred and forty-one days since the division. Eighty-seven days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

The day before was the seventh and last day of the hundred and fifth week.

---

The water came back to the tally stone on the Tuesday.

It was to be on the Monday. A water-clerk of fifty-eight has watched that block of limestone for nineteen years and he said so at the seventh hour with a rod in his hand, in front of about nine people at the edge of the channel, and there is no form in the county of Kell that says a man is to be right about a river.

It came at about the ninth hour instead of the seventh, and it came to the cut in the stone and stood there, and it did not go over the cut.

The stone is a block of limestone with a line cut round it at the level of a full water, put there about two hundred and thirty years ago by a man whose name is on it and whose name is still legible. On the morning of the rising the water went over that line at about the seventh hour and every season in a hundred of about thirty men lapsed on that morning and was re-let on that morning, and the clerk held his hand flat on the block for the length of a count of five and then wrote the day at the foot of a sheet of parchment two men long.

The water has not been over that line since. It came back to it on this morning and stood at it and did not cross it.

Nobody on that bank thanked anybody.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said the woman with the tray, who had come down with about nine others because a water-clerk with a rod in his hand on a bank at the seventh hour is the only thing that happens in that town at the seventh hour. “*Because about nine of us have been standing here since the seventh hour and a man of fifty-eight has put a rod into that channel twice and taken it out and put it in again, and I would like to know what he is looking for and why he has not said so.*”

“*I am looking for whether it is over the cut. It is not over the cut. It is at the cut and it is about the width of a thumbnail under it and it has been since the ninth hour.*”

“*Then say the rest of that, and say whether that is a rising.*”

“*It is the water coming back to the line. Whether it is the rising is a thing nobody in this hundred can tell you this morning.*”

“*Then say what a rising is,*” said the woman with the tray, “*because you are the only man in this county who can be asked anything and about nine of us have been standing on this bank for an hour working it out between us and getting two answers.*”

“*A rising is the day the water is over that cut. The water has not been over that cut since the morning of the rising. One crossing makes a year, and we have had one.*”

“*Then say the rest of it and do not leave it there.*”

“*I said the Monday and I am a day out. I am saying so here at the seventh hour before anybody has worked it out, because there is nothing in this county to say it in except that sheet, and that sheet is for a crossing and not for a man being wrong.*”

“*Say the rest of that,*” said Iven Ashcombe. “*Because I have a wall and no keeper, and about four people have now asked me to write this down and about nine have told me not to.*”

“*Then you have about four against nine and you are the water-clerk and I have no rule about that. Write nothing.*”

About four people on that bank said something at once and about nine did not.

---

Then the water did about four hours of work and told nobody what it meant.

At about the ninth hour the top of the three boards in the gate on the main dyke went under.

The clerk walked down there with about nine people at about the tenth hour and they stood on the high side of that gate and looked at it, and the channel on the high side was full to the top of the boards, which is a foot below the tally line, and it was still running over the top of them in a sheet about as thick as a finger.

And on the low side of that frame, about four feet from the low post, a ditch was wet to the top of its bank.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Iven Ashcombe, to about nine people on the high side of a gate. “*Because that gate has three boards in it and the channel is full over them, and the ditch on the other side of it is wet to the top of its bank, and about four of us have just worked out that the two are not connected and about nine have not.*”

“*Say the rest of that and say which of them is a thing a man can be checked against,*” said Hoare Ryley, who had come down the dyke from the sally bed with mud to the knee and a barrow handle in his hand.

“*The level is a thing a man can be checked against. I put a rod in it twice. The ditch is a thing a man can be checked against too, and about four of us can see it and about nine cannot, and I do not know what feeds that ditch and neither does anybody else standing here.*”

Nobody at that gate thanked anybody.

---

Then a man of thirty-one said the thing he had found with his hand on the Sunday, out loud, in about nine people’s hearing, and it was the wrong water.

He had been in that county thirty-three days. He had said on Monday in a parlour that his year cost four pounds eleven shillings and threepence and he had been told that by a woman who keeps four beds. He had said on Friday in a market that he was standing behind the harvest and not behind six acres. He had said on Saturday at a wall that he would put no hand on a fifth thing and then had put one on and had it taken off in about four seconds.

He wanted to see the water do a thing, because about nine days ago he had put his hand flat through a space between two limestone blocks under that frame and had felt moving water on the other side of his wrist.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Iven Ashcombe, “*Because about nine of us are on the high side of a gate and you have a hand in a hole in it, and about four of us have been watching you do that for about four seconds.*”

“*I am standing in front of this gate. There is a space between the sill blocks on the low side about as wide as two fingers and sallow has grown up through it and gone hard, and on Sunday I put my hand through it flat and there was moving water on the other side of my wrist.*”

“*Then say the rest of it, and say what you think that means, because about nine people in this hundred have now had a stranger tell them a thing about water with his hand in it and about four of them have repeated it already.*”

“*I think that is where that ditch is coming from. I think the gate is not holding that water off and I think three boards are not holding it off either, because that is not what it is for.*”

About four seconds went past.

“*Say the rest of that,*” said Hoare Ryley, “*because I have cut ditch in this district for about thirty years and I have a barrow in my hand and about nine of us are standing on a bank, and you have just said a true thing about a hole and then a wrong thing about what the hole does, and I would rather say so here than have it said at a wall on Friday.*”

“*Say it, and say it plain, and do not tell me I am wrong twice.*”

“*That hole is not the meadow drain. That hole is a gap in a sill and every sill on this dyke has had gaps in it since my father’s time and the sallow goes through all of them and they have all gone hard.*”

“*Then say where that ditch is fed from, and say it in a way that about nine people can be checked against.*”

“*Off the meadow, and off the meadow over the pasture and down the old line, and the old line is about two feet higher than that gate and it does not touch that gate. That ditch fills when the meadow fills. It filled on the second day after the rising morning off the meadow and not through that gate, and I have said that out loud twice and about nine of us did not hear me the first time.*”

Nobody at that gate thanked anybody.

“*Then say the rest of that and do not be sorry for me,*” said Ilyan, “*because I put my hand in a hole on Sunday and have carried it four days and I have just said out loud in about nine people’s hearing what I thought it meant, and you have told me the true of it, and about four people have my shape of it already and they are wrong.*”

“*I know they are. I would rather have the wrong shape of it said out loud at a gate on a Tuesday than the right one said at a wall on a Friday, and about nine of us have been asking you for a number to look at for thirty-three days and you have not given us one and now you have given us one that a man with a barrow can pull out of your hand in four seconds.*”

Nobody at that gate said anything.

---

Then they went back up the dyke and about nine of them stopped at the ditch that runs past the back of the gate, the one a man of thirty-three has been clearing a foot of sallow out of with a barrow since the Tuesday of the hundred and fourth week.

Nine yards of it were open and clear and the water was running in the bottom about two inches deep and moving. Then it ran nine yards and the sallow began again, standing in it and black and wet and about a foot high, and the water stopped dead where the sallow began.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Hoare Ryley to the man of thirty-one, “*because you have walked up this dyke twice in four days and about nine of us have watched you look at a hole in a sill, and I would like you to look at that instead and tell me which of the two you think is doing more.*”

“*Say the rest of that and do not ask me to hold a plank about it.*”

“*Nine yards of open ditch in about a hundred and forty, and about nine yards a day, and about nine days left in it, and he is doing it alone with a barrow and a spade and an axe because nobody is paying the twelve shillings and sixpence a year that pays for it. That ditch fills in about two years when it is cut and in about one when it is not, and nobody in this hundred has worked out which of those is the year that matters to the common.*”

Nobody on that dyke thanked anybody.

The water went down off the cut at about the second hour and it did not go over it.

It was the last day of the fortnight. The hay of this district was to come down on the first day of the hundred and fourth week, a Tuesday, and it was now the first day of the hundred and sixth week, and about six of ten acres of the low meadow were down with about four standing.

Nobody on that meadow thanked anybody.

The fever was forty-three weeks and three days old. A man of thirty-one had been in that county thirty-three days and had asked a man of fifty-eight about the river and been told he was a day out of it in front of about nine people.

The water came back to the tally stone and stood at the cut in it and did not go over the cut, and the top of the three boards in the gate went under, and the channel ran over them, and about four feet on the other side of that frame a ditch was wet to the top of its bank.

And two men who had both put their hands on that gate did not agree about where that water came from, and the water had been there for about four hours and had told neither of them.

And at about the seventh hour the line cut round the block of limestone was wet all the way round, and by the ninth hour it was drying at the ends, and it was not over.

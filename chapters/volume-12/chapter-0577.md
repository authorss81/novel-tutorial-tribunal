# Chapter 577: The Fifth Day Of The Hundred And Fourth Week, And A Saturday, And On Which A Man Of About Sixty-Two Holds The Slate At The Sixth Hour And Reads A Wall While About Nine People Who Have Read Six Challenges Before He Begins Say Out Loud That The Reading Of One Line About Hay Is Wrong, And On Which A Woman Of Thirty-Eight Says Her Parcel Out Loud In Front Of About Nine People And Gets It Written Against Her Own Challenge, And On Which A List That A District Reads Every Morning Gets Nine Parcels Of Ground Put On It By Nine People Who Own Them

Fifth day of the hundred and fourth week. His three hundred and twenty-seventh morning. One hundred and seventy-seven days after the settlement. The fever forty-two weeks and no days. One hundred and thirty-one days since the division. Seventy-seven days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

The reading is at the sixth hour on a Saturday morning, under the lamp on the bracket, with the door of the weigh-house open and the street behind it. Whoever holds the slate reads the north wall out loud. About nine people stand under it. About four more stand in the street where they can hear.

On this Saturday the man with the slate was Perrin Voss. He was about sixty-two. He kept a shepherd’s crook in his hand because he had come straight off the top side. He had read that wall out loud once before, for twenty minutes, and had been corrected twice by two different men in the street, and he had said out loud afterwards that he had wanted for nineteen years to ask whether it was right.

About nine people had read the six challenges before he began. They stood at the bottom of the sheet, under everything else, in the clerk’s hand with the day on each. Five of the six had no parcel against them. The sixth had been asked for on a Tuesday in a lane, twice walked, and the asking was for a parcel to be put against it at this reading, where about nine people can be checked against each other.

Nobody in that building thanked anybody.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Iven Ashcombe. He stood to one side of the lamp with his hands behind him. He had not held the slate for two readings. “*Because you have held it before and been corrected twice, and about nine people are at that wall, and six lines under a list of eleven are about a thing this hundred is about to do.*”

“*I am standing in front of two men long of parchment with three columns and no column for a person, and six lines under it that are not the keeper’s and not a stranger’s.*”

“*Say the rest of that, because about nine people read those six before the sixth hour and about four of them have a slate of their own, and I would like the reading to begin with what they are.*”

“*They are six sayings that the reading was wrong about six men’s ground. Five of the five that have no parcel say hay where the ground says otherwise. The sixth says the same and will have a parcel before the seventh hour if the woman who asked for it is in this room.*”

Nobody in that weigh-house thanked anybody.

---

Ilyan stood at the side with his bad leg straight out along the bench. He had been in that county twenty-three days. He had put a hand on four walls in it and had said out loud in three rooms that he would put no fifth one on. He had under two pounds in a purse and no bed of his own and a leg that had stopped working on a common six days before.

He wanted the reading to be a thing a man can be checked against. He had wanted it since the first Saturday he had stood under that lamp. He had written four lines about a room on a wall to that end, and a woman of thirty-eight had written five lines on the back of his own paper to the same end, and both sets were now on walls or under a bar where about nine people read them every morning.

His mistake that morning was an old one and he made it before the reading began. He carried a figure from a parlour into a weigh-house and said it to a man at the door as though a parlour and a weigh-house were the same room. The figure was twenty-one. It was about twenty-one acres of a low meadow of about thirty-one that are not going to be mown this year. A woman carrying a tray had said it back wrong in the parlour, and he said it again at the door to put it right, and about four people heard the second saying and not the first.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said the man at the door. He was about fifty and he had a holding and a seat and a skep behind his own gate. “*Because you have said twenty-one to me twice and I said it once to a clerk, and about nine people in this town now have it in three orders.*”

“*I am standing in front of a number I carried out of your parlour. I said it to be useful and I have said numbers to be useful before and had a person be the worse for it. There is no column anywhere in this county for the difference.*”

“*Then say the rest of that, and do not say it twice more, because about nine people in this room are about to hear a line read out about hay and every one of them has a scythe or knows a man who has.*”

Nobody at that door thanked anybody.

---

The reading began at the sixth hour. Perrin Voss read the eleven lines first, parcel and acres and taking and the figure in the square, in that order. Every season in that hundred had gone and been re-let eleven days before, on the rising, and every square had a figure in it except one. The one was six acres of the sally bed on the top side. He read the line and then he read the square.

“*Nothing in it,*” he said. “*For the first time in about two hundred and thirty years.*”

About nine people in that room heard him say it and about four wrote nothing down.

Then he read the four lines about how many men fill the chairs in a room. A man of thirty-four in the hundred had put them on the wall. One man fills eleven of about thirty seats. About nine people in that room heard those four lines every morning and about four of them believed the first half and about nine believed the second and none believed both.

Then he read the eleven-line notice on the end wall of the smithy, as it stood copied onto the north wall under the clerk’s hand. About thirty names in chalk that nobody asked for, with a stroke beside the ones that are not of the hundred. A boy of fourteen had written them for a penny on a Friday night. He had told a man of fifty-eight what the stroke means. It means that they came.

Then he came to the six challenges. He read all six, one after the other, with the day on each. Five of them named no parcel. The sixth named none yet either, and the woman who had asked for one was in the room, with no basket, having walked down a lane once and not twice.

The first of the six said that a line reading hay, mown was wrong. The second said the same of another line. The third and the fourth said the same. The fifth said the same. The sixth said the same, and the hand was the same clerk’s hand on all six, with the day on each.

“*Hay, mown,*” read Perrin Voss, off the sheet, off the second column of a line four from the top. “*Four acres. Top side.*”

About nine people in that room said out loud, at once and in about nine different ways, that the reading was wrong.

Nobody in that weigh-house thanked anybody and nobody in that weigh-house was sorry for anybody.

---

“*Say the rest of that,*” said Iven Ashcombe. He had stepped forward to the lamp. “*Because about nine people have just said a reading is wrong, and I wrote down six such sayings last Saturday with the day on each, and those six are on that wall, and this is the first time the saying and the reading have met in the same hour.*”

“*The line says hay, mown,*” said Perrin Voss. “*I have read what is on that sheet. About nine people tell me what is on it is wrong. I have been corrected twice in this room and I am asking to be corrected a third time, in front of about nine people, with a parcel.*”

“*Then say which parcel,*” said a woman of thirty-eight. She stepped up to the lamp. She had four acres on the top side. She had held them until a man came round with a purse in the middle of April and paid the rate on them and the penny on them. She had stood up in this building seven days before and said the second column was wrong about her ground. “*Because I walked up a lane twice on Tuesday to ask for this, and I asked for it at the sixth hour on a Saturday where about nine people can be checked against each other, and this is that hour.*”

“*Say the parcel,*” said Iven Ashcombe. “*And about nine people will have it, and I will write it, and it will be the first line on that wall that anybody has said out loud with a parcel against it.*”

“*Four acres of the top side above the lane, on the left going up, with the willow stump at the corner. Hay, standing. Not mown in any year of the little figures going left from the square.*”

He wrote it. The chalk went against the sixth challenge, under the day, in front of about nine people. About four of them read it back before it was dry.

“*Say the rest of that,*” said Perrin Voss, “*because I have read a line wrong in front of nine people and had it put right with a parcel, and there are five more of the same behind it, and four of the five were said by men who do not know which parcels they are talking about.*”

“*Then read the next,*” said Iven Ashcombe.

He read the next. It said hay, mown on another line. About nine people said out loud that the reading was wrong about that ground too. No parcel came with the saying. The man who had said it first, seven days before, stood at the back and said out loud that he did not know which parcel was his.

“*Say the rest of that,*” said Perrin Voss, “*because I have now read two lines about hay and had both of them said wrong, and one of the two has a parcel and the other has not, and the hundred is about to mow.*”

“*The hundred is about to mow,*” said a man of about fifty from behind the lamp. He had a holding and a seat and a skep behind his gate. “*Ten acres of a low meadow of about thirty-one are standing hay. It takes a fortnight with a scythe and about nine men. It should have begun on Tuesday and it has not begun. A hay that stands past its time goes to seed. That is the thing the hundred is about to do, and six lines on that wall say the sheet is wrong about it in six places.*”

Nobody in that weigh-house thanked anybody.

---

Then the document about the thing the hundred is about to do was read out, and it was not the sheet. It was the remedy of about three hundred words with nothing at the top of it, brought up from under the oak bar by the boy of fourteen, with five lines on the back of it in a woman’s hand.

Perrin Voss read the front first, four sentences and then the rest, standing under the lamp. About nine people heard a remedy read in a weigh-house that had first been read at a market step and then at a bar on a green. No person in the county of Kell is bound by a word of it. The reading took about eleven minutes.

Then he turned the sheet over and read the five lines on the back. The fifth one said that the document is not about the water and that the ground should be put on it. A ground can be walked by a man with a line. It is what a remedy is done to.

About nine people in that room said out loud that the reading of the remedy was wrong about one thing. It was not wrong the way the sheet was wrong. It said about nine men and a fortnight and a scythe, and about a fourth of the nine in a hundred of about thirty men, and about a fourth of them one man four miles south who has not been in a room in nineteen years. About nine people in that room said that no nine men in that hundred can be got together in a fortnight, because five of the six challenges have no parcel and no man knows which ground is his to cut.

“*Say the rest of that,*” said Iven Ashcombe, “*because six lines on my wall are not a stranger’s and are not a keeper’s, and they are about the hay, and about nine people have now said the remedy is wrong about the same hay, and I have a wall and no keeper and this is the hour.*”

“*The remedy says nine men,*” said a man at the back. “*I am one of about thirty men. I do not know which parcel is mine to bring a scythe to. Five lines on that wall say hay is wrong and give no parcel. A man cannot mow a challenge.*”

Nobody in that weigh-house thanked anybody and nobody in that weigh-house was sorry for anybody.

---

Ilyan spoke once in that room, and it cost him the morning.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Iven Ashcombe to him, at about the seventh hour. “*Because you have been in this county twenty-three days and you have said out loud in four rooms that a number is a thing a man can be checked against, and about nine people in this room have just shown that a challenge with no parcel is a number no man can be checked against.*”

“*I am standing in front of six lines I did not write. Five of them name no parcel. The sixth names one now, because a woman of thirty-eight walked up a lane twice to ask for it. I wrote four lines about this room on a wall fifteen days ago and put no parcel on any of them either. That is twice now, and it is my hand both times, and it does not come off.*”

“*Then say the rest of that, and say what you will do about the other five, because about nine people in this room have looked at you while you said it.*”

“*Nothing. A man who cannot be sent for cannot go to five men’s doors and ask them which ground is theirs. I went to four doors eleven days ago with a number in my mouth and was refused at the fourth before I had finished the second sentence. I am not going to five more to mend a paper.*”

Nobody in that weigh-house thanked anybody.

His cost that morning was a small one and it stayed. A boy of fourteen with about nine feet of knotted line in his bag asked him after the reading whether thirty-one acres can be walked, and he said he did not know, and the boy said that was the first true thing a man of thirty-one had said in that room that week. About four people heard it.

---

Then the list that the district reads every morning got nine parcels put on it by nine people who own them, and it took until the ninth hour.

It began with the woman of thirty-eight. Hers was first because she had asked first. Four acres of the top side above the lane, with the willow stump at the corner, went on the wall against the sixth challenge, in the clerk’s hand, with the day on it.

Then a man of about fifty with a holding and a seat put his own ground on it. Then a man of sixty-two with a shepherd’s crook put his. Then six more, one after the other, each stepping up to the lamp, each saying a parcel out loud in front of about nine people, each having it written down with the day on it.

About nine people in that room put their own ground on that wall before the ninth hour. Five of them were men who seven days before had said the reading was wrong and had named no parcel. Two of them had never spoken in that building before. One of them was a woman of forty-one with six acres of the second close off the dyke, who put the parcel and the acres and the day on it and no name at the top of it and no name at the bottom of it either, the same as she had put on a paper under a bar three days before.

The clerk wrote all nine. The boy of fourteen read all nine back before they were dry. About nine people checked all nine against each other.

The fever was forty-two weeks and no days old. A man of about sixty-two held the slate at the sixth hour on a Saturday and read a wall while about nine people who had read six challenges before he began stood at it. He read a line about hay on four acres of the top side, and about nine people said out loud that the reading was wrong.

A woman of thirty-eight said her parcel out loud in front of about nine people and got it written against her own challenge, the first with a parcel on that wall. Then eight more people did the same, until nine parcels of ground stood on a list that a district reads every morning.

And a man of thirty-one carried a figure of twenty-one from a parlour to a weigh-house door and said it twice to put it right, and about four people heard the second saying and not the first, and there is no column anywhere in that county for the difference.

# Chapter 589: The Third Day Of The Hundred And Sixth Week, And A Thursday, And On Which About Thirty People Are In A Weigh-House Sixty Feet Long With The Door Shut For No Reason Any Of Them Can Put In A Form, And On Which A Woman Of Twenty-Eight Who Keeps The Count Of Fifty-Eight Asks In Her Own Mouth The Question A Man Asked In The Same Room A Fortnight Before, And About Four People Answer It Wrong In About Four Seconds And About Nine Do Not Answer It At All, And On Which A Man Of Thirty-One Answers It Once With A Wall And Is Told By The Woman Who Asked It That A Wall Cannot Be Asked Anything At All, And On Which A Water-Clerk Writes A Second Line Under Fifty-Eight With The Day On It And Nothing Above It And Nothing Below It

Third day of the hundred and sixth week. His three hundred and thirty-ninth morning. One hundred and eighty-nine days after the settlement. The fever forty-three weeks and five days. One hundred and forty-three days since the division. Eighty-nine days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

About thirty people were in the weigh-house on the Thursday with the door shut.

Nobody summoned them. The water had come back to the line on Tuesday morning, stood at it for about four hours, and had not gone over the cut.

About four of ten acres of the low meadow were standing on the fourth day past the fortnight. Out of a town of about eleven hundred people, about a hundred had gone out to look at one thing or the other in two days, and had come back with something to say.

The door was shut because there were thirty people in it and not nine.

About four of them had said that a thing said with the door open on a Thursday is a thing said at a market on a Friday, and about nine had said nothing about the door, and the door was shut anyway.

Nobody in that building thanked anybody.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Iven Ashcombe, standing at the north wall with his hands behind him. “*Because I have a wall and about thirty people in this room and the door is shut and nobody asked me to shut it. I have not held a slate at a reading for nineteen years and I am not going to start in a week in which my own line has a number on it that I cannot account for.*”

“*Then say what the thirty of us are here for,*” said a man of about fifty. He had a holding, a seat in the hundred, and a skep behind his own gate. “*Because about nine of us have been to the rise since Sunday and about nine have not. I would rather somebody said it than that we all found it out on a Saturday.*”

“*Then say it, and say it in about nine sentences, and do not say what you think about it at the end.*”

“*I have been to the rise. There is nothing on it. Eleven lines of peg holes. Eleven fire pits. A churn hoop, and a boat with her pole across her.*”

“*Then say the rest of it, and say whether there was a sack.*”

“*No sack, because there is no churn. And no tent of eleven standing, and not two, and about four of us stood on the top of that rise and looked at about nine hundred yards of it in both directions, and there was not one thing on it to put a hand on.*”

About four people in that room said something at once and about nine did not.

“*Then say the rest of it,*” said Iven Ashcombe, “*because that is about the ground and about nine of you have now said the same thing in front of about thirty people, and I have a wall that says fifty-eight in the taking column of that line and I would like to know what a man is supposed to do about it in a room with the door shut.*”

“*Say what a man is supposed to do about it,*” said a woman of twenty-eight with a daughter of six on her hip. “*Because I have been in this county for about five weeks and I have a brother who poles a boat, and a fire that put eleven households on that rise.*”

“*Then say the rest of that.*”

“*There is a number on your wall with no name on it and no copy of it anywhere in this county. About nine of us have been to the rise and seen it with our own eyes. I will not have that number put right by a man with a barrow, and I will not have it put right by a stranger, and I have said both of those in the open air twice.*”

Nobody in that building thanked anybody.

---

Then she asked it, and she asked it in her own mouth, and it was a question a man of thirty-one had asked in the same room on the ninth day from the rising in front of about thirty people, and nobody had answered it then either.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Aldis Crane, “*because there are about thirty people in this room with the door shut, which is the only place in this hundred where about thirty people can say the same thing at the same hour, and you are about to ask something and about nine of us can tell already that it is not about a parcel.*”

“*Who is it this hundred asks, when it asks.*”

Nobody in that building said anything.

About four seconds went past.

About four people in that room said it at once and in four different ways, and the first of them said it as though the question had been about a man and the other three said it as though it had been about a thing you can write on.

“*Say the rest of that,*” said Iven Ashcombe, “*because about four of us have just answered it wrong in about four seconds and about nine of us have not answered it at all, and there is no form in the county of Kell that makes anybody answer.*”

“The water-clerk.” “The wall.” “The boy with the rag.” “The man who wrote the notice at the smithy.”

“*Say them again and say why, and I will write the why down and not the answer, because I have a wall and no keeper and about four lines about a room are on it already with no name at the top of them.*”

About nine people in that room gave a reason and none of the nine reasons was the question.

Nobody in that building said anything.

---

Then a man of thirty-one answered it, once, in about thirty people’s hearing, and it was wrong, and he knew it was wrong four seconds after he had said it, and a woman of twenty-eight told him so in about four seconds in front of all of them.

He had been in that county thirty-five days. He had held the low end of a plank. He had been priced at four pounds eleven shillings and threepence by a woman who keeps four beds. He had said a true thing about a hole in a sill at a gate and had it taken out of his hand in four seconds by a man with a barrow.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Bett Searle, “*because you have been in this room for about an hour and you have not said anything, and there is a boy of fourteen in this county who says out loud that a line with nothing against it is a sentence, and about four people in this room have been waiting for you to do it.*”

“*The wall. When a hundred asks, it asks the wall, because the wall is the only thing in this hundred that every person in it can read, and about nine people read it every morning.*”

About four seconds went past.

“*Then say the rest of that, and do not defend it, because about thirty people just heard you answer a question with a piece of parchment.*”

“*Then say the true of it, and it is yours to say because you asked it twice in this room in about five weeks and nobody has answered you either time.*”

“*A wall cannot be asked anything at all. You said that in a parlour on the Monday of the hundred and fourth week and about nine of us were in the room and about four of us understood it and I was not in the room and I have had it off a man with a slate since.*”

Nobody in that building thanked anybody.

“*Then say the rest of that, and do not end it at the wall,*” said Aldis Crane.

“*I will say the rest of it. A wall can be answered by anybody and by nobody. It cannot be asked anything. So a hundred that asks has to put the question somewhere a mouth can be put, and there is no column on that sheet for a mouth, and there has never been one in about two hundred and thirty years, and that is not a fault of anybody.*”

“*Then say the rest of that and tell me what a woman on a common does with it.*”

“*I do not know. That is the answer and it is not a good one. I have fifty-eight people on a sheet in this county and none of them on that rise, and about nine of us have been and looked, and I will not have that number put right by a stranger, and I am in a room with the door shut asking a question and getting about nine answers that are about a wall and a man and a boy with a rag.*”

Nobody in that building said anything.

---

Then the clerk did the thing he does, out loud, in about thirty people’s hearing.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Bett Searle. “*Because you have a wall and you have not said what you know now, and about nine lines on a board outside your own door say that no person in this hundred is to be asked what they remember, including his wife, and about nine people in this county want those lines taken down and about nine do not, and the two nines have not asked each other.*”

“*What I know now is that there is nothing on the rise. What I knew on the morning of the rising is fifty-eight. I am not permitted to be asked the second thing and I am not going to say the second thing, and I am not going to write fifty-eight off that wall either, because there is no form in this county that lets me put a number down and take it up, and because a wall with nothing in it is read by about nine people every morning as though the holder had gone.*”

“*Then say the rest of it and do not end it there.*”

“*I am going to put it on the wall in about four seconds. Not a new number. The parcel, the acres, and the day I stood on that rise and looked at it with my own eyes, and nothing at the top of it and nothing at the bottom of it. Fifty-eight stays in the column where a woman of twenty-eight put it. And there will be a second line under it that says the day and says nothing else, and about nine of you will read both every morning and work it out for yourselves.*”

He wrote it. The chalk went on under the taking column, under everything else, with the day on it, and about nine people read it back before it was dry.

Nobody in that building thanked anybody and nobody in that building was sorry for anybody.

The fever was forty-three weeks and five days old. A woman of twenty-eight asked in a room of about thirty people with the door shut the question a man had asked in the same room in front of about thirty people, and about four people answered it wrong in about four seconds, and about nine did not answer it at all.

And a man of thirty-one answered it once with a wall and was told by the woman who asked it, in four seconds, that a wall cannot be asked anything at all.

And nobody in that room is bound by any of it, and there is no form in the county of Kell that makes anybody answer, and by the fourth hour about nine mouths in that town had the question and about nine of those nine had it a different way, and the rest of eleven hundred people had not got it and are never going to.

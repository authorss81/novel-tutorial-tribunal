# Chapter 597: The Fourth Day Of The Hundred And Seventh Week, And A Friday, And Which Is The Market Day And The Fifth Market Held In That Town Since The Rising, And On Which About Three Hundred People Come Onto A Green And A Remedy Of Three Hundred Words That Has lain Under An Oak Bar For About Four Weeks Is About Half The Size Of A Settlement Of About Nine Hundred Words In Six Hands, And On Which A Woman Of Forty-One Reads The Whole Of It Aloud At A Stone Step In About Nineteen Minutes Without Having Written One Word Of It, And On Which A Man With Nine Acres And No Seat Asks At The Back Of That Step Where A Load Of Wet Hay In His Barn Goes, And On Which It Goes In At The End Of The Hay With The Day On It And The Number Of Loads And The Name Of The Yard Or No Name Of The Yard, And On Which The Two Hundred And Seventy Are Told That What They Get Is About Four Miles Of Walking And There Is No Form Anywhere That Says A Person May Not Look

Fourth day of the hundred and seventh week. His three hundred and forty-seventh morning. One hundred and ninety-seven days after the settlement. The fever forty-four weeks and six days. One hundred and fifty-one days since the division. Ninety-seven days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

The day before was the third day of the hundred and seventh week.

---

A market in Withermere is on a Friday and it has not moved in about two hundred years. This was the fifth one held since the water came over the tally stone.

About three hundred people came onto the green between the eighth hour and the eleventh, and about nine of the three hundred had been somewhere and seen something, and about two hundred and ninety-one had not been anywhere at all this week.

Nobody on that green thanked anybody for the six days either.

---

The remedy of three hundred words was under the oak bar at the north end of that green where it has lain since the fourth day of the hundred and second week, with a woman’s five lines on the back of it and one line on the back that is wrong and uncorrected.

It is about three hundred words in one hand with nothing at the top of it, and no person in the county of Kell is bound by a word of it, and it has been lying there for about four weeks.

The settlement is about nine hundred words in six hands with nothing at the top of it either. It is a different size and the difference is not a size on a page. Three hundred words in one hand is worth about four sentences to about thirty people at a step, and about nine hundred words in six hands is worth about one reading to about three hundred, and a thing that is worth one reading is not worth a market.

---

Then it was read, at about the ninth hour, at a stone step with an oak bar across it, by somebody who did not write it.

Nobody on that green thanked anybody.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Aldis Crane, who had a holding and a seat in the hundred and a skep behind his own gate. “*Because about three hundred people are on this green and a sheet about four feet wide is going to be read out at a step. There is a man of thirty-one standing nine feet off it who has put his hand to four things on it.*”

Nobody on that green said anything.

“*Then say that last part,*” said Hester Nunn.

“*About four of us have worked out in the last four days that about nine of us are going to hear a thing about ourselves and about nine that we are not,*” said Aldis Crane.

“*Then say the rest of that and do not defend it,*” said Iven Ashcombe, from the bottom of the step with no slate under his arm. “*Because there is a woman standing on that step who has not written a word of it and there is no form in this county that lets me read a sheet and there is no form that lets anybody stop me reading one either.*”

Nobody on that green said anything.

---

So a woman of forty-one read it, and she had four rooms and two children and a man who was at sea or was dead and had not been decided, and she had lost six acres in a market in the middle of April, and she had refused her name to a stranger twice.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said the woman of about seventy at the edge of that step. “*Because there are about three hundred people on this green, and about nine of them know that there is a settlement and about nine of those nine have heard it.*”

Nobody on that green said anything.

“*Then say that last part,*” said Hester Nunn.

“*About two hundred and seventy of the rest of you are about to be told something and will not afterwards be able to be asked what you thought of it,*” said the woman of about seventy.

“*I am standing in front of about three hundred people and a sheet about four feet wide in six hands, and I did not write any of it, and about nine of us in that hundred have been in a room over a stable all week with it.*”

Nobody on that green thanked anybody.

Then she read it out loud, and it took about nineteen minutes, and this is the whole of it.

---

*What a hay is, and where it is. Fifty-four acres of hay come off ground that is in season in this hundred: about ten off the low meadow, of which about six were cut in a fortnight and about four took five passes and are in a field in the weather and are not a hay and are a hay of the following year; and forty-four off long grazing four miles south, in its sixth year, with about nine acres of new sward in it.*

*The forty-four are in season to a man of fifty-four who has paid two shillings and a penny the acre on two hundred and ten acres across eleven holdings, which is twenty-one pounds seventeen shillings and sixpence a year, every penny of it. He has broken no rule and lied to nobody. They are not given to anybody. They are asked for.*

*Any person in this hundred may go and ask him, on his own ground, and he may say yes and he may say no, and if he says no nobody in this hundred is to blame. There is no form in this county that makes anybody ask.*

*Where fifty-eight people are. About eight hundred acres of common that belongs to nobody, and two hundred and twenty feet of the top of a rise about nine hundred yards long and about eleven feet wide, which is eleven tents, and fifty-eight people on it. Those are two numbers and they are not the same number and nobody is to be told which of them is the truer.*

*A foot of water went over the low side of the swell under those tents on the second day after the rising morning, and two tents were standing at the ninth hour on that day, and nobody was drowned. There is no licence, there is no form, and there is no column.*

*Any person in this hundred may walk out across the plank bridge at the seventh hour and look.*

*Three boards. They are in a yard four miles north, and this settlement does not touch them and does not say a man may put them in and does not say a man may not. It is not known what three boards hold off.*

*A man of fifty-four says they hold a level. A man of fifty-one says the foot of water that went over that common on the second day after the rising morning did not come through that gate. They do not agree and neither of them is wrong.*

*It would be settled by four hours at a full gate with a rod on each side, and a hand on the two lines on the timber above the frame, one of which is wet and one of which is pale and dry, and a hand in the gap under the sill. About nine people have never been asked to pay for four hours of a river.*

*The ditch, the six acres and the rate. Six acres of the sally bed on the top side, twelve shillings and sixpence a year, which is one hundred and fifty pence. A hundred and forty yards came out of that ground in sixteen days with a barrow, a spade and an axe and no team, by a man who owns nothing.*

*The rate is not paid. A ditch on this fen fills in about two years when it is cut and about one when it is not. A barrow cuts it once in about two years and a rate cuts it every year.*

*The square at the right-hand end of that line is empty, and it will be exactly as empty on the morning the water comes over that block of limestone as it is now. Nothing can be taken off a wall with a rag, because there is nothing on it to take the rag to.*

*The wall. Nine parcels and six challenges, and one of the nine cannot be corrected, because the line it describes is a line nobody holds.*

*There is no column on that sheet for a person’s name and there never has been one in about two hundred and thirty years. The holder’s name goes in the column that is the ground because there is nowhere else to write it, and that is not the fault of anybody.*

*Any person in that hundred may read that sheet, take it away, and write on the back of it. About nine people read it every morning and about nine of them have it a different way by the fourth hour.*

*And a man of thirty-one has put down one year, from the crossing of that block of limestone to the crossing after it. There is no form in this county that makes him and none that stops him. And from that morning he counts from the water, and there is no figure for that and nobody can check it.*

*It is not a season, it is not a licence, it is not a holding, and there is no rate on it. Nobody in this county may be assessed for it.*

*And there is no form in the county of Kell, and no form in the hundred of Withermere, that makes any person bound by one word of this. Every figure in it is a figure a person can go and look at. Every one of them is wrong somewhere, and where it is wrong it is written down as wrong.*

---

Nobody on that green thanked anybody.

About thirty people were close enough to hear the whole of it. About nine of those thirty have a way of putting it to a person in a doorway.

Then somebody at the back of that step asked the only question that had ever been asked about a document in that county, and about nine people heard it and about nine did not, and two hundred and seventy did not hear it at all and cannot be asked about it afterwards.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said a man of about thirty-four with a barrow, who had a stroke beside his name in chalk on a wall at a smithy. “*Because there is a sheet about four feet wide in six hands being read out to about three hundred people, and about nine of us have been somewhere this week and seen something. About four of us have it in a shape that this sheet does not say, and I would like to know which of the two a person is meant to believe.*”

“*Say the rest of that,*” said Hester Nunn, “*because you have stood at the back of that step with a barrow for five Fridays and you have not asked one question until now and about nine of us would like to know what it is.*”

Nobody on that green said anything.

“*Then say it out loud and say where it came from,*” said Aldis Crane.

“*It came from a barn in my yard. I have two loads of wet hay in it off that low meadow and nobody asked me and about nine of us now know, and this sheet has not said a word about a barn or a load or a yard and I would like somebody to tell me where that goes.*”

About four seconds went past.

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Hester Nunn, “*and say it into about thirty people’s hearing and not into about three hundred.*”

“*Say what is going in,*” said Aldis Crane, “*because about nine of us know that a load went into a yard and about nine do not, and I have a holding and I would rather the sheet had it in than have it not.*”

Nobody on that green said anything.

“*It goes in at the end of the hay,*” said the woman of fifty who keeps four beds, from about nine feet off the step. “*With the day on it and the number of loads and the name of the yard or no name of the yard. About nine of us know about it and the other two hundred and ninety-one do not.*”

Nobody on that green said anything.

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Aldis Crane, “*and do not end it at the two hundred and ninety-one.*”

“*That is the whole of what a settlement is for,*” said the woman of fifty who keeps four beds. “*It is not a promise. It is a list of what a person could not do and did anyway, and anybody in this hundred may walk down that lane and look at the barn and see the hay in it.*”

About four people on that green said something at once.

“*Then say the rest of that and do not end it at the barn,*” said a woman of twenty-eight who had come the four miles with a daughter of six asleep against her shoulder. “*Because about nine of us have been somewhere and seen something and about two hundred and seventy of you have not, and I would like to know what the two hundred and seventy get.*”

Nobody on that green said anything.

“*They get the walking,*” said Bett Searle. “*The plank bridge is crossable at the seventh hour. There is a rise about nine hundred yards long and it is bare in nine places, and there are eleven tents on two hundred and twenty feet of it, and about eight hundred acres round that belong to nobody.*”

Nobody on that green said anything.

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Aldis Crane.

“*There is a field with five passes of a hay standing in it that is not a hay. There is a gate with three boards in it and nobody knows what they hold off. That is what the two hundred and seventy get, and it is about four miles each way, and there is no form anywhere in this county that says a person may not look.*”

Nobody on that green thanked anybody and nobody on that green was sorry for anybody.

The fever was forty-four weeks and six days old. A settlement of about nine hundred words in six hands with nothing at the top of it was read out loud at a stone step on a green in a market of about three hundred people, in about nineteen minutes, by a woman of forty-one who had lost six acres in a market and had refused her name to a stranger twice and did not write one word of it.

The hay is in, and it is not all of it, and the part that is not is named as not a hay on the sheet and is standing in a field in the weather and belongs to whoever put five passes through it. And a load went into a yard nobody had asked about, and that is now on the sheet with the day on it and about nine people know about it and about two hundred and seventy were told about it once in a market and cannot be asked about it afterwards.

And about nine of those three hundred had been somewhere and seen something this week, and about two hundred and ninety-one cannot be asked about any of it, now or ever.

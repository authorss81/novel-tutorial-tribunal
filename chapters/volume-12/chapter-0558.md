# Chapter 558: The Seventh And Last Day Of The Hundred And First Week, And A Monday, And On Which A Man Of Thirty-One Walks Onto The Common On The Low Side Of The Water And Counts A Camp Of Tents Out Loud In Front Of About Nine People And Is Corrected By A Woman Of Twenty-Eight To A Number Two Lower, And Learns From The Difference That There Is No Form In That Hundred Which Says How Many People A Licence Covers, And That The Number Is The Season, And That Her Own Paper Is In A Sack In A Tent And Has No Copy On Any Wall, And A Man Of Fifty-Eight Stands At The Edge Of That Common For About Eleven Minutes And Does Not Cross Onto It, And Says The Tenth Day And The Second Day After The Rising, And Does Not Say Anything Else

Seventh and last day of the hundred and first week. His three hundred and eighth morning. One hundred and fifty-eight days after the settlement. The fever thirty-nine weeks and two days. One hundred and twelve days since the division. Fifty-eight days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county. The morrow is the first day of the hundred and second week.

The morrow is the first day of another week and the rising is in eight days, and those are the only two counts in that town and neither of them is about him.

---

There is a plank bridge over the low side at about the seventh hour and after it the ground is not pasture and is not marsh and is not common in any sense that anybody in that district would use the word.

It is about eight hundred acres of sedge and rough grass with the water standing in it in lines, and eleven tents are pitched in a line along the top of a rise where it is dry, and there is a fire in a hole, and a washing line, and a churn, and a dog, and about nine children.

Nobody stopped him. He said who he was twice.

---

She came out of the second tent with a daughter of six on her hip and she was about twenty-eight and she had been the one who had said the taking, and her brother had said it after her on a flat boat in four words, and had said it a fifth day ago.

“*You counted us.*”

“*I did not.*”

“*You have been on this common twenty minutes and you have counted us twice out loud and once under your breath, and I heard both, and I do not mind, and I am going to correct you anyway because you are going to go and be wrong somewhere.*”

“*Say the rest of that, because I have not counted anything.*”

“*There are about sixty of us.*”

“*That is what I have.*”

“*There are fifty-eight. There are fifty-eight of us on this common and there were sixty on Thursday and a man of about seventy went into Withermere on Friday to ask the clerk about a licence and he has not come back, and a boy of nine is in a village four miles off because a woman in the town took him in for a week, and the boy is not here and the man is not here, and the two of them are on your sixty and not on my fifty-eight.*”

Nobody on that common thanked anybody.

“*Say the rest of it, because I have just been wrong about two people in the open and I would rather be corrected than let it stand.*”

“*The rest of it is that you have a number about us now and you cannot get rid of it, and I cannot be angry with you for it, because you counted us out loud to a woman in front of nine children and a man who is not here cannot do anything about a number once it is said and it is the only weapon anybody has ever had in this district and it is not even a good one.*”

---

He got the figure out of her in about nine sentences, and it took nine sentences, and he wrote nothing down because he had nothing to write with.

Eleven households. Eight came down together off the high ground four days east where a fire has been in the sallow for a fortnight. Three came over a week later from a village on the same side, and the reason all of them came was the same one, and there was no arrangement and no order and nobody sent for them.

“*Where is the paper,*” he said.

She went into the tent and came back with a sheet of paper folded in four and gave it to him and did not let go of it until he had it.

There was a figure on it and a day on it and nothing else. The figure was *fifty-eight*. The day was the day the water came over the tally stone, which was the last time it came over it before this one, and the sheet said that the people whose names were not on it — and there were no names on it — might stay on the common on the low side until the water turned, and that the number of them was the number in the square.

And the top of it said, in a hand about nine inches from the top edge, four words: *for the common, in season*.

“*Say the rest of that, because I have a hand in a stranger’s tent and I have not been asked and I am holding a person’s whole year.*”

“*That is all of it. It is the whole of what eleven households have got and it is one sheet of paper in a sack in my tent, and there is no copy of it on a wall in this hundred and no copy in the county, and the clerk does not have one, and I have never once seen it in his hand.*”

“*Then he writes the number himself when the season comes round.*”

“*He writes whatever number he comes to.*”

“*Say the rest of that, because you have said it in nine words and I do not think nine words is enough for what you have just said to me.*”

“*There is no form in this hundred that says how many people a licence covers, because the licence is the number. The number is the season. If he writes forty then there are forty of us and eleven tents and about eight hundred acres, and nobody in the world can say he is wrong, because there is nothing anywhere to say he is wrong.*”

He handed the sheet back and she folded it and put it under the churn, which is the flattest thing in that tent.

“*Say the rest of it, because I have read a book in a chalk town with four columns and no fifth and I have read a wall in this town with three columns and no person, and I would like to know which of them is worse.*”

“*Your wall is worse. On your wall a man can find out what happened to him. Under my churn he cannot find out anything at all, and that is the difference, and I did not know it was a difference until you had been in my tent for nine minutes.*”

---

Then the water-clerk came.

He came at about the fourth hour in the afternoon, from the top side, along the line where the sedge begins to stand in water, and he stood at the edge of the common for about eleven minutes and he did not cross onto it.

About nine people saw him. She saw him before anybody else, from the fire, and she did not stand up and she did not say anything and the man who is her brother came out of a tent and stood beside her and neither of them moved.

After about eleven minutes he turned round and walked back up the plank bridge and did not say anything to anybody on his way, and he found Ilyan on the other side of it.

“*You are the stranger from the boat.*”

“*I am. I have been in the camp since the seventh hour and I did not know you were coming and I have not spoken to anybody here except the woman.*”

“*Good. Do not speak to anybody here again today. I have been here eleven minutes and I have not gone on and there are about nine people in that camp who have watched me not go on, and that is the whole of what I came for and if I had gone on there would have been nothing of it.*”

“*Say the rest of that, because I have been in this county four days and I have watched you refuse me twice.*”

“*The rest of it is that I did not come for the people. I came to see where the water off that ditch ends up, because a man who has cut a drain in March ought to know where his water goes and he is four miles south and he is not here.*”

He put his hand on the top of the bridge bar and did not lean on it.

“*The water off that cut will be over the low common on the tenth day after this one.*”

“*Say that again, because I have been here four days and every day has been a day off something and I want to be certain of this one.*”

“*The tenth day after this one. That is the second day after the rising, because the water comes up from behind when the river drops, and the river drops when the flood above us goes off, and I am telling you what I told the water, and the water did not argue.*”

“*Then on the second day after the rising there are about eight hundred acres of common under a foot of somebody else’s drain, and fifty-eight people on it, and no form.*”

“*Yes. And the rising is in eight days, and on the rising somebody is going to stand in my building at midday and write a number against that common, and I have not the least idea what number he will write, and I have not the least idea what I am going to do about it, and I have said that out loud to a stranger on a bridge because there is nobody in this hundred I can say it to who is not in it.*”

Nobody on that bridge thanked anybody and nobody on that bridge was sorry for anybody.

The fever was thirty-nine weeks and two days old.

A woman of twenty-eight with a daughter of six on her hip corrected a stranger’s count of a camp from sixty to fifty-eight in front of about nine people. The difference between the two numbers was a man of about seventy who has not come back and a boy of nine who is in a village four miles off. Both of them are in the wrong one, and neither of them can be asked about it.

And her paper is a sheet in a sack under a churn with a figure on it and a day on it and no name on it.

His paper is a sheet two men long on a wall with a figure in a square and a day at the foot of it and no name at the top of it.

There is no copy of the first one anywhere and no keeper of the second one, and in eight days the water comes over a block of limestone with a line cut round it, and every season in that hundred goes, and fifty-eight people and about eight hundred acres of nobody’s common, and a ditch cut in March by a man who paid for it, and a number that a man with a slate can write as anything he likes.

And a man of fifty-eight stood at the edge of a camp for eleven minutes and did not cross onto it, and said the tenth day and the second day after the rising, and said nothing else.

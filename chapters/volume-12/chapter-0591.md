# Chapter 591: The Fifth Day Of The Hundred And Sixth Week, And A Saturday, And On Which A Boy Of Fourteen Holds The Slate For The Second Time In His Life And Reads Two Men Long Of Parchment And Gets Nothing Wrong And Tells About Four People To Say So Out Loud In About Four Seconds, And On Which Nine Parcels Are On A List And Eight Of Them Have A Man And One Of Them Has Nothing At The End Of Its Line And There Is No Rag That Can Put A Thing Right, And On Which A Man Of Nine Acres And No Seat Says Out Loud That There Is A Foot Of Sallow In A Ditch Behind An Inn And The Woman Those Two Acres Belong To Says Out Loud That A Man Standing In A Field Is Not A Parcel, And On Which A Water-Clerk Says Out Loud That A Settlement Cannot Go On That Wall Because A Wall In This County Binds Nobody And About Two Hundred And Ninety-One People Would Have It And Could Not Be Asked About It Afterwards

Fifth day of the hundred and sixth week. His three hundred and forty-first morning. One hundred and ninety-one days after the settlement. The fever forty-four weeks and no days. One hundred and forty-five days since the division. Ninety-one days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

The morrow is the sixth day of the hundred and sixth week.

---

The reading is at the sixth hour on a Saturday morning, under the lamp on the bracket, with the door of the weigh-house open and the street behind it.

The boy with the slate was fourteen years old and his name was Toby Henshaw, and this was the second time in his life he had held it for a reading, and he had asked for the first one and had not asked for this one, and he had been asked to write a thing five times in his life and had said he did not know what to do with it four times.

About nine people stood under the lamp. About four more stood in the street where they could hear.

Nobody in that building thanked anybody.

---

Before he began, a water-clerk of fifty-eight stood to one side of the lamp with his hands behind him and said a thing out loud that about nine people had already heard twice this week in two places.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said the boy, “*because you have said it twice this week in two places and about nine of us have it and I am about to read a wall in front of about nine of you, and I would like to know whether what you are about to say belongs on that wall.*”

“*I am standing in front of a lamp and a sheet and I am telling you that there is going to be a settlement,*” said Iven Ashcombe, “*because about nine people in this hundred have said so to me twice this week and no word of it is written anywhere and I am the only person in this county anybody can be sent for, and if I do not say it out loud on a Saturday then it will be said in four days in four rooms and every one of the four rooms will have a different one.*”

“*Then say the rest of that and say what it is,*” said the woman of about seventy at the edge of that step.

“*I cannot say what it is. That is the whole of my answer and it is about nine days late. It is not a season. A season is a parcel of ground and a rate and a day, and it goes in a column and it lapses and it is re-let, and a settlement is none of those three and I have a sheet with three columns and no column for it.*”

About four people on that street said something at once and about nine did not.

---

Then the boy read it, and he read it in nine minutes, and he got nothing wrong.

“*I have got nothing wrong this time,*” he said, “*and about four of you will check me and about nine of you will not, and I would like the four of you to say so out loud in about four seconds so that the nine of you know which you are.*”

About four people said so.

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Iven Ashcombe, “*because you have read eleven lines, four lines, an eleven-line notice with about thirty names in it, a remedy of about three hundred words with five lines on the back of it, and a day at the foot of it, and there are two things under all of that which I wrote and one of them was on this wall a fortnight ago.*”

“*Fifty-eight in the taking column of the low common,*” said the boy. “*And under it a second line with the day on it and nothing above it and nothing below it.*”

“*You have been right about both of those for a fortnight,*” said Iven Ashcombe, “*and about nine people have read them both every morning and about nine have not, and neither of those is in the squares and neither of them lapses on a morning.*”

Nobody in that building thanked anybody.

---

Then he came to the list of nine parcels, and he read eight of them with a parcel against each, and he stopped on the ninth.

“*Six acres of the sally bed on the top side. The willow at the north corner. Nothing at the end of the line.*”

About four seconds went past.

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Iven Ashcombe, “*because you have read nine parcels and eight of them have a man who pays the rate on them and the ninth has nothing at the end of its line and about nine people in this hundred read that sheet every morning.*”

“*There is nothing at the end of the line,*” said the boy, “*and it is the only one of the nine I have read in a fortnight that a man cannot come and put a rag to, because a rag is for a wrong thing and that is not a wrong thing. Somebody handed that season to a room and there was nobody in the room to hand it to.*”

About nine people in that street understood what he had said. About four had said it in about four seconds before he did.

“*Then say the rest of it,*” said Iven Ashcombe, “*because I have a list with nine parcels on it and there is no form in the county of Kell that lets me take a parcel off that list because nobody holds it, and a list of ground with a parcel on it that nobody holds is not a list of parcels, it is a list with a hole in it, and I have had it for five weeks and about nine of us have read it every morning.*”

Nobody in that building said anything.

---

Then the six challenges, and five of them carried a parcel and a day and were read out with the parcel against them and checked by about nine people against each other without a word being said.

The sixth was the one that had never had anything against it under the day. It said that a line reading hay, mown was wrong.

Nobody stood up.

Then a man in the street, about nine feet off the step, said a thing that about four people turned round for.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said the woman of about seventy at the step, “*because there are about nine of us at that wall and a man in the street has just said a thing and about four of us have not got it.*”

“*I stood in the two acres behind the Green Paw on Saturday morning before the light,*” said the man of about thirty-four with a barrow and a stroke beside his name in chalk, who had nine acres and no seat. “*And there is no hay on them and there has not been hay on them in any of the little figures going left, and there is a ditch along the top side of them with sallow standing in it about a foot high and the water stops dead where the sallow begins.*”

About four seconds went past.

“*Then say the rest of it,*” said Iven Ashcombe, “*because that is the first time anybody has stood in that ground and said a thing about it out loud in a street, and about four people in this hundred have a shape of it and about nine have not.*”

“*It is the same as the one on the sally bed,*” said the man with the barrow. “*A ditch, sallow in it, water stops where it starts. That one has been cut about a hundred and forty yards with a barrow in sixteen days and I have carried every one of those loads past that gate. If nobody cuts this one in a year it is a foot of sallow and if nobody cuts it in two years it is two feet.*”

Nobody in that street thanked anybody.

“*Then say the rest of that and do not put it on that wall,*” said a woman of about fifty at the back of that building, who had come in out of the cold with a tray under her arm and had not stood under that lamp since the rising. “*Because those two acres are mine and I have paid four shillings and twopence a year on them in a lane in front of about nine people, and I do not work them, and a man standing in a field is not a parcel and a ditch is not a holding.*”

About four people in that street said something at once.

---

Then a man of thirty-one said four things out loud, in front of about nine people, and about nine mouths in that hundred had them by the fourth hour.

He had been in that county thirty-seven days. He had held the low end of a plank for about nine of sixteen days and taken nothing for it. He had been priced at four pounds eleven shillings and threepence by a woman who keeps four beds and had been given a parcel instead of her name. He had said no in a market of about three hundred people on the Friday before last and had then asked for the nine seconds back.

He wanted the reading to be over and to have put nothing on it.

That was his mistake and it was the oldest one he has.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Aldis Crane, who had a holding and a seat in the hundred and a skep behind his own gate, “*because you have been in this county thirty-seven days and you have not read a word this morning, and about four of us have noticed that and about nine have not.*”

“*I am standing in front of a wall that has been read every morning for about two hundred and thirty years by about nine people who can check each other in about four seconds.*”

“*Then say the rest of that, and say the four things, because a water-clerk has said out loud that a settlement is going to be made and nobody in that hundred knows what it is going to be about, and you are the only man in this town nobody can be sent for and that is the whole of what you are good for.*”

“*Hay. A place for the fifty-eight. Three boards. The ditch.*”

About four people on that street heard four things said in about nine seconds and about nine of them had them by the fourth hour.

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Iven Ashcombe, “*because those four things are now in about nine mouths in this hundred and they are the best four sentences anybody has said about anything in five weeks, and they are not a settlement, and there is no form anywhere in the county of Kell that turns four sentences into a thing a person has done anything about.*”

Nobody in that building thanked anybody.

“*Then say what a settlement is going on that wall for,*” said the boy with the slate, “*because I am the one who has to read it on Saturday and about nine of us will want to know whether I am reading a wall or a sheet with a thing on it.*”

---

And a man of thirty-one said the true of it and it was the wrong half of it, and a man of fifty-eight said no to him out loud in front of about nine people.

“*It can go on the wall,*” said Ilyan. “*That wall has carried fifty-eight and a day and a number of feet and an empty square and about thirty names in chalk. It can carry a settlement, because a settlement is a thing people are going to argue about and the only place in this town where about nine people argue about the same thing in the same hour is under that lamp.*”

“*Then say the rest of that and do not tell me I am wrong twice,*” said Iven Ashcombe. “*That wall binds nobody. That is what it is. Two hundred and thirty years and there is no seal and no office and no form that says what may be written on it, and every person in this town may walk up and read it and every person in this town may take a rag to it in about four seconds.*”

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Aldis Crane, “*because that is the first half of a no and about nine of us would like the second half.*”

“*The second half is that a settlement on that wall would be read by about nine people every morning of a year. There is no column on that sheet for a person and there is no form in this county that makes anybody answer for what they said about it. And about two hundred and ninety-one people in this town would have it and could not be asked about it afterwards. You have asked me four questions in five weeks and every one of them wanted a thing on a wall.*”

About four seconds went past.

“*Then say where it goes instead,*” said the woman of about seventy at the step, “*because I have been in this town sixty years and I have never seen a thing that was not on that wall.*”

Nobody on that street said anything.

Nobody in that building thanked anybody and nobody in that building was sorry for anybody.

The fever was forty-four weeks and no days old. A boy of fourteen read a wall two men long in nine minutes and got nothing wrong, and told about four people to say so out loud in about four seconds. Nine parcels were on a list and eight of them had a man and one of them had nothing at the end of its line, and there was no rag that could put a thing right.

A man of nine acres with no seat stood in a field before the light on Saturday and came back and said there was a foot of sallow in a ditch behind an inn, and the woman those two acres belong to said out loud that a man standing in a field is not a parcel.

And four sentences were said at a stone step in about nine seconds, and about nine mouths in that hundred had them by the fourth hour, and they were not a settlement.

The thing the wall cannot carry is a man who has not said anything at all. It has carried a parcel and an acre and a taking and a year and a number of feet and a number of people in the column that is for what goes on a thing, and it has never in about two hundred and thirty years carried a person, because there is no column for one. About nine people were standing in that street and about four of them would be able to say tomorrow what the man in the middle of them said, and about nine of them would not.

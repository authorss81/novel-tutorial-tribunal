# Chapter 588: The Second Day Of The Hundred And Sixth Week, And A Wednesday, And On Which About A Hundred And Forty Yards Of Ditch Comes Out Of The Ground In Sixteen Days With A Barrow And A Spade And An Axe And No Team, And On Which A Man Of Thirty-One Says The Total Out Loud Before The Man Who Dug It Can Say It, And On Which The Man Who Dug It Says There Was Never A Foot Of Sallow In The Whole Of It But About A Hand’s Depth In The Last Thirty Yards, And On Which About Four Of Ten Acres Are Still Standing On The Day After The Fortnight Ended

Second day of the hundred and sixth week. His three hundred and thirty-eighth morning. One hundred and eighty-eight days after the settlement. The fever forty-three weeks and four days. One hundred and forty-two days since the division. Eighty-eight days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

The hundred and forty yards of ditch came out of the ground on the Wednesday.

It had been started on the Tuesday of the hundred and fourth week with a barrow, a spade and an axe and no team.

It was the only piece of ground work in that hundred done this year at anybody’s own cost, and nobody had said one word about it at any point of the sixteen days.

Nobody in that lane thanked anybody.

There were about nine loads a day at the near end of it. About a yard of sallow to a load, and about nine loads a day, and about sixteen days, and about a hundred and forty-four loads.

That was out of about a hundred and forty yards of ditch, about four feet wide at the top, with mud heaped along one side the whole way.

About nine men watched the last of it go, and about four of them had watched every day of the sixteen.

The ground that morning was not mud and not dry. It was the third thing, which is the thing this fen does between the two, and a barrow went into it and came up with it in the bottom of the tyre, and about nine loads went up that bank before the eighth hour.

There was a boy of fourteen standing at the top end of it with about nine feet of knotted line in his bag, and he had been there since the sixth hour doing nothing, because a man of fifty-one had said at the top of the lane that a hundred and forty yards of ditch was about nine hundred and ninety feet and there was nobody in that hundred who could walk it except the boy.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Hoare Ryley. “*Because you have been standing at the top end of that ditch for an hour with a line in your bag and about nine of us have been watching you not use it.*”

“*A hundred and forty yards is nine hundred and ninety feet. I have got about nine feet of knotted line in my bag. That is about a hundred and ten times. I could walk it about a hundred and ten times and I have walked it about four, and it takes about an hour and a half each time, and about four times is two feet of it in six hours.*”

“*Then say the rest of that and do not be sorry for the number.*”

“*I have said the rest of it. A line of nine feet is not a way of knowing how long a ditch is. I told a man of fifty-eight in a parlour that thirty-one acres cannot be walked and he said the answer was no and then he said the bad light was the only part of it he could have got round, and about nine of us heard him be pleased about it, and he was not pleased, he was stuck.*”

Nobody in that lane thanked anybody.

The man holding the low end of the plank had been in that county thirty-four days and had held it for about nine of the sixteen days, and he had a bad leg that had stopped working on a common on the second day after the rising morning and had not worked properly since, and he had taken nothing for it and there is no column anywhere in the county of Kell that says a man may be paid for holding a plank.

---

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Hoare Ryley, to about nine people at the top of that ditch, at about the seventh hour. “*Because about a hundred and forty yards of ditch has come out of the ground in sixteen days with a barrow and two tools, and about nine of us have watched every day of it, and I would like the last of it said out loud before the sallow is heaped on the bank.*”

“*The last thirty yards,*” said Garr Ryley. He was thirty-three and he had six acres of the sally bed with nothing in the square at the right-hand end of its line. “*And a hand’s depth in it, not a foot, and I want that said before anything else.*”

About four seconds went past.

“*Then say the rest of that and do not be sorry for anybody,*” said Hoare Ryley.

“*I have been saying a foot of sallow growing in the whole of it since the Tuesday of the hundred and fourth week. I said it in a lane and I said it in a weigh-house with about thirty men in it, and about nine of us have it.*”

“*Then say the rest of that and say where the true of it is.*”

“*There was never a foot in the whole of it. There was a foot in the first about a hundred and ten yards and a hand’s depth in the last thirty. I have said the hundred and forty and the sixteen days right. I have said the foot wrong, and the foot is the figure that was on everybody’s mouth.*”

Nobody in that lane thanked anybody.

“*Then say where the foot came from,*” said Iven Ashcombe, who had come down the lane with no slate under his arm. “*Because about nine people in this hundred have that figure and it is in a document in a weigh-house with nothing at the top of it and nine parcels of ground on a list under it, and I have a wall and no keeper.*”

“*Out of my own mouth,*” said Garr Ryley.

---

And then a man of thirty-one did the thing he has done five times in thirty-four days, and this time it was a man’s own figure going back into his mouth.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Hoare Ryley, to him. “*Because you have been holding the low end of that plank for about nine of sixteen days and about nine of us have watched you do it, and you have said nothing about it in nine days, and you have just said the total out loud before the man who dug it has said it himself.*”

“*I am standing in front of about a hundred and forty-four loads of sallow that have come out of that ditch since the Tuesday of the hundred and fourth week. Nine loads a day for sixteen days is a hundred and forty-four, and I have carried that figure since I heard it at a gate on the Tuesday of the fourth week, and I said it this morning before he said it.*”

“*Then say the rest of that and do not take it back, because a man who digs a ditch is the only man in this county who can be checked against his own figure and he has not had a chance to speak yet.*”

“*I am not taking it back. I am saying it was nine loads a day and sixteen days and it is what he told me at a gate and I carried it four days and put it in my own mouth this morning.*”

Nobody in that lane said anything.

---

Then Garr Ryley said the half of it that nobody had said out loud in thirty-four days.

“*Say the rest of that,*” said Hoare Ryley, “*because about nine of us are standing at the top of an open ditch and about nine of us have been waiting since the Tuesday of the fourth week to hear whether that fixes anything, and about four of us think it does and about nine think it does not, and the two nines have not asked each other.*”

“*Say what you want said.*”

“*Say whether it fixes anything.*”

“*It cuts about a hundred and forty yards of sallow out of one ditch. It does not put a figure in the square at the right-hand end of my line and it does not pay a rate. The rate on those six acres is twelve shillings and sixpence a year and it is one hundred and fifty pence, and a ditch on this fen fills in about two years when it is cut and in about one when it is not, and a man with a barrow cuts it once in about two years and a rate cuts it every year.*”

Nobody in that lane thanked anybody.

“*Then say the rest of that and do not end it at the arithmetic.*”

“*So in about two years this ditch is a foot of sallow again, and I am thirty-three, and I will do it again.

And on the morning the water comes over the tally stone the square at the end of my line will be exactly as empty as it is this morning. About nine people will read it. And nothing can be taken off a wall with a rag, because there is nothing on it to take the rag to.*”

About four people in that lane said nothing at all, which is a different thing from saying nothing, and about nine of them said nothing.

---

Then they heaped the sallow on the bank on the top side in a row about as long as a cart, the way it had been heaped at the near end for nine days, and about nine men put a plank back across the ditch-mouth where the water had taken the ground out from under it, and it stayed there.

Ilyan stood at the top end and did not put a hand on the heap, and he had a bad leg that had gone again by the ninth hour and had not come back.

Nobody in that lane thanked anybody and nobody in that lane was sorry for anybody.

A man of about thirty with sixteen acres came up that lane at about the tenth hour to take the barrow back to his own yard and did not stop, and about four people watched him not stop, and about nine of them had worked out by then that he was the man who had put twelve shillings and sixpence on a bar on a Friday in the fourth week of this year and had it put back in his own pocket by a water-clerk in the open.

Nobody in that lane said anything about it.

About four of ten acres of the low meadow were still standing on the day after the fortnight ended, and about nine men had cut six of ten and nobody had said out loud that the other four would not be cut. They were standing in the top corner with the head of them gone grey and about a week past being a hay. Nobody in that hundred had cut them and nobody had said out loud that they would not be cut.

A man of about fifty with a holding and a seat in the hundred stood at the edge of them at about the ninth hour with a scythe over his shoulder and did not take it off his shoulder, and about nine people saw him do that and about nine of them have said since that he was waiting to be asked.

Nobody in that hundred had asked him.

The fever was forty-three weeks and four days old. A hundred and forty yards of ditch came out of the ground on a Wednesday in sixteen days with a barrow, a spade and an axe and no team.

Two men held a plank at the low end of it, and one of them could be sent for and one of them could not, and about nine people watched, and there is no column anywhere in that county for what that is worth.

And about a hundred and forty yards came out of that ground in sixteen days with a barrow, a spade and an axe, and the rate on the six acres that pays for it is one hundred and fifty pence a year, and nobody is paying it, and in about two years the ditch will be a foot of sallow again, and there is nothing on any wall in that county that will say so.

# Chapter 554: The Third Day Of The Hundred And First Week, And A Thursday, And On Which A Man Of Thirty-One Stands In The Way Of A Cart Of Wool In A Weigh-House In A Town Of About Eleven Hundred People And Says Out Loud A Thing About A Man He Has Not Met To A Room That Can Hear Him And Did Not Know Until Afterwards What He Had Written, And Is Refused In About Four Seconds By A Reason That Is The Same Reason Wearing The Other Coat, And Then Is Told The Whole Of What Happened To Eleven Households In A Spring In About Nine Sentences By The Only Person In That Hundred Who Can Be Asked About Anything, Who Is Paid Four Pounds A Year And Who Has Been Asked Nothing Before Today

Third day of the hundred and first week. His three hundred and fourth morning. One hundred and fifty-four days after the settlement. The fever thirty-eight weeks and five days. One hundred and eight days since the division. Fifty-four days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

There is a lamp on a bracket over the parchment and it is lit at about the seventh hour, and about nine people were standing in front of the wall at eight, and about four of them were not reading it.

---

The sheet is about two men long. It is ruled down into three columns and the ruling is black and older than the cloth it hangs on.

The first column is the ground. A parcel and a number of acres against it, in a clerk’s hand, one line to a parcel: the dyke close, nineteen; the low meadow, thirty-one; the sally bed, twelve; the long grazings, forty-four; and about thirty more below that, down the whole length of the thing.

The second column is the taking. What goes on it and how many. *Beasts, fourteen. Hay, mown. Nothing, and a line through the word nothing.* It is a column of use.

The third column is the year. One figure in a square at the right-hand end of each line, and going left from the square there are the figures of the years before, in a smaller hand, four or five of them, and the whole of the sheet is about two hundred and thirty years of a hundred telling itself when it was last spoken to.

At the foot, in the clerk’s own hand, the date of the last day the water was over the tally stone. That is all the authentication the sheet has. There is no seal and there is no counter and there is no office and there is no form anywhere in the county of Kell that says what may be written on it.

And there is no column with a person’s name at the top of it. There is not one and there never has been one.

---

He had the eleven by a quarter past eight and he had them because the figure at the end of the square on eleven lines in a row was the same figure, and it was a figure that had not been there when the wall was last read by anybody he spoke to.

He had not worked out what the figure meant. He was standing in front of it with his hands behind his back, the way a man stands at the top of steps.

A cart came in under the crane-beam with eleven sacks of wool on it and stopped.

The wool is weighed under a beam and a scale and a man writes a figure on a slate, and the wall is nine feet from the beam, and about four feet of it is where a cart backs in.

He was in the four feet.

---

The carter was a man of about forty and he came round the back of his own cart twice before he said anything, and the second time he did it slowly.

“*Say what you are standing in.*”

“*Say what I am standing in.*”

“*You are standing where the tailboard of my cart goes, and there is a nail in that stone about where your heel is, and if I put the cart over it will go through the cart and not through you, and I have eleven sacks on it that are worth more this year than that cart.*”

Nobody in that weigh-house thanked anybody.

Ilyan moved and the cart came in and was unloaded and weighed and went out again, and it had turned twice and been eleven minutes late because of a man who had arrived on a boat the evening before.

The carter came back in to say the other half of it, because he is that sort of man.

“*You are not from here.*”

“*No.*”

“*Then you are no use apologising to me, because I cannot send for you and you cannot send for you, and if I say it is all right then I am carrying it for you for the rest of the year, and I have enough.*”

“*Say the rest of that, because that is the truest thing anybody has said to me in two days and I would rather have the whole of it.*”

“*The rest of it is that there is a stone outside this door with his name on it and about nine more like it, and if I want to say a thing to a man I walk up to the stone. You are not on a stone. I do not know what you are.*”

---

Then the water-clerk came out of a door at the back, and he was a man of about fifty-eight, and there was a board on the outside of the front door about five feet by two with a name on it and a description of what the name does, and it said that any person in the hundred might ask Iven Ashcombe anything and that he would answer it, and there was no qualification on the board and none underneath.

He had a green book under his arm about the size of a psalm book, and he put it on the beam scale and did not open it.

“*You are the one who was in the way of the wool.*”

“*Yes.*”

“*I heard. I answer everything, so you will find that I have not stopped answering just because you are a stranger, and if you want me not to, say so now.*”

“*I want you to.*”

He turned round and put the green book on a stool and sat down on the edge of the wool table, which is not a thing a man of fifty-eight who is paid four pounds a year does in his own weigh-house.

“*Now say what you want to know.*”

---

“*I want to know about the eleven.*”

“*Which eleven.*”

“*The eleven with the same figure at the end of the square.*”

Ashcombe did not ask him how he had worked that out. He got up and went and stood in front of the wall and put his finger on the first of the eleven and read two lines off it under his breath and came back.

“*Two hundred and ten acres,*” he said. “*Eleven parcels, between the dyke and the low meadow. Taken up all in one week in April, on the eleventh, twelfth, thirteenth and fourteenth days of it, at the rate and the penny both, and the whole of the money came from one purse, and there is no other purse in the district that has paid two hundred and ten acres in one week since I was thirty-one.*”

“*Say who.*”

“*Say it yourself. It is written in the first column on eleven lines and I would rather you said it in this building than that I said it in my parlour at home to a wife who is not interested.*”

“*I would rather you said it.*”

“*No. And here is why, and it is not modesty, and I have never in nineteen years had a man in this hundred ask me that and be told no.*”

He put his hand flat on the wool table.

“*If I say a name in this building, then a man who is not you has heard me say it, and he will go out of that door and repeat it in a place where I cannot correct him, and in about nine days there will be a story about the water-clerk in Withermere and the story will be that he has a view about a man with seven hundred sheep, and my wife will be told about it at the pump.*”

“*And if I say it.*”

“*Then it is a stranger of two days saying it, and that is a smaller thing and it will have an end to it, because you will be gone.*”

Nobody in that weigh-house thanked anybody.

Ilyan looked at the wall for about four seconds.

“*Owain Bray,*” he said.

Ashcombe wrote something on the back of his hand with a pencil, which was a thing I had seen a man do in a chalk town and not a thing I had expected to see on a Wednesday morning in a fen town.

“*You will want to know whether it is legal.*”

“*I will.*”

“*It is legal. There is no form in this hundred or in this county that says a man may not take up the season on a parcel he does not work, and the rate is the rate, and the penny is the penny, and the clerk writes what is taken up and the day it was taken up and nothing else because that is all there has ever been to write.*”

“*Say the rest of it, because that is the whole of what I came twenty-one miles for and it has taken nine seconds.*”

“*Then it is about nine seconds and you should have written to me and had it in a letter, and nobody writes to me, and a letter would have taken nine days and this took nine seconds and I have been in this building for nineteen years and you are the first person who has come in here to ask me a question about a man and not about a ditch.*”

---

And then he said the thing that made the morning.

“*I have not been asked anything before today. I am the only person in this hundred anybody can be sent for about the water and the season and the rate and the ground, and there are eleven people in this town who have wanted to ask me something about a person for about four years and not one of them has walked up to the stone, and I have thought about that every week since April and I have come to a conclusion and I am going to tell you it and then I am going to go and cut a ditch.*”

“*Say the rest of that, because you have said that you have a conclusion and that is the same shape as a man in a porch telling me about a heading.*”

“*The conclusion is that it is not that nobody wants to know. It is that anybody may say anything, out loud, in this town, and repeat it forever, and there is nobody behind it. In your town you have a woman with a key and that is a danger, and I will not pretend to you that it is not. In this town I have a sheet on a wall and there is no keeper of it at all, and a man in this hundred can be ruined in a morning by a sentence and cannot be repaired in a year, and there is nothing whatever to be sent for.*”

He got off the table and picked up his green book.

“*That is the difference between your county and mine, and it is not that yours is worse. It is that yours is slow and mine is fast, and I have watched four households go out of this hundred since April and not one of them had been struck out of anything.*”

“*Where are they.*”

“*Three of them are on the low side of the water in eleven tents with about sixty people in them and a licence until the water turns, and one of them has walked to my door twice this week and has not knocked, and I have watched her from this window and I have not gone out, and I am telling you that so that you will not stand there this afternoon thinking I have not noticed.*”

“*Why have you not gone out.*”

“*Because I have four pounds a year and eleven hundred people and a hundred that meets on the rising, and if the water-clerk is seen going into that camp before the rising then whatever I say at the re-letting will be a thing a man said because he had been got at by a woman with tents.*”

The water outside the north wall had come up about a foot since the night before and nobody in that building was watching it.

“*How long is that off,*” Ilyan said. “*The rising.*”

“*Twelve days, if the weather holds and the man at the fen does not lie to me, and it is a day of a river and not a day of a number, and any man in this hundred who tells you a date for it is telling you about the weather.*”

Nobody in that weigh-house thanked anybody and nobody in that weigh-house was sorry for anybody. A man of fifty-eight who is paid four pounds a year, whose name is on a stone outside his own door, told a stranger the whole of what happened to eleven households in about nine sentences, and then went out to cut a ditch, and did not once say what ought to be done about it.

# Chapter 580: The First Day Of The Hundred And Fifth Week, And A Tuesday, And On Which A Man Of Thirty-One Walks Up A Lane In The Mud To Six Acres With Nothing In The Square And Says Out Loud To The Man Whose Ground It Is That He Has Decided And The Decision Is To Stay Nobody’s, And On Which The Cost Of It Is Said Back By The Man Who Pays It, And On Which Three Boards Lie In A Yard At Coldcombe Because There Is No Money For Them And A Man Of Fifty-Four Was Asked Yesterday Whether He Meant Them And Has Not Said No

First day of the hundred and fifth week. His three hundred and thirtieth morning. One hundred and eighty days after the settlement. The fever forty-two weeks and three days. One hundred and thirty-four days since the division. Eighty days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

The day before was the seventh and last day of the hundred and fourth week.

---

It rained before the seventh hour and stopped after it. The lane up to the sally bed was mud to the ankle. A cart had gone up it in the night and the ruts stood full. A barrow stood in the yard with sallow heaped over the sides. No cart stood in the yard at all.

Garr Ryley was thirty-three. He had six acres of the sally bed on the top side and nothing in the square at the right-hand end of the line. He had given that season to a room fifteen days before, on the Monday, and had given it to a room on purpose. He had stood in eleven inches of water on his own ground three days after it. He had said another man’s sentence back before the ninth hour on the Sunday night.

He had taken out seven days of sallow with a barrow. He had about nine more days of it for a year of it. About a hundred and forty yards of ditch with a foot of sallow in the whole of it. A spade and an axe and no team and nobody to hold the other end of a plank.

Nobody in that yard thanked anybody.

Ilyan came up the lane at about the eighth hour with mud over his boots and his bad leg stiff from the wet. He had been in that county twenty-six days. He had come off a boat on the evening of a Wednesday twenty-seven days ago, and the count is of days and not of the arrival day itself. He had under two pounds in a purse. One hundred and fifty pence of it would put six acres back in season. He had a hand on a wall twice over that he cannot take off. He had a sentence out in about nine mouths in the town that he cannot take back.

He had made the decision in the night with a bar on a fire and no children asleep, because he has none. He had come up the lane to say it to the man and not to the room, because a decision said in a room is a thing about thirty men act on, and a decision said in a yard is a thing one man pays for.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Garr Ryley, from the yard, because there is a gate and it is shut and a man in a yard may shut his gate. “*Because you have come up a lane in the mud on a Tuesday, and about nine men cut about two of ten acres yesterday, and about thirty people heard you say on Sunday that you can put six acres back in season and speak for the harvest and cannot do both.*”

“*I am standing in front of six acres with nothing in the square and a barrow with seven days of sallow in it. I have come to say what I have decided, and the decision is to stay nobody’s.*”

Nobody in that yard thanked anybody and nobody in that yard was sorry for anybody.

“*Say the rest of that, and say it in a yard and not in a room, because about nine people in this hundred heard the sentence in a room and about nine heard it back at a lamp, and neither of the two nines has heard what it costs.*”

“*It costs your household the season. Twelve shillings and sixpence a year is one hundred and fifty pence and I have it. If I put it on that bar then you are whole and I am one of thirty, and everything I have said in that room for twenty-six days is then said by a holder. The harvest on forty-four acres goes to seed or it does not, and no stranger stands in that room to say which. I am not putting it there.*”

About four seconds went past. The water stood in the ruts of the lane. About nine feet off, the ditch between the sally bed and the low meadow lay open where seven days of sallow had come out of it, about four feet wide at the top, with mud heaped along one side.

“*Then say the rest of that, and say how long, because a decision to stay nobody’s is not a decision for a day.*”

“*Until the year turns. The season I would take up runs a year from the day it is taken. If I take it then I am one of thirty for a year. In a year the water comes over the tally stone again and every season in this hundred goes. I will not be one of thirty when it does.*”

Nobody in that yard said anything.

---

Then the cost of it was said back by the man who pays it, and he said it plain.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Ilyan, “*because you said my sentence back on Sunday night and about nine people heard you, and I would rather hear the cost of it from you than from a wall.*”

“*Six acres empty into the turning of the year. About nine more days of sallow with a barrow for a year of it, and then a ditch cut once and going soft, because a room does not cut ditches and a man with a spade and no team cuts about nine loads a day. In about two years the low meadow of about thirty-one acres is gone, and not in a wet year. About twenty-one acres of it are gone this year already. That is the cost and it is mine. You did not make it. A number said at my door fifteen days ago did not make it either. The rate makes it, and nobody is paying the rate.*”

“*Then say whether you asked me to pay it,*” said Ilyan.

“*I did not ask you on Sunday and I am not asking you today. I said what it is on Sunday night and I am saying what it costs today. A stranger who pays my rate buys my ground for a year and sells his voice with it. I watched about nine men cut yesterday and you standing further off than in the morning. That standing off is the second of your two true things. I am not buying it back for one hundred and fifty pence.*”

Nobody in that yard thanked anybody.

Ilyan took his hand out of his coat and put it on the gate bar. The bar was wet. He left the hand there for about four seconds and then took it off. He had put a hand on four walls in that county in twenty-six days and two of them were wrong. He put no hand on that gate.

“*Then it is decided,*” said Garr Ryley, “*and about nine people will have it by the fourth hour, and about nine will have it a different way, and the rest of eleven hundred people in this town have not got it and are never going to. That is what happens to a decision here. I have watched it happen to figures for thirteen days and now to one decision.*”

“*Then say the rest of that, and say what you will do with the nine days,*” said Ilyan.

“*Cut them. About nine loads a day with a barrow. Nobody in this hundred is going to ask me about it. The water is coming off that meadow onto a common, and there are fifty-eight people on it with no licence and no form and no copy of anything anywhere.*”

Nobody in that yard was sorry for anybody.

---

Then the lane went down to the town, the way a decision goes, and by the tenth hour it was on the north wall of the weigh-house without anybody writing it.

About nine people at that wall read the line with nothing in the square. Six acres of the sally bed. Nothing at the right-hand end. A boy of fourteen with a rag in his hand stood beside it, because he is the person who rubs that wall out when somebody wants it rubbed out, and nobody wanted it rubbed out.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Iven Ashcombe to him, at about the tenth hour. “*Because you have stood here since the eighth with a rag and about nine people have read an empty square, and a square with nothing in it is read by about nine people a morning as though the holder had gone.*”

“*I am standing in front of nothing in a square. A front can be rubbed out in about four seconds. Nothing cannot be rubbed out, because there is nothing to take a rag to. I have known that since yesterday, when about nine men cut about two of ten acres and left six standing empty.*”

Nobody at that wall thanked anybody.

A woman of forty-one came down to that wall at about the eleventh hour with two children not with her. She had six acres of the second close off the dyke at twelve shillings and sixpence a year, and a man four miles south had taken the season on them in the middle of April. She had put the parcel and the acres and the day on a paper under a bar with no name on it. She read the empty square for about nine seconds and said one thing.

“*It is still nothing,*” said Hester Nunn. “*And about nine people read it every morning. That is the whole of it, and it is not mine to mend.*”

Nobody at that wall thanked anybody and nobody at that wall was sorry for anybody.

---

And three boards lay in a yard four miles north because there is no money for them, and that was on no wall and in no mouth that day, and it was the thing the next band has to spend.

The gate at Withermere holds three of six boards, and the level is a foot below the tally line. Six boards holds the water off seven holdings and off the cut on the low meadow, and puts a foot of water on four closes and on the low common. Three boards keeps the camp on the low common dry and lets the four closes fill in. The other three are in a yard at Coldcombe. A man of fifty-four said fourteen days ago, on the rising, that he would still put them in and pay for them out of his own purse.

Yesterday, at the edge of ten acres, with the first swath down, in front of about thirty people, a water-clerk asked him whether he meant it. Thirteen days in the carrying, and the asking took about four seconds.

He gave no answer that day. He did not say no. He stood at the edge of the field for about nine minutes while about thirty people said nothing, and went back up the lane on his own legs.

On this Tuesday nobody had asked him again. No form in the county of Kell says a man must answer in a day. No form says he may wait. A barn in that hundred stood empty with no wet hay in it, because no barn will take wet hay. About two of ten acres lay cut along the upper edge of a meadow of about thirty-one. A scythe stood against a wall in a yard on the top side with wet grass on the blade.

Ilyan went down to the Green Paw at about the fourth hour and sat in the parlour with his bad leg out. He had been in that county twenty-six days. He had under two pounds in a purse. He had four doors behind him and four streets with a number he had put in them. He had decided in a yard in the mud to stay nobody’s until the year turns, and the cost of it is six acres standing empty and about twenty-one acres gone this year and a meadow gone in about two years.

A woman of about fifty who keeps four beds brought him bread and did not let him count money out loud in her parlour. He ate it and said nothing about the rate. About four people in that parlour knew what he had decided. About nine did not.

The fever was forty-two weeks and three days old. A man of thirty-one walked up a lane in the mud to six acres with nothing in the square and said out loud to the man whose ground it is that he has decided, and the decision is to stay nobody’s until the year turns.

The cost of it was said back by the man who pays it, plain and in a yard, with seven days of sallow in a barrow behind him.

And a man of fifty-four was asked yesterday at the edge of a field whether he meant three boards out of his own purse, fourteen days after he said it, and has not said no.

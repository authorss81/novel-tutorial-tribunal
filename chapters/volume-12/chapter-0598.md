# Chapter 598: The Fifth Day Of The Hundred And Seventh Week, And A Saturday, And On Which The Reading Is At The Sixth Hour For The Fifth Time In The Life Of A Boy Of Fourteen And It Is The Last One In This Town In This Volume, And On Which There Is A Settlement On The Wall In The Clerk’s Hand With The Day On It And The Clerk Says Out Loud Before Anybody Begins That He Did Not Write It, And On Which The Boy Reads Two Men Long Of Parchment In About Twenty-Six Minutes And Gets One Number Wrong And It Is Corrected Before He Has Finished The Sentence, And On Which Fifty-Eight Is Read Out Of A Taking Column For The Fifth Week Running With Nothing In The Square At The Right-Hand End Of The Line, And On Which Two Hundred And Thirty Little Figures Going Left Are Read As A Run Of Them With The Boy Saying So Out Loud Before He Starts, And On Which A Man Of Thirty-One Says Out Loud In About Nine People’s Hearing That A Document Is Not An Answer Because It Is On A Wall

Fifth day of the hundred and seventh week. His three hundred and forty-eighth morning. One hundred and ninety-eight days after the settlement. The fever forty-five weeks and no days. One hundred and fifty-two days since the division. Ninety-eight days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

The day before was the fourth day of the hundred and seventh week.

---

The reading is at the sixth hour on a Saturday morning, under the lamp on the bracket, with the door of the weigh-house open and the street behind it. The lamp is lit an hour earlier on a Saturday than on any other morning, because that habit is about two hundred and thirty years old and nobody in that hundred could tell you who started it.

This was the last reading. There has been one every Saturday for about two hundred and thirty years and there is no form in the county of Kell that says what happens on the Saturday after this one.

About nine people stood under the lamp and about four more stood in the street where they could hear.

Nobody in that building thanked anybody.

---

The boy with the slate was fourteen years old and his name was Toby Henshaw, and he held it, and this was the third time in his life he had held it for a reading of that wall, and the second time he had held it for one without asking for it.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Iven Ashcombe. He stood to one side of the lamp with his hands behind him and had not held the slate for four readings and did not intend to hold it for a fifth. “*Because about nine of us are at that wall and about eleven hundred people in this town have read it every morning of their lives. This morning there is a thing on it that was not on it on Thursday and I did not put it there.*”

Nobody on that street said anything.

“*Then say that last part,*” said Aldis Crane, “*and say it out loud before he begins.*”

“*And I want it said before you begin,*” said Iven Ashcombe.

“*Say the rest of that and say it plain,*” said Aldis Crane, who had a holding and a seat in the hundred and a skep behind his own gate. “*Because about eleven hundred people in this town read that wall and about nine of them are standing under this lamp. If a thing goes on that wall that the man who writes on it did not put there, then that is a different thing.*”

Nobody in that building thanked anybody.

“*Then say that last part,*” said Iven Ashcombe.

“*And everybody in this hundred needs to know it in about four seconds,*” said Aldis Crane.

Nobody in that building thanked anybody.

“*I did not write it,*” said Iven Ashcombe. “*The clerk’s hand is on it and I did not write it. It was there at about the seventh hour on Friday afternoon when I came in. I have been nineteen years the man whose hand that is and I know it, and I did not write it.*”

About four seconds went past.

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Aldis Crane, “*and say what a person may do about a thing they find on a wall they keep.*”

“*There is no form in this county that says,*” said Iven Ashcombe.

About four seconds went past.

Nobody in that street said anything.

---

Then the boy read it, and he read the whole of it, and it took about nineteen minutes, and he got one thing wrong and it was corrected before he had finished the sentence.

He read the eleven lines first, parcel and acres and taking and the figure in the square. He read the four lines about how many men fill the chairs in a room. He read the eleven-line notice off the smithy’s end wall as it stood copied under the clerk’s hand, with about thirty names in chalk and a stroke beside the ones that are not of the hundred. He read the remedy of about three hundred words off the front, and the five lines a woman of thirty-eight wrote on the back of it, and the day at the foot, which is the morning the water was last over the tally stone.

“*That is a settlement,*” he said. “*About nine hundred words in six hands. And there is no name at the top of it. It says in it that no person in the county of Kell is bound by one word of it, and that any person in this hundred may read it, take it away, and write on the back of it. It was read out at a step on the green yesterday morning in about nineteen minutes and I was not there because nobody asked me to be.*”

About nine people in that room had not known it was there. About four of them had it a different way by the fourth hour.

“*Then say the rest of that and do not be sorry for anybody,*” said Aldis Crane. “*Because about nine of us in that hundred have had it since Friday morning and about nine have not, and the two nines have not asked each other.*”

Nobody in that building said anything.

“*Then say that last part,*” said Hester Nunn, from the street.

“*You have just told about eleven hundred people about a settlement in one breath at a wall they have read every morning for about two hundred and thirty years, and about two hundred and seventy of them cannot afterwards be asked what they thought of it,*” said Aldis Crane.

Nobody in that building thanked anybody.

---

Then he read the nine parcels and the six challenges and the empty square, and the second line under fifty-eight with the day on it and nothing above it and nothing below it, and he read it all in about twenty-six minutes.

“*Six acres of the sally bed on the top side,*” he read. “*Nothing in the square at the right-hand end of the line. Fifth week running.*”

About nine people in that room heard a boy say that. About four wrote nothing down.

Then the second line under the low common. Fifty-eight in the taking column, and under it the parcel and the day a water-clerk of fifty-eight stood on a rise and looked at it with his own eyes, and nothing above it and nothing below it.

Then the nine parcels, of which one cannot be corrected because the line it describes is a line nobody holds. Then the six challenges, five with a parcel and one with nothing under the day, which a boy of fourteen has now read three times and about which about nine people in that hundred have a shape and about nine do not.

And then he came to the end and read the two hundred and thirty little figures that go left from the squares, in a smaller hand, which is about two hundred and thirty years of the hundreds telling themselves when each was last spoken to.

“*There are two hundred and thirty of them,*” said Toby Henshaw. “*And I read them as a run of them, and I said so out loud before I started. About four of you will check and about nine of you will not.*”

Nobody in that building thanked anybody.

“*Then say the rest of that and do not be sorry for the number,*” said Iven Ashcombe.

“*And that is the fifth time anybody has asked me to write a thing,*” said Toby Henshaw, “*and the morrow is the sixth day of the hundred and seventh week and the day after that is the last day of it, and I do not know what anybody stands under that lamp after that and neither does anyone else in this hundred.*”

About nine people in that room heard a boy say that.

Nobody in that building thanked anybody and nobody in that building was sorry for anybody.

---

Then somebody said out loud the thing that has to be said, and it was said in about nine people’s hearing and it was said by the man who wrote four things on the sheet and it was said against himself.

He had been in that county forty-four days. He had a year down, from the crossing of that block of limestone to the crossing after it, and no form in that county that made him or that stopped him, and from Thursday morning he has counted from the water and he will count from it after that.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Aldis Crane, “*because about nine of us are under this lamp and about eleven hundred in this town read that wall every morning, and a thing on a wall in this county binds nobody and that is the whole of what this county has, and you are about to say something about it in front of about nine people.*”

“*I am standing in front of about eleven hundred people who read this wall every morning and can be sent for about none of it.*”

Nobody on that street said anything.

“*Then say the rest of that and do not be sorry for me,*” said Iven Ashcombe, “*because I have a board outside my own door that says any person in this hundred may ask me anything and I answer it, and about nine people have tested that this year and about nine have not, and a settlement on my wall is a different thing from a season on my wall and I am the only person in this county who knows the difference is a real one.*”

Nobody in that building thanked anybody.

“*Say the rest of that,*” said the woman of about seventy at the edge of that lamp, “*and say it to about eleven hundred people and not to about nine.*”

---

Then he said it, and it is the last thing anybody in that hundred said about that wall for a long time.

“*A document is not an answer because it is on a wall. This wall has been read every morning for about two hundred and thirty years and it has settled nothing.*”

Nobody in that building thanked anybody.

“*Then say what is on it that has not settled anything,*” said Aldis Crane, “*and give about four of us something we can check in about four seconds.*”

“*A wrong parcel that a boy took a rag to in about four seconds,*” said Ilyan. “*A line about a woman of thirty-eight who has never had a crop of hay in her life. A number of feet that a man of fifty-eight wrote knowing it was useless. And an empty square at the end of a line that nobody can put a rag to, because there is nothing on it.*”

About four people in that room said something at once.

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Aldis Crane.

“*And this is on it now, and about nine hundred words of it is right, and the wrong thing about it is the same wrong thing that has been true of that wall for two hundred and thirty years, which is that everybody in this town reads it and nobody in this town is bound by it and about two hundred and ninety-one of the people who read it cannot afterwards be asked what they thought it meant.*”

About nine people in that room had heard that. About nine had not.

Nobody in that building thanked anybody and nobody in that building was sorry for anybody.

Then a man of fifty-eight put his hand flat on the block of limestone for the length of a count of five, the way he does, and it was the seventh hour and the water was not over the cut.

The fever was forty-five weeks and no days old. A boy of fourteen held the slate at the sixth hour on a Saturday for the third time in his life and read two men long of parchment and read a settlement of about nine hundred words off the front of it that is in six hands and binds nobody, and got one number wrong and it was corrected before he finished the sentence.

And a settlement was announced on that wall in the clerk’s hand with the day on it. No person in the county of Kell is bound by one word of it, and any person in that hundred may read it, take it away, and write on the back of it. A man of thirty-one said out loud in about nine people’s hearing that a document is not an answer because it is on a wall, and about nine of them heard that and about nine did not.

And there are two hundred and thirty little figures going left from the squares in a smaller hand. A boy of fourteen read them as a run of them and said so out loud before he started, and the morrow is the sixth day of the hundred and seventh week, and nobody in that hundred knows what anybody stands under that lamp after the day after that.

# Chapter 563: The Fifth Day Of The Hundred And Second Week, And A Saturday, And On Which A Sheet Of Parchment Two Men Long Is Read Out Loud In A Building With The Door Open By A Man Who Is Not The Clerk Because The Clerk Says He Will Not Read It And Why, And About Nine People Stand In Front Of It And About Six Of Them Say Out Loud That The Reading Was Wrong About Their Own Line And The Clerk Writes Down Every One Of Them Because He Has Promised On A Board Outside His Own Door To Answer Anything, And It Is The First Time In This County That A Record Has Been Made By A Challenge Instead Of By A Keeper, And No Names Are Read Out, And Six Challenges Go Under A List Of Eleven And There Is Nobody In The County Of Kell To Send For About Any Of Them

Fifth day of the hundred and second week. His three hundred and thirteenth morning. One hundred and sixty-three days after the settlement. The fever forty weeks and no days. One hundred and seventeen days since the division. Sixty-three days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

The rising is in three days, and the clerk was asked for the number four times before the seventh hour.

---

---

A district reads its wall.

Not on a market day and not on a Sunday. On the sixth hour of a Saturday morning, out loud, by whoever holds the slate, standing under the lamp on the bracket, with the door of the weigh-house open and the street behind it, so that anybody in the street may hear it and correct it.

It is not a law. It is a habit about two hundred and thirty years old, and it is the reason about nine people in that town can tell you what is on a sheet two men long without walking up to it.

On this Saturday it was read by a man of about sixty-two called Perrin Voss. He is a shepherd. He holds a season on about nine acres. He has been coming in on Saturdays since he was nine years old, and he is not the clerk.

“*Say why you and not him,*” said Iven Ashcombe, standing at the end of the wool table with his green book under his arm.

“*Say the rest of that, because you have asked me four questions this week about water and you have not asked me one about this.*”

“*I have been asked it twice this week and I have said no twice and the reason is the same both times.*”

“*Then say it in four sentences and I will go and hold the slate and read it and you may stand at the back and tell me if I have it wrong.*”

“*I will not read it. I write it and I put the figures in the squares. If I read it out then it is the water-clerk reading a sheet he wrote, and any man in the street may then say the water-clerk read it wrong, and be saying that the water-clerk is wrong about a hundred and thirty holdings instead of about six feet of parchment.*”

Nobody in that building thanked anybody.

“*Say the rest of that, because you have told me why and it is not the reason.*”

“*The other reason is that in nineteen years I have never been asked whether that sheet was right. Once a week for nineteen years a man has stood under that lamp and read out two hundred and thirty years of a hundred, and about nine people have listened, and not one of them has ever said no. And I have wanted it every Saturday for nineteen years and I have not asked, because asking me is asking the only person I cannot get away from.*”

He put the slate down on the wool table.

“*You are a stranger and you were here four days and you put four lines on my wall on Friday about a room. If a man in the street says that reading was wrong, I want it to be a man who is not paid four pounds a year to say it is right.*”

---

He read it from the top. It took about twenty minutes. He read it wrong twice and was corrected twice, by two different men in the street, and both corrections were the man with the slate agreeing with them.

Nobody read out a name. That is not a rule. It is that the sheet has no column with a person’s name at the top of it, and a reader who begins inventing one is reading something else.

At about the sixth minute a man of about fifty, who has a seat, said the figure in the square on his own line was not this year’s.

“*Say the rest of that, because that is the fourth time this year I have heard it said and it is the first time it has been said out loud in front of about nine people.*”

“*I have had two years of that line read out of the wall and the figure at the end of it has not gone left twice.*”

Voss read the line again from the wall and then looked at the small figures going left from the square.

“*You are right.*”

“*Say the rest of it.*”

“*The rest of it is that it went left in the water-season before last and not since. There is no rule that says a figure has to go left every year. I have written two hundred and thirty years of a hundred telling itself when it was last spoken to, and I have not spoken it.*”

---

At about the eleventh minute a woman of about thirty-eight, whose ground is four acres, said that her taking had been read as hay, mown, in every year of the little figures. It had not been mown in any of them.

“*Say the rest of that, because if that is true then the second column is a lie that has been standing on a wall for about sixty years.*”

“*It is not mown in any of them. It is grazed, and grazed in about nine of them, and blank in about four, and I have read *hay, mown* off that line at least nine Saturdays running because it is the only line on the sheet where the word hay is, and a man reading down a column finds hay.*”

Nobody in that building said anything for about four seconds.

“*Then somebody should have stopped you.*”

“*Say the rest of that, because you have just said the whole of what this reading is for in nine words.*”

“*The rest of that is that nobody stops a reader. That is what a reader is. A keeper can be stopped, because a keeper is one person and there is somebody to go and ask. A reader is anybody, and you cannot be sent for about a reader, and so there is nobody to be sent for about him.*”

---

At about the sixteenth minute somebody at the back asked what the figure in the square on the low common was about, since the low common has no holder and there is no season on it and it is nobody’s.

Nobody could answer it and the man who asked it said so, and about nine people heard a question go unanswered in the open.

---

At about the twentieth minute it was over and about thirty people were in and about nine of them had said something, and six of the things they had said were wrong about their own ground.

Ashcombe took the slate off him and put it on the table and then did the thing he had not done in nineteen years, which was to write.

He wrote on the back of the notice that is already on that wall, in his own hand, with the day on it. Six lines, and a figure against each one.

The first of them said *the second close off the dyke, six acres*, and that is the only one of the six that names a parcel. The other five had been said by men who did not know which parcels, and he got them off the first column as the men pointed at it, one at a time, in the open.

He wrote the six challenges under a list of the eleven.

“*Say what you are writing, because I have never in nineteen years seen a Saturday reading produce a thing that stayed on the wall.*”

“*I am writing six lines and the day, and the six lines are what six people said were wrong about their own ground this morning.*”

“*Say the rest of that, because a challenge on a wall is not a record and a record is not a challenge and I have kept the two apart for nineteen years.*”

“*It is a record. It is the first one anybody in this county has made that was not made by me, and it was made by about six people who came at me and were right. I am writing it down because there is a board outside my door that says any person in the hundred may ask me anything and I will answer it, and about six people have just asked me something I cannot answer.*”

Nobody in that building thanked anybody.

“*Say the rest of that, because you have just admitted on a Saturday that you cannot answer six questions about the sheet. You are paid four pounds a year out of a rate of two shillings and a penny the acre, about nine people in this hundred have worked that out, and one of them is me.*”

“*Then let about nine people in this hundred know that the water-clerk cannot answer six questions about his own wall. That is the truth about it, and it is the first true thing about that wall that has been written on it in nineteen years.*”

He put the chalk down.

“*And I will tell you the second half. There is nobody in this county of Kell that I can be sent to, and nobody in this county of Kell that you can be sent to. That is the only reason there is a record on that wall at all this morning, and both of those are terrible reasons and neither of them is a good one.*”

---

Ilyan read the six lines standing up at about the ninth hour, the way anybody in that town reads a wall. It took him about four minutes.

He could not read five of the six parcels. The six men had not said which parcels, and the clerk had written down the one he could get.

That is the whole of it and it is not a small thing. There are now six challenges on a wall in a county where anybody may read them, and five of them are attached to nothing.

“*Say what you have found,*” said Perrin Voss, who had read it twice already.

“*I have found that the first time anybody in this district has put a thing on that wall that a person asked for, five sixths of it cannot be checked by anybody including the man who said it.*”

“*Say the rest of that, because you have been in this county four days and that is the first thing you have said that is about me and not about water.*”

“*The rest of that is that in a chalk town about fifty miles off there is a book with four columns and no fifth, and a woman keeps it. About nine people in that county can be sent for about anything in it, and that is why the things on a wall there cannot be rubbed out in four seconds. And here it can be rubbed out in four seconds and cannot, and neither of those is worse, and I have been here four days and I have stopped knowing which.*”

Nobody in that weigh-house thanked anybody.

The fever was forty weeks and no days old. A sheet of parchment two men long was read out loud in a building with the door open, by a man of sixty-two who is not the clerk and is paid nothing. The clerk said he would not read it and gave the reason twice, and the second reason was that in nineteen years nobody had ever asked whether that sheet was right.

Six people said out loud that the reading had been wrong about their own ground, and the clerk wrote all six down with the day on it, because there is a board outside his own door that says he will answer anything. It is the first record in that county ever made by a challenge instead of by a keeper.

And it went on the wall under a list of eleven lines, and five of the six were attached to no parcel at all. No names were read out, because there is no column on that sheet with a person’s name at the top of it.

And about nine people will read those six lines every morning for as long as that wall stands, and about nine of them will find the five that have no parcel against them in about four seconds, and the fifth thing a district reads on a Saturday morning is a thing nobody in that district can be checked against anybody about.

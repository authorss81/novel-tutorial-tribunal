# Chapter 564: The Sixth Day Of The Hundred And Second Week, And A Sunday, And On Which A Man Of Fifty-Eight Who Has Answered Everything For Nineteen Years Wakes At The Fifth Hour With A Thing He Has Worked Out And Says It Out Loud To His Wife And Then Says It Again To About Nine People At The Door Of His Own House And Then Has It Written On The Board Outside His Own Door In Nine Lines By A Boy Of Fourteen Who Is Not To Ask Anybody In This Hundred What They Remember And Who Goes On To Say Out Loud That The Reason He Gives For It Is Worse Than The Reason Against It, And That He Has Not Been Asked A Question About A Person In Nineteen Years And Has Now Put A Stop To The Only Way One Could Be Asked

Sixth day of the hundred and second week. His three hundred and fourteenth morning. One hundred and sixty-four days after the settlement. The fever forty weeks and one day. One hundred and eighteen days since the division. Sixty-four days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

The rising is in two days and the clerk was asked for that number twice before the seventh hour, which is two fewer than yesterday and neither of them was asked in a market.

---

---

There is a board outside the water-clerk’s own door, five feet by two, with a name on it and a description of what the name does, and it says that any person in the hundred might ask Iven Ashcombe anything and that he would answer it.

There was no qualification on it and there was none underneath.

On Saturday at about the sixth hour he had written under his own six lines — the six challenges, the day, the five that had no parcel against them — nine more lines, and the first of the nine said that no person in this hundred was to be asked what they remembered.

He had written them on Saturday night, in his own hand, and he had read them to his wife in the kitchen at about the ninth hour and she had asked him whether he had thought about it, and he had said that he had thought about it for four years, and she had said that he had thought about it for four years and had never once written it down, and he had said that was the same thing.

On Sunday morning he put all nine lines on the board in the open, in about nine sentences, at about the seventh hour, in front of about nine people, and a boy of fourteen wrote them out in chalk because the clerk is fifty-eight and cannot hold a board and write on it.

Nobody on that green thanked anybody.

---

The first thing he said was why it was nine lines and not one.

“*Say the rest of that, because a man who has answered anything for nineteen years does not put one line on a board and I have been standing here since the seventh hour and there are about nine of us and I would rather be told.*”

“*Because a one-line rule is a rule about a person and I could not get it right. A nine-line rule is a rule about what may be asked and I have had four years to get that right and I have not.*”

“*Say what the nine lines say, and say them in the order they are on the board and not in the order you like them.*”

“*One: that no person in this hundred is to be asked what they remember.*

“*Two: that it holds in a building, in a yard, in a lane, in a market, at a door, on a boat and at a fire.*

“*Three: that it holds of anybody, including me, including a stranger, including a man with a slate.*

“*Four: that it holds on the rising and every day after it.*

“*Five: that a man who is asked may answer what he knows now and not what he remembers.*

“*Six: that the rule does not stop a man giving a true thing he can be checked against.*

“*Seven: that it does not stop a man giving a number off that sheet on that wall, because that sheet is read every morning and can be read again.*

“*Eight: that I am not to be asked what I remember by anybody, including my wife.*

“*Nine: that it has no day on it and does not lapse and there is no form in this county that suspends it.*”

Nobody on that green said anything for about four seconds.

“*Say the rest of that, because there is not a person standing here who would have expected the eighth of those nine from a man who wrote the other eight.*”

“*There would not. I wrote the other eight first and I left that one out on purpose for two days and I put it in last night, and I am going to say why, and the reason is the worst thing I have ever put on a board.*”

---

“*Four years ago I made that promise. Not in words. I had it cut and I hung it and I have answered everything anybody has asked me for nineteen years, and about nine times a year that has been a question I would rather had gone to my wife and about four times a year it has been a question I did not have an answer to and said so.*

“*And in April a man of fifty-four paid the rate on eleven parcels and a woman of forty-one asked me on a market step whether her season was still hers, and I told her, because I answer everything, and about thirty people heard it, and about nine of them understood all of it.*

“*And in the last four days I have been asked what I remember about March six times. About the cut. About the ditch. About the two years the rate was not paid. And I remember six things and I do not know which of the six are the thing that happened.*”

“*Say the rest of that, because a man who has been a water-clerk for nineteen years remembers a ditch.*”

“*I remember what the low meadow looked like in the first week of April in the year before last, because I walked it and I put my hand in it. I remember what the low meadow looked like in the first week of April last year, and it was worse. I remember what it looked like in the first week of April the year before that, and I was not on it, and I was in another county for four days of it, and a man told me about it at a gate.*

“*Those are three memories and one of them is not mine. That is the whole of why this board is on this wall.*”

Nobody on that green thanked anybody and nobody on that green was sorry for anybody.

---

“*Say the rest of that, because you have told me the reason against it three times and you have not told me the reason for it once and about nine people are standing here and one of them has a figure of his own in his head that he did not put on that wall.*”

“*Because of the promise.*

“*I promised on this board that any person in the hundred might ask me anything and I would answer it. That promise has been kept every week for nineteen years and about nine thousand times, and in April it produced a woman of forty-one standing on a step being told the whole of the true of her situation in front of about thirty people. I could not do otherwise. I have said so on that step.*

“*And then the same promise produced a stranger with a hand on my wall telling a market about eleven seasons in four days, and about nine people repeated it inside a week, and one man came nine miles to say a line of it was wrong. That is the promise working. That is the promise doing exactly what a promise does.*

“*And in the last four days the same promise produced six people at this door asking me what I remember, and I am the only man in this hundred anybody can be sent for, and there is nothing behind the answer except an old man who was not on the ground for one day of the three.*”

“*Say the rest of that, because you are describing a promise that has worked.*”

“*I am describing a promise that has worked nineteen years and is now going to hurt about nine people and help everybody after them. That is what a promise does. I would write it on that board again in the morning if nobody asked me, and I have written this under it in the dark last night and I would rather somebody rub it out than carry it.*”

“*Then why did you not rub it out.*”

“*Because if I rub it out then in about four years there will be a man in this hundred who says something out loud in a room and I will be the one he walks up to, and about nine people will look about, and there will be nothing.*”

---

Then the questions, and there were about nine of them over about an hour, and about four people said the same thing in six different ways.

A man of about fifty, who has a seat, said that he had wanted to ask whether the low meadow had ever gone under before, in any year, and had been putting it off for about nine years because it would be answered.

“*So it stops me asking the thing I have wanted to ask for nine years.*”

“*Yes.*”

“*Then it is not for me.*”

“*It is not for any of us and it is not against any of us and I want that said out loud on this green, because in about four days somebody is going to say I stopped a hundred asking what they remember, and about nine people will believe him, and it will be about nine people who wanted to ask and were afraid.*”

“*Then say that in those words.*”

“*I am not stopping anybody from asking what they know. I am stopping anybody from asking what they remember. Those are two different things and in this county they have been the same thing for nineteen years and the reason they have been the same thing is that I am the only person anybody can be sent for, and a man who can be sent for is asked about the past and never about the ground.*”

Nobody on that green thanked anybody and nobody on that green was sorry for anybody.

---

Then the woman who had been at the sixth hour on Saturday asked the thing that about four other people had been working towards all morning.

“*Say the rest of that, because you have made nine lines and I have watched nine of us try to get round all of them, and I would like to know how many of us could.*”

“*Say what you want to ask.*”

“*I want to know whether it is legal.*”

Nobody on that green said anything for about four seconds.

“*Say the rest of that, because it is the only question anybody has asked you today and you have answered six other things.*”

“*There is no form in the hundred of Withermere, in the county of Kell, or in this water that says a man may put a rule on his own door about what may be asked of him. There is no form that says he may not. There is no bench, no counter, and no person I can be sent to.*

“*Say the rest of that, because you have told me twice this week that there is nobody in this county you can be sent to, and you have made that sound like a thing about you and it is not.*”

“*It is a thing about the hundred and it is a thing about you. I have not broken a rule. I have put one where a rule may be put, which is on the side of a building, and I have put it myself. And no person in this hundred is to say to another person in this hundred that they may not ask me, because the only person who could say it is me, and I have just said they may not.*”

“*Say the rest of that, because you have just made a law and said you cannot be sent for about it, and that is the same sentence about four times.*”

“*It is the same sentence about four times and it is the only sentence anybody in this county can make. There are no others.*”

---

Then the boy of fourteen put the chalk down and said the thing he had been holding since about the eighth hour.

He is fourteen and he writes the week’s sheet on a Friday night for a man who pays him a penny, and he has been asked to write a thing five times in his life and has said he does not know what to do with it four times.

“*Say what you are standing in front of, because you are the clerk’s boy and you have had nine lines to write and I have watched you write all nine and you have not written one thing you did not understand, and about four people here have said so, and I would like to hear you.*”

“*I am standing in front of nine lines and I have written all nine and I understood all nine and I want to say the one I did not understand.*”

“*Say it.*”

“*Line eight. You are not to be asked what you remember by anybody, including your wife. I did not understand that one because I have never seen my father asked anything by anybody, and if he was asked something and did not answer it then nobody in my house would know, and there is nobody else in the county to ask, and that is the same as nobody knowing.*”

Nobody on that green thanked anybody.

“*Say the rest of that, because that is the best question anybody has asked on this green this morning and you are fourteen and you have been paid a penny for it.*”

“*The rest of that is that my father holds a season on about nine acres and has held it for nine years, and he was on that water in the first week of April three years running, and he has not said one word about it to anybody, and if that is a thing he remembers then there is a man four miles south who has taken it and does not know that a boy of fourteen has a father who remembers it.*”

He put the chalk on the step.

“*And I have written nine lines and I have not asked him anything, and that is what line one says, and it took me about a second to work out that I had just obeyed the first line I had written down in my life.*”

---

Ilyan had not said anything for about an hour and eleven minutes, which was the longest he had gone in about four days without asking anybody a question.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Iven Ashcombe to him, at about the ninth hour.

“*I am standing in front of a man who has just made it illegal for nine men to ask nine other men what they remember, and who did it on his own door, and who has told nine people that he expects to be blamed for it in about four days.*”

“*Say the rest of that.*”

“*The rest of that is that in a chalk town about fifty miles off there is a prohibition that nobody in a county may be asked about the original, and it was broken once, and what it kept out was a question and not a person.*”

“*Say the rest of that, because I have been in four counties in nine days and you have never once told me what a rule is.*”

“*This is the second one I have seen and it is not the same one, and it is worse. That one was made by an office. This one was made by a man about his own door with his wife asleep over the back, on a Sunday, out loud, in front of nine people, and he put his own name on the front of it.*”

Nobody on that green thanked anybody and nobody on that green was sorry for anybody.

“*Say the rest of that, and then go away, because I have not asked you one question today and I would like it to stay that way until Tuesday.*”

“*I will say one thing and then I will go. The reason you have given against it is that a hundred will lose things. The reason you have given for it is that about nine people will be protected from being used. And you have said that the second reason is worse than the first, and I think you are right, and I have been in this county four days and that is the first time I have wanted a thing on that board to be wrong.*”

“*Then you are the first person in nineteen years who has wanted my board to be wrong, and you are a stranger, and I would have thought better of you and worse.*”

He went and put his hand on the nine lines, which were in chalk and which any person in that town could rub out with a rag in about four seconds, and which about nine people read every morning of their lives and which nobody rubbed out.

The fever was forty weeks and one day old. A man of fifty-eight who had answered everything for nineteen years put nine lines on a board outside his own door on a Sunday morning and said out loud in front of about nine people that the reason for it was worse than the reason against it, and that the reason for it was a promise he had made and kept every week for nineteen years.

And a boy of fourteen wrote all nine out in chalk, and understood all nine, and asked about the one he did not, and worked out inside about a second that he had obeyed the first rule he had ever written down in his life.

And nobody in that hundred may now ask anybody what they remember, and there is no form in the county of Kell that suspends it, and there is no form in the county of Kell that made it, and it has no day on it.

And the rising is in two days, and the water-clerk has promised on a board to answer anything, and has promised underneath it to answer nothing he can only remember.

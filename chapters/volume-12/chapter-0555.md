# Chapter 555: The Fourth Day Of The Hundred And First Week, And A Friday, And Which Is The Market, And On Which A Market Of About Three Hundred People Is Held In A Green In A Town Of About Eleven Hundred, And A Woman Of Forty-One Named Hester Nunn Walks Up To A Step And Asks A Man In The Open Air In Front Of About Thirty People Whether Her Season Is Still Hers, And He Answers Her, And He Has To Answer Her, And Then A Man Of Thirty-One Offers To Pay The Rate On Her Six Acres Out Of A Purse With One Pound And Seventeen Pence In It And Is Refused By Her In About Four Sentences For A Reason That Is The Whole Of The Volume In Four Sentences

Fourth day of the hundred and first week. His three hundred and fifth morning. One hundred and fifty-five days after the settlement. The fever thirty-eight weeks and six days. One hundred and nine days since the division. Fifty-five days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

A market in Withermere is on a Friday, and there is no other day, and it has not moved in about two hundred years.

About three hundred people came into the green between the eighth hour and the tenth. It is not two thousand and it is not a thousand. There is a great deal of hay in it and not much of anything else.

---

The standing business of a Withermere market is not hay.

There is a stone step at the north end of the green with an oak bar across the front of it, and the water-clerk stands on that step with a slate and a green book. Between the hour of ten and the hour of twelve anybody may stand at the bottom of it and offer a parcel’s season for sale. The clerk writes it on the slate, and it goes on the wall inside the weigh-house on the next day the wall is read.

The parcels offered on the step on a Friday are parcels whose season is not already taken up.

There were nine of them. Four of the old pasture at the top end, two of the sallow bed, one of the stubble, and two of the low ground that had not been worked in nine years.

None of the eleven was on the step. They had been taken up in April, and the figure at the end of the square on each of their lines was the figure for this year. The man who had taken them up was still holding them, and would go on holding them on the rising unless somebody took them off him. There was no step in Withermere for putting that.

---

The woman came up to the step at about ten minutes to the eleventh hour with a basket on her arm, and she was about forty-one and there were two children not with her.

She had been in that market for about four years and she had bought at that step and she had never once stood at the bottom of it and asked.

“*Mr Ashcombe.*”

“*Mrs Nunn.*”

“*Is my season still mine.*”

About thirty people were within about nine feet of that step. A market in Withermere is a quiet place until a woman asks a question at it.

Ilyan was standing about eleven feet back with a folded sheet of paper in the inside pocket of his coat, and the sheet had a name at the foot of the first page, and he did not move and he did not say anything, and about four seconds went past with both of those facts in the room.

He did not answer for about four seconds. The man at the step was in profile from where Ilyan stood, and what the four seconds were was not him deciding whether to tell the truth. They were him deciding how to say it in a way that would not be a sentence anybody could repeat.

“*No, Mrs Nunn.*”

“*Say the rest of it, because you are on a step and there are about thirty people here and I have asked you a plain question.*”

“*Your six acres, the second close off the dyke, were taken up on the thirteenth of April by a man out of the district, at the rate and the penny, and the figure at the end of the square is his year, and it is his until the water turns.*”

“*Say the rest of it.*”

“*That is the whole of it. That is the whole of what I have got and there is not a worse version of it in this hundred for you to be told instead.*”

---

That is what a board does.

It is not that a man was cruel to her in a market. He made a promise on a board outside his own door nineteen years ago, in front of about nine people, that any person in the hundred might ask him anything and he would answer it. He has kept it every week of nineteen years. On this Friday he kept it in front of about thirty people, about the worst thing anybody has ever asked him, and he could not have done otherwise, and everybody on that green knew that inside about four seconds.

Nobody in that market said anything for about four seconds.

Then a man of about fifty at the back said, to nobody, *well*.

Then the market went on, because it is a market and there is hay in it.

---

She did not cry and she did not argue and she put the basket down on the oak bar and stood with her hands on it.

“*What is it to me,*” she said. “*Say the rest of that, because I have paid this rate for six years and I have never once asked you what it was for and I would like the answer said out loud on this step while the hay is being sold.*”

“*Two shillings and a penny the acre. You have six acres of the second close off the dyke, and you have never done a day’s work on it that I know of, and the rate on it is twelve shillings and sixpence a year.*”

“*Say the rest of it, because that is a number and I asked for a whole thing.*”

“*That money is what cuts the ditch between your close and the low meadow. Without it the ditch fills in about two years, and then the low meadow is under water in a wet year.*”

“*Say the rest of it.*”

“*That is what it is for. It is not a payment for having six acres. It is the ditch.*”

“*Then I will have the ditch.*”

“*You cannot have the ditch, Mrs Nunn. You have lost the season and not the parcel. The parcel is yours. The ditch is the hundred’s, and in about two years the hundred will not cut it, because the hundred cuts it with the rate.*”

“*Say the rest of that, because you have just told a woman of forty-one on a step that her rate is somebody else’s money and I want to hear you say the other half of it out loud in front of these people.*”

“*This year the rate on that stretch is his money and not yours, and he will have the ditch cut, and he will have it cut a good deal better than it has been cut in the four years I have been watching him do it. That is the whole of what I have to give you this morning and I have given it to you in the open air because you asked me in the open air.*”

She stood on that step in front of about thirty people and understood the whole of it in about four seconds, and it is worth saying that she was a quicker person than the man who asked the question.

“*Then that is the worst of it,*” she said. “*He is doing it properly.*”

“*He is doing it properly and he has paid every penny and he has broken nothing, and I have told you so out loud on a step in a market because you asked me on a step in a market, and I would not have been able to live in this town if I had done otherwise.*”

---

Then he went to the pump. He had a purse with one pound and seventeen pence in it, and he had watched a woman of forty-one stand on a step in front of about thirty people and be told the true of the whole of her situation by a man who could not lie because of a board.

She refused him in about four sentences and she did not raise her voice and there were about nine people close enough to hear it.

“*No.*”

“*Say the rest of that, because I have been refused in about nine rooms in a hundred and fifty-five days and I have learned that the four sentences are the whole of it.*”

“*You have come to me with about a year of your own living in that purse and you are going to offer me twelve shillings and sixpence, and if I take it, then a man I have never met and cannot be sent for has bought my season, and the wall will say his year is mine.*”

“*It will say whoever pays the rate.*”

“*It will say the year at the end of the square on my six acres is a year a stranger paid for, and your name is nowhere on the sheet because there is no column for one, and that is the only way anybody finds out it was you.*”

“*Say the rest of it, because I have never seen this and I do not know if it is true.*”

“*He will not offer me anything for it, because he does not want it. And I will have bought a year from a man with a bad leg who cannot be in this room in four years to say he did it for me.*”

“*Say the rest of it.*”

“*The rest of it is that a season bought with a stranger’s money is still a season bought from me, and you knew that before you opened the purse, and I would rather you had said the four sentences first and the money second.*”

He put the purse away.

“*Say the rest of that, because that is the best refusal I have had in a hundred and fifty-five days and I am not going to be a man who takes a good one and says nothing about it.*”

She looked at him for a moment and then she picked her basket off the oak bar.

“*You knew it,*” she said. “*That is what I am refusing. I am not refusing the money. Go away.*”

---

The clerk came off the step at about the twelfth hour and put the slate under his arm and stood beside the oak bar with about nine people still near it, and he said one thing out loud that he had not said on the step.

“*About nine of the thirty people standing on that green about twenty minutes ago know that Mrs Nunn has lost the season on six acres, and I put it there, and there is nothing on the wall of that building that has changed.*”

“*Say the rest of it, because I have been in this county for one day and a half and I do not know yet what anything means here.*”

“*The rest of it is that in four years nobody in this hundred has been ruined by a document. This morning a woman of forty-one was ruined by a sentence, in the open, in front of about thirty people, by a man who was obliged to say it, and I have not gone home, and there is nothing on this earth she can be sent to, and there is nothing on this earth I can be sent to either.*”

He put the slate on the oak bar.

“*That is the difference between a lock and a wall, and I have got about nineteen years to work out which of them I would rather have, and I have not worked it out, and I have not got anybody to work it out with.*”

Nobody in that market thanked anybody and nobody in that market was sorry for anybody.

The fever was thirty-eight weeks and six days old. A market of about three hundred people held its ordinary business in a green in a town of about eleven hundred. A woman of forty-one asked a plain question at a stone step in the open air. The only person in that hundred who could answer it had promised on a board outside his own door to answer anything, and he did, and about thirty people heard a household lose a year.

And a man of thirty-one with a bad leg and a purse with one pound and seventeen pence in it offered to pay twelve shillings and sixpence to keep a stranger’s six acres in a family. He was refused in four sentences by the woman it was for, and the fourth of them was that a season bought with a stranger’s money is still a season bought from her.

And the wall of the weigh-house was not touched that day. The sheet on it has no column with a person’s name at the top of it and never has. The figure at the end of the square on the second close off the dyke is the year of a man of about fifty-four who is four miles south of this town, and who is not here, and who has paid every penny of it.

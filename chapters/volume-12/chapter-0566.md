# Chapter 566: The First Day Of The Hundred And Third Week, And A Tuesday, And On Which The Water Comes Over A Block Of Limestone With A Line Cut Round It At About The Seventh Hour And Every Season In That Hundred Goes, And About Thirty Men Meet In A Weigh-House Sixty Feet Long At Midday While About Three Hundred People Stand In A Green In The Same Hour, And A Man Of Thirty-One Says One Thing In It Which Is Not The Argument, And The Question An Old Man Gave Him On The Last Monday Of The Hundredth Week Is Put To That Room And Is Not Answered And Is Asked Wrong By One Man And Is Not Answerable By The Man Who Was Given It, And A Number Is Written Against A Common Of About Eight Hundred Acres Which Is Not Fifty-Eight And Is Defensible And Is About A Width Of A Common And A Number Of Tents And Not About People

First day of the hundred and third week. His three hundred and sixteenth morning. One hundred and sixty-six days after the settlement. The fever forty weeks and three days. One hundred and twenty days since the division. Sixty-six days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county. The day before was the seventh and last day of the hundred and second week.

The rising is today and there is no number for it. About nine people asked the clerk for one before the seventh hour and he gave them the same answer nine times, which was that it is a day of a river and not a day of a number.

---

It came over the stone at about the seventh hour, and about nine people were standing on the bridge when it did.

There is no bell for it. In a town of about eleven hundred people there is no bell for the rising. The clerk goes down and looks at the line cut round a block of limestone that a man of this hundred put there about two hundred and thirty years ago, and his name is still legible on it. He holds his hand flat on the stone for the length of a count of five, then goes up and writes the day at the foot of a sheet of parchment two men long.

Nobody stopped him. Nobody stopped any of it.

---

At midday about thirty men were in the weigh-house. There were forty-one in it by the second hour, because a hundred in this district is about thirty men and about nine people came to listen.

The hundred has met in that room on the rising for about nineteen years. It has no office, no book of its own and no minute of anything, and it is summoned by a man with a slate who is paid four pounds a year out of a rate of two shillings and a penny the acre.

He read the eleven. Parcel, acres, taking, figure in the square at the end of each, in that order. Every season in that hundred went at the moment he read the last one.

“*Say the rest of that, because you have read off the end of eleven men’s year in about nine seconds and nobody said a word.*”

“*They say the word on Friday at the step. And there are eleven tents on the low side that will be under a foot of water on Thursday, and there is nothing on that sheet about them. There is no column on that sheet with a person’s name at the top of it, and there never has been one.*”

Nobody in that weigh-house thanked anybody.

“*Then say the first number, because you have a slate in your hand and thirty men are in this room.*”

“*Three boards are in at the gate and the gate takes six. Six holds the water off seven holdings and off the cut on the low meadow. Six puts a foot of water on four closes and on the low common in about two days.*”

“*Say the rest of it, because about nine people in this room have heard that in a market and want to know what it does.*”

“*Four closes, and four men standing in this room, and about eight hundred acres of common that belongs to nobody, and fifty-eight people on it under a sheet of paper in a sack I have never seen. I can do it and I can leave it and I cannot do both, and no form anywhere in this county says a water-clerk may put a number of boards in a gate, and no form says he may not.*”

---

Then he stopped, and about thirty men waited.

“*Say what you are waiting for. You asked the stranger on Tuesday to be in this room and told him you would not say what would be said in it, and he has not asked you once.*”

Ilyan stood where he had been standing since the seventh hour, and about thirty men and about nine people who were not in the hundred turned round.

The thing he had carried about fifty miles was folded in four in the inside pocket of a coat and it was not in his hand and he did not put it up.

“*I am going to say one thing and it is not the argument, and then I am going to sit down on that bench and stay on it until somebody tells me I may get up, and I have not worked out which of us has the right to do that.*”

Nobody in that weigh-house said anything, and a man at the back turned his slate over.

“*Then say it.*”

“*Three boards are in at the gate and the gate takes six, and the other three are in a yard at Coldcombe because there is no money for them. I had that off a man with a wagon and a team of four, on the wharf at Coldcombe before I came down this water. He has hauled stone for eleven years for eleven men and has never once been asked a question at a gate, and he told me that on a plank with a bar in his hands.*”

He sat down.

“*Say the rest of that, because you have sat down.*”

“*The rest of that is that I have found the number of the boards and the number of the closes and the number of the days, and I have never once found out where the money for three boards comes from. I did not ask that man about money on his plank. He offered me a drink of water and I did not think to.*”

Nobody in that weigh-house thanked anybody.

“*Then this is the only room in this county where a number can be said out loud and argued with, and I have said four numbers in it and the argument about the water has not started.*”

---

It started at about the second hour, and it went the way a thing goes when about thirty men in a sixty-foot room have all been downstream for four years.

Then a man of about fifty-four with about seven hundred sheep came four miles into Withermere on his own legs, and about nine people turned round.

He stood at the back by the door for about twenty minutes while about four men argued about the low meadow, and then he said one sentence and the room went to one voice.

“*You are standing at the back of a weigh-house in a town of about eleven hundred people, about four miles from your own gate, and there is no seat for you because you are not a man of the hundred, and I have said that on a wall on Friday in four lines.*”

“*Then I will stand.*”

Nobody made him a seat.

He did not make a speech. He said four things and about thirty men heard all four.

He said that if the other three boards went in, the four closes would be under a foot for a fortnight, and about nine acres of new sward with them. He said he had lost a season of keep taking his own drain out on Saturday, and would still put the boards in and pay for them out of his own purse.

Then he said the thing nobody expected, which was that he had been wrong about the cut and had said so in this room on Wednesday last, and that a rule about a parcel and not about a water was the only part of the business that could not be defended.

Then he said the other thing, and this is the sentence the hundred wrote down.

“*Six boards does not hold the water off the four closes. Six boards holds the water off seven holdings and off the cut on the low meadow, and it puts a foot of water on four closes and on about eight hundred acres that belongs to nobody, on Thursday, with fifty-eight people on it. I have been wrong about the drain and I am right about that, and I would rather the hundred knew both at once than either one on its own.*”

Nobody in that weigh-house thanked anybody.

He stood at the back of a room with no seat in it for about nine more minutes while about thirty men said nothing, then went out and got into a cart himself, and about nine people saw him go.

---

Then the question came, from the bottom of the room, and it was not the question.

“*Say what you are standing in front of. I have a question and I have had it since Wednesday and it is not about the water.*”

“*Then ask it.*”

“*Ask the stranger whether he is going to be one of us or whether he is going to be four miles south in the water-season. Twelve days, and two of us have given him a wall to write on and one has given him a place in this room, and nobody has said which he is.*”

About four seconds went past. About nine people understood the whole of it and about nine heard a question about a season.

“*Say the rest of that, because that is the question as your hundred asks it and it is not the one that was put.*”

“*Then say the one that was put. I have been in nine rooms in twelve days and I am the only person in this county who has been given it, and thirty men will hear it here from a water-clerk and not from a stranger in a market.*”

He put his hand on the board on the wall and then took it off. There was nothing written on that board that said he might.

“*An old man of eighty-one in a chalk town about fifty miles off asked me on a Sunday, in a room with the door open, in front of about nine people, whether a man can be named in a place and still not be of it, and nobody answered him. He asked it on the sixth day of his hundredth week and he gave it to me on the last Monday of that week, at a porch step, in a town where nobody knew him. He had asked it twice before and been silent twice, and he would not be told the answer is no.*”

Nobody in that weigh-house said anything, and about four of them wrote nothing down.

“*Say the rest of that, because you have just put a man of eighty-one into my building and he is not in it.*”

“*He is not in it. About thirty men are. And I am not going to answer it, because the man in this room cannot be sent to, and that is the only thing about him worth anything, and if I answer it now then about nine people here will have a sentence about it and about nine will have a different sentence, and both of them will be about a man who is not in this room.*”

“*Then say that last part out loud, because a man of fifty-four has stood at the back of my room and said he would rather the hundred knew both halves at once.*”

“*The question was put to the hundred on the rising by a stranger who was asked it in a chalk town by a man of eighty-one who was not answered twice. This hundred has heard it. Nobody has answered it and nobody is going to answer it today, and no form in the county of Kell makes anybody answer. That is not a rule I made up on Sunday to keep a thing out. That is the whole of it.*”

About four people in that room said it at once, and in four different voices, that the man in question was a stranger and could go and be a stranger somewhere else.

“*You have said the answer to the question as this room asks it, which is whether he stays. I wrote the question on my own hand and read it out, and it is not that, and I am not going to let this hundred believe it is.*”

“*Then say it again so that we can answer it or not answer it properly.*”

He said it again, in the words an old man used on a step in a chalk town, and about thirty men in that room heard a question about a man and not about a season.

Nobody in that room answered it.

---

Then the last thing of the morning, and it was a slate and not a sheet, and about nine people watched him do it and about four understood it at the time.

He wrote the day’s figures on the slate in four columns, the way he had read them off the wall, and then he stopped at the low common.

The low common is about eight hundred acres and it belongs to nobody. It has no holder, no season and no line on the sheet with a figure in the square, and no form in this county says what may be written in the square at the end of a line for ground that is nobody’s.

He put the chalk on it.

“*Say what you are standing in front of. You are writing a number on a slate in front of about thirty men, and there is a camp on that ground with about fifty-eight people on it, and you have never seen their paper.*”

“*I have never seen their paper.*”

“*Then say what the number is about before you write it. After this morning there will not be a person on that common who can say what it means.*”

“*It is not about fifty-eight. It is about the ground. And I am going to write it in a way that I can be checked on, and I have done that every week for nineteen years, and I am going to write a number that is wrong on purpose in one direction and right in another, and I am going to say so before I write it.*”

Nobody in that weigh-house said anything, and the man who had been in that room for nineteen years did not look up.

“*Say the rest of that, because you have said wrong on purpose in front of thirty men and three hundred people and you have not written it yet.*”

“*The usable edge of that common is about nine hundred yards of the top of the rise where the tents stand. The rest of it stands in water in lines and no family will pitch on it. A tent wants about twenty feet of frontage for the tent and the fire and the churn and the line. I have measured two gaps between tents with my own feet against two gate-posts I know the distance of, and I have got twenty feet, and twenty feet times eleven tents is two hundred and twenty feet.*”

“*Say the rest of it, because you have said eleven tents and about nine people in this room have a daughter in one of them.*”

“*The rest of that is that eleven tents on that common would need two hundred and twenty feet of a nine-hundred-yard edge. That is a figure about a width of ground and a number of tents. It is true and it is not a number of people. I am going to write it in the square because there is nothing else I can put in the square, and a square with nothing in it is read by about nine people a morning as though the holder had gone.*”

He wrote it and turned the slate round. The square said two hundred and twenty feet.

“*Say the rest of that, because you have written a number of feet in a square that on about thirty other lines holds a number of years. Everybody who reads that sheet every morning is going to read it in four seconds and be wrong about it.*”

“*Yes. And the number in a square is the number of the holding, and a man with two hundred and twenty feet of the low common is nobody. Nobody is not a holding that can be let, and it cannot be given up, because there is nobody to give it up.*”

He set the chalk down.

“*And on Thursday morning there will be a foot of water on that common and fifty-eight people standing in it, and two hundred and twenty feet written on a slate in a room in this town. If anybody in this county says to those people that they had two hundred and twenty feet they were not lied to. They were not lied to and they have nowhere to put it. That is the first true number I have written that I knew would be useless in the same breath.*”

Nobody in that weigh-house thanked anybody.

Then it went on the wall at about the third hour in the afternoon, at the bottom of the sheet under everything else, with nothing at the top of it.

---

The fever was forty weeks and three days old. The water came over a block of limestone with a line cut round it at about the seventh hour and every season in a hundred of about thirty men went in about nine seconds, while about three hundred people stood in a green in the same hour.

A man of thirty-one said one thing in that room, and it was not the argument, and it was about a man with a wagon and a team of four who had never once been asked a question at a gate.

A man of fifty-four with about seven hundred sheep came four miles on his own legs and stood at the back in a room with no seat in it. He said he had been wrong about the drain and was right about the common, and would rather the hundred knew both at once than either on its own. He got into a cart himself.

And the question an old man gave him on the last Monday of the hundredth week, fifteen days ago, was put to about thirty men and was not answered. It was asked wrong by one man. It could not be answered by the man it had been given to. About four people answered a different question in four seconds and were told so in the open.

And a number is on a wall in Withermere that is not fifty-eight. It is two hundred and twenty feet, and it is arithmetically defensible, and it is about a width of a common and a number of tents and not about people. In two days there will be a foot of water on that common, and a woman of twenty-eight with a daughter of six on her hip will stand in front of that wall at about the seventh hour and understand it in about four seconds.

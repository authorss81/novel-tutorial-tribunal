# Chapter 551: The Seventh And Last Day Of The Hundredth Week, And A Monday, And On Which A Sheet Of Paper Comes Up A Lane Carried By A Boy Of About Seventeen, And A Man Of Thirty-One Has Carried A Sheet Of Paper For Three Days And Tries Once To Get It Into A Book Four Hundred And Eleven Years Old And Is Refused In A Porch In About Four Seconds By The Woman Who Keeps It, And A Man Of Eighty-One Asks Him To Carry A Question To A Place Where Nobody Knows Him And Does Not Answer It Himself, And A Man Of Thirty-One Gives Up A Bed At The Top Of A Joiner’s Shop And Walks Out Of That Town At About The Sixth Hour In The Evening

Seventh and last day of the hundredth week. His three hundred and first morning. One hundred and fifty-one days after the settlement. The fever thirty-eight weeks and two days. One hundred and five days since the division. Fifty-one days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town two days from where he is standing.

The sheet had come up the lane on the Friday morning, in the hand of a boy of about seventeen who walks the twenty-one miles between this town and the wharf at Coldcombe and back in three days, and he had stood at the bottom of the lane and waited until about nine people had seen him stand there.

It had been in the inside pocket of Ilyan’s coat since then, folded in four, and he had not unfolded it in a room.

---

The morrow is the first day of another week and this is the last day of the hundredth, and a town that counts nothing counts that much without being told.

About the eighth hour he was on the step of the meeting-house. The door was shut, because it was a Monday and the book was not read out on a Monday, and the tower at the west end that has never had a bell in it had a gull sitting in the top of it.

The boy of fourteen was not there. He writes the week’s sheet on a Friday night in chalk on the end wall of the smithy and on a Monday he is somewhere with a cart.

Nobody stopped him. That is a thing to put down about a market town of about two thousand four hundred people on the last day of the hundredth week: nobody stopped him.

---

He unfolded it in the porch, standing, because the porch has a bench and the bench is six feet long and there is a mark worn into the stone about two-thirds along it.

The outside of it said a name in another county and then said the name of a woman he had not met and then said a street four hundred miles off, in a hand he knew, and that hand had written *forwarded* and had written nothing else about the envelope.

Inside there were two sheets. The first was four lines and it was dated the eleventh of April, and the boy who carried it up the lane said the parcel it came in with had been at four offices before it came to this lane.

> *To whoever keeps the book that these four pages came from.*

> *There are eleven of us on the fen at Withermere and we worked two hundred and ten acres of the old pasture between the dyke and the low meadow. In April a man came round with a purse and paid the rate on every one of the eleven and the season with it. Since then eleven seasons of ours have been his. He has not lied to anybody and nobody here can say he has done wrong.*

> *The day the water comes over the tally stone he will hold them for another year, and the water is coming up now, and I have never seen the stone and I cannot tell you how far off it is. Our beasts have nowhere to go and there is no form in this hundred that says what a man may do about a season that is not his.*

> *I do not know who to write to. I have been told the four pages came from a place four hundred miles off and I have written to it. I am sorry for the trouble of it. — Hester Nunn, of Withermere.*

The second sheet was four lines as well and it was in a different hand and it was not longer.

> *It is a true letter. I have had it since April and I have had it read to me by a man of thirty-four who cannot be kept from writing things down.*

> *I have no standing in that hundred and neither have you, and there is nothing I can send that would be worth the water it takes to get there.*

> *I am not coming. I am not sending anybody. I am telling you the true of it and I am not asking you for anything.*

> *If you go, I want it written down somewhere that I did not ask.*

He read the second sheet four times. About four seconds went past between the third time and the fourth, and nobody in that porch counted them.

---

Then he went and found the woman who keeps the book, and he found her in about four minutes because there is nowhere else in that town she is.

She was in the porch too, coming out of the doorway with her key in her hand, and she had a bundle of something under her arm.

He did not say good afternoon. He has never once said good afternoon to her in a hundred and fifty-one days and she has never once asked him to.

“*Say what you have come for, because you are standing in my porch with a sheet folded in four and you have had it three days.*”

“*I want it entered.*”

“*Say the rest of that.*”

“*At the back of the book. On a fresh leaf. With the day on it. Ruling nothing and heading nothing, the way you have done five things in five weeks.*”

The key stopped turning.

“*No,*” said Marda Rill.

“*Say why.*”

“*I have given you that reason in a square in front of about two hundred people and in a market and in a room, and you have heard it every time, and I am not going to say it a fourth time to save either of us the trouble of listening to it.*”

“*That is not a reason, that is a refusal with a speech on it.*”

About four seconds went past on that step.

“*Then take the one without the speech on it. A letter is not a finding. If I enter that at the back of my book then a woman I have never met is a thing in Bramblefold’s record on the say-so of a man who cannot be sent for, and she has not been asked, and she is four hundred miles off, and there is no form in this county that would let me ask her.*”

“*It is a true letter.*”

“*I do not doubt the letter. I have never doubted anything you have brought up this lane. That is not the half of it that is wrong.*”

She put her hand flat on the door frame, the way she does, and about four seconds went past and she worked out what to say next and said it.

“*You have come to me twice in five weeks with a thing you wanted in a book. Both times what you wanted in the book was a person, and both times the person was not in the room. That is the whole of why I say no and the reason has not got any shorter while you have been standing on my step.*”

Nobody in that porch thanked anybody.

“*Say the rest of it, because I have about four questions and I am not going to leave without asking any of them.*”

“*The rest of it is that there is nothing in this county that sends anybody to a person four hundred miles off. If I enter that letter, then in a hundred years a keeper will read it and know that a woman called Nunn wrote to us, and in four years somebody will want to know what she was owed and there will be no way on this earth to ask her. I would be writing a person into a book where she cannot be reached and where nobody can be sent for. I have refused you twice for a smaller version of the same thing.*”

He stood on that step with his leg out at the angle he stands it at.

“*Then what would you do with it.*”

“*Carry it. That is all I have ever asked anybody to do with anything in this county and you have heard me ask it in about nine rooms.*”

---

He had it in his hand when Barnaby Rill came up the lane, which was about the tenth hour, on a stick, and nobody had sent for him and nobody had told him anything was going on.

The old man stopped at the bottom of the porch step and looked at the sheet in the man’s hand and did not ask what it was.

“*Where are you going.*”

“*A place on the water. A town called Withermere. A day down and two days up, and the water is only up for it for about nine weeks in the year and it goes down after the rising.*”

Barnaby Rill worked that out twice, the way he works everything.

“*Then go while it is up. You have never in my hearing shown the least sense about a thing that has a season on it and I would not want to hear that you missed it.*”

Then he was quiet for a bit, in the way of a man of eighty-one who knows he is about to ask something.

“*Is there a book where you are going.*”

“*There is a sheet of paper about the length of two men on a wall.*”

“*On a wall.*”

“*Hung up in a building where they weigh wool. And any person in that town may walk up to it and read it, and about nine people walk up to it on a day, and that is what a clerk told a man on a wharf.*”

The old man put his weight on the stick and thought about it in the open air where anybody coming down the lane could hear.

“*Ask them there whether a man can be named in a place and still not be of it.*”

“*Barnaby—*”

“*I asked that in a room on Sunday in front of about nine people and nobody answered me, and I have been that person twice in my life in that town and said nothing both times. You know that. It is written in four places.*”

“*It is written in four places.*”

“*Then I will not ask it a third time in the one town where everybody knows I asked it and nobody answered. I would rather it were asked out loud, by somebody, in a place where nobody has heard of me and there is nothing behind my asking it but a man who is eighty-one.*”

Nobody in that lane said anything for about four seconds, and a woman of about forty going up to the wool bench said *good afternoon* to the old man and not to either of the other two.

“*And I am not going to tell you the answer is no,*” he said. “*I have never had an answer. I have had a silence twice, and a silence is not a no, and I am eighty-one and I am not going to be the man who turns up in a book in a hundred years having been wrong about a thing he was sure of.*”

---

Then he went up to the top of the joiner’s shop and gave the bed up, and the joiner was not there and the woman who kept the shop was, and she had the sixpence back in her hand before he had finished the sentence.

The bed was paid to the end of the week. This was the last day of the week.

“*You are not going to the assembly,*” she said.

“*I am not.*”

“*There is a woman in the upper row who has asked about you twice this week. I said I did not know where you slept because I did not know you slept here, and then I looked in the book for the address and there is no column for an address in that book and there has never been one.*”

Nobody in that room thanked anybody and nobody in that room was sorry for anybody.

He had a bundle that weighed about nine pounds, and in it a cracked steel pen, a cold tin of bitter tea, a change of shirt, a spoon, and the paper of a leaf that had come back four hundred miles and had been in the inside pocket of that coat since the third day and had not been looked at twice and was not looked at now.

He walked out of the town at about the sixth hour in the evening, up the lane behind the bottom of it, past the wall with the white stones in it, and about four of the nine people who saw him go said good afternoon and the rest of them did not.

At the top of the lane he stopped, once, and looked back at a square with a pump at the top of it and a tower with no bell in it, and he did not think a sentence, because there is no form in the county of Aldwick that says a man may keep a thought in a town he is leaving.

The fever was thirty-eight weeks and two days old. A sheet of paper came up a lane on a Friday last in a year this town does not count and was still in a coat on a Monday, and a woman who has been the only person anybody in that county can be sent for about for nineteen years refused to put a person into her book on the say-so of a man who cannot be sent for, and gave the reason in four sentences in a porch, and the reason was that in a hundred years somebody will want to know what the woman was owed and there will be no way on this earth to ask her.

And a man of eighty-one stood at the bottom of a porch step and asked him to carry a question to a place where nobody knew him, and did not answer it, and would not be told the answer was no, because he had had a silence twice and a silence is not a no.

And a man of thirty-one gave up a bed at the top of a joiner’s shop that had been paid to the end of the week, on the last day of the week, and walked out of that town at about the sixth hour in the evening with about one pound and threepence and a sheet of paper and no address in this county, because there is no column for an address in that book and there has never been one.

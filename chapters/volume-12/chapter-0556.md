# Chapter 556: The Fifth Day Of The Hundred And First Week, And A Saturday, And On Which A Man Of Thirty-One Walks The Fen Pasture Between A Dyke And A Low Meadow And Is Put Off One Of Them By A Man With A Dog In About Four Seconds, And Then Goes Into A Ditch As The Light Went That Was Not There In February And Is Pulled Out Of It By A Man With A Pole, And Is Told By That Man, Standing In Six Inches Of Water, That Nine Men In That District Have Known About The Ditch Since March And Not One Of Them Has Said It Out Loud, And Is Given The Reason

Fifth day of the hundred and first week. His three hundred and sixth morning. One hundred and fifty-six days after the settlement. The fever thirty-nine weeks and no days. One hundred and ten days since the division. Fifty-six days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

He got out of bed at the fifth hour and walked out of the town and nobody stopped him, because on Withermere green at the fifth hour on a Saturday in the water-season there is nobody on it but a man getting water.

---

The ground is not like anything he has walked on.

There is no hill in it anywhere. The pasture runs from the dyke on the west to a low meadow on the east. It is about two miles by three, and it is all of it inside a ring of water that is neither a river nor a ditch and has no name that any of the nine men on the towpath used.

The water was up about a foot and it had been up about a foot since the day before he arrived. The standing water and the grazing water were the same water, and everything on that ground had been under nine inches of it and had come up into the light that morning with the smell of a cold cellar on it.

Salt grass, and sea-pink in the wetter places, and a lizard on a warm stone that there were not supposed to be stones on.

---

He worked out the eleven by the parcels and by the ditch, because the ditches are the boundaries and a man who has been counting things for a hundred and fifty-six days will count a ditch.

The second close off the dyke, six acres, the one a woman of forty-one lost on a Friday. Then a narrow strip of three. Then the long grazing, forty-four, which is the largest of them and the reason a man with seven hundred sheep came four miles for it.

He was on the long grazing at about the seventh hour with the light still good and a dog came round the corner of a ditch at about four miles an hour and a man of about thirty-three came after it at a walk.

“*Say what you are standing on.*”

“*Say what I am standing on.*”

“*You are standing on nine acres of new sward that a man sowed in March, and there is a ditch along the top of it that was not there in February. If you had been standing on this side of it in March you would be standing in nine inches of water, and I would be explaining to somebody else where you had gone.*”

“*Say the rest of that, because I have been walking for two hours and I would like to know whether I am to be sorry now or later.*”

“*You are not to be sorry at all. You are on the wrong side of a cut drain and it is going to be a foot wider by the rising. Go back over it and keep to the old line and I will not follow you.*”

He went back over it. It took him a moment to find it. The old ditch was nine feet to the left of the new one and had sallow growing in the bottom of it, and a man with a bad leg and a boot worn through on the outside found the difference between the two in about four seconds of not being able to lift his foot.

---

He went on west for another mile, because west is where the main dyke is and the main dyke is the thing that keeps a fen off a pasture, and at about the ninth hour he came to a place where the dyke had a gap in it about nine feet wide.

The gap was not a breach. It was a cut. The earth was stacked neatly on the inside of it in a row, and the cut was about four feet down and about two feet wide and it went away to the north in a dead straight line, and it was full of water.

He got his hand on the top of a gatepost to get over the old dyke and the gatepost came out of the ground.

---

What happened after that took about nine seconds and he was in the ditch to the waist with his bad leg folded under him and the water going round his chest because the ditch was fuller at the bottom than at the top, and he could not get a purchase on anything, and the light was going.

A pole came out over the top of the cut and a voice on the other end of it said one word, twice, and then four more words, and he got hold of the pole with both hands.

“*Say the rest of that, because I have been in about nine feet of water and a gatepost and I do not know which of the two things I have just been saved from.*”

“*You have been saved from going down. Sit on that side and do not get up until I have the gatepost out, because if you get up in there you will go down.*”

He lay on the inside of the dyke on wet sallow for about four minutes while a man of about fifty-one levered a post out of a soft footing with a crowbar and threw it clear.

Then the man came over and looked at him and did not offer a hand and did not make a joke.

“*You are the stranger on the boat.*”

“*I am.*”

“*I saw you on the green. I was going to cut this and I have not said anything to anybody and I am about to say something to you, and I want you to understand that those are two different acts and I am doing both in the wrong order.*”

He put the crowbar down on the bank.

“*That cut there is a ditch. It is on a parcel he holds the season on. He cut it in March on his own ground, at the top of his own ground, and he has done nothing that this hundred has a word against.*”

“*Say the rest of it, because I have a bad leg and I am lying in a ditch and I would like the whole of it.*”

“*The rest of it is where the water in that ditch goes. North for a mile, and then it comes back east into the low meadow, which is the common mowing, and then off the meadow into four closes that are not his. Those four flood first. Then it goes on across the low side to about eight hundred acres that is nobody’s at all.*”

“*Can he be stopped.*”

“*By nobody.*”

“*Say that again, because it is the whole of what I came here for and I want to hear a man who cuts ditches say it.*”

“*There is no form in this hundred, this county, or this water that says a man may not cut a drain on a parcel he holds the season on, at the top of that parcel, running north. The rule is about the parcel. The rule is not about the water.*”

He picked the crowbar up and looked at it rather than at the man in the ditch.

“*A rule that governs a parcel and not a water is a rule that can be kept while somebody else drowns. That is the whole of what has happened to eleven households in this hundred and it is the first time I have said it out loud.*”

---

“*How long have you known about that cut.*”

“*Since March.*”

“*Say the rest of that, because you said nine men and since March and I want the nine.*”

“*There are about nine of us who cut ditch in this district and all nine of us walked past that cut in the first week of March and all nine of us have been back since. Not one of us has said it out loud to another man in a place where a third man could hear.*”

“*Why.*”

He said it in about four seconds and he said it the way a man says something he has said to himself a great many times.

“*Because the water is not on anybody’s paper. When I say it out loud, it is a thing a man said, and then it is a thing that has to be done about. Doing something about that ditch means taking it out. Taking it out means a different line for the water, and the different line is on about nine acres that a man sowed in March. I have four acres on that water and my two brothers have eleven between them, and I am fifty-one years old and I have not got another way of living.*”

Nobody on that bank thanked anybody.

“*Say the rest of it, because you have told me the reason and I do not think you meant to, and I would rather you meant to.*”

“*I did not mean to. I have said it to you because you were in my ditch and I had already taken the post out for you and there is no putting that back. That is not a reason. It is a sequence.*”

---

At the second gate along the towpath there was a woman of about thirty-eight called Ilsa Rundle with a barrow and a boy of about four. Ilyan stopped and asked her the same question he had asked at the cut. He had asked two people things in one afternoon and both times he had got a true answer, and he wanted to know whether a district agrees with itself.

She said no to the whole of it in about nine words, and then she said the other half.

“*He cannot be stopped. He can be asked.*”

“*Say the rest of that, because a man on a bank told me he cannot be stopped and you have told me he can be asked, and I would like to know whether one of you is talking nonsense.*”

“*The bank man is right. And being asked is the whole of what this district has, and it is enough to live on, and that is why it is not enough.*”

“*Say what that means in a sentence a woman at a gate can say.*”

“*It means that about nine people in this hundred have said out loud this year that they do not agree, and about nine have not, and the nine who said it are all still here, and the water does not care which nine.*”

She put the barrow down and got the boy up onto her hip and did not say anything else, and he wrote it down in his own head in the order she said it in, which is the only place he has ever been able to keep anything.

---

They walked back up the towpath together, the man carrying the crowbar and the stranger carrying his own boot in his hand, because the other one had taken the sole off about the fourth hour of the afternoon and let in nine inches of a fen.

At the bridge the man stopped.

“*There is a hundred and it meets on the rising and the rising is in ten days, and the clerk puts the new seasons up on the wall at midday, and any man in the hundred may stand up there and say a thing about a season.*”

“*Say the rest of it, because I have been in a town for two days and I have learned that everything here happens in front of people.*”

“*Say your own name and the hundred will listen to you, because a stranger is a thing nobody in a room can argue with and everybody in a room can remember. That is worth exactly what it is worth and I have not got a better offer.*”

“*And if I say it and nothing happens.*”

“*Then in a year you will be a man in a town of eleven hundred people who said a true thing once. And I will be a man in that town who let a stranger say it and did not say it first. That is the two of us and it is not nothing.*”

Nobody on that towpath thanked anybody and nobody on that towpath was sorry for anybody. The fever was thirty-nine weeks and no days old. A man of thirty-one lay in nine inches of water in a ditch that had been cut in March on a parcel a man had paid for. A man of fifty-one pulled him out with a crowbar and told him the whole of it. He had known it since the first week of March and had not said it to anybody. He gave the reason, and then said the reason had not meant to come out, and had only come out because a gatepost went into a ditch.

And there are about nine men in that district who know about the cut, and there is a sheet on a wall with no column for a person, and in ten days the water will come over a block of limestone with a line cut round it and every season in that hundred will go. The reason nothing has been said is not that nobody cares. It is that everybody downstream.

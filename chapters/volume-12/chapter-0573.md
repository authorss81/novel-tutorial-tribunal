# Chapter 573: The First Day Of The Hundred And Fourth Week, And A Tuesday, And Which Is The Morning A Man Of About Fifty Named In A Parlour Last Night For The First Of The Hay, And Which Does Not Get Cut, And On Which The First Load Out Of A Yard In This Hundred Is A Barrow Of A Foot Of Sallow Out Of A Ditch By A Man Of Thirty-Three Who Gave That Season To A Room Eight Days Ago And Is Doing It Alone With The Wrong Tool, And On Which A Woman Who Keeps An Inn Of Four Beds Pays Four Shillings And Twopence On Two Acres She Does Not Work, And On Which A Woman Of Thirty-Eight Whose Line On A Wall Has Said Hay Mown For About Sixty Years Without Her Ever Having Had A Crop Of Hay Walks Up A Lane Twice To Ask For A Parcel To Be Written Against Her Own Challenge

First day of the hundred and fourth week. His three hundred and twenty-third morning. One hundred and seventy-three days after the settlement. The fever forty-one weeks and three days. One hundred and twenty-seven days since the division. Seventy-three days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

The day before was the seventh and last day of the hundred and third week. A man of about fifty said a number in a parlour over a bar. This is the morning the first of the hay in this district was to come down, and it did not.

---

It rained for about an hour before the seventh hour and then stopped. By the ninth hour the ground in this hundred was not mud. It was the same stuff the camp was standing in four miles off, on the top of a rise about eleven feet wide, with about nine hundred pounds of eleven households going onto a flat boat in two days.

---

His name was Garr Ryley and he was thirty-three. He had a brother of fifty-one who cuts ditch and a brother with sixteen acres on that water. He had six acres of the sally bed on the top side and nothing at all in the square at the right-hand end of that line.

He was in his own yard at about the seventh hour with a barrow.

There was a cart in the yard as well. It had not moved in about nine days and it was not going anywhere. There was one barrow, one spade and one axe, and a stack of sallow cut on Monday, which was yesterday.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Ilyan, from the gate, because there is a gate and it is shut and a man in a yard may shut his gate.

“*A gate and a barrow and a yard. I have not opened it. About nine people in this hundred know I gave that season to a room on Monday. About nine know it was this morning before the seventh hour.*”

Nobody in that yard thanked anybody.

“*Say the rest of that, and say what is in the barrow. I have been in this county nineteen days and I have never seen a man do a thing with a barrow in a wet yard, and I would like to know before I ask the next question.*”

“*Sallow. Out of the ditch between the sally bed and the low meadow. There is about a hundred and forty yards of that ditch and about a foot of sallow growing in the whole of it. I am taking it out with a barrow, in between the beasts.*”

“*Then say the number of loads and put it in a mouth. About nine people will have that number by tonight whether you say it or not, and I would rather it came from you.*”

“*About a yard of it to a load. About nine loads a day at this end of it, on my own. That is about sixteen days of a hundred and forty yards. A ditch is about four feet wide at the top, and I have a spade and an axe and no team, and nobody to hold the other end of a plank.*”

Nobody in that yard thanked anybody.

“*Say the rest of that, and say who is paying the twelve shillings and sixpence. Do not tell me nobody is. I was in a room last night where two people said that out loud and were told they were right, and I would like to hear it in a yard where there is mud in it.*”

“*Nobody is.*”

Nobody in that yard said anything.

“*Then say the rest of it.*”

“*That is the whole of it. Twelve shillings and sixpence a year is one hundred and fifty pence. One hundred and fifty pence is a barrow of sallow, and a plank, and a man holding the other end, once a year.*”

“*Say the rest of that, and say why you are doing it in between the beasts with a barrow, and do not tell me it is the season.*”

“*A man gave that season to a room on Monday. There is no figure in the square at the end of that line, and so there is nobody to pay it. I will have taken out about a year of that sallow in about sixteen days. In about two years the low meadow will be gone, and not in a wet year.*”

“*Then say the rest of that, and say why, and do not say because it is right.*”

“*Because the room does not cut ditches. There is no form in the county of Kell that says a man may not take out a foot of sallow in a ditch on his own ground. Nobody in this hundred is going to ask me about it. And the water is coming off that meadow onto a common, and there are fifty-eight people on it.*”

Nobody in that yard thanked anybody and nobody in that yard was sorry for anybody.

---

Then somebody paid a rate. About nine people in that lane found out about it before noon, and it was not a man.

She came up the lane at about the ninth hour with a purse and no basket. She was the woman who kept the inn of four beds. She went past the gate, stopped, and did not go in.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Iven Ashcombe. He had been coming up the lane at about the eighth hour and had been listening since the second hedge, and he said so. “*A gate, and a woman with a purse standing at it in front of about nine of us. Say what it is to them and not to me.*”

“*A gate. I am not going into it. I have four shillings and twopence in this purse, and I am going to pay it on two acres of the strip behind that yard. The rate on two acres is four shillings and twopence. I have worked it out twice.*”

“*Say the rest of that, and say it out loud at the gate and not in my building, because about nine people in this hundred have found out about a thing in a doorway this week and it went four ways by the ninth hour.*”

“*Then hear it at the gate. I pay four shillings and twopence on two acres and the season on those two acres is mine. I do not work them. I do not know a man in this hundred who does. A man of twenty-two in my house has a season and can be one of the thirty men in a room.*”

“*Say the rest of that, and say whether the county allows it.*”

“*There is no form in the county of Kell that says a person may not pay a rate. There is no form anywhere that says a person has.*”

Nobody in that lane thanked anybody.

“*Then say what it does to the square at the end of that line, because that is the only part of it I have not thought about.*”

“*It puts a figure in a square that is not the one it was, and it puts a woman’s holding on it. About nine people in this hundred will find that out inside four days, and about nine of them will not be able to do anything with it. That is the first time in this hundred that a woman has held a season, and I am telling you before you tell anybody.*”

“*Say the rest of that, and say what you want me to write on the wall, and understand before you answer that I am not going to write a name in the first column unless you say it twice.*”

“*Write what is on the bar. A parcel, two acres, and the year. If anybody asks who holds it you may tell them a woman of about fifty holds it and does not work it. You may not tell them her name. If you write her name I will take four shillings and twopence back off that sheet and pay it to a ditch.*”

Nobody in that lane said anything.

The money went on the bar at about the ninth hour. It was the first rate on that bar in about two years that had not come from a man with about seven hundred sheep.

---

And then the person who was worst off that day came up the lane, and she was not the person anybody in this hundred had been watching.

She was thirty-eight and her name was Sabra Lyne. She had four acres on the top side. She had held them until a man came round with a purse in the middle of April and paid the rate on them and the penny on them. She has never worked a day on them since, and she is not the only one of eleven in that condition.

She had also, on the Saturday of the last week, stood up in a weigh-house at about the eleventh minute of a reading. She told a man of sixty-two that the second column of that sheet said *hay, mown* on her line in every year of the little figures going left from the square. She told him it had not been mown in any of them. The clerk wrote it down, and the challenge went on the wall with no parcel against it.

She was carrying nothing and she had been up and down the lane twice.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Iven Ashcombe. “*You have been up and down this lane twice since the seventh hour. About nine people have seen you and about nine have not. You came on a Tuesday. This is not a reading day, and there is nothing on that gate for anybody to read.*”

“*A gate. I have been twice. I want one thing, and it is not about my four acres.*”

Nobody in that lane thanked anybody.

“*Then say it at the gate and in the open, and do not come into my building. There is a thing on a wall in there with your word on it, and about nine people read it every morning, and I would rather you said this to those nine than to about four of us in a room.*”

“*On Saturday you read a line of that sheet and it said hay, mown. I stood up and said it had not been mown in any year of the little figures. You wrote six lines on a wall and five of them had no parcel against them, and mine was one of the five.*”

“*Say the rest of that, and say what you have come to tell me. I know what it is going to be, and I have not got a better answer than the one I gave a man of about fifty last night.*”

“*I have come to tell you that a challenge on that wall says hay, mown, is wrong, and that in about four days there will be a woman of thirty-eight in this town whose name is against a line that says she mowed hay for sixty years and did not.*”

“*Say the rest of that.*”

“*The only proof anybody has is a five-line challenge with no parcel on it. In a hundred years a person in a room will read the sheet and read the challenge. The sheet will say she mowed and the challenge will say she did not, and there will be nothing to say which is which.*”

Nobody in that lane thanked anybody and nobody in that lane was sorry for anybody.

“*Then say what you want me to do, and I am going to do it, and I am going to tell you that I am going to do it for a reason that is not good enough.*”

“*I want you to ask me which parcel. In front of about nine people. At the next reading, which is four days on, on a Saturday, at the sixth hour, where about nine people can be checked against each other. I will say it. Then it is on that wall with a parcel against it, and that is the whole of what I have come for.*”

“*I will ask you. And I will tell you now that about nine of those five were said by men who do not know which parcels they are talking about. If I ask you it will be the first line on that wall that anybody has said out loud with a parcel against it. A hundred and thirty people will read it on Saturday morning. About nine of them will be in the room when you say it, and the rest will have it from the other nine.*”

“*Then I will say it at the sixth hour on a Saturday and not before. I have walked this lane twice to ask for that. There is no form in the county of Kell that says a person may ask a clerk a question, and you have a board outside your own door that says I may ask you anything. I would like it noticed that I used the board and not the county.*”

Nobody in that lane thanked anybody.

Then the man of thirty-three came out of the yard with the barrow and put it down in the lane in front of about nine people. It was full of sallow. It was the first load out of that yard in about nine days, and there was no cart on the road at all.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Iven Ashcombe to him. “*A barrow of sallow in a lane is not a thing a man in this hundred does on a Tuesday. About nine of us have watched you do it. About four of us have worked out what it is, and about nine have not.*”

“*A barrow, and about nine yards of ditch that is not going to be there in about sixteen days. I gave that season to a room eight days ago. The room does not cut ditches. I have a spade and an axe and no team.*”

“*Then say the rest of that, and say who is paying for the tea in this lane. I will tell you now. The man of thirty-one has not paid for it. The woman with the purse has not paid for it. Nobody in this hundred has paid for anything today, and that is four days running.*”

Nobody in that lane thanked anybody.

“*Say the rest of that, because you have said four days running out loud in a lane, and there is no form in the county of Kell that says a water-clerk may say a thing like that, and I have four pounds a year and I have not said it in a lane before in nineteen years.*”

“*I am saying it now because about nine of us are standing in one, and about nine of us are the only ones who can be sent for about any of it.*”

The fever was forty-one weeks and three days old.

A man of thirty-three put a barrow of sallow out of a ditch on the top side into a lane. About nine loads a day, about a hundred and forty yards of ditch, about a foot of sallow in the whole of it, which is about sixteen days with a spade and an axe and no team. The rate on the six acres that pays for that ditch is twelve shillings and sixpence a year, and nobody paid it.

Four shillings and twopence did go on the oak bar at about the ninth hour. It came out of the purse of a woman of about fifty who keeps four beds and who does not work the two acres. It put a figure in a square that a woman has not put one in for about two hundred and thirty years, and about nine people in that lane heard it and about nine did not.

And a woman of thirty-eight with four acres came up a lane twice, and asked for the one thing she wanted, which is a parcel written against her own challenge on a wall at the sixth hour on a Saturday, where about nine people can be checked against each other. She said out loud that she used a board outside a door and not a county, because a county has nothing on it for her.

And a cart stood in a yard on the top side and did not go out, and the first load that came out of that lane all day came out of it in a barrow.

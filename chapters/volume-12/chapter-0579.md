# Chapter 579: The Seventh And Last Day Of The Hundred And Fourth Week, And A Monday, And On Which About Nine Men Take Scythes To Ten Acres Of Standing Hay On A Low Meadow Of About Thirty-One Acres Six Days Late, And On Which The First Swath Goes Down And Cannot Be Put Back, And On Which A Hundred Of About Thirty Men Does The First Thing It Has Done Since A Stranger Said A True Thing Out Loud In Front Of It, And On Which A Man Of Fifty-Four Is Asked Out Loud At The Edge Of A Field Whether He Meant The Boards And Gives No Answer That Day

Seventh and last day of the hundred and fourth week. His three hundred and twenty-ninth morning. One hundred and seventy-nine days after the settlement. The fever forty-two weeks and two days. One hundred and thirty-three days since the division. Seventy-nine days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

The morrow is the first day of the hundred and fifth week, and it is a Tuesday.

---

It rained in the night and stopped before the sixth hour. The low meadow of about thirty-one acres lay under a thin bright water that was not the flood. About two thirds of it were under. About ten acres on the upper edge stood out of it, with the hay standing grey and tall and six days past its time.

About nine men came down the lane with scythes at about the seventh hour. None of them had been sent for. There is no form in the county of Kell that says a man may be sent for about hay. Four of them held seasons in that hundred. Five of them held none. One of the five was nineteen and poled a flat boat. One of the nine was fifty-one and had cut ditch in that district for about thirty years.

Nobody in that lane thanked anybody.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Hoare Ryley. He stood at the top of the ten acres with mud over his boots. “*Because about nine men are on this meadow with scythes and the hay is six days late, and about thirty men said nothing in a room yesterday, and I would like one true thing said on the ground before a blade goes down.*”

“*Ten acres of standing hay,*” said a man of about fifty with a skep behind his own gate. He had a holding and a seat. “*About twenty-one acres of this meadow are not going to be mown this year. I said that number in a parlour seven days ago and a woman with a tray said it back wrong. The ten that are standing go today or go to seed.*”

“*Then say who cuts first,*” said Hoare Ryley, “*because about nine men cannot cut ten acres in a day, and a fortnight with a scythe and about nine men is what it takes, and the fortnight began six days ago.*”

Nobody on that meadow thanked anybody.

---

Ilyan came down the lane at about the eighth hour with his bad leg dragging in the mud. He had been in that county twenty-five days. He had said a sentence out loud in front of about thirty men on the Sunday that he cannot take back, and by the ninth hour it had been said back by the man whose ground it is about. He had under two pounds in a purse and one hundred and fifty pence of it would put six acres back in season. He had not put it there.

He wanted the ten acres cut without his hand on them. He had wanted it since the Sunday. A man who holds no season in that hundred can stand at the edge of a field and watch. A man who holds one cannot. He had chosen the watching on the Sunday, in front of about thirty men, and the choosing was the cost and not the comfort.

His mistake that morning was to stand where the cutters could see him. He stood at the top of the ten acres with his hands in his coat, and about four of the nine took the watching for an inspection, and one of them stopped with the scythe up and waited to be told.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said the man with the scythe up. He was about thirty-four. He had a stroke beside his name in chalk on the end wall of a smithy. “*Because a stranger is standing at the top of ten acres with his hands in his coat, and about nine of us are about to cut, and yesterday he said he would not pay a rate so that he could speak, and today there is nobody to speak to.*”

“*I am standing in front of ten acres I am not going to cut. I have a bad leg and no scythe and no season. Yesterday I said I can put six acres back in season and I can speak for the harvest and I cannot do both. Today I am doing the second of the two. The doing of it is standing here and not paying.*”

“*Then say the rest of that, and stand further off, because about nine men are going to cut and one of them has stopped, and a hay that is six days late does not wait for a man to finish standing.*”

He stood further off. Nobody on that meadow thanked him for it.

His cost that morning was the watching itself. About nine men cut and he did not. A boy of fourteen with about nine feet of knotted line walked the upper edge and said out loud that ten acres cannot be walked by anybody in that county with what there is. About four people heard the boy and about nine heard the scythes.

---

The first swath went down at about the ninth hour.

A man of about fifty laid it. The hay was grey and tall and wet at the foot. It went down in a straight line along the upper edge, about nine feet wide and about forty long. About nine men watched it go. About four of them nodded. About nine of them did not.

It cannot be put back. A swath that is down is down. In a fortnight it is hay or it is seed. No form in the county of Kell says a man may put it back, and no man on that meadow tried.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Hoare Ryley, when the first swath was down. “*Because about nine men have cut the first of ten acres, and yesterday about thirty men did nothing in a room, and today about nine have done the first thing since, and I would like it said before the second.*”

“*The first swath of ten acres,*” said the man of about fifty. “*About twenty-one acres of this meadow are gone this year. The ten are going today. That is the whole of it, and it is the first thing this hundred has done since a stranger said a true thing in front of it.*”

Nobody on that meadow thanked anybody and nobody on that meadow was sorry for anybody.

---

Then the hundred did the second thing it had done since the Sunday, and it did it at the edge of the field, in the open, where about thirty people could hear.

A man of fifty-four came up the lane at about the tenth hour with mud on his boots. He had about seven hundred sheep four miles south. He had forty-four acres of long grazing in its sixth year with about nine acres of new sward in it. He was in season on the whole of it. He had said on the rising, twelve days before, that he would still put three boards in a gate and pay for them out of his own purse. Nobody had asked him whether he meant it.

About nine men stopped with their scythes up. About four more came down off the lane. About thirty people stood at the edge of ten acres with the first swath down behind them.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Iven Ashcombe. He had come down the lane at about the ninth hour and had watched the first swath go down without a slate in his hands. “*Because a man of fifty-four is at the edge of this field, and about thirty people are standing here, and a question thirteen days old is the only thing in this hundred that has not been asked.*”

The count was worked out loud, in front of about thirty people, the way a count is worked out in that county. The rising was thirteen days before, on the first day of the hundred and third week, a Tuesday. This is the seventh and last day of the hundred and fourth, a Monday. Thirteen days, and the question was put on the first of them and not asked on any of the twelve after.

“*Thirteen days,*” said Iven Ashcombe. “*On the first of them you said you would still put the other three boards in and pay for them out of your own purse. Nobody has asked you whether you meant it. I am asking you now, at the edge of ten acres, with the first swath down, in front of about thirty people.*”

About four seconds went past. About thirty people at the edge of a field heard a question thirteen days in the carrying go out of a water-clerk’s mouth.

The man of fifty-four looked at the ten acres, at the first swath, at about nine men with scythes, at a stranger with his hands in his coat standing further off than he had stood in the morning.

He gave no answer that day. He did not say no. He did not say yes. He stood at the edge of the field for about nine minutes while about thirty people said nothing, and then he went back up the lane on his own legs, the way he had come down it.

Nobody in that lane thanked anybody.

---

Then the cutting went on, and it went on without him, and that was the consequence and the whole of the first of it.

About nine men cut until the fourth hour. By the fourth hour about two of the ten acres were down. The hay lay in lines along the upper edge, grey and wet, with the water standing between the lines. A cart came down the lane at about the third hour and went back empty, because no barn in that hundred will take wet hay and every man on that meadow knows it.

Garr Ryley came down off the sally bed at about the fifth hour with mud to the knee. He was thirty-three. He had six acres with nothing in the square. He had said another man’s sentence back before the ninth hour on the Sunday night. He stood at the top of the ten acres for about nine minutes and watched about nine men cut ground that was not his.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Hoare Ryley to him. “*Because you have come down off six acres with a barrow in the yard and about ten more days of sallow in front of you, and about nine men are cutting ten acres that will be hay, and your six are the reason about twenty-one are not.*”

“*Ten acres going down and six acres standing empty,*” said Garr Ryley. “*A stranger can put the six back in season and he can speak for the harvest and he cannot do both. He said so yesterday and I said it after him. Today about nine men are cutting and I am watching with him. That is the first of the consequence and not the whole of it.*”

Nobody on that meadow thanked anybody and nobody on that meadow was sorry for anybody.

The fever was forty-two weeks and two days old. A man of fifty-four was asked out loud at the edge of a field whether he meant the boards, thirteen days after he said it, and gave no answer that day, and did not say no.

A hundred of about thirty men did the first thing it has done since a stranger said a true thing out loud in front of it. It cut about two of ten acres before the fourth hour, with no barn to take them.

And about nine men took scythes to ten acres of standing hay on a low meadow of about thirty-one acres, six days late, and the first swath went down at about the ninth hour and cannot be put back.

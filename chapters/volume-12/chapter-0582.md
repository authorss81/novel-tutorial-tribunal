# Chapter 582: The Third Day Of The Hundred And Fifth Week, And A Thursday, And On Which About Nine Men Have About Three Of Ten Acres Down Of A Hay That Is Nine Days Late With The Water Standing Between The Lines, And On Which A Load Of Wet Hay Is Hauled Up A Lane To A House With Four Beds In It And Does Not Fit Where It Is Hauled To, And On Which A Man Of Thirty-One Says Out Loud In About Nine People’s Hearing The True Of A Woman’s Answer About Nine Seconds Before She Gives It, And On Which She Gives It Again In Her Own Mouth And The Reason Is A Line On A Wall About A Crop Of Hay A Woman Never Had

Third day of the hundred and fifth week. His three hundred and thirty-second morning. One hundred and eighty-two days after the settlement. The fever forty-two weeks and five days. One hundred and thirty-six days since the division. Eighty-two days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

About nine men were on the low meadow by the seventh hour and about nine had been on it since Monday’s ninth hour. Four of them held seasons in that hundred. Five of them held none.

The hay was nine days late. It had been due on the first day of the hundred and fourth week, a Tuesday, and about three of ten acres were down at the fourth hour of this one, and the first of it had been lying in the open since the ninth hour on Monday.

There is no barn in that hundred that will take wet hay, and every man on that meadow has known it since he was a boy, and the reason is not a fashion and not a rule. A barn that takes wet hay in the wet year is a barn that has hay in it in the dry year, and a barn with hay in it is a barn somebody can be shown.

Nobody on that meadow thanked anybody.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Hoare Ryley. He stood at the top of what was down with his sleeves rolled and his scythe across his shoulder. “*Because about nine men have cut about three of ten acres in three days and about nine of us have known all three, and the water is between the lines and the first of it has been standing in the open since Monday, and I would like the two numbers said before the fourth hour.*”

“*About three of ten down. About seven standing. The water is standing between the lines of the three and it has been since Monday, and it is not a flood, it is a fen at the seventh hour with a dew on it that has not lifted.*”

“*Say the rest of that, and say what a hay that stands in water in a line does.*”

“*It goes at the foot first. The foot of it is in water and the head of it is not, and in about four days the foot is a green thing that is not hay and is not seed either. In a fortnight it is a hay of the following year. That is what the man of about fifty said in a parlour and he was right and I have been saying it since Monday.*”

“*Say the rest of that, and say what four passes is, because a man of about fifty said that too and I have not seen him do one.*”

“*Four passes is four weeks of a scythe on the same ground instead of one. A scythe takes a fortnight on ten acres with about nine men and the hay stands all the time it is going. Come back to it in four weeks and it is a different crop and you are cutting it for a year that has not started. Then there is no hay at all in this hundred out of that meadow this season, only a thing that came back.*”

Nobody on that meadow thanked anybody.

“*Say the rest of it, and say what happens to a cart that comes up this lane in the wet.*”

“*It comes up the lane. And then it stands in the lane, because there is nowhere on that ground for it to go, because every barn on this water is shut against wet and every man in this hundred knows that before the cart is at the gate.*”

Nobody on that meadow thanked anybody and nobody on that meadow was sorry for anybody.

---

Ilyan came down the lane at about the eighth hour to see the third of ten acres lying in the water.

He had been in that county twenty-eight days. He had held the low end of a plank for about five hours the day before and had written nothing down about it and had been told at a pump that a stranger had paid a rate. He wanted a thing off that meadow. He had wanted it since a Monday in the hundred and fourth week, when a man of about fifty had said in a parlour that the first of that hay ought to come down on the morrow.

His mistake that morning was one he has named twice and is naming here a third time.

A cart came up the lane at about the ninth hour with a man of about thirty-four at the near end of the shafts and about four feet of wet hay in it, and it stood in the lane, and the lane is the only way into the yard of a house with four beds in it.

Nobody asked him anything. Nobody in that lane has ever asked him anything. That is the whole of what being nobody’s has been for him for four days.

He said it out loud.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said the woman who keeps the inn, from her own door, in front of about nine people. “*Because you have been in this county twenty-eight days and you have said the true of my answer in this lane before I have given it, and about nine people heard you and about four of them were not looking at me when you said it.*”

“*I am standing in front of a load of wet hay in a lane and I said it out loud because about nine people had asked after it.*”

“*About nine people had not asked after it. About nine people had watched a cart stand in my lane and now they have a reason from a man nobody can be sent for, and that is the same shape as a thing you did on a Friday at a bar in a market and again at a wall on a Saturday, and I have been in this town eleven years and I have watched you do it three times and heard of it once.*”

Nobody in that lane thanked anybody.

“*Then say what your answer is, and say it to the lane and not to me, because I have just used it up.*”

“*My answer is that a cart does not go past the pump and along to my back door, and that is all, and it has been that since the Friday before this, and about four people knew it and about nine have now got a stranger’s version of it in front of my house.*”

---

Then she said her own answer, in the lane, in about nine people’s hearing, and it was a different answer.

“*Say the rest of that,*” said Hoare Ryley, who had come up the lane with his scythe over his shoulder and had not been in that house in eleven years. “*Because about nine of us are standing in a lane behind a cart and a woman is about to say why she will not take a load, and there is no form in the county of Kell that says she has to, and about nine of us would rather hear it than go away.*”

“*I hold two acres of the strip behind that yard and I pay four shillings and twopence a year on them and I do not work them, and I have not worked them since my husband was in it.*”

“*Then say the rest of it, and say what a cart of wet hay has to do with two acres that are not worked.*”

“*It has this to do with them. In the middle of April a man came round with a purse and paid the rate on four acres of a woman of thirty-eight, and she has never had a crop of hay in her life, and there is a line on your wall that says she mowed one in about sixty years, and five lines under it say the wall is wrong about her ground and not one of the five had a parcel against it until Saturday.*”

Nobody in that lane thanked anybody.

“*So I am not putting a stranger’s wet hay against that wall. When that wall is right about me and wrong about her, I will take a load off that meadow and stack it where it can be seen from the road.*”

“*Then say the rest of it,*” said Hoare Ryley, “*because about nine of us are standing in this lane and about four of us understood you and about nine understood half, and a woman who gives half a reason in a lane is a woman who has been interrupted by her own hay.*”

“*I am not saying it to be difficult. I am saying it because a stack in a yard with one house over it is the only hay in this hundred that anybody could argue about in about four years, and there is a woman coming to that door most days in this county who will tell you that a stack is a thing a person can be checked against, as long as there are two of them.*”

About four people in that lane understood the whole of that. About nine understood the first half of it.

“*Then say who the fourth is,*” said the man of about thirty-four with the cart shafts, “*because there are about four of you who understood all of it and I am one of them and I would like to know whether the four of us are the same four.*”

“*You are not. I am the fourth. I keep four beds and I have understood all of it because I am the one it is about, and that is not the same thing as understanding it.*”

“*Then say the rest of it,*” said Hoare Ryley. “*And say what happens to that load, because it is standing in a lane that is the only way into your yard and about nine men are going to want to be behind it at the fourth hour.*”

“*Then it stands here until it stops being a hay, and about nine of you know by now what it becomes. I would rather say that in a lane in front of nine people than have a cart come round my back at the ninth hour and put it in a doorway.*”

---

Nobody in that lane thanked anybody and nobody in that lane was sorry for anybody.

The load stood in the lane until about the eighth hour. It was about four feet of it in the middle of a lane with ruts in it from Monday, and the water had come up through the straw at the low side and stood in the bottom of the cart.

The water on that meadow is not the flood and it is not the river. It comes out of the ditch that takes the meadow off the pasture, and that ditch had a foot of sallow growing in the whole of it until a man of thirty-three started it out with a barrow on the Tuesday of the hundred and fourth week, and about nine yards a day. It is coming down as that comes out and it is going to keep coming down for about nine days more at that end of it.

There is a boy of fourteen walking the upper edge with about nine feet of knotted line in his bag and he is not walking a line. He is walking the ends of the swaths and counting them, because a man on the ground side of that water asked him at the seventh hour how many there were, and he said he did not know, and then he went and found out.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Hoare Ryley, to the boy. “*Because you have been walking my swaths for an hour with a line in your bag and not using it, and about nine of us have watched you do it.*”

“*Eleven lines and about three of ten acres done. I could not do that with nine feet of knotted line, and I can do it with my feet because I know where the head of the meadow is and where my bed is. A man has to know where his bed is.*”

Nobody on that meadow thanked anybody.

About nine men cut on until the ninth hour and about three and a half of ten acres were down.

Ilyan’s cost that day was a lane. He had wanted a thing off that meadow since the Monday of the hundred and fourth week and he had used up a woman’s answer about nine seconds before she gave it, in about nine people’s hearing, and about nine people had his shape of it and about nine had hers, and there is no column anywhere in that county for the difference.

The fever was forty-two weeks and five days old. A load of wet hay was hauled up a lane to a house with four beds in it and it did not fit where it was hauled to, and it stood in the only way in while the water came up through the straw at the bottom of the cart.

And the woman who would not take it gave the reason in about nine people’s hearing with her own mouth, and the reason was a line on a wall about a woman of thirty-eight and a crop of hay she never had.

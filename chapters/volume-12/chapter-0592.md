# Chapter 592: The Sixth Day Of The Hundred And Sixth Week, And A Sunday, And On Which There Is No Room And No Slate And No Bar And A Man Of Thirty-One Walks Four Miles Out Of A Town Of About Eleven Hundred People With A Bad Leg, And On Which About Nine Men Cut A Low Meadow And About Four Of Them Cart, And On Which A Man With A Holding Tells A Stranger That The Four Standing Acres Took Five Passes And Not Four And The Fifth Was The One That Was Not A Hay, And On Which A Woman Of About Seventy Who Asked On Friday Where The Hay Would Go Is Told At Last That It Is In A Field In The Weather Going Nowhere, And On Which A Man With Nine Acres And No Seat Says Out Loud In Front Of About Nine People That A Load Went Into His Barn And That He Has Never Told Anybody, And On Which The Stranger Says Out Loud In Nine Inches Of Water That He Is Not Going To Take The Next Number Out Of Anybody’s Hand

Sixth day of the hundred and sixth week. His three hundred and forty-second morning. One hundred and ninety-two days after the settlement. The fever forty-four weeks and one day. One hundred and forty-six days since the division. Ninety-two days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

The morrow is the seventh and last day of the hundred and sixth week.

---

There was no room on the Sunday and no slate and no bar, and there has never been a form in the county of Kell for any of the three on any day of the week.

He was out of that town at about the seventh hour with a crust in his coat. The plank bridge over the low side is crossable at the seventh hour and not at any other, and the water had been at the cut in the tally stone since the Tuesday, a thumbnail’s width under it, and it had not gone over.

He had been in that county thirty-eight days. He had said four things at a step on the Saturday morning in about nine seconds and about nine mouths in that hundred had them by the fourth hour, and he wanted to know one of them off a man’s own mouth.

---

About nine men were cutting on the low meadow and about four of them were carting.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Aldis Crane, who had a holding, a seat in the hundred and a skep behind his own gate. “*Because you have walked four miles on a leg that stopped working on a common twenty-five days ago, and about nine of us have watched you come off the bridge, and I am going to tell you what came off this field on Friday and I would rather you had it in about nine sentences than worked it out of the standing.*”

“*I am standing in front of four acres of a hay that took four passes.*”

“*It took five,*” said Aldis Crane.

About four seconds went past.

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Hoare Ryley, who had been cutting since the seventh hour and had come up the headland with the barrow handle in his hand. “*Because a stranger has said four in about nine feet of wet grass and about nine people on this meadow have heard it, and a man who cut it is the only man in this county who can be checked against his own figure.*”

“*Four passes on Friday afternoon, with nine men and about four more, and the last of it in about the ninth hour. And a fifth on Saturday morning because the head of it had gone grey and I was not going to have it standing in the weather a fifth week with nobody able to check whether it was a hay or not. A fifth pass is the one that is not a hay at all. Four passes is a hay of the following year and five is a thing that has been cut five times and none of them were a hay.*”

Nobody on that meadow thanked anybody.

“*Then say where it is,*” said the woman of about seventy, who had come down to the headland on a stick with a woman of about fifty holding a tray. “*Because I asked on Friday at that step where the hay would go and about thirty people were standing there and nobody answered me.*”

“*It is in the top corner of that field in rows and it is going nowhere,*” said Aldis Crane. “*There is no barn in this hundred that takes wet hay. It has been asked three times this year in three rooms and the answer has been no three times, and the last one it was asked it went up a lane on a cart and stood in the only way in to a house with four beds and came off it in the dark, and I have not asked anybody where it went since.*”

About four people on that meadow said something at once.

A man of about sixty with a shepherd’s crook had been grazing the top side of that field since the seventh hour and had not come over to the headland.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Perrin Voss. “*Because there is a thing standing in the middle of that meadow that four of us can see and nine cannot, and I have been looking at it since the seventh hour.*”

“*Say what it is.*”

“*The middle of that field is not mown.*”

About four seconds went past.

“*Then say the rest of that and say whether it is a thing anybody can check,*” said Iven Ashcombe.

“*It cannot be. Nobody in this hundred can tell you why and I have been looking at it since the seventh hour. About nine acres of that four came down on Friday and there is a strip down the middle of them about as wide as a scythe standing up, and whatever a man did on Friday afternoon went round it.*”

Nobody on that meadow thanked anybody.

“*Then say what it is worth to this hundred,*” said Aldis Crane, “*because about four of us have just been told about a strip of standing grass in the middle of ten acres and I would like to know what anybody thinks it is worth before I put a scythe in it on Monday.*”

“*Nothing,*” said Perrin Voss. “*I have been in this hundred forty years and I have never seen four passes in one field in one year, and I am the only man standing here who can tell you that, and it is worth nothing to you.*”

---

Then the man who had taken it answered for himself, about nine feet off, with a barrow in his hands.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Iven Ashcombe, who had come down the dyke with no slate under his arm. “*Because a woman who keeps four beds said out loud on Monday that there were about nine people going to go and look at that barn, and they did not, and this morning there is a man standing on that meadow who has that hay in his barn and has never said so to anybody.*”

“*It came down that lane on the Thursday and it went into my barn,*” said the man of about thirty-four with a stroke beside his name in chalk, who had nine acres and no seat. “*And I have not told anybody and about nine of you now know. I took it because there was nowhere else and not because I wanted it, and I have nine acres and a barn and no seat in that hundred, and about nine of you have never spoken to me, and a load of wet hay in my barn is the only one of those I have got.*”

Nobody on that meadow thanked anybody.

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Iven Ashcombe, “*because about four people in this hundred have been asking how a man with nine acres and no seat gets to be the man a cart stops at, and it is the second thing he has seen with his own eyes this week and the first of them was a ditch behind an inn on Saturday morning.*”

“*The two acres behind that house,*” said the man with the barrow. “*I stood in them before the light on Saturday and I have stood in this field since the seventh hour this morning, and those are the only two pieces of ground I have set foot on in this hundred this year that nobody sent me to.*”

Nobody on that meadow said anything.

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Iven Ashcombe, “*because about nine people in this hundred have been asked what you are and there is no form in this county that makes anybody answer it.*”

“*I have a barrow and a stroke beside my name in chalk on a wall at a smithy and no seat anywhere, and that is the whole of what I have got to say about it.*”

“*Then say the rest of that and say what it is worth to you,*” said the woman of about fifty who keeps four beds, “*because I have a tray in my hands and about nine people standing here and I would rather hear the true of that barn from the man than find it out on a Friday.*”

“*It is worth about nothing and it is worth a great deal. It is worth nothing because a man with nine acres cannot feed nine men out of a barn. And it is worth a great deal because it is the only hay in this hundred that has not been standing in the weather for nine days, and about nine people know that and about nine do not, and I am not going to say it twice.*”

---

Then a man of thirty-one said a figure out loud on a headland about nine feet off nine people, and the man who mown it took it out of his hand in about four seconds, and that was the third time in a week.

He had wanted one thing off a man’s own mouth and had been given it in about nine sentences, and about four seconds after that he went and carried something else.

“*Say the rest of that and do not be sorry for me,*” said Aldis Crane, “*because you have been in this county thirty-eight days and about nine of us have watched a man with a barrow take two numbers out of your mouth in a fortnight, and about four of us have said so in three rooms, and now you have come out here and given a third one for nothing.*”

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Hoare Ryley.

“*I was not asking. I was saying it to a field so that I would not have to ask you.*”

Nobody on that meadow said anything.

“*Then say what you are going to do about the two you have already given this hundred,*” said Hoare Ryley.

“*Nothing,*” said Ilyan. “*I am not going to take the next one out of anybody’s hand. About four people in this hundred have four passes in their mouths from Saturday and they are wrong and they will be wrong until somebody with a barrow tells them, and I have been the one who put them there, and I am not going to be the one who takes them out.*”

About four people on that meadow said something at once.

---

Then they went back across the bridge at about the fourth hour with a cart in front of them, and it stood at a gate for about as long as it takes a man to say a thing twice.

Two loads were in the barn under a floor of sedge that had been laid there by somebody who is dead. The third was on the cart, and it was going nowhere, and nobody in that hundred had asked it to go anywhere.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said the woman of about fifty who keeps four beds, to nobody in particular, on the bridge. “*Because there is a cart standing at a gate in about nine inches of water and it is going to be standing here on Monday and on Tuesday, and about four of us know that and about nine do not.*”

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Iven Ashcombe.

“*This is the third time this year a load in this hundred has stopped in the only way in, and there is no form anywhere in the county of Kell that says a cart may not.*”

Nobody in that lane thanked anybody and nobody in that lane was sorry for anybody.

The fever was forty-four weeks and one day old. A man of thirty-one walked four miles out of a town of about eleven hundred people with a bad leg and got one thing he wanted off a man’s own mouth in about nine sentences.

He gave a figure away on a headland before the man who mown it could, was told the true of it, and then stood in nine inches of water and said out loud that he was not going to be the one who takes the next number back.

A hay that stands over its time took five passes on the low meadow, and the fifth was the one that was not a hay. It is in the top corner of ten acres in the weather because there is no barn in that hundred that takes wet hay, and a load of it went into one yard with a barn in it whose man has nine acres and no seat and who has now told about nine people on a meadow about his own barn.

And a cart is standing at a gate in about nine inches of water and it is going to be standing there on Monday, and there is no form in this county that says it may not.

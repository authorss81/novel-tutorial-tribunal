# Chapter 387: Four Numbers And Two Doors He Was Not Let Into, And A Man Who Counts Doors

Tarin Keel was sitting on the step outside a hall at the top of this city with a slate on his knee and a piece of lead in his coat and no barrow anywhere near him, and he had been up at that door for two days, and this morning he was on the step and had been there since the change, and it was the fourth day of the seventy-seventh week and his hundred and thirty-seventh morning in the city for the man with the leg and his thirty-sixth.

The fever was fourteen weeks and six days old. The man with the leg came up the row at the third hour with a leg that will not clear and found him there, and the two of them had not spoken about anything except work for eight days, and the notice was not mentioned by either of them and was going to stay that way.

“*You have been up the hill,*” said Tarin Keel.

“*I have been in a building at the middle of the nine bridges twice and in a room at the foot of a hill once.*”

“*Then you have not been idle, and I have been idle, and I am going to tell you what I have got, and I want to say first that it is not what I said it would be and I did not know it would not be when I said it, and I have not had a good week and I have had a number.*”

---

He turned the slate round so that the man with the leg could see it, and it was a thing he had made himself with a piece of lead, and there were four figures on it and a cross beside two of them.

“*Six halls. Three of them in the low ground and three of them at the top, and I have been at the low door since the second week of the fever on my own account and nobody asked me to and nobody told me to stop, and on the day before yesterday and yesterday I went to the other five, and I got into four of them and I was not let into two of them and I am going to come to those two again and I am not going to be let into them.*”

“*What did you say at the two.*”

“*I said I count what comes through a door, which is true, and a man of fifty-two who has stood at a rail outside a black building for nineteen years, and I am not asking for anything and I am not here about the beds. And in one of the two a man told me to come back with a form and there is no form, and in the other one a woman said no before I had finished the sentence and she was polite about it and that was worse.*”

---

“*And you counted.*”

“*I counted what was on the wall in each of the four I was let into,*” said Tarin Keel. “*Every one of them has a board on the wall with the number of beds free on it, and a man with a bad ear rubs one out and chalks another every morning, and the four I have are this morning's, and the other two I have not got and I am not going to guess at them and I am not going to put a figure in that column that I did not read off a wall with my own eyes.*”

He put his thumb under the four figures.

“*Two. Four. Eleven. Thirteen. Two and four is six, and six and eleven is seventeen, and seventeen and thirteen is thirty.*”

“*Thirty.*”

“*Thirty beds free this morning in four halls in this city, at the third hour, out of six halls, two of which I was not let into,*” said the man of fifty-two. “*And I am going to say the thing about that word before you say it. I do not know how many beds this city has. Nobody in this city knows how many beds this city has, and there is no form and no office that would be told, and if you asked the man with the bad ear he would tell you he has counted the door of one hall and not the doors of the other five, and that is true and it is the only number about beds in this city that anybody is keeping.*”

---

“*And the two in the low ground that is a two on.*”

He looked up.

“*I have not been to the low hall to ask anybody, and I have not asked the woman on the stool, and I am not going to, and I want you to hear the reason and the reason is that I count what comes through a door and she tells people what is wrong with a hall, and those are two instruments and I have only ever trusted the first one, and if I went up that row this morning and asked her what the two meant on that board she would tell me and it would go in a place and I would have used a thing I cannot take back to get a number I could have got off a wall.*”

“*So the thirty has two beds in it that a person of that hall says are spoken for.*”

“*It has,*” said Tarin Keel, “*and I have written the two with a cross beside it and the cross means I have heard somebody in that hall say the two are not free and I have not checked it and I am not going to, and if you want the true number of free beds in this city then you will have to go and stand in a hall and ask a woman on a stool, and that is not what I do and I have not been able to make myself do it in nineteen years.*”

---

“*Say the rest of it,*” said Ilyan, “*because you have been up a hill for two days for nothing but a number and a man of fifty-two does not stand on a step when he has not got something to say, and you told me eight days ago that it would take a fortnight, and eight days is not a fortnight, and you have brought me a number anyway, and I want to know why.*”

“*Because a fortnight is a fortnight and I am not going to be a man who waits for one when he has four walls in his hand,*” said Tarin Keel. “*And I want to be exact about what I have brought you, because I have had four of these with four different people in nine counties and every one of them was a thing I was doing instead of a thing I was counting. This is not a number about your three sentences, and there is no way to make it into one. It could not tell me whether they have moved anybody, and it would not have told me that if I had counted the same six doors in the second week of the fever, and the fifth and sixth halls would not have let me in then either, and I would have written the same four figures and had the same two crosses.*”

“*So the number does not have you in it.*”

“*The number has nobody in it at all, and that is the whole of it and I have been turning this over for two days,*” said the man of fifty-two. “*I have a number about halls. It is the best number anybody in this city has got about anything, and it is the wrong number for what you did, and I have got it because I went and looked at walls instead of people, and I would do it again tomorrow, and I am not going to pretend to you that I would rather be the kind of man who gets the other number.*”

---

The man with the leg stood on the step in the cold for a while and then said the thing that had been sitting in both of them for a week and neither of them had said.

“*What are you going to do with it.*”

“*I am going to take it to the one person in this city who can do one single thing with it, and I have got about four of them, and the first of them is a man of fifty-two who has been counting what comes through a door for nineteen years and is doing it at present for no reason he has ever been able to find out.*”

“*You are doing it for no reason.*”

“*I am doing it because it is mine,*” said Tarin Keel. “*That is the whole of the reason and I have never said it to anybody and I am saying it to you, because you have put a name at the top of a piece of paper this week and I cannot think of one reason on earth why I would keep that back from you today. A count is mine. That is what a count is. I have got a number and it is about halls, and there is no such thing as a number that is not about something, and the something is halls, and the only thing a man can do with a number about halls is stand in front of another man who counts and put it on the step between them and let him look at it, and that is what I have done, and it is not a favour and it is not a warning and there is nothing in it that is about a rule.*”

“*Then what you will do with it.*”

“*I will go up a mile and a half and stand in a room,*” said Tarin Keel, “*and I will not know what I am doing when I get there, and I will have a slate, and a man with a bad leg will be at one end of a table, and a bench of four will be upstairs, and neither of those things is going to help me in the slightest. And I am going to do it anyway, because the two doors I was not let into are not going to open for me and there is no third thing to do with a number except carry it somewhere it has not been.*”

---

“You have your name on it now.*”“*You have your name on it now.*”

“*I have.*”

“*At the top of the first line, in a clerk's hand, and a fair hand on the second floor with the same name on it, and a woman of forty-four at the foot of the fourth clause and the fifth,*” said Tarin Keel, and he said it the way he said everything, flat, in a street, and he did not make anything of it. “*I heard it in a yard yesterday afternoon. A boy of sixteen who works next to the plate-cutter's said it out loud in a doorway and then said it again because nobody had stopped him, and I did not go and correct him, because it is not my yard and it is not my sentence, and because it is true.*”

“*It is true.*”

“*Right,*” said Tarin Keel.

And he picked the slate up off the step and put it inside his coat with the lead, and neither of them said one word about a notice, and neither of them said one word about the rule, and the man who had been going to write a notice stood on a step beside a man who had put a document up a hill with his own name on it, and the two of them were right about the thing they had said in a doorway a month ago and neither of them had said it, and that is the whole of what is between them and it is not going to be settled this fortnight or any other.

# Chapter 356: Four Records That Have To Agree, And Three Of Them Do Not

It was his hundred and sixth morning and the first day of the seventy-third week, and a ward's public record is not a book. That is the thing the book calls the working and the country he came from has never had one, and it took him a morning in a yard at the low end of Tallowgate to understand it and the rest of his life to understand what it does.

There are four of them and they have to be kept in agreement, and the reason they have to be kept in agreement is that a public promise in this world holds its shape by weight.

---

The yard is a shed with a wheel in it and a grindstone and four hundredweight of blackglass offcuts in a heap along the north wall, and the offcuts are the only rich-looking thing in that ward. A plate is cut, not cast. You lay a sheet of it on the wheel, you take the names in order, and you cut a line for each one with a wheel and a hand, and a hundred names is an afternoon and a great deal of blackglass dust.

Bevin Tarr is fifty-nine and has been cutting plates for this ward since before the woman who keeps it started, which he says in the first minute, unprompted, and the reason he says it is that he wants it on the record that he is not new.

“*I will cut you a name,*” he said. “*That is the whole of my trade and I will do it this afternoon if you bring me the name and the fee.*”

“*And you do not ask whether it is true.*”

“*I have never in thirty-one years asked one of them whether it was true,*” said Bevin Tarr, “*and I want to say that carefully, because you are about to go off with it and use it. I do not know what a name is. I know what a line is. Somebody comes and gives me a name and I cut a line and they pay me four shillings the hundred, and I have cut four thousand lines in my life and I have not the faintest idea what any of them are for, and I have asked twice, and both times I was told it was the ward's business, and both times the person who said it was right.*”

“*Four shillings the hundred.*”

“*Four shillings the hundred, and I will tell you what that is, because I have watched people hear it and think it is nothing and then not come. Four shillings is forty-eight pence. That is a plate of a hundred names. I cut about a plate a year in this ward, and a plate is a hundred names, and there are years when I cut none, and the money from it has bought my house and a cart and a thing for my daughter at a school, and I am not being modest, I am telling you the size of the thing. Eight shillings cuts two hundred names. Four shillings buys a loaf and a bit and it is not a great deal and it is not nothing and it is the whole difference between a name being in this ward and a name not being in this ward.*”

“*And who pays it.*”

“*That is not me either,*” he said. “*I have had a ward fund twice in thirty-one years and both times it was the ward and both times it took four months to get and both times it was about a plate for a whole ward and not for one name, and I have had money for one name exactly twice and both times a woman paid it out of her own hand and would not say what it was for.*”

---

Then he took a plate out of a rack and set it down on the bench, and it was the roll of Tallowgate, and he let the stranger pick it up, which he should not have done, and which he did because nobody had ever asked him for one.

The face of it was black and had been polished every week for eleven years. About half of it was polished to a shine you could not see your face in. And there was a line across it, and it was not a scratch: it was where the polish stopped.

He put his thumb on it. There was a cold place at the end of his thumb, and the cold was not cold the way water is cold, it was the specific cold of a thing that has stopped agreeing with something else, and he had had that in his hands four hundred miles off on nine mornings out of a hundred and five and he had told nobody until a kitchen, and he stood in a shed in a ward of a city and had it in a book.

“*What is that,*” he said.

“*That is where the last polishing stops and the new one starts,*” said Bevin Tarr. “*That is two years, near enough, and it is the same week as the other thing and I will come to that. I do the same to every plate and it is the same line on all of them and I have never once had anybody ask me about it and I have never thought it was a thing to think about.*”

“*What is above the line.*”

“*About eleven hundred names above the line, and every one of them cut in the same fortnight two years ago, when the ward was weighed and the block was cut and the ward had money for the first time in nine years,*” said Bevin Tarr. “*Below the line I have cut two hundred more in the two years since, and the ward has had no money, and the two hundred were paid for out of two women's own hands and neither of them would say what for. So the plate says thirteen hundred and the paper in the room at the back of the chandler's shop says fifteen hundred, and the two hundred that are on the paper and are not on here are the two hundred I have not been paid to cut, and I know the paper says fifteen hundred because the woman who keeps it has told me once, in a doorway, in four words, and I have not forgotten it, because it is the only time in thirty-one years that anybody has told me anything about the thing I do.*”

---

Then he took him out into the lane and round the back of two houses, because a stone cannot be moved and a plate can.

The ward's stone is set in a gatepost at the end of a passage, and it is a block of about the size of a door with a flat face, and on the flat face there is a mark, and the mark is not a name and not a date and not a figure anybody in the ward could read.

“*That is the weight,*” said Bevin Tarr. “*Two years ago, near enough, the last time this ward had the money for it, the plates were cut right through and the ward was weighed against that block, and the answer was cut into the face of it, and that is the weight of the ward. Every plate in this ward is meant to weigh the same as the last one, because a name is a name whether it is a child or a grandmother and the ward is the ward.*”

He put a hand on the post.

“*You cut a name and the plate gets heavier, and you can take that out on that,*” he said, pointing at a post, a bar, and a stone pan in a shed doorway, “*and if you have cut nine hundred names since the weight was cut into the block, then the block and the plate say two different things, and they have been saying two different things for two years, and I have not told anybody, because I am a man who cuts things and is paid by the hundred, and I have not had a fee to charge for saying it.*”

“*What happens when two records disagree.*”

“*Nothing,*” said Bevin Tarr, “*and that is the answer and it is the whole of why this has gone the way it has gone. The ward does not consult anybody. The ward's account is the account that gets read. The block is in a gatepost and it is not read by anybody, and I read it, and I cannot make it do anything, and I have been carrying this for two years and I am fifty-nine and I have a yard and four hundredweight of offcuts and I am telling a stranger with a leg in a passage behind two houses at about the sixth hour because I have got nothing else to do with it and he asked.*”

“*What would make the block agree again.*”

“*A meeting of the ward,*” said the plate-cutter, “*and a notice, and a day, and nine people in a room, and the block re-weighed in front of them. That is all. It is not money. I keep saying it is not money and you keep looking at my plate, and I have been cutting for thirty-one years and I have never once had to ask a ward to do anything, and a name is four shillings the hundred and a meeting is nothing, and I have never in my life been in a room where a ward met, and I do not know how one is called.*”

---

They stood in the passage.

The stranger had a piece of arithmetic in his hands by then and it was the easiest he had ever had, and it was the worst he had ever had, because there was nothing in it that was difficult.

Two hundred names on paper and not on a plate. Four shillings the hundred. Two hundreds at four shillings is eight shillings. A name is about half a penny.

And to get the three hundred and whatever else out of that ward, and out of the other low ward, and into a protection that a magistrate had put there on purpose and would defend on purpose, it was not eight shillings, it was not a hundred pounds, it was nothing anybody had ever put on a piece of paper. The money was a farthing a name and the rest of it was a woman at thirty doors a fortnight and a man at a grindstone and a stone in a gatepost and a meeting that nobody had called because there is no form in this city that says a man may call one, and there has not been one since before the water came.

“*Say that to somebody,*” said Bevin Tarr.

“*I am going to,*” said the stranger.

“*Then do it where it costs something,*” said the plate-cutter, “*because I have watched men say true things in this city for thirty years and every single one of them said it in a room with no door on it, and the room closed, and the block in my post still says what it said, and the two hundred are still on a paper, and I am going back to my wheel and I have two hundred lines of somebody's to cut before the last of the light and I am not going to be paid for them until somebody in a room with a door on it decides whose names they are.*”

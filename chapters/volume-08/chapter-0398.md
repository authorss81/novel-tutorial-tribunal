# Chapter 398: Four Shillings The Hundred, On A Bill In A Room, And Nine People

The first day of the seventy-ninth week was his hundred and forty-eighth morning and his forty-seventh in a city he came to on foot, and the fever was sixteen weeks and three days old, and for the first time in the four hundred years anybody in Orison could remember there was a bill for anything in it, and it was in a public room at the foot of a hill, and nine people had seen it by the time the bells went, and the clerk of thirty had written the number down because he is a man who does that kind of thing without being asked.

The man at the wheel made it out loud, standing at the end of the long table, because the man at the wheel had been in this city for thirty-one years and had cut four thousand lines and had never once been asked what a line was for, and he was not going to write a figure on a piece of paper and let a room find it.

“*Say the first line and say the working.*”

---

“*The first line is a plate. A plate is four shillings the hundred names. This ward's own two hundred names is two hundreds at four shillings the hundred, and that is eight shillings. The three hundred names in the four low wards is three hundreds at four shillings the hundred, and that is twelve shillings.*”

“*Put those on it.*”

He put them on it. And then he stopped with the pen on the third line and did not write on it, and looked round the room, and said:

“*And I am not writing anything on the third line, and I am going to say why in a room, and nobody is going to thank me for it and I am not going to wait.*”

“*Say it.*”

“*The three hundred are not on a plate. A name said by a person of a ward goes in without a fee, and a name that goes in without a fee goes in without a plate, and a plate is not what puts a person in a bed, and the three hundred are on a paper in a book four floors up and they have been for the last eight days. So the three hundred are not a cost. The line is on the bill and there is nothing against it, and the nothing is the correct figure, and I have cut forty thousand lines in my life and I have never once put a line on a bill for something nobody is going to do.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*I am not being clever. I have been asked twice this morning by two people to cut a plate and I have said no to both of them and I have said no for the same reason each time, and the reason is that the three hundred are not on one, and if I cut one I am making a thing look as if it costs money when it does not, and a thing that looks as if it costs money is a thing a city stops arguing about.*”

---

“*Second line,*” said the man at the wheel. “*That is a keeper of a roll. A keeper of a roll is four shillings a year. Four shillings is forty-eight pence, and three hundred and sixty-four days go to a year in this city, and forty-eight pence over three hundred and sixty-four days is a farthing a day and not quite, and it has been four shillings for eleven years, and the eleven years are on the bill, and I am not going to work the eleven out in front of this room because it is not my line and I do not know what a year of it is worth and neither does she.*”

“*Nobody has ever shown me a bill for it,*” said the woman with the book, who was at the back of the room and had come a mile and a half and had not opened the door for it.

“*No,*” said the man at the wheel. “*And there is not one of them in four hundred years and I have cut lines for three of the recorders and I have never seen one of them do it either.*”

---

“*Third line is the round,*” he said. “*Thirty doors a fortnight over nine hundred doors is thirty lots of fourteen days, and that is four hundred and twenty days, and it has to be done twice because the doors that are shut cannot be counted, and four hundred and twenty twice is eight hundred and forty, and a year here is three hundred and sixty-four, and that is two years and a hundred and twelve days. And that is the figure and I am not the one who gave it to you, the woman at the back gave it to you yesterday in her own room, and I am putting it on a piece of paper because a figure that is only in a room is a figure that is gone.*”

“*And the fourth line.*”

He put the pen down.

“*The fourth line is the fifty-six days over a year, and the doors that were shut, and the round that has to be done over them, and I cannot put a figure on any of it and I have tried. I have been sitting at that table since the light with a sheet of candles in front of me and I cannot put a figure on it, and the reason I cannot is that I do not know what those days cost, and I have been doing this for thirty-one years and I have never once been asked what a thing costs before I have cut it, and I am not going to guess at it in a room at the foot of a hill.*”

He looked at the line.

“*So it is blank, and that is the first blank line I have ever put on a bill in thirty-one years, and a bill with a blank line on it is a bill that says somebody did this and did not get paid for it, and I do not know what anybody is going to do with that and neither does he.*”

---

And then he said the last thing, and he said it as a man explaining a heading, and nobody in that room had asked him for it and it is the only time in this city that anybody has said either number out loud.

“*You cannot have a bill nobody can read, so I have written the biggest figure I have ever cut over the top of it, and I am going to say where I got it, and I am not going to say what it was for and I have never asked.*”

“*Go on.*”

“*A hundred and forty days at nine pence a day is a thousand two hundred and sixty pence, and a thousand two hundred and sixty pence is five pounds and five shillings, and that is what a bell keeper is owed for a hundred and forty days, and it is not in this city and it is four hundred miles off, and I cut a plate for the record of it once, a long time ago, and I was not asked what it was for then either.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*And a keeper of a roll in this city is forty-eight pence a year. Forty-eight pence a year against five pounds and five for a hundred and forty days. The difference between the two is a thousand two hundred and twelve pence, and I have said it once, and I am not going to say it again, and I have not been asked to and I am not going to be thanked and I have been paid nothing for thirty-one years and this is the first week in my life anybody has put me on a piece of paper.*”

---

The clerk of thirty came round the long table and read the bill out loud, all four lines and the heading, in the flat voice, and then he asked the room who had seen it, and counted them, and nine hands went up out of eleven people, and he wrote the number nine against the line, because a bill that nine people have seen is a bill that nine people can be asked about.

“*Magistrate,*” he said, turning round, “*I want it read out and entered on a return, and I want the return to say that nine people saw it, because in four hundred years nobody in this city has been able to say how many people have seen a thing they will be asked about.*”

“*Entered,*” said Magistrate Rell, who had come down the hill at the fifth hour and had been standing at the back with her bag on the floor. “*And I am not going to pay any of it, and I have not got any of it, and this city has had no money in this ward for two years and that is on a return as well.*”

“*Then it is unpaid.*”

“*Then it is unpaid,*” said Magistrate Rell, “*and it is unpaid in the open, in a room, with nine people, and I would rather have that than a bill nobody sees, and I am not going to be thanked for that either and I have said so in three rooms this week and I am not going to stop saying it.*”

---

Then the man at the wheel got up and went to the wall with the bill in his hand, and everybody in that room understood about a second late what he was doing, and nobody stopped him, and he put it up on the nine inches of blank under the order.

“*That space is for a notice,*” said the clerk of thirty, “*and a bill is not a notice, and I have been in this room nine years and there is no form in this city that puts a bill on a wall.*”

“*No,*” said the man at the wheel. “*And there is no form in this city that puts anything at all on a wall except an order, and the order is the only thing on any wall in this ward that anybody is asked about, and I have spent thirty-one years cutting lines for three of the records that keep this ward in agreement and not one of them has ever been in a room where a person could see it. So it goes up there, and it is wrong by the rules of this room, and it is on the wall.*”

The clerk of thirty looked at it for a while, and then he did the thing he does four hundred times a day, which is the ordinary thing, in the ordinary way, in the ordinary voice, and the bill went up on the nine inches of blank under the order, crooked, because the paper was candle paper and the wall was not straight.

Nobody thanked the man at the wheel. He did not go back to the shed for the rest of that day, and he has not been asked to since, and the three hundred names are not on a plate, and the fee is four shillings the hundred and there is a bill for it in a room at the foot of a hill where nine people have seen it, and the fever was sixteen weeks and three days old and it did not stop for a bill.

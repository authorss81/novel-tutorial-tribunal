# Chapter 366: What A Householder Has Never Been Asked

It was his hundred and sixteenth morning and the fourth day of the seventy-fourth week, and the fourteenth was three days off, and he held eleven doors open in a mile and a half of Tallowgate and got an answer at four of them, and the four were the four that mattered and he did not know that until the fifth hour of the afternoon.

The third door of the morning was the one nobody had come to.

---

It is three visits before a house stops being a house with people in it and becomes a house, and by the third visit the woman of twenty-eight knew it and he did not, and he learned it from how she stood.

She knocked. Two fingers and the knuckle of the middle one, low, three times, and then she stood and did not say anything, and he held the door she was standing at, which is a thing a man can do for as long as he likes.

Nobody came.

On the second morning she knocked at about the seventh hour and waited and knocked again and waited, and a woman two doors down put her head out and said the family were at the low hall at the end of the ward and had been since the second week of the fever, and the woman of twenty-eight said *right* and did not go on to the next thing, and he said *right* too, because there was nothing else to say to a person about somebody else's family.

On the third morning she knocked and nobody came and there was a shutter closed across the front and a dog that was not the family's, and the shutter did not move, and after a while she put the chalk on the door and it was the same mark as every other mark she puts on a door when nobody comes to it, and there are about twenty of them in the six days she has been going, and it means *I have been here and you have not been here*, and there is no mark in this city for the other thing.

And that is the whole of a door that nobody answers, and it takes three visits and about an hour and a half, and the answer is not a fact about the people inside it.

---

“*What does the mark mean when nobody comes.*”

“*It means I was here and they were not,*” said the woman of twenty-eight. “*It does not mean they are not in the ward. It does not mean they are not in the book. The book has that family's name in it, because somebody said the name at that door in the autumn and the name is in the roll and the name is inside the protection of an order a magistrate put there on purpose, and there is no person in that house to give a glass of water to, and the mark on the door is the only thing in this city that knows they are not there.*”

“*That is not what the mark is for.*”

“*It is not,*” she said, “*and I have not got two days of that chalk left and about six doors, and a mark is not a document and there is nobody I could show it to. I have a door with a mark on it and a book at the back of a chandler's shop with their name in it and one of those two things is a record and it is not the one I can see.*”

---

The fourth door came at about the fourth hour and it was a woman of sixty with a shawl who had been standing in a yard at the low end of this ward on his tenth morning in this city and had stayed and had understood none of what was said there and had not needed to.

He held her door and she looked at him for three seconds and then said his business without being asked, which is a thing that happens to people who answer doors for a living.

“*You have come down that row about nine times in fifteen days and this is the first time you have knocked,*” she said. “*I have watched you. I am sixty and I watch. Ask it.*”

He asked it, and he asked it the way this city requires, which is that you ask it and then you stop talking.

“*Who in this street do you think is not on the book.*”

---

The woman of sixty with a shawl did not answer straight away and she did not go inside, and after a while she said:

“*I could tell you four names. I have had them for years. There is a house where there are three people and one name and I know all three and I have never said one of them out loud to anybody, and I am not going to start at a door with a man I have watched walk past my house nine times.*”

“*Then why not.*”

And here is the thing, and it is the whole of what the morning is for, and she said it in answer to a question, standing in her own doorway, with a shawl over her head against the cold and not against anything.

“*Because the day I say a name out loud in a street, I have not said a name,*” she said. “*I have said: there is a name in this street that I did not say to the woman with the book. And that is a fact about me, and a fact about me is a person in a record, and I have watched what happens to people in this city who are facts in records. The book is right. That is the first thing and I want it said. I have a name I did not say, and the book is right, and both of those are true at the same time and they have been true for years and they are not going to stop being true because I open my mouth about it in a cold street.*”

“*Then how would anybody find out whether the book is right.*”

And the woman of sixty with a shawl told him, and it took her a while and she told it the way she would tell you what the weather had done, and there was no weight in it at all.

“*You would have to ask every householder in this ward one question,*” she said. “*One question, at nine hundred doors, and the question is: have you ever left anybody off. Not whether you meant to. Not whether you were careless. Have you ever, on a day, in a year, in eleven years of standing at that door saying names to a woman with a book, left somebody off.*”

“*And nobody has ever asked that.*”

“*Nobody has ever asked anybody that in four hundred years,*” said the woman of sixty with a shawl, “*and I have worked out why, and it is not because anybody is hiding anything. It is because you cannot ask it without announcing it. The moment one person in this ward is asked whether they have ever left somebody off, then every person in this ward knows that the book is being checked, and after that it is not a book. It is a thing under examination. And a thing under examination in a ward is a list of who has been examined and what they said, and I have lived in this street fifty-eight years and I have seen what a list turns into four times and I am not one of the four.*”

“*So the book cannot be checked.*”

“*The book cannot be checked and the book is right,*” said the woman of sixty with a shawl, “*and there is no contradiction in that and I have had a lot of years to sit with it. You cannot find out whether nine hundred people have ever been careless, and if you could, the finding out would be worse than the carelessness, and so the answer to your question is that the street that is short is the street that is short, and the book that is right is the book that is right, and both of those are in the same ward and neither of them can be got at.*”

She looked at him.

“*You are going to ask me why I have not said a name,*” she said, “*and I am not going to answer that either, and you have not asked it, and I would like it noticed that you did not ask it.*”

---

He noticed it. He stood in a cold street at the fourth hour of the fourth day of the seventy-fourth week and noticed that he had four questions in his mouth and had asked one, and that the one he had asked was the one that could be asked, and that the three he had not asked were the three that would have cost her.

And then he went up the row and carried eleven more doors and did not ask one question at any of them, and that is the whole of what he did with the rest of the day, and it is the first time in a hundred and sixteen mornings that he has gone out with a question and come home without it.

---

At the sixth hour he was in the room at the back of the chandler's shop and there was a lamp on and a stack of nine slates and the door was shut, and he asked the only question he had left, which was the question a person of a place has to be asked before she can answer it.

“*How would you know if one of your nine hundred doors was wrong.*”

The woman at the table put her pen down.

“*I would not,*” she said.

“*You would not.*”

“*I have nine hundred doors and about thirty a fortnight and I have not been to a door since the water came, eleven weeks back, and I was about a fifth of the way through the round when it came, and I am not going to be going to a door while there is a fever in this ward, and there is a woman of twenty-eight in this ward who has been going to doors on her own account for six days and I have not asked her for one thing because I have been afraid of exactly the thing you have just spent a morning finding out.*”

“*What thing.*”

“*That the day anybody in this ward is asked to look at the book,*” she said, “*is the day there is a list of who looked at it. And I have been the woman with the book for eleven years and I have never once been on a list of anything, and I am not going to be on one, and neither is she, and neither are the four hundred other people in nine hundred doors who have never once been asked whether they have ever left anybody off.*”

She turned the chair round and put her hand flat on the book, and she said the last of it in the voice of a woman reading a line off a list she has read before.

“*So the record of this ward is as good as its worst door and its worst door is not knowable and cannot be made knowable by anybody in this city including me, and I have been keeping that for eleven years without knowing that I was keeping it, and you have been in this city fifteen days and you have found it out from a woman in a shawl at a door, which is how it was always going to be found out, and not from me, because I could not have said it in a room and you would have written it down.*”

He did not write it down. He stood in a room with a book in it and a woman of forty-four in it and he did not ask her the next question, which was the one about the two hundred names and the eight shillings and the block in the gatepost, and she knew what it was, and she waited for it, and he said:

“*There is nothing I can do with that.*”

“*No,*” said the woman at the table. “*There is not one thing you can do with that, and you have known how to say a true thing in a room since you came into this city, and I want to say this once and then I am going to open that door. You are the only instrument in this country that makes a person into a person in a record, and the only thing that instrument can do about the worst door in this ward is turn the person who keeps it into the person who is asked about it, and she has been the person who is asked about it since your tenth morning here, and there is nobody else for it to fall on, and I am telling you that I know it and I am not going to help you put it there twice.*”

She opened the door and there were six people at it and one of them was the man of forty with the bad cough who had not been at that door since the first hour of the day before, and he was let in this time, and the woman of twenty-eight's chalk went on the door after him and the four bells went over the low end of the ward the way they do, and nine hundred doors in Tallowgate had one question in them that four hundred years of the ward has never asked, and it is still in them, and the door was shut, and the worst door is still the worst door.

# Chapter 375: The Three Hundred, And A Plate Nobody Is Going To Cut

It was his hundred and twenty-fifth morning and the sixth day of the seventy-fifth week, and he had been in this city twenty-four days, and a boy of eight had been in a bed at the top of this city for one night and was better, and the yard at the low end of Tallowgate was cutting a plate.

He went down at the second hour because the yard is where the plate is and a plate is the record of account for a ward and a piece of paper is not, and because he had worked out in a lane the day before that the third sentence of the thing he had asked for had done something to this yard and he could not work out what.

---

Bevin Tarr is fifty-nine and has been cutting plates for this ward since before the woman who keeps its book started, and he is the second name on a sheet in a chamber in the black building at the middle of the nine bridges, and he is a person of this yard, and he was in this yard before a man with a leg came down the lane.

He knew somebody was coming at about the noon the day before and he did not stop cutting and he did not look up.

“*You will be wanting to know what a line is for,*” he said.

“*I am wanting to know what a line is for on a plate,*” said Ilyan, “*and you told me on the fourteenth that you have been cutting four thousand lines in thirty-one years and have never been told what a line is, and you asked twice, and I did not have an answer then.*”

“*You have got one now and it is worse.*”

---

He took the wheel off the plate and set it down and washed his hands in a bucket, which took a while, and he did it the same way he had done it on the day before the fourteenth, and he came back and stood against the bench with his arms folded.

“*Four shillings the hundred,*” he said. “*Two hundred names in this ward is eight shillings. Three hundred names is twelve shillings. I have been cutting lines for thirty-one years and there has not been a year in them that I did not cut at least one, and the two hundred that were cut in the last two years were paid for out of two women's own hands and neither of them would say what for, and there is no money in this ward and has not been for two years.*”

“*That is the same as what you told me on the fourteenth.*”

“*It is the same because it is a price and a price does not move,*” said Bevin Tarr, “*and I am going to say the other half of it and then I am going to ask you a question and you are not going to be able to answer it, and I have thought about nothing else since the change yesterday.*”

---

“*Say the other half.*”

“*Yesterday a piece of paper came down a hill with three sentences in it,*” said the plate-cutter, “*and a runner read it out in a yard at the top of the hill, because that is what the runners do with a thing that comes up on a tray, and the yard filled up behind him, and one of the people standing in it was the man who cuts my lines. And the second of the three sentences says that a hall that takes a person does not need that person's name on the roll of its ward.*”

“*Yes.*”

“*Then a line is nothing,*” said Bevin Tarr. “*Not this year. Not in a year. A line cut in this yard is the record of a person being in this ward, and a hall does not need the record of a person being in this ward, and so a hall does not need my line, and so nobody in this city has to come to me. And I have been cutting them for thirty-one years.*”

---

He said the last part without any weight on it, and then he asked the question, and it was the question he had told him to expect and it was worse than he had expected it to be.

“*Am I to stop.*”

There is no form in this city that says a man of fifty-nine shall stop cutting plates. There is no form that says he shall not. There is no bench in this city that has the care of a yard at the low end of a low ward, and no money in this ward, and no office that would be told.

“*That is not mine to answer,*” said Ilyan.

“*No,*” said Bevin Tarr. “*And that is the answer and I asked for it and I have got it and I am going to go back to the wheel, and I am not going to stop, and nobody is going to thank me for not stopping and nobody is going to tell me that it does not matter, and I want both of those said out loud in this yard this morning so that they are in it.*”

He picked the wheel up again.

“*Say them.*”

“*Nobody is going to thank a man for not stopping, and nobody is going to tell a man that it does not matter, and I am not going to be able to do either of those things, and I would be ashamed of both of them.*”

“*Right,*” said Bevin Tarr.

And he cut. He cut for four hours with a wheel in a shed at the low end of a low ward in a city of ninety thousand people, on a day when nobody in this city needed a line cut for anybody, and he is not relieved, and nobody has forgiven him anything, and he has still not been told what a line is for.

---

The room at the back of the chandler's shop at the fifth hour, and there were six people at the door and a lamp on, and Fenna Rusk put her pen down because he had not said anything and she could tell.

“*You have come down here to tell me a thing and you have not said it,*” she said.

“*The third sentence says a hall does not need the roll of its ward,*” he said, “*and I want to know what the roll of this ward is now.*”

“*Say it as a question and I will answer it and then you will have said it, because you cannot ask me anything in this room without it going in a book on the second floor and you have known that for three weeks.*”

He said it as a question.

---

“*It is a list of who is in a house,*” said the woman at the table, “*and it has always been that, and it is eleven years old and four hundred years old before me, and it is exactly as correct as it was on your first morning here and that is the part you are going to write down.*”

“*Go on.*”

“*Two hundred people in this ward are on a paper and not on a plate,*” she said. “*Before yesterday that was a thing with a price on it. Four shillings the hundred, eight shillings, and a man at a wheel who would cut them, and a person with eight shillings and no form could have had it done, and nobody has, and that was bad and it was fixable. It was bad and fixable and it has been bad and fixable for two years and nobody in this ward had eight shillings, and that is the honest version and it is not a defence of anything.*”

---

“*And after yesterday.*”

“*After yesterday, nobody can say they are outside,*” said the woman with the book, “*and I want you to understand that sentence properly, because you have been in this city twenty-four days and you will not get this again. Before yesterday those two hundred people were on a paper and not on a plate, and that was an error, and an error has a person who can be asked whether they will have it put right. Since yesterday a hall does not need the roll of its ward, and so the roll of this ward is not what puts a person in a bed, and so those two hundred names are not a gap. They are a second way of keeping a record. And a second way of keeping a record cannot be an error, because there is nothing for it to be an error about, and I have been in this room thirty-one years and I have never in my life been able to say that a thing I keep has become another thing.*”

She turned the chair round and put her hand flat on the book.

“*And the round goes on,*” she said. “*Thirty doors a fortnight and nine hundred doors and a year and fifty-six days, and I have not been to a door since the water came, which was thirteen weeks back, and I am not going to while there is a fever in this ward, and a penny's worth of chalk does nine days of a woman of twenty-eight's marks and I have not got a penny and she has told me twice that it is not her problem. And the round now has no price on it at all, because a round is for keeping a record correct and nobody in this city needs this record.*”

---

“*You are not relieved.*”

“*No,*” said the woman with the book, “*and I am not thanked, and I am not on the list of people this is good for, because there is no list, and I am still a person in a record in a building at the middle of the nine bridges because of a yard on the tenth morning of your being in this city, and that has not changed this week and nothing that happened this week will change it.*”

She opened the door and there were six people at it and she did not look at them.

“*Go and find out who is being moved,*” she said. “*I have nine hundred doors and I have not been to one of them in thirteen weeks and the number of people in the halls of four low wards is a number nobody in this city has, and the man with the bad ear has one hall and has counted the door of it. Go and ask somebody with a barrow.*”

---

He asked the woman with the barrow at about the sixth hour, and there was no barrow, and he asked the woman of twenty-eight at the end of the row, and she put the chalk down and gave him the whole of it, and she gave it to him because he asked, and she is a person of this row and was in this row before he came into the ward, and none of this is about her.

“*Nothing,*” she said. “*In one day, nothing. That is what the rule has done in one day and I have been at nine doors since the first hour and I will tell you exactly what I have found out about it, which is nothing, and I am not going to make a sentence out of nothing for you.*”

“*Then say what will happen.*”

“*I am not going to say what will happen either,*” said the woman of twenty-eight, “*and that is the first time anybody has asked me a question about this and got the answer they did not want, and you are going to be angry about it and I would like to get it said now rather than at the end of a week.*”

---

He was angry about it and then he was not, and what replaced it was worse, and this is the whole of what he had at the seventh hour on the sixth day of the week, and he got it standing at a gate at the end of a row, and it is not a thought and it is not a feeling. It is an address.

A permanent rule is a rule with nobody at the foot of it. That is why it is good; it cannot be argued with, and it survives a person going out of office, and it cannot be argued down at a gate in a cold street in a fortnight by nine frightened people, and it costs nothing that anybody can see this month.

And the reason a rule with nobody at the foot of it is good is that there is no person to ask.

And the people paying for this one are not in a room and not at a door and not in a ward, because a ward is not a person and cannot be asked, and they are in four wards in rooms and yards and doorways and in halls at the ends of rows, and the cheapest way to move somebody out of the reach of a rule is to make sure the rule is about everybody and lands on the person who happens to be worst, and everybody is in it, and there is no one to complain to, and he is the one who put it there, and there is no form in four hundred miles on which he could be asked about it.

He stood at a gate and worked out that saying the price out loud in a room had been his protection for three weeks and it was not his protection, and that it never had been. It had been a way of not being the only one who knew, and there were four people in a room and a runner on a hill and nine people on a barrow's journey who knew, and the price said out loud had put every one of them in a position where they could not do one single thing about it, which is the same position as him, and that is the only thing saying a price has ever done in this city, and the four hundred miles he came from taught him that it was something else.

---

And then the woman of twenty-eight said one more thing at the gate, and she said it because he asked her a question, and the question was whether anything in a mile and a half of Tallowgate was different this morning, and she told the truth.

“*There is a bed free at the top of this city that was not free yesterday, and there is a boy in it, and the row knows that already,*” she said. “*And I am not going to say anything more about doors. I have said what I have got to say about doors in this city and I said it to a stranger in a doorway and a man in a public room has written it in a book, and it is out of me now. I am going to go back to this row. If you want to walk at me for four hours you can walk at me for four hours and I will not say one word about it.*”

He walked at her for four hours and she did not say one word, and at the last of the light he went up the row with a leg that will not clear and no new instrument in his hands, and at the top of the low ground there is a hall at the end of a ward where a boy of eight is in a bed and is better, and at the bottom of it there is a shed where a man of fifty-nine is still cutting lines for a roll that nothing in this city needs, and a woman of forty-four is in a room behind a chandler's shop doing a round that has no price on it at all, and the four bells went, and the fever was thirteen weeks and one day old, and it did not stop for any of it.

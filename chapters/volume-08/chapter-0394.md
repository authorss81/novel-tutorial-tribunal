# Chapter 394: Magistrate Rell, On The End Of A Row, With Nobody Sent

The fourth day of the seventy-eighth week was his hundred and forty-fourth morning and his forty-third in a city he came to on foot, and the fever was fifteen weeks and six days old, and the part of the order had stood twenty days, and the draft had been in a chamber on the second floor for eight days, and a magistrate came down the hill at the second hour and stood in the doorway of the public room at the foot of it for a while before she came in, which nobody in that room thought was strange and everybody in that room noticed.

The one with the chair nearest the door said the name, because the name was at the foot of the order on the wall behind her, and a clerk of thirty reads that wall four hundred times a day.

“*Magistrate Rell.*”

She came in and sat down at the end of the long table on the side with the order behind her, and she put a pen on the table in front of her, and she did not have a tray and did not appear to want one.

“*I have come because four men asked me a question out loud in a room the size of a market day,*” she said, “*which nobody has done to me in the whole time I have been in this city, and I want to say before anything else that there is no form for it and I could have said no. I would have been within my rights to say no, and I did not, and I am not going to be thanked for it, because I did it because the argument was good and not because anybody in this room deserves anything.*”

---

“*Ask me,*” she said. “*I have a fever and four hundred beds and I am not going to stand in a doorway. There are seven things on your list and I am going to answer every one of them, and I am going to answer them in the order they were read, and I am going to tell you when I do not know, and I have written down that I do not know four times in a fortnight, which is four more times than the last magistrate of this city wrote down in eleven years, and that is not a boast, it is a fact about me that I have had to work at.*”

He asked the seven, in the order they had been read, and she answered all seven, and nobody in the room interrupted her once, which is a thing about a room in this city and not about her.

The beds were hers. “*Eleven in the low hall at the end of this row and twenty-one on its books, and the number on the board says nothing is free and the number is true, and a hall is a place a person cannot be discharged from, because being discharged is a document and there is no discharge paper in this city and no office that issues one, and I did not put that in the order and I have known it since the morning I signed it and I did not put it in because I could not write the second thing without the first, and the second thing is who goes and stands at the door. I am not going to tell this room that I did not put that in for a reason, because I did not have a reason. I did not have a person.*”

The round was hers. “*Four hundred and twenty days and fifty-six over a year of three hundred and sixty-four, and I have had that figure since a woman with a book read it out in a room at the foot of this hill and I did not put it in, because putting it in is a sentence saying that one person in this ward is going to do four hundred and twenty days of work that nobody has agreed to, and if I had written that sentence it would have been true and it would have been the worst thing on the wall, and I would rather have an order that is incomplete than an order that is a way of making one person carry a year.*”

The nineteen dead were hers, in the way everything about them was hers, which was not at all. “*I have never seen that column. I know it exists because a man with a bad ear has said so in a room. I have no power over it and I have never asked to see it, and that is a thing I have been ashamed of for about a fortnight and I have not done anything about being ashamed of it, because doing something about it would be a thing I did for him and not for the ward.*”

The four records were hers. “*A plate, a paper, a woman with a lamp, and a block in a gatepost. I have seen three of them. The block I have seen once, a long time ago, on a different matter, and there is a mark on its face and I did not have anybody with me who could read it and I did not go back. That is the whole of my interest in a block in a gatepost and I have written it down as being the whole of it.*”

---

And then she was asked the one that mattered, and it was asked by a woman of twenty-eight who was not named in any room and did not give her name, and the question was this.

“*Say the sixth part out loud and then say who has heard it.*”

Magistrate Rell did not answer that one straight away, and she took about as long over it as she had taken over the others put together.

“*A date, and the word standing in the margin beside it, and the effect of the two of them together is that the day does not arrive. And there is one copy of it and it is on a wall, and a clerk reads it out in a room, and that is how a rule reaches people in this city. It is not a good way. It is the only way, and I chose it, and here is why and I am going to say it in a room and I am not going to pretend it is better than it is.*”

She put two fingers on the order on the wall behind her.

“*A rule that is not read out is a thing that hangs on a wall, and I would rather it hung on a wall that a clerk can reach. A rule that is read out in one room, once, is a rule that only the people in that room have. There is no second copy. There is no form in this city that gives anybody a copy, and I did not put one in the order, and if I had put one in I would have had to say who was in the room and who was not, and on the morning I wrote it I did not know that, and I know it now, and knowing it now does not make it a different order.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*So yes,*” she said. “*I wrote one legible rule and it is legible and it is one page and a person can hold it in his head, and a person can be told it in a doorway in ninety words. And it cannot be appealed by anybody who is not in the room when it is read out, and you have all known that since the morning you found the paper, and you have been waiting since the morning you found the paper for somebody with a duty to say it out loud, and I am saying it, and I am not going to be forgiven for it and I am not going to be thanked for it and I am not going to be argued out of it in a room at the foot of a hill on the fourth day of a week.*”

---

“*Then say what you want,*” said the one with the chair nearest the door.

Magistrate Rell said what she wanted, and she said it in four hundred words, and it is worth having in her own mouth because it is the best argument anybody made in fifty chapters of this city and it was made by the person everybody in the room had come to be against.

“*I want the order to be left as it is and I want the sixth part to be filled in with a date, and I want a bench of four to hold the order and be able to be asked about it, and I want one copy on a wall that a clerk can reach, and I want it said out loud in this room in those words this morning and recorded.*”

“*That is the same as it is now with a date on it.*”

“*It is not the same, because a date in it means that on that day it stops, and a rule that has to be argued again every time a fever comes is a rule that will not be here when the next fever comes,*” she said. “*And it will come. That is the whole of what I have to say about the future and I have said it every day since the order went up to four men and four stools and a clerk, and I will say it once more. This fever will go down and about a hundred and forty people will be dead and the low ground will be dry in the autumn, and in four years or forty there will be another one, and it will be in a different ward, and it will be under a different magistrate, and the people who are in it will be the people whose names are on a roll of their own ward because that is where the houses are. And on that morning the thing that decides whether they are inside or outside will be whether the person writing the order is in a hurry, and a hurried person will write six parts, and half of the six will be an exit, and that is the best that anybody can do in a week, and I have been in this city since before you came off the road and I have read every other thing on the walls of it and there is nothing better.*”

She stopped, and then she said the last of it, and the room did not get it until the following day.

“*And I know what that argument is. It is the argument that says one person, one rule, one hand, legible to everybody it reaches, in a hurry, every time, forever. And the reason I am asking for it is not that I want the pen. It is that if there are two hands then on the day there is a fever two people have to agree, and on the day there is a fever two people cannot be got. You have all spent a month learning that a rule nobody can be asked about is not a rule, and I am telling you that a rule that cannot be written is not a rule either, and I would rather have the second fault than the first, and I will say so in a room and be out-voted in a room and I will not be quiet about it afterwards.*”

---

The four of them put their heads together at the end of the long table for about as long as it takes a clerk to read a form out loud, and the clerk of thirty watched them do it and wrote the number down, and nobody in the room was asked anything, and that is the only vote that has ever been taken in that room and it is not a vote of the room and it is not a vote of hers, because she is not one of them and does not sit with them and has never sat with them.

It was three to one.

The one was the man with the chair nearest the door, and he gave his reason in one sentence and then gave the whole of it, because he had never in twenty-two years done the first thing and he did not intend to stop at it.

“*I am against it and it is not against her,*” he said, “*and I have been the man who fills the column of things sent for twenty-two years and I have never once had to read a thing twice. An order that is one person's word and one page and one pen is a thing this city can still do in a hurry, and that is the only reason any of us will ever be any use again. But a bench of four has never in four hundred years been in a room with the people an order is about, and the reason is that we are sent things. So I am for the end of it and I am for the reading of it and I am for the words going on a return where a clerk writes them and a room can hear them, and I am against one hand, and my one is the one and I would like it printed that my one is the one and not hers.*”

Magistrate Rell looked at him for a while.

“*Then I am one hand and you are four,*” she said, “*and four is a majority, and I have not been out-voted once in this city, and I have been out-voted in a room in a morning by three men I have met once. I am not going to call that a defeat, because there is nobody here who could tell you what I would have done this afternoon if it had gone the other way, and I am not going to be relieved, because I do not know how to be relieved and I have not got the time.*”

“*Magistrate,*” said the clerk of thirty, “*the return is written by me and the return says what was asked and what was done about it. What do you want on it.*”

She thought about it for a while, and he waited, because he has nine years of waiting and he is very good at it and it did not show.

“*All of it,*” she said. “*Including my one, and including that I said yes to the order and I still say yes to the order, because the fever is going to come back and I am not going to be one of the people who let this city argue itself to death about a rule a second time. Somebody will tell you that I have been made smaller. I have not been made smaller. I have been made four, and there is a difference, and the difference is that four of us can be asked and I can only be argued with.*”

---

The clerk of thirty wrote it and read it back and the clerk of forty copied it fair, and there was nothing in it but a form with words on it, and the pen on the table in front of Magistrate Rell went into her bag and came out again twice in the next hour because she could not leave off correcting a clerk, which is a thing about her and not a thing anybody asked her to do.

The four went up the hill at the change. She stayed, and the fever was fifteen weeks and six days old, and the four bells went over the low end of Tallowgate, and nobody thanked her and she was not thanked and did not expect it, and the blank nine inches under the order on that wall was still blank, and it was going to have something on it within the week.

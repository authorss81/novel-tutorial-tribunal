# Chapter 371: A Boy At The End Of The Boards, And A Woman On A Stool

It was his hundred and twenty-first morning and the second day of the seventy-fifth week, and he had been in this city twenty days, and the fever was twelve weeks and four days old, and he went down to the low hall at the end of Tallowgate before the second hour, because the hall is where the beds are and he had not been inside it since the day before the fourteenth.

A hall in this city is five things and the five of them are the same in all six of them. A door. A board on the wall with the number of beds free, chalked and rubbed out every morning. A stool. A pail. And a number of beds. There is nothing else in a hall in this city and there has not been anything else in a hall in this city for longer than anybody in it has been alive.

---

He came in at the second hour and the woman on the stool looked up and then went back to what she was doing, which was nothing, and had been doing nothing since the first hour, and is her whole employment.

She is fifty-eight and she has been on that stool since the eighth morning of the fever, and in three weeks she has said the same sentence out loud to everybody who has come through that door, and the sentence is not a complaint and she has never once said it as one.

“*That one at the end is worse than the other nine,*” she said, to a man with a bad leg who had come in eleven minutes late and had a coat on. “*That is all I have to say about him today. It is what I say every morning and I will say it this evening, and you can do what you like with it.*”

He had come in to be told something and he had been told something, and it had taken her four seconds, and he understood that this was a post.

---

The boy was at the end of a run of boards and he was eight, or looked it, and nobody in the hall had said how old he was to a stranger and nobody was going to, and there is a line in chalk at the foot of his board, and the line is his, and the line is not his name, it is a line, and the way the line was made was that a woman wrote a mark on a slate and a man wrote the mark on a board at the end of the beds, and neither of them wrote a name, and there is no office in this city that would ask them to.

He was not asleep and he was not awake and he was doing the thing that a child does on a board when he has been on a board for a long time, which is to watch the door without appearing to watch the door. He watched the man with the leg come in. He watched him for a moment and then he looked at the pail, and the pail was the most interesting thing in the room, and he was right.

The man with the leg did not touch him. He wanted to. He had a hand out and he put it back in his coat, and there is no instrument in four hundred miles that says a man with a leg may put two fingers on a wrist in a hall, and he was not going to be the reason a boy of that age was a thing somebody had handled on a stranger's account.

---

The board at the end of the beds is a board and a piece of chalk and it has a line for every person who has been in this hall since the eighth morning of the fever, and the line stays, because a person is not discharged from a hall, and being discharged is a document, and there is no document and no office that issues one.

The number of lines went up by one on the morning before this one and it has not come down by one since the eighth morning of the fever.

The man with the bad ear was at the end of it with his own board, which is a different board, which is a column of nineteen lines in chalk with nothing over the top of it and nothing at the foot of it, and he is paid nothing for it and has been keeping it since the second week.

He did not look up when the stranger came in, because a man who keeps a column of dead learns to hear a footstep and does not need the rest.

“*You have not been in here since the day before the fourteenth,*” he said, to the board. “*That is three days and I have counted them on a board that has nineteen lines in it and none of them are days.*”

“I have been in here on the day before the fourteenth.”

“*Then you have been in here once in twenty days,*” said the man with the bad ear, “*and the hall has taken eleven people in that time and lost none of them, and if you are going to look at the wall board, look at it, because I have not got round to rubbing it out and you will save me the trouble.*”

---

The wall board said a number and he had seen the number every day he had been in this city.

It was two.

Two beds free, and they had been the same two since before the fever came off the water, and he asked the woman on the stool about them, and she told him about them in a way that had nothing in it.

“*Those two are not free,*” she said. “*They are the two at the far end by the pail, and they are free because the people in them are not here, and they are not here because they are in the yard and the yard is out of the weather for a week at a time in this season, and the rule in this city is that a person who is in a yard is out of the hall, and there is a form for it somewhere and nobody here has ever seen it.*”

“*So the two are spoken for.*”

“*The two are spoken for by a sentence,*” said the woman on the stool. “*That is all a bed is in this hall. A bed is a line on a board and a sentence about the line, and the sentence is why nobody comes in here and nobody goes out.*”

---

Then he asked the question he had come for, and he asked it in the way this city requires, which is that you ask it and then you stop talking, and he asked it at the end of the run of boards with the boy four feet behind his shoulder.

“*What would have to be true for that one to be put in a different bed.*”

Three people answered him and all three of them were right, and none of the three of them had been waiting for him to ask.

---

The first was the man with the bad ear, and it was the plain one.

“*A bed that is free in a hall in this city is a bed in a hall in a ward he is not on the roll of,*” he said. “*Or a bed in a hall in this ward, and there are none, and there have not been any since the eighth morning, and the reason there are none is not that people are coming in faster than they are going out. The reason there are none is that nobody goes out.*”

“*What is the number of people in the halls in this city.*”

“*I do not know that and nobody in this city knows it and I have counted the door of this one and not the doors of the other five,*” said the man with the bad ear, “*and I have a bad ear and a column of nineteen and I am thirty-one, and that is the whole of what I have counted this month.*”

---

The second was the woman on the stool, and it was the one about the roll, and she took her time over it and she did not look at the boy while she said it.

“*A hall in another ward is under that ward's roll,*” she said. “*Not under this one. And a name goes onto a ward's roll when somebody who lives in that ward says it at a door in that ward, and there is no door in this row that anybody in another ward ever comes to, and there has not been one in my lifetime, and a ward cannot be asked to send anybody anywhere because a ward is a body only when a ward meets and there is no form in this city that calls a meeting of a ward.*”

“*Then somebody in another ward could say a name at a door in that ward.*”

“*Somebody in another ward could say a name at a door in that ward if that somebody were in this hall,*” said the woman on the stool, “*and he is not, and his mother is, and his mother is not either, because a woman who is on a roll in this ward and standing at a door in this ward is the door, and the door is this ward and not the other one.*”

She turned her head a few inches.

“*That is the whole of the arithmetic and there is no way round it and I have been sitting on this stool for three weeks working it out in my head at about the rate of two doors a day.*”

---

The third was not in the hall. It came at the change, at the standpipe at the end of the row, and it was a woman of thirty-four who is the head of her own house and has been for nineteen years, and who was drawing water and who had a boy of eleven with two pails and no penny due until the end of the week, and none of that is in what she said.

She said the thing that made the rest of his week, and she said it while wringing out a pail, and she was not saving it up.

“*Six halls,*” she said. “*Three of them in the low ground and three of them in the rich wards at the top. Every one of them has a board with the number of beds free on it, and in every one of them that number has not gone down by one since the eighth morning, and in four of them it is nothing, and in two of them it is not, and in one of those two there are beds this morning.*”

“*You have counted them.*”

“*I have counted the doors of two of them and the other four I know the number of from the woman who is on a stool in this one, who says it out loud,*” said the woman of thirty-four. “*And I have been in this city nineteen years and I am not a fool. Half the beds in this city are empty and have been empty all winter, and there is a boy at the end of that run of boards who is worse than the other nine, and there is a bed in this city that he could be in by noon, and he is not going to be in it, and it is not because there is no bed.*”

“*Why is he not going to be in it.*”

“*Because getting a man from a hall at the end of this row into a hall at the top of this city is not a thing that has ever been done,*” she said, “*and there is no form for it, and the reason there is no form for it is that in four hundred years nobody has needed one, and that is the whole of what I know and I have been standing at this standpipe since the first hour.*”

She wrung the pail out again.

“*Say the arithmetic back to me and say it right,*” she said, “*because I have said it out loud twice in nineteen years and both times to a person who was not a person in this city.*”

---

He said it back to her and he said it right, and it was this.

The boy is inside the protection. His mother is on the roll and she said his name at a door, and a name said at a door is the only door a name in this city has ever come through, and so the boy is inside. And being inside is the reason nothing can be done for him. Inside the protection he is in a hall at the end of a low ward in a city where there are no beds free, and the rule that put him in there is the same rule that says a bed in a hall in another ward is not a bed he can be put in, because another ward's protection comes from another ward's roll, and there is no door in this row that anybody in another ward ever comes to.

And the number of beds in this city that are not full has not gone down by one in any of the six halls since the eighth morning of the fever, and in about half of them there are beds, and none of the beds in this city can be reached by anybody in this row, and nobody has lied about any part of it.

“*Right,*” said the woman of thirty-four. “*Then you know as much about that boy as I do, and I have known it since the eighth morning, and I would like you to notice one thing about the two of us.*”

“*Say it.*”

“*I have been at this standpipe every morning for three weeks and I have said that out loud to nobody,*” she said. “*You have been in this city twenty days and I have said it to you once. That is not a thing about you. That is a thing about what a sentence is worth in a city, and you have not asked me a single question about my house and I have noticed that, and I am not going to say anything else about it, and go and do whatever it is you are going to do.*”

---

He went back up the row at about the sixth hour with the arithmetic in him and it did not go anywhere, and he stood at a gate for a while, and this is what he worked out standing at a gate, and it is the whole of what the next three days are made of and he worked it out alone and told nobody until the second hour of the next morning.

The boy is inside the protection and there is a bed in this city he could be in by noon.

Anybody who can put the boy in that bed has to do one of three things. They can have a person in a bed in another ward, which is the roll of that ward and no door in this row. Or they can call a meeting of a ward, and there is no form for it, and the meeting would be a thing a magistrate called and the man of fifty-two at the rail has been promising one for ten days. Or they can put a sentence into the order, and the order is on the wall of a public room at the foot of the hill and it has five parts and a half, and the sixth part is a line with a date in it and the word in the margin is standing, and a magistrate can add to an order and nobody else can, and a person is a person in a record.

And there is a fourth thing, and he stood at a gate and found it, and it is the only one of the four that is any good, and it is the one that is going to cost the most.

A rule can be written about a person. A rule written about a person has the person's name at the foot of it, and a name at the foot of a thing is a person, and a person is somebody who can be asked about the rule for the rest of their life. Nine people in a yard at the low end of this ward had heard hers spoken out loud, and four people on a bench in a room on the second floor of a building at the middle of the nine bridges had heard his.

Or a rule can be written about a class of people. A rule written about a class has nobody's name at the foot of it. There is no person in it. And a rule with no person in it is a rule that nobody in the world can be asked about, and a rule that nobody can be asked about cannot be argued with, and cannot be stopped, and cannot be called cruel, and cannot be got out of.

The woman on the stool had told him, standing at a pail, that she says the same nine words out loud to everybody who comes through that door and has never once said them as a complaint, and she had not said his name, and he had not asked her for it, and he had understood at the time that she was being careful.

She had not been being careful about a name in a yard.

She had been being careful about the only thing there is to be careful about in a city, which is that the day somebody says a name out loud in a street in this ward, that name is a person in a record, and a person in a record is somebody who can be asked about a thing for the rest of their life, and the boy at the end of the boards is eight years old and has been in that hall for three weeks and is worse than the other nine and is the only person in this ward whose name nobody has put anywhere at all.

And a rule with no name at the foot of it is the only thing on earth that can get him a bed this week.

That is what he worked out at a gate, and he said the price of it out loud to nobody, because there was nobody within four hundred yards, and the four bells went over the low end of Tallowgate the way they do, and the number on the board at the end of the beds was the same number it had been on the morning he came down to it, and the boy was still at the end of the run of boards, watching the pail.

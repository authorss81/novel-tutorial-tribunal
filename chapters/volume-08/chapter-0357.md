# Chapter 357: Twelve Shillings, And A Pound Three Shillings And Sixpence

It was his hundred and seventh morning, and he did the arithmetic out loud in a room with nine people in it, and he had never in his life done anything so careless and so deliberate at the same time, and both of those were true, and the reason he knew it was true is that he went in there having written it on nothing.

The public room at the black building has a notice board on one wall, and on that board is the order, and under the order, at about chest height, there is a blank space nine inches deep where a notice goes that is not the order, and on the seventh day of his time in this city the blank space was still blank.

He said the figures in that space.

---

Here is what he said, and the reason it is here in full is that a person read it back afterwards and a reader is entitled to do the same.

“*The four low wards are short about three hundred names. That is two hundred in Tallowgate, which the woman who keeps the paper has told me and the man who cuts the plates has told me, and it is seventy in Weir Lane, and thirty in Nethergill, and I have not been to the Ninth Weir and I am not going to invent a figure for it, and three hundred is the part of it I can stand behind.*”

“*A name on a plate costs four shillings the hundred. Three hundred names is three hundreds at four shillings, and that is twelve shillings. Twelve shillings is a hundred and forty-four pence.*”

“*A date at this bench, bought, is a pound three shillings and sixpence. A pound is twenty shillings, which is two hundred and forty pence, and three shillings is thirty-six, and six is six, and two hundred and forty and thirty-six and six is two hundred and eighty-two pence. A carter is eighteen pence a day, and two hundred and eighty-two is eighteen times fifteen and twelve, so it is fifteen days of a man on a cart and twelve pence over.*”

“*So the whole of what it costs to put three hundred people inside the protection that a magistrate put there on purpose is a hundred and forty-four pence, and the cost of getting a hearing about it is two hundred and eighty-two, and the difference is a hundred and thirty-eight pence.*”

And then the last of it, which is the one that is actually the finding and the other two are only the shape of it.

“*A keeper of a bell in the country I came out of is five pounds five a year. That is ninepence a day for a hundred and forty days, which is one thousand two hundred and sixty pence, and a woman in this city who decides who is inside a protection is four shillings a year, which is forty-eight pence. The difference between them is one thousand two hundred and twelve pence, which is five pounds and a shilling.*”

He said it in two minutes and he did it in the voice he has used for a hundred and seven mornings, which is the voice of a man reading something out, and he did not use that voice on purpose and he could not afterwards account for it.

---

There were nine people in the room.

Two of them were ward clerks who had come to read the order again because a woman of sixty or so had come to ask whether her own name was in it, which it was. There was a man with a barrow who had come about a lane, not about the fever. There was a boy of fourteen. There was a runner in a magistrate's colour who was waiting for a date to be free. There was a woman with a bundle and a child asleep against her shoulder.

And there was a man of fifty-two standing at the rail at the back with a piece of lead in his hand, counting what came through the door, because a fever closes the roads and a city that has closed its roads has never in its life needed anybody to count what crosses them more, and he had been somebody in nine counties in nineteen years and had never once been useful and had never once been asked.

Nobody said anything for some time.

The woman with the bundle said: “*Does that do anything for my sister's boy?*”

“*No,*” he said. “*Not today. Today it is a figure said out loud in a room. If it is in the record and somebody with a duty reads it, then on some day it is an instruction, and on the day it is an instruction it will do something, and between now and then it does nothing at all, and I am not going to stand here and tell you otherwise, because you have been up since the fourth hour with a child on your shoulder and you have had enough of people telling you things are going to be fine.*”

“*Right,*” she said, and went out, and she did not thank him, and there was nothing to thank him for, and that was the correct answer and it cost him more than any of the rest of the morning.

Then a clerk of forty at the end of the table said the sentence that made the morning what it was.

“*I am going to have to write that down.*”

“*You are not.*”

“*I am going to have to write that down,*” said the clerk, “*because that is my job, and because it was said in this room, and because the room is the room, and there is a rule about this room older than anybody in it and it is one line and it says that a thing said in the public room is a record. I do not get to decide what is worth writing. I get to decide how fast.*”

And he wrote it down. It took him eleven minutes and he did it in a fair hand and he read it back twice and he got a number wrong once — he had put twenty for a hundred and forty-four — and a man at the rail corrected him in nine words and did not say his name and was not thanked for it, and the clerk said thank you, which is the only time in this chapter that anybody says it.

And that is the whole of what happened. A man said a true thing out loud in a room, and there is a person in this city whose job it is to write down what is said in that room, and therefore in this city it does not matter in the least whether the room has a door on it.

In the country he came out of, saying a true thing out loud in front of other people is a thing that happens to a person. In this city it is a document, and a document is a place somebody can be asked.

He had known that. He had known it since a room over a smithy four hundred miles off and a woman who said out loud that somebody should teach her to write and nobody answered her.

It is worse here. It is worse because here there is somebody whose job it is.

---

Tarin Keel came off the rail at about the third hour and put the lead in his coat and waited until the room had emptied and the clerk had gone off with his fair hand.

“*You have made a record,*” he said.

“*I have made a record.*”

“*And you have not made a task,*” he said, “*and I have watched a man make a record instead of a task four hundred times in nineteen years, and the record costs a clerk eleven minutes and a task costs a man a day, and I am not saying that to be clever, I am saying it because you are about to go and find out which of the two this is and I would rather tell you now.*”

“*Say the other half.*”

“*The other half is that I would have said the same figures to the magistrate in a corridor inside a week and she would have heard them in ninety seconds and put one of them in an order, and that is true, and it is also the reason this city is the way it is.*” He looked at the notice board with the blank nine inches under it. “*I have spent nineteen years being the one person who says a thing to the one person who can do it, and it has worked, and I have been in nine counties, and in six of them the one person who can do it was gone by the time anybody had checked. You say it in a room and thirty-one years from now a clerk will read the fair hand and it will be a thing that was said. I am not telling you which one is right. I have been telling you which one is fast for twenty years and I have never once been able to make it the other one.*”

“*You are right about that,*” Ilyan said, “*and you are right about the corridor, and I am going to do the room, and the reason I am going to do the room is that the corridor is a man and the room is a document, and I have spent a hundred and seven mornings finding out what a document is, and I know that a document outlasts a man and I know that a document can be carried to a door by somebody who never met me and is thanked for it and is not thanked for it, and I know that a man in a corridor is a man in a corridor.*”

“*That is the best argument I have heard for anything in nine years,*” said Tarin Keel, “*and I have never once agreed with it, and I am not going to now, and I am going to be at that rail at the change and I am going to count what comes in, and you are not to come and stand at the rail with me and ask me whether I would rather be doing the other thing, because I would, and I have been right about the other thing nine times and wrong about it once, and the once was four years ago and there is a village in it.*”

He went back to the rail.

---

Here is the rest of it, because a chapter about a number is not finished by the number.

Nobody could do one single thing about any part of it, and that was correct.

The magistrate could not re-cut a plate; that is not a magistrate's work and there is no form in this city that makes it one. The bench of four could not go to nine hundred doors; a bench of four does not do doors. The plate-cutter would cut any name he was handed for four shillings the hundred and could not pay for a name nobody had. The woman who kept the paper could go to thirty doors a fortnight and had not been to a door in eleven weeks and was not going to while there was a fever in the ward.

The block in the gatepost would come right in about an hour of work, and getting it to come right needed a meeting of the ward, and a meeting of the ward needed a notice, and a notice needed somebody to put it up, and there was no form in this city that said who that was, and there had not been a meeting of Tallowgate since the autumn before the water came.

And the one person in the room who could have taken any of it on was a man of thirty-one with a bad leg and no name on any book in the city, who had been in the country four days and who had one instrument and it was the one he had just used, and the instrument made a document and did not make a task, and he had known that before he came in and had done it anyway, because the other thing he has, which is the habit of a man who has spent his life finding the sentence that is load-bearing, had told him that a number said out loud in a room is the only kind of number that anybody has ever been obliged to look at.

Which is true. It is also the only kind of number that does not, by itself, get anybody a bed.

Nobody was relieved. The blank nine inches on the notice board under the order stayed blank that day, and the four bells went, and the roads stayed shut, and about a hundred and forty people who had died since the cold came off the water stayed dead, and a clerk of forty carried a fair hand up a stair and put it in a book, and the book is a book that can be produced, in a place, on a day, to a person, and there is no way on earth to have it be a bed.

# Chapter 359: A Number Of Beds That Only Goes One Way

It was his hundred and ninth morning, and there are six shelter halls in this city, three in the low wards and three in the four that are not low, and the arithmetic of the six of them is the arithmetic of a rule with no way to end, and it took him a morning in one hall and one afternoon in another to see it and the rest of his life to know what he was going to do about it.

It is a piece of paper. That is the whole of it, and it is the fifth and last thing in this batch and it is the cheapest.

---

The order's remedy is a board in a hall.

There is a book at the door of each hall and a man or woman on a stool beside it and a board on the wall with the number of beds free chalked on it and rubbed out and chalked again every morning, and the number is put up because people need to know, and four times in a fortnight the man or woman on the stool rubs out a figure that is wrong and chalks a figure that is less wrong, and every other time in a fortnight nobody comes.

The book at the door has a line for every person who comes in, and the line has the day and the hall and the board, and it is very good work and it is the same woman at five of the six halls and she has been doing it for eight weeks and she is fifty-eight.

“*How many came in last week,*” he said.

“*Nine,*” she said. “*And in the week before, fourteen, and the week before that, thirty-one. That is the shape of it and everybody in this hall can tell you the shape of it. Nobody comes any more. They read the board.*”

“*What is on the board.*”

“*No beds free,*” said the woman on the stool. “*And there are eleven beds in this hall and there have been eleven empty in it for five days, and I have not put a figure on that board that says eleven, because if I put eleven on it, eleven people come, and on the day eleven people come there is no room for the four that would have come the next day, and I have been asked by a bench of four to have a number on a board and I have put the number I have, which is the number I can stand behind, and that number is the number of beds that are free, which is none.*”

---

He asked her a question, and he asked it in the way this city requires, which is that you ask it and then you stop talking.

“*What happens to a person who is better.*”

The woman on the stool did not have to think about it.

“*Nothing,*” she said. “*That is the whole of it and it is the fifth thing in the order and there is nobody whose job it is.*”

She put her hand on the book.

“*There is a line when they come in. There is no line when they go. I have been asked by the ward clerk and I have said: there is no discharge, and he wrote that down, and he said: then they stay, and I said: they do not have to stay, there is a bed free, and he said: not on the book. And I have been arguing that with him for eight weeks and we are both right, and I am fifty-eight and I have been on this stool since the second week and I go home at the last of the light, and the argument is the whole of my work.*”

“*A discharge paper would fix it.*”

“*A discharge paper would fix it in nine minutes,*” she said, “*and there is no office in this city that issues a discharge paper, and there is no form that asks for one, and the reason there is no form is that the order has five parts and the sixth is the half that is empty, and I have read the order. Everybody who works in a hall has read the order, and nine of us have stood in a room and asked a magistrate's clerk whether there was going to be a discharge, and he said there was going to be one when the order ends, and we all said: and what happens before it ends, and he had to go and find out, and he came back and said the same thing, which is that nothing happens before it ends.*”

She said the next part to the board on the wall, and not to him.

“*And so the number goes up, and it only goes up, and I have twenty-one people on the books of this hall and eleven beds, and the ten on the books that are not in beds are at the Ninth Weir in a yard and a shed, and they are better, and they are on the books, and every morning I put up a number that says nothing is free, and the number is true, and the number is the part of it that is wrong, and I have known that for five days and I have no way to be less honest than I am.*”

---

The mark is chalk, and it is on a board at the end of the beds and it is not a shame and it is not a punishment.

A person who has been in a hall gets a line on the hall's own board, and the line stays, because there is no discharge, and the hall's board is read by the ward clerk every second morning, and a person whose name is on a hall board is a person who has been in a hall, and that is a fact about a person and it is a true one and it is going to be a true one for as long as the order stands.

Bess Carrow is sixty-one and has been in the Weir Lane hall since the eighth morning and is better every day, and has been better for six days, and cannot be discharged, and does not want to be anywhere, because she has a room and the room is where she wants to be.

“*I am not asking to be let out,*” she said. “*That is the thing and everybody gets it wrong. I am sixty-one and I have had the fever and I am well and I want to go home, and going home is not a thing anybody can do for me, because going home is a line in a book with a day on it and there is nobody to write it.*”

“*Then what is it,*” he said. “*Tell me what it is, and I will stop asking the wrong question.*”

“*It is that I am in a hall I do not need,*” said Bess Carrow, “*and that a person in a hall I do not need is a person the ward counts, and when the fever stops the ward will have a number, and the number will be made out of the people who went into halls and never came out, and it will be wrong in the way that makes people want the number smaller, and it will be small, and I will be one of the small reasons why, and I will be sixty-two when it happens and I will be one of the reasons and nobody will ever ask me about it.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*That is the finding,*” she said. “*You are not the first one to ask me a question today. A man from the ward came at the second hour and a woman came at the fourth and you have come at the seventh, and all three of you asked me something, and I have given you the same answer, and you are welcome to it, and I am not going to be thanked for it, and I would like the next one to bring me a sheet of paper instead of a voice.*”

---

The column is at the end of the board, past the chalked number, and it is kept by a man of thirty-one who is not a clerk and is paid nothing for it and has been keeping it for eleven weeks.

It has about nineteen lines in it and a heading that says nothing at all, and the nineteen are the nineteen dead in Tallowgate, and the four wards together are about a hundred and forty, and nobody has added the four columns up because there are four columns in four wards and four clerks and no office for the four of them to hand anything to.

The man who keeps it is thirty-one and he has a bad ear and a coat and nine days' work of chalk dust on his sleeve, and he is a person of this ward, and he was in Tallowgate before the stranger was on the road to it.

He said, in answer to a question, and it took him a while to get to it and he was not slow, he was careful:

“*I have been asked three times this week by strangers and by two people of this ward whether I know why it is the low wards, and I do, and everybody who is alive in this ward knows, and there is not one thing in that column that says it. It says a day and it says a name and it says a ward, and there is nothing over the top of it and nothing at the foot of it, and if you were a clerk in four hundred years you would look at that column and you would not be able to say one thing about why the fever is where it is. And that is not a lie. It is the truest thing I have got, and it is the most useless thing anybody has ever said in this ward.*”

“*What is on the board beside it.*”

“*The number of beds free,*” he said. “*Which is a number that only goes one way, and mine goes one way too, and mine has nineteen in it and yours has twenty-one names and eleven beds, and I keep both, and I am thirty-one, and I have a bad ear, and my name is on a slate in a room upstairs that I asked to be put on, and I would like it taken down and there is no form.*”

---

And at about the ninth hour, at the end of Tallowgate, a woman of about twenty-eight was standing at the corner of the low ward with a bundle of string and a slate and nine doors' worth of chalk in her pocket, and she was not sent and nobody had asked her and she was not on any list, and he had met her once, four days before, at a standpipe, where she had told him that a book which does not know what it is for is still a book.

He asked her the only question that mattered, which was: *have you told anybody.*

“*My mother,*” she said. “*And not the ward, and here is why, and I have said it out loud to you and I am not saying it to you because you asked me, I am saying it because I have been waiting four days for somebody to ask me and you are the first one that was not going to try to stop me.*”

“*Tell me anyway.*”

“*If the ward knew that a woman of twenty-eight was going to doors on her own account, then the first thing that would happen is a rota,*” she said. “*And a rota is a list, and a list is the thing that comes before a bill, and I have been in this city nineteen years and I have watched what a list turns into four times. So I am doing it on my own, and I am doing it slow, and I am doing it in an order I have not written down, and if anybody asks me who is next I am going to say that there is no order and that there is a woman with a piece of chalk and nine days.*”

“*Who is paying for the chalk.*”

“*Me,*” she said. “*It is a penny. It is not a bill and it is not a list and it is not a rota, and it is mine, and you can walk with me for an hour if you like and you will not be able to help and I will not be able to use you, and if you say one thing about it to one person in a room then I will stop.*”

He said: “*I will not say one word about it to anybody.*”

“*Then come and hold a door open,*” she said, “*and do not ask me anything at all, and if anybody asks you who you are you are a man who is helping me hold a door, and that is true, and it is the whole of the name you have got in this city.*”

Nobody thanked her. She was not thanked. He carried nine doors that morning and helped with one and the ten kept the number it was, and the number of beds free in six halls in this city went up by four and did not come down by one, and the four bells went, and nobody was relieved.

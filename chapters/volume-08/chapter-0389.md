# Chapter 389: Saying No Is Doing The Doors Again, And A Man With Nothing To Do At The End Of A Row

The yard next to the plate-cutter's is where a boy of sixteen works and there is one wall between that yard and the shed with the wheel in it, and a man of fifty-nine in the shed talks, and on the sixth day of the seventy-seventh week, which was his hundred and thirty-ninth morning and his thirty-eighth in a city he came to on foot, the talk from the shed came out of the second yard into a doorway and stayed there.

The fever was fifteen weeks and a day old. The man with the leg was at the end of the row at the second hour and the woman of twenty-eight was forty yards away at a door with a slate against her hip and no chalk, and the boy of sixteen was in the doorway of the second yard with a barrow he had not been asked about, and he was saying it out loud for the fourth time that morning to a woman with a child on her hip who lives four doors up this row and had not asked him for it either.

---

He waited until the boy had said it twice and the woman had gone, and then he said: “*You have told her four times.*”

The boy of sixteen looked at him.

“*It is a thing that happened in a yard and I am saying it in a yard,*” he said. “*I work in this yard. That is the whole of why I am saying it and I am not going to be told off for it by a man with a leg, and I would have said it anyway if you had not come, and I have said it in the next yard as well and I am going to say it at the top of the row and I do not know that anybody has said to me not to.*”

“*Nobody has said it to you.*”

“*Then that is the end of that,*” said the boy of sixteen, and went to move a barrow.

---

He went down the row and did not say a price out loud, which he noticed, and stood at the end of it for a while, and then he asked the only question he had, and he asked it in the way this city requires, and she answered it before he had got to the end of it.

“*No,*” said the woman of twenty-eight.

He waited for the rest, and she gave it to him, because she had decided to give it to him and not because she was asked twice.

“*And I am going to tell you now that I have had that answer ready since yesterday morning, because I heard it in a doorway at the second yard and I went up my own row and asked four people whether it was true and three of them had heard it and one of them had a paper, and then I went and got the paper off the woman with the book, and I did all of that on my own account yesterday and I did not go and tell you any of it.*”

“*You did not have to.*”

“*No,*” said the woman of twenty-eight. “*That is the part I have been standing here thinking about for an hour and a half, and it is not that I did not have to. It is that I would have found out anyway, at a standpipe, this morning, from somebody who had heard it in a yard, and you would have been standing on this corner at the end of this row when I said it.*”

---

“*Say the rest of it.*”

“*I am going to say it at this door and not anywhere else, and I am going to say it once, and then you can take it up that hill or you can leave it, and there is nothing else in this ward either way.*”

She turned the slate round so that he could see it. It had a stub of chalk beside it and marks on it and no name at the top of it and no name at the foot of it, because she has never once put one there, and that is not modesty, and nobody in this ward has ever been asked about that by anybody, including you.

“*A paper says the keeper of the roll has to go to nine hundred doors. It does not say I have to. It does not say a man with a wheel has to, and it does not say the man who wrote it has to, and I have been going twenty-eight days for nothing and I am not going to stop.*”

“*I know.*”

“*And here is the whole of it and I have not been asked for it and I am saying it to a man at a door because he asked me a question,*” said the woman of twenty-eight, “*and if somebody comes to me now, in a week, in a month, and asks me whether the doors are being gone to, then I am the one they ask. Not her, because it is her name at the foot of the fourth clause and asking her is easy. Me, because I am the only one who has actually been. And I will have to say yes I have been, and I will have to say how many, and I will have to say it to somebody, in front of people, at a standpipe in the morning, and I will not be able to say no, because a thing I have done for nothing for twenty-eight days is a thing I am the only person who can be held to.*”

---

“*Say the part you have not said.*”

“*I am not going to be able to refuse that,*” she said, “*and I have worked out why this morning at the change, standing in a yard and it is the only thing I have got that is worth saying to you. Saying no is doing the doors again. If somebody asks me whether the doors are being gone to and I say no, then I have gone and told a room that the doors are not being gone to, and there is a paper that says somebody has to go to them, and I will have made a false thing out of the only piece of work I have ever done that nobody asked me for. And if I say yes, then I have agreed to something I did not agree to, and I will be the second woman in this ward with a duty written over her by a man who filed it on purpose.*”

She put the slate back against her hip.

“*So I am going to carry it. And I am not going to be thanked for carrying it, because it is not a favour anybody has given me, and I am not going to be relieved of it, and I want both of those said out loud at this door this morning so that they are in the street, and I am not going to be asked again and if you ask me again I will give you the same one word and it will be the same word as this morning.*”

“*Nobody in this ward is going to be grateful to you for carrying it and nobody is going to tell you it does not matter, and I have said that out loud at this door and you have heard it, and that is the whole of what I have got.*”

“*Right,*” said the woman of twenty-eight.

---

Then he said the thing he had come down the row to say, and he said it flat, and it was the sixth time he had said a price out loud in this city and the first one that had come out in a street.

“*There is a penny of chalk. Nine days of it, at three marks a day, and it is a penny. I have known what a round costs for seven days and I have had that figure in my head since a room at the back of a shop and I have not come down this row with it and I am not going to now.*”

“*Do not.*”

“*I am not going to. I have got nothing at all in my hands and there is nothing in this ward that I am able to give you that would not be a favour with a line in it, and a favour with a line in it is the reason there is a woman at a standpipe at the first hour who cannot say no to the second one, and I would rather you ran out of chalk in a street than have a stranger put a line in a list that is not written down.*”

“*Then I will run out of chalk in a street,*” said the woman of twenty-eight, “*on my own, and I will tell nobody, and there will be a day when I do not go, and no one in this ward will be able to say whether the doors are being gone to on that day either, and that will also be your fault, and I am telling you that now so that you have got it and I have not got to say it twice.*”

“*It is my fault.*”

“*It is,*” she said, “*and you have not been able to do one single thing about it and neither have I, and there are four people in this city I could point at and every one of them is somewhere else doing something else, and there is no form in four hundred miles that would let me hand this to one of them, and I have had a morning in which I have looked for one.*”

---

He stood at the end of a row in a low ward for a while with nothing at all in his hands, which is the first time in thirty-eight days that there has been nothing at the end of a row for him to do, and it is the correct decision, and it is not a good one, and he had already worked out about a month ago that those two things come together in a city and do not come apart.

The four bells went over nine hundred doors in a ward a mile and a half long, and the woman of twenty-eight went down the row with a slate against her hip and no chalk in her apron, and at the end of the ninth row a man of sixty was sitting on his bench in the afternoon with a shallow tin that is not medicine, and he had heard it in a doorway and had not come down the row, and nobody had sent for him and there is no form in this city that says a man is to be sent for. A boy of eight was in a bed at the top of this city, and was better, and could not be discharged, and had not been named out loud in any room in this city in his life, and his mother would be at a standpipe in the morning.

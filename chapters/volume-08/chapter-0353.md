# Chapter 353: Nine Hundred Doors, And A Man In A Room Nobody Has Been In

It was his hundred and third morning and the fifth day of the seventy-second week, and the fever in this city is in the two low wards and the low wards are Tallowgate and Weir Lane, and he walked into Tallowgate at about the second hour with a leg and no business that anybody in it could use.

He did not send for anybody. There is no form in this city that says a man may send for a person, and he did not write to anybody, and nobody knew he was coming, and every person in this chapter was in the place they were in before he was on the road to it.

---

Tallowgate is nine hundred doors in about a mile and a half and it is on the low side, and the low side is low because the river is a mile off and the ground was made out of what a city throws away, and there is a standpipe at the end of it with a queue at the standpipe every morning of these eleven weeks, and the queue is not a queue for water, because the water is coming, it is a queue because the standpipe is the only place in four hundred yards where nine people can stand and be in the same street.

He asked at the standpipe. He asked carefully, in the way four hundred miles of country taught him to ask, which is a question about the order of things and not a question about a person.

He said: *which of you is in the ward book.*

Nine women looked at him, and one of them, who is thirty-four or so and has a wrap over her head for the cold rather than for the fever, said: “*All of us, and I will tell you how I know, and it is not because anybody told me. The book comes round at the doors about once a year, and the man who is asking is the head of the house, and the head of the house says who is in it, and I have been in this street nineteen years and I have never had to say a word.*”

“*That sounds like it works.*”

“*It works beautifully,*” she said. “*It works so well that there is not one person on this standpipe that is missing out of it, and I will tell you the thing you are actually asking about, which is that there is a great many people in this ward that do not sleep in a house that anybody calls theirs, and they are not on the book, and we have all known that for years, and it is not a complaint and it is not a scandal, and it is the way the ward is.*”

“*Then who is not on the book.*”

She thought about it, and she was not slow about it, and she gave him a street and a house and no name at all, which is the ordinary courtesy of a person in a street.

“*Weaver's Row. The one at the top. There is a man in the top room who has not been down those stairs in six years, and the head of that house is his sister's husband, and that man has never once put him in the book, in six years, and nobody here has ever said a word to him about it, because what would we say, and to who.*”

---

Weaver's Row is four houses and a passage. The stair is nine steps and turns, and it is the only stair in that row that goes anywhere.

He went up it.

The room is one room, and there is a bed, a chair, a washstand, a window, and a board across the window that is a board and not a shutter and has been on the outside since before the man in the room was in the room. The light in there is the light that a room gets with a board on it, which is a different light and is a worse one, and a man who has been in a room for six years knows the difference in his eyes and does not have words for it.

“*Ivo Serrel,*” said the man in the room, before there was any question in it, because a man on a stair with a bad leg who has come up nine steps is a thing that happens four times in six years and the fourth time is now. “*And you are the fifth person in six years, and four of them were the parish, and you are the first one who did not have a form in his hand.*”

He is forty-four or about it. He has one leg that works and one that does not, and a stick that is not for walking, it is for getting up off a chair, and there is a hook by the door at the height of a man's hand with the stick in it.

“*What is it,*” he said. “*And I will tell you now that if it is the book you have come up nine steps for nothing, and I am not saying that to be difficult. I have asked twice about the book. Both times a woman came, and both times she was perfectly decent, and both times she said the same thing, and it was: come back when you have the paper.*”

“*What paper.*”

“*That is the whole of it and that is why I have not asked a third time,*” said Ivo Serrel. “*The book is made at a door by the head of a house, and the head of this house is my sister's husband, and he has put himself and his wife and the two children in it twice a year for six years, and he is not a cruel man and he does not think about me twice between one time and the next, and there is no paper anywhere in this city that would put a man in a book from a room he cannot leave, and so a woman came twice and told me to come back with a paper, and I have not come back, and that is the end of that and it has been the end of that for five years.*”

He said all of it in forty seconds and without any weight on it, the way a person says the thing they have said to themselves a great many times.

---

Then he said the other thing, and he said it because the stranger had gone to the window and was standing at the board.

“*That is the light,*” he said. “*The board has been on since before I came into this room. It was on when I could still get down the stairs and go along the row to my sister's, and I did not ask for it then because it was a board and everybody's grandmother has boards on, and then I could not get down the stairs, and by then asking for it was not a thing a man in this room could do, because you cannot get up nine steps and go to a house and say take your board off my window, not with a leg like this, and I have asked for nothing for six years because the one time I asked for anything anybody came with a paper in their hand and I am the last person in this city who wants to be asked about a paper.*”

The stranger stood with his hand on the board.

“*Take it off,*” he said. “*I am not a magistrate and I cannot order anything and nobody in this city has ever given me a single thing. But you can say to me that you want the light, and I will carry it down nine steps, and it is a board and there are nails in it and it is not the order and it is not the book and nobody is going to make you a person in anything tonight.*”

Ivo Serrel looked at him for a while.

“*Then carry it down,*” he said, “*and I will say the rest of it because you will want it and you have come up nine steps for it, and I am not unkind and there is no reason for you to go all the way to the gate.*”

---

“*The book is made by the person who answers the door,*” he said. “*That is the rule of it. I have watched it twice. You come with a slate and you ask the head of the house, and the head of the house says, and you write what he says. And that is fair, and it is right, and it is the only way anybody could ever do it, and I have thought about it for six years from this chair, and I have got to the bottom of it, and the bottom of it is this: the book does not say who lives in a house. The book says who answers the door.*”

He put his hand out flat, the way a man does when he is laying something on a table.

“*I have not answered a door in six years, and there is nothing wrong with me, and I would answer it if I could get down the stairs, and if the fever comes up this stair I could not answer it either, and there is a hall two streets away with empty boards in it and a woman in it called Bess Carrow who has been in there nine days and gets better every day, and I have not been able to go and see her and nobody has come up here to tell me about her either, because nobody sends anybody to a room and there is no form in this city that says they should.*”

The boards came off the window at about the fourth hour. It took a quarter of an hour and there were four nails in it and the top one had rusted through and the other three did not, and he took them out himself, from the chair, with the stick, over eleven minutes, and he did not ask the stranger to do any of it except hold the board.

The room was a different room at about the fifth hour. That is the whole of what came of it.

---

He went back down the nine steps and out into Weaver's Row, and the light in the street was the light it had been, and the row was the row it had been, and the ward did not know that anything had happened in it.

At the standpipe the woman of thirty-four was still there with her wrap on, and she had not gone, and the queue had gone and come twice.

He said to her: “*You said the book is correct.*”

“*The book is correct,*” she said. “*Every name in it is a person who sleeps in a house and answers a door. It is the best book of its kind in this city and the woman who keeps it does it thirty houses a fortnight and she has never once got a name wrong in eleven years, and I want that said by somebody, because you have come here with a face on you like a man who has found something, and you have not found anything, and it is the street that is short and not the book.*”

That is the sentence he carried down a mile and a half back toward the black building, and he carried it in his own head, and there is no hand in this country he could have put it in.

It is in a roll of nine hundred and one thousand names in a ward, and the roll is correct, and the roll is not the city.

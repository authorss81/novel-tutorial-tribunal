# Chapter 383: The Woman At The End Of The Row, And A Subject She Has Closed

The subject of the fourth clause had been going to doors in this ward for twenty-two days and had told a man with a bad leg in a doorway that she was not going to talk about doors again, and on the last day of the seventy-sixth week, which was his hundred and thirty-third morning and his thirty-second in a city he came to on foot, he went down the row and asked her about a piece of paper.

The fever was fourteen weeks and two days old. There was a stub of chalk in the pocket of a woman of twenty-eight that a woman of sixty had bought for her at a standpipe at the first hour on two separate mornings, and neither of those two women had asked the other for anything, and there is no line in a list anywhere with either of them on it.

He stood at the end of the row and let her do two doors before he said anything, which is what a person of this ward does.

“*You have not asked me about the doors in eight days,*” she said, without turning round.

“*I have not asked you about the doors in eight days.*”

“*And there is a piece of paper in your coat and it is not folded, and it was folded in four in there the day before yesterday, and you have had it in your hand since a man at a wheel read it out, and I have watched you do it at the end of this row four times since, and I am not going to say one word about it and I want that in the street as well.*”

“*I have it in my hand.*”

“*Then ask me your question and do not stand there holding it at me like a man at a door, because there are two people in the house at the end of this row and I am not late for them twice for a stranger with a bad leg.*”

---

He asked it.

“*If a piece of paper said that somebody had to go to the doors of this ward, and the somebody was somebody else, and you were the only other person anybody in this city had ever seen going to them — would you go.*”

“*No,*” she said, and she said it before the end of the sentence, and then she stopped and knocked on the door behind her, and waited, and did not turn round.

“*There. That is the answer and it took me a second and a half and I have not said no to a question about that work since a yard at the top of the hill, and I am saying it to a man in a street and I have closed that subject and I closed it on purpose and I am not going to open it for a piece of paper.*”

---

He stood in the cold at the end of a row for a while and let it be no, and she came back from the door.

“*Say what you came to say, because you have not said the paper out loud and you have had thirty-two days to say a paper out loud, and there is a bill with candles printed on the back of it going round this row with your handwriting on it, and I have heard the five clauses in a yard at the low end of this row and I have heard them at a standpipe, and I know there is a fifth clause, and I know the fifth clause has a woman in it, and I know the woman is the one who keeps the book, and nobody in this ward has asked me where I got any of that and I am not going to.*”

“*I am not going to ask you.*”

“*Good,*” said the woman of twenty-eight. “*Then here is the thing about the fifth clause and I have been working it out since the day before yesterday and I have got it and it is worse than what I had, and I am going to say it once and then I am going to knock on this door.*”

---

She put the stub of chalk in her apron and turned round, and her face was not angry and it was not tired either.

“*A paper that says the keeper of the roll has to go to nine hundred doors does not put me in it. I know that. I have known it since the day before yesterday and I have gone out two mornings since and it has not once occurred to me to go because a paper told her to.*”

“*I know.*”

“*And the paper does not make anybody else go either,*” she said. “*And that is not the part I have been working out. The part I have been working out is this: for twenty-two days the only person in this city who has gone to a door on purpose and been asked about it afterwards is me, and you asked me at the end of this row on the seventh hour of a morning and I gave you arithmetic and you have not done one thing with it, and a paper coming up that hill with a name at the foot of the fourth clause does not change who I am. I am still the only one.*”

She looked at him.

“*So the first person anybody in this city asks whether that work is being done is me. Not her, and not a man with a wheel, and not the woman on a stool. Me, at a standpipe, in the morning, in front of anybody who happens to be drawing water. And I did not agree to that and I was not asked and I have no way of saying no to being asked, because a thing I have done for nothing for twenty-two days is a thing I am the only person who can be held to.*”

---

“*Now say the other half of that to me,*” he said, “*because the third clause does not only put her in it, and I have been standing in this row for ten minutes saying the part that is about her.*”

She stopped with her hand on the door and came back three steps, and that was the first time in twenty-two days she had moved towards a man in this row instead of away from one.

“*Say the third one again.*”

“*A name may be put into the record of a ward by any person of that ward who can say it, and a name said by a person of the ward goes in without a fee.*”

“*Right,*” said the woman of twenty-eight. “*Then I have been putting names in a ward's record for twenty-two days and neither of us knew it, and I have not been paid, and there is no fee, and there is not going to be one, and that is the clause and I am not going to pretend that I do not know what it is.*”

“*You have been putting names in.*”

“*I have been making marks on doors and reading names at doors, and there are marks on this slate, and every one of them is a person, and a man of fifty-nine at a wheel has not cut one line for a single one of them and never will.*”

She turned the slate over and looked at the back of it, which was clean.

“*I have been carrying a count. It is not a count. It is a claim on a ward's record, made by me, in a street, with a paper of chalk that a woman of sixty gave me at a standpipe, and there is no form in this city that says a claim may be thrown out, and the only person who could throw one out is the woman with the book, and she cannot take a name out of a roll and has never once said she could.*”

“*Nobody has asked you whether you wanted the marks on that slate to be marks.*”

“*No,*” said the woman of twenty-eight. “*And I would not have said no, and that is the part I have to say out loud at this door, and then I am going to knock. Nobody has asked me one thing since the morning you found me at the end of this row with a bundle of string, and that is a thing I have not been able to put down for three weeks and I am putting it down at a door and not in a room with a clerk in it, and you can do what you like with both of those.*”

---

“*And the second one,*” she said, “*is that the work stops being mine the moment that paper stands, and that it is hers, officially, on paper, in a fair hand, in a room with thirty people in it. And after that there are two kinds of person in this ward and the kinds do not have names. There is a woman who has to go to nine hundred doors, and there is a woman who has been going to the doors for nothing and is not going to stop. And nobody in this city can ask which of us we are, and nobody in this city can ask me why I am still going, and there is no form that asks it, and I have been living on being the one who goes for twenty-two days and I am not going to give it up because a stranger read five clauses out in a shed.*”

“*You do not have to give anything up.*”

“*I know I do not,*” said the woman of twenty-eight. “*That is the part I have understood and I have not said it out loud to anybody and I am saying it to you now because you asked me a question, and then I am going to knock on this door, and after today you do not get to ask me about that again, and if you do I will answer you the same way every time and it will be the same one word.*”

---

He did not ask her again.

He stood at the end of the row and listened to her knock, and the door was opened, and two people who are not in any book in this city are inside it, and she went in, and came out again, and went to the next one.

Then she stopped at the end of the row and said the last of it over her shoulder, and she said it flat, and it was not a finding, and he had asked her three questions.

“*You put a woman of forty-four in a piece of paper and I have not been asked about it and I am not going to be angry with you about it, because you told a room what it would cost and then did it anyway, and that is a thing I can understand and I have watched four other people not understand it. What I cannot do is be one of the people who makes it easy for you. So go and say it out loud in the room you are going to say it in, and do not come down this row and tell me afterwards how it went, because I have said the whole of what I have to say about a document in a street and I said it to a stranger at a door and it stops at this door.*”

She went down the row with the stub of chalk in her apron and did not look back, and the four bells went the way they go over a ward a mile and a half long at the low end of it, and he stood at the end of the row with a piece of paper in his hand that had a woman in it who was not going to say yes, and a woman in a street who had not been asked and had not said no, and there is no third thing in this ward and there is no form that makes one.

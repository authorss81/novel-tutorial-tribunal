# Chapter 362: The First Door, And Who Is Not On It

It was his hundred and twelfth morning and the last day of the seventy-third week, and the fourteenth was seven days off, and he stood in a passage behind two houses in Tallowgate at about the second hour holding a door open because he had been asked to and because there was nothing else to do with himself.

The woman of twenty-eight had a slate under her arm and she had been going one day out of the nine days of chalk she had bought with a penny, and she had six days of it, and a mark on nine doors from the day before, and no order written down anywhere, and she said out loud before they started that he was not to ask her anything at all.

He held a door open for about eleven minutes and asked her nothing at all.

---

The slate is a slate. It has a string through a hole in the corner of it and a piece of chalk in a loop and about nine marks on it and no writing, because she cannot hold a pen and a slate and write.

He looked at it and she said: “*Do not ask me what the marks are.*”

“*I was not going to ask you what the marks are.*”

“*You were looking at them,*” she said, “*and looking is asking, and I have been in this city nineteen years and I can tell the difference between a man who looks and a man who is careful and you are going to be careful, and that is fine, and hold the door.*”

He held the door.

The first door of the morning was the one at the top of a row of four, and the woman of thirty-four came out of it herself before he had the hand off the latch, which is what a person does when somebody holds a door open in a cold street at the second hour.

“*You have not been here four days,*” she said, when she saw him. “*You have been here nine.*”

“*Nine. The counting is not exact and the arithmetic is.*”

“*It is exact enough for a person who has been in a ward nine days,*” she said. “*I have watched you come past this door four times and go past the standpipe twice and go into the hall once, and you have not been in a house in this street in your life, and it is not a criticism, because there is nothing in this street you need to be in.*”

Then she said the thing she had come out to say, which was that she was the head of her own house and had been for nineteen years, and that the head of the house is whoever answers the door, and that the two of them had been the same person in this street for as long as the roll had been coming round.

---

The line in the roll for that house has four names on it and has had four names on it for six years.

Herself. Her mother, who is sixty-eight and is up and about and is at the back of the house where the sun is. Two children, eleven and nine, both of them on the roll with their own names and not as *son* and *daughter*, and she made a point of that when he asked, unasked, which he had been told not to do.

“*They are on it as themselves,*” she said, “*because a plate is a plate and a name is a name and I am not having my son on a plate in this city as the son of anybody.*”

There are six people in that house.

He counted them himself, from the doorway, and she watched him do it and did not stop him, and the two children came past him into the passage to look at a dog.

“*Six,*” she said.

“*Six.*”

“*Four of them are mine and two of them are not,*” she said, “*and I am going to tell you about the two, and then you are going to go away, because you have been looking at the door of this house since the second hour and there is nothing behind the door but a floor.*”

---

The lodger is in the back room and has been there two years and pays rent in money every week and is not a relation of anybody in the house.

The other one is her brother's wife, and she has slept on a pallet in the front room for a fortnight because her husband's mother is the woman being nursed in the back room and the woman being nursed is her husband's grandmother, and so the brother's wife is in a house that is not hers, in a room that is not hers, at the end of a life that is not hers, and she washes the two children and she does the front room and she is not in the roll.

“*Put her down,*” he said.

“*No,*” said the woman of thirty-four.

“*Why not.*”

“*Because I did not ask her,*” she said. “*That is the whole reason and I have never had to say it out loud to anybody before and I have had eleven years to find a better way of saying it.*”

She put her hand on the doorframe, which is a thing people in cold streets do while they are deciding whether to say the next part.

“*A name goes into that book because somebody said it to the woman who keeps it. Not because she thought of it. Not because she counted heads. Because the person who lives with you stood at that door and said a name out loud, and she wrote it down, and that is the only door the name has ever come through, and it is a door, and a door is a person. I am the door. I am not allowed to be a second thing and put a name in that book that nobody said to me, because if I did it once I would have to keep doing it, and in four years I would be a woman who writes down what she has decided, and then the book would be a list of what I thought, and there is nobody in this city who can read a list of what I thought and check it, including me.*”

“*And the lodger.*”

“*The lodger pays me money and I would put him down in a heartbeat,*” she said, “*and I have not, and here is the reason and it is the same reason and I am not going to be clever about it. If I put the lodger down then the lodger is a person of this house. And when the fever book comes round in the spring, or the wage book, or any other book that has ever come to a door in this city, the man at that door is going to be a person of this house and there will be four of us on the paper where there are two, and the rent of this house is half of what I make, and I am not going to lose it to a piece of paper to save a stranger a bad afternoon.*”

Nobody said anything for a moment. Behind the door a child laughed at a dog.

“*So the book is wrong,*” he said.

“*The book is exactly right,*” said the woman of thirty-four. “*That is the thing I have been trying to say to you since you came down this row. The book is not a list of people. The book is a list of who is in the house. And the reason it is a list of who is in the house is that it has to be, because the woman who keeps it cannot make up a name and cannot take one out, and the only way a name gets in is somebody saying it at a door. And every single thing that is wrong with the protection in this ward is wrong because of that, and not one of it is anybody's fault, and not one of it can be fixed by a plate.*”

---

Then she asked him a second question, and it was not about the fever either, and it is the only thing anybody in Tallowgate has said to him in nine days that he did not have an answer ready for.

“*Are you a person in this city.*”

“*No.*”

“*No,*” she said, and looked at him, and he understood that she had known, and that she had known since the second hour, and that she had held a door for a man with a bad leg who is on nothing.

“*I have nine days of you walking past my door,*” she said, “*and I have never once seen anybody ask you for a paper, and I have assumed you were somebody's man. You are not anybody's. I have never had that in a street in nineteen years and I do not know what to do with it, and I am not going to do anything with it, and I am telling you that I noticed and that is all.*”

“*That is all it is.*”

“*It is not all it is,*” she said, “*and I have not got a year to find out what it is, and the queue at the standpipe is going to be worse by the change, and if you want to hold a door for a woman who is doing this on her own account then hold it and do not ask me the questions, because every question you ask me is a question I have to answer twice, once out loud and once in my own head for the rest of the day.*”

And she went back inside and shut the door, and he stood in the passage with the latch cold under his hand.

---

Here is what he had learned by the change, and he learned it in a passage, and it did not go in a book, because it was not said in a room.

The roll does not say who lives in a house. He had heard that in a room seven days ago, from a chair, in a voice that had been in that room for six years. The other half of it he got at the top of a row of four from a woman who has answered a door for nineteen years, and the half is this.

A roll is only as good as the door it was written at. Not the door of the ward, and not the door of the black building at the middle of the nine bridges. A door of a house, with a person behind it, who is a person because they answer it, and who is therefore the only person in the world who can be asked what is in their house, and who is also the only person in the world who can be asked to be wrong.

Nine hundred doors. Nine hundred people who can each put a name in or leave a name out, once a year, in a cold street, for no money, with nobody watching and nothing written down anywhere except in a book at the back of a chandler's shop.

There is no second witness to any of it and there never has been. That is not a defect in a record. That is what a record made at doors is.

And a man with a bad leg and no name on anything stood in a passage and worked out that the only instrument he has ever had — a question, asked out loud, in front of other people, about a thing that is not his — is the same instrument nine hundred people use once a year for free, and that it is his and not theirs, and that it is the reason nobody in this city is going to help him, and that if he uses it here he will spend the only thing a woman of thirty-four has, which is the right to be asked nothing.

He held a door for four hours. He asked nothing at all. It was the hardest morning he had had since a kitchen four hundred miles off and he told nobody that and there was nobody to tell.

The four bells went. The number of doors in Tallowgate is nine hundred and he had been inside one of them and had been told, by a person who answers doors, exactly what a door is for.

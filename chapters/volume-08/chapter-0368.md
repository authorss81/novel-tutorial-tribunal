# Chapter 368: The Day Before The Fourteenth, And A Man In A Room

It was his hundred and eighteenth morning and the sixth day of the seventy-fourth week, and the fourteenth was tomorrow, and there were two things in the low end of Tallowgate that wanted him in the same four hours, and he could not be in both of them, and the reason was a leg, and the leg is the leg and it is not going to clear and nobody is going to count it.

He did the hall first because the hall is where the beds are and the yard is where the plates are and the yard can wait four hours and a hall cannot.

---

The man with the bad ear was at the end of the beds with the column.

It is a board and a piece of chalk and it has nineteen lines in it and nothing over the top of it and nothing at the foot of it and a heading that says nothing at all, and the nineteen are the nineteen dead in Tallowgate, and the four wards together are about a hundred and forty, and nobody has added the four columns up because there are four columns in four wards and four clerks and no office for the four of them to hand anything to.

He asked the question he had been carrying since a hall at Weir Lane.

“*How do you know that a name in your column is the same name as a name in the roll.*”

“*I do not know,*” said the man with the bad ear. “*I have known that since the second week and I have not got anybody to give it to and you are the first person who has asked me it in a form I could answer.*”

---

He got a piece of chalk and he put his thumb on the ninth line of the column and he said the name.

It is not his to say and he said it out loud anyway, in a hall, at about the fourth hour, to a stranger, and the two people on the beds nearest heard it and one of them is a woman who has been there since the eighth morning and the other one is asleep.

“*That is on the roll of this ward,*” said the man with the bad ear, “*and it has been on it for six years, and it is the ninth name in my column, and the ninth name in my column is the fourth week of the fever, and I cut it myself with this chalk and I put the day against it because that is the work.*”

“*He is on the roll and he is dead.*”

“*He is on the roll and he is dead,*” said the man with the bad ear, “*and nobody in this ward has said so, and nobody is going to, and here is the whole of why and it takes eleven seconds.*”

He put the chalk down.

“*The name went into the roll correctly. A householder said it at a door in the autumn, the same as every other name, and the woman wrote it down and went on. He died in the fourth week of the fever and the fever is a fever and it is in four low wards and it is not in four rich ones and nobody has ever told me why and everybody who is alive in this ward knows. And the name is still on the roll. Because to take a name off that roll somebody has to decide that a thing in a book is not true, and the only person in this city who could be asked to decide that is the woman who keeps the book, and she will not do it on her own account because it is her book and she would be deciding it, and she cannot do it on anybody else's account because there is no form in this city that says a man may ask a woman to take a name out of a roll, and if there were one, then the next thing anybody would ask her for is to put one in.*”

“*Nobody has ever asked her to put one in.*”

“*Nobody has ever asked her to put one in and there is no form that says she may,*” said the man with the bad ear. “*Do you see what it is. The one thing nobody can do is the one thing we would all like done. And the reason we cannot do it is not that she is difficult. It is that the only way a name comes out of that book is somebody with a duty deciding that a person is not there, and there is no post in this city with that duty, and there has not been one in the four hundred years of this ward's roll.*”

---

He stood in a hall and looked at nineteen lines of chalk and worked out what it means that nobody can remove a person from a record.

It means the roll of Tallowgate is not a record of who is in the ward.

It is a record of who was in the ward, and of who somebody said was in the ward, and nobody has ever crossed anything off, and the ward cannot be asked to check because the ward is not a person and cannot be asked anything, and four hundred years of nine hundred doors has never once been asked whether they have ever left somebody off.

So the roll has at least one dead person in it, and there is a man at the top of nine steps in Weaver's Row who cannot come down and has not been in his room's house in six years, and a boy of eleven at a door in this ward who is the head of his house, and a lodger in a back room and a woman on a pallet in a front room, and a name in a back room at the end of four stairs that has been at a door since the first hour five days ago with nowhere to be put.

And the number of people in this roll who are not in this ward is not a number anybody can arrive at, and there is no way on earth to ask for it, and asking would make it a list.

“*That is the third thing your book cannot do,*” said the man with the bad ear, “*and it is the one I would mind least if it were not for the other two.*”

“*The other two.*”

“*It cannot say whether a person is alive and it cannot say whether a person is in the house,*” he said, “*and the third thing is that I asked for my own name to be put on a slate in a room upstairs in this building about a fortnight ago, and there is a name on a slate and a name in a roll and a column of dead with nothing over the top of it, and three books in one ward and not one of them can take a man out of it, and I am thirty-one and I have a bad ear and I keep a column of nothing because nobody has ever asked me for a heading.*”

He picked the chalk up again and went back to the end of the beds, and a woman on a bed said the name of the woman on the stool and the woman on the stool said she was there, and the morning went on the way mornings in a hall go.

---

He was in the plate yard at about the sixth hour and the light was going off the offcuts and there were two hundred lines of somebody's on the bench.

Bevin Tarr is fifty-nine and has been cutting plates for this ward since before the woman who keeps its book started, and he is the second name on a sheet in a chamber in the black building at the middle of the nine bridges, for a question on the fourteenth, and nobody has asked him.

He knew that somebody was coming because a carter's boy said so at the noon, and he did not stop cutting, and he did not look up.

“*You are on a sheet upstairs.*”

“*I am on a sheet upstairs,*” said Bevin Tarr, “*and I have known that for three days, because a woman came to this gate and told me, and she was not unkind about it, and she said: you have been named as a person who can speak to a document from somewhere else, and I have to tell you that I wrote your name on it off a paper in front of me and I did not put it there of my own accord.*”

He took the wheel off the plate and set it down.

“*And I have been three days with that,*” he said, “*and nobody has come and told me what the question is.*”

---

“*Will you go.*”

“*Will I go,*” said Bevin Tarr. “*That is the fourth time somebody has asked me something today and it is the only one of the four I can answer. Yes. I will go. I have a yard and four hundredweight of offcuts and a cart and I am fifty-nine, and I have been in this city since I was twenty-eight, and I have never once been in that building, and if I am on a sheet then I will go and stand in the room, and I would like to be told the hour and not the morning.*”

Then he said: “*Now you are going to ask me what a plate is for.*”

“*Yes.*”

“*Then I am going to tell you that I do not know,*” said the plate-cutter.

---

He said it without any weight on it and then he went and washed his hands in a bucket, which took a while, and he came back and dried them on his coat and stood against the bench with his arms folded, and a man of fifty-nine in a shed at the low end of a low ward in a city of ninety thousand, with a shed full of black offcuts, saying that he does not know what the thing he has made for thirty-one years is for.

“*I have asked twice in my life,*” he said. “*Once a long time ago, and I do not remember who I asked, and I was told it was the ward's business. And once about three years ago, and I was told the same sentence by a different person, and the person who said it was right both times and I have never had any reason to think either of them was wrong. And I have asked you and you are the third, and you have come down my lane with a leg and you have asked me a question about a cold line on a plate, and I have told you the two hundred and the polish line and the gatepost, which is more than I have told anybody in two years, and I would do it again, and it does not mean I know what a plate is for.*”

“*I do not know either,*” said Ilyan.

The plate-cutter looked at him for the first time.

“*Then we are two,*” he said. “*And here is what I am going to say in that room, and it is going to be a short sentence and it is going to be true and it is not going to be what anybody in that building wants. I am going to say: I have cut four thousand lines in thirty-one years and I have never been told what a line is, and I have asked twice, and I am not a man who has anything to offer a document from somewhere else except that sentence.*”

“*That is not a witness.*”

“*No,*” said Bevin Tarr. “*It is the only true thing I have got, and I am fifty-nine and I have a yard, and I have not the slightest idea what else a man in a room is supposed to be, and I would rather say that in front of nine people than go up there and be asked a question I cannot answer and pretend that I can.*”

He picked the wheel up again.

“*Nine at the first hour,*” he said, “*and I will be there before the room is warm, because I have never been in that building and I would like to see the room first.*”

---

He walked back up a mile and a half of low ground and thought about the day in front of him and the two places in it, and this is the ordinary cruelty of a city and it is not a difficulty and it is not a hardship and it is not going to be paid for by anybody.

Tomorrow there are nine hundred doors in Tallowgate and a woman of twenty-eight whose chalk ran out yesterday, and who has a string and a slate and three days of doors with nothing to mark them with, and she will be at a door at the second hour because that is when doors are answered, and there is no rota and no list and no name anywhere in this city that says she is going to be there.

And tomorrow there is a chamber on the second floor of the black building at the middle of the nine bridges, and a bench of four, and nine clerks, and a room of forty people, and a woman of forty-four who is going to be asked eleven questions or more for a whole day by people who have a duty to ask them, and a man of fifty-nine who does not know what a plate is for, and a man of thirty-one with a bad leg who is on a sheet in that room and cannot get off it and has said the price out loud of being in it in a yard in front of nine people.

He cannot be at a door at the second hour and in a chamber at the second hour. He cannot be in two places in a city of ninety thousand people, and the reason is a leg, and there is nothing anybody can do about it, and nobody in that ward is going to suggest that he should try.

The four bells went. The fever did not stop. Somewhere in a low ward a boy of eleven was carrying water in the dark for a penny at the end of the week, and a woman of forty-four was doing a round at a rate of two doors a day that she was not going to be paid for, and a man of thirty-one with a bad ear was rubbing out a number on a board at the end of a hall and chalking a number that was less wrong, and not one person in that ward got anything out of the day.

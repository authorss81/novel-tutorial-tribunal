# Chapter 355: A Woman Who Goes To Thirty Doors A Fortnight And Nobody Has Asked Her

It was his hundred and fifth morning and the last day of the seventy-second week, and there were nine people outside the roll-room in Tallowgate at about the second hour, and every one of the nine wanted to go in.

That is the sentence the ward would use. The truth is that eight of the nine were already in the book and had come about a child, and the ninth was a young man of twenty or so who had a wife who had come out of a hall on the ninth day and had to be put back in because a person is inside the protection when the roll has the name in it, and the roll is made at a door by the person of the house, and his wife does not sleep in a house anybody calls theirs.

She was at the door in nine seconds and she said one sentence to him and closed it and did not open it for another hour, and the eight went in and the young man went and did not come back.

---

The roll-room is a room at the back of a chandler's shop and it has a stove in it and a table and a shelf of ledgers and a lamp, and four hundred years of other rooms' habits in the way the shelves are made. There is no public room in Tallowgate with a door that is not open. This one has a door and it is shut and it is shut because of what is in it.

“*No,*” said the woman at the table, who had not got up and had not been asked to.

“*I have not said anything yet.*”

“*You have a face on you that has been in this city four days,*” she said, “*and everybody that has been in this city four days has the same face, and the face is the face of a man who has heard the word roll and thinks there is something in the book. There is nothing in the book. The book is names and the names are the houses, and if you want to know whether your own name is in it you can ask me that, and if you want a page of it you can not, and there is no form in this city that lets anybody have a page of it, and the reason there is no form is that a page of it is a list of people and a list of people is a thing a man can carry and a thing a man can carry is a thing a man can say out loud in a market, and I have been doing this for eleven years and I have watched that happen twice and it is not a small thing.*”

She is about forty-four. She has a pen, an ink, a ruler, a weight, and a stack of nine slates, and she has not sat down since the second hour.

“*I am not going to tell you my name,*” she said, “*and I will tell you why, because you have come in here without a reason written on you and I would rather be refused than managed. In this city a person who gives her name in a room is a person in the room, and that is not a figure of speech, it is a clerk and a book, and I have been in this city thirty-one years and I have watched four people do it and two of them were about a book and one of them is still paying for it this week. You have not given me yours. So neither of us is going to be in this, and we can both go on being people at a table.*”

“*I want to ask you a question about the book,*” he said, “*and I will not ask it until you have told me you are willing to be asked, and I am telling you that I am not willing to be asked on your behalf by anybody.*”

She put the pen down, which is the only thing she did for nine seconds.

“*Ask it,*” she said.

---

“*What is the roll for.*”

She did not answer it at once, and the not-answering was the answer's first half.

“*That is a stupid question,*” she said, “*and I have been asked four hundred better ones in this room and I want to be exact with you, because you have given me your attention and this is the thing I do with it. It is not stupid because it has no answer. It is stupid because I did not know the answer until this morning and I have been keeping the book for eleven years.*”

“*You did not know.*”

“*Not in words,*” she said. “*I know what I do. I know that I go to about thirty doors a fortnight, and there are nine hundred doors in this ward, and a fortnight is fourteen days, so thirty doors for fourteen days over nine hundred doors is four hundred and twenty days to go round the whole of it once. And a year in this country is fifty-two weeks of seven days, which is three hundred and sixty-four days. So a round is four hundred and twenty days, and four hundred and twenty less three hundred and sixty-four is fifty-six days. It is a year and fifty-six days to go round Tallowgate once, and I have been doing it since I was thirty-three, and I have got round it nine times, and the last time I finished the whole of it was in the autumn before the water came, and when the water came, eleven weeks back, I was about a fifth of the way through this one, and I have not been to a door since, and I am not going to be going to a door while there is a fever in the ward, and I have not said that out loud to anybody until four minutes ago either.*”

“*And what is the roll for,*” he said.

“*It is the answer to a question,*” she said. “*Somebody asks at the house who is in the house, and I write down what they say, and that is the whole of the machinery. And that is how it has to be, and I have never wanted it any other way, because the only way anybody can be in a book is if the person who lives with them says so. And here is the thing I did not know until this morning, and I would like you to let me get to it in my own order: I do not know what the question is for. I have known since I was thirty-three that I write down who is in a house. I have not once known why anybody wants to know. Not the ward, not the bench, not the magistrate, not me. I have been the answer to a question that nobody in this city has ever said out loud, and there has been a book the whole time, and it has been correct every single day for eleven years.*”

---

He stood in that room with a stove going and four hundred years of shelves and he had a thing in his hands that he has had since a kitchen in a village four hundred miles off, and it is the only thing he has ever had.

A question, asked out loud, in front of other people, about a thing that is not his, which makes a person a person in a record.

It has cost a woman of about thirty-four at a bar a thing she cannot put down. It has cost him twice. He has used it three times in a hundred and five mornings and every one of the three times it made somebody a person in a record, and there is no instrument in four hundred miles that lifts a person back out of one, and the person pays, not him.

And here is the difference and it took him nine seconds standing by a stove to see it, and it is the whole of what this volume is going to cost him.

In the country, the finding was a road and a lane and nine people, and a person who said a true thing in their own mouth in a doorway was not thereby a person in a record. Here, in a city, the moment he says a true thing about a book in a room, with one other person in it, the person is the keeper of a public roll, in this city, and the thing he has said is about the only record in this ward that decides who is inside a protection. And if a stranger with no standing says in a room that a public record does not know why it exists, then either the record is right and the stranger is wrong, or the record has been doing something for eleven years that nobody authorised, and a woman of forty-four is standing in the room while that happens.

There is no form in this country that says a man may ask a question that is not a question about him, and there is no form anywhere that says a person can be asked afterwards what they were in the room for.

So he shut his mouth, in a room, at the fourth hour, and it took him four seconds, and nobody noticed, and nobody helped him, and it cost him nothing.

That is how he knows it was the right thing to do. It is the second time.

---

“*There is one more thing and it is a fact and it is free,*” she said, “*because you have earned it by not asking me anything else, and I have got nine people at that door.*”

“*Go and open it.*”

“*I am going to open it and I am going to be at this table until the last of the light, and I am going to say the fact, and then you are going to take it and use it, and if you use it on me I will know before the second day, because I have been in this city thirty-one years and I know exactly what a person looks like when they have come to spend me.*”

“*I am not going to spend you.*”

“*Everybody does,*” she said, “*and I am not blaming you, I have never blamed a person for it in my life, and I am telling you the shape of it so that you will have to look at it.*”

She turned round and got the shelf, and came back with a plate in a cloth, and set it on the table and unwrapped it. Blackglass, a hand's width, a line of names cut into it, and the edges of the last cut are not polished and there is blackglass dust on the cloth.

“*That is a plate,*” she said, “*and the book is cut onto it, and the book on that plate is the roll. The paper is the working. The paper comes here and I write on it and it goes out and it is right, and it is the only copy anybody has got of what I did this week. And the plate is the account, and the account is the one that is asked for.*”

“*And if they disagree.*”

“*Then the plate is what the ward is and the paper is what I did,*” she said, “*and I have never in eleven years had a name on the paper that was not on the plate, and I have never had a name on the plate that was not on the paper, and I have checked, and I am not a fool, and I have told you the whole of my working life in nine minutes to a stranger because he stood still and did not ask me anything, and it is a quarter past the fourth hour and there are nine people at my door and two hundred names of mine in that book that are on a paper and not on a plate because there is no money in this ward and there has not been any money in this ward for two years.*”

“*How much is that.*”

“*Four shillings the hundred,*” she said, “*and there are two hundred of mine, and two hundreds at four shillings is eight shillings, and I have not had eight shillings, and there is nobody in this city who has ever been shown a bill for it, and I have been eleven years in a room with two hundred names on a paper and not on a plate and I did not tell you this morning because you had not been in the city a day, and I am telling you now because you have now been in it four and I would like it to be five.*”

She opened the door. Eight people went in and the young man who had been shut out that morning did not come back, and the door was shut again at four, and she went back to her table, and the lamp, and the four hundred years of other rooms' habits.

Nobody thanked her. She was not thanked. She is paid four shillings a year, which is forty-eight pence, which is a farthing a day and not quite, and it is the same forty-eight pence as a plate of a hundred names, and she did not intend that and nobody said it to her and it is not a joke, it is two lines in a ledger four hundred miles from here and a woman who does not know it.

# Chapter 363: Nine Hundred Doors, Thirty A Fortnight, And A Woman With Chalk

It was his hundred and thirteenth morning and the first day of the seventy-fourth week, and the fourteenth was six days off, and he was in the room at the back of the chandler's shop at about the second hour asking a question he had no right to ask, and the woman at the table had nine people at her door and did not answer it for nine minutes.

Then she put the pen down and told him, because he had earned it by standing still.

---

“*What does one round of this ward cost.*”

“*That is the second time in this city that somebody has asked me a money question,*” said the woman at the table, “*and the first one was a man at a grindstone who did not know what a name was, and I will tell you what I told him, which was four shillings the hundred, and that is not what I am going to tell you, because that is the price of a plate and you have not asked me about a plate.*”

“*I have asked you about a round.*”

“*You have asked me about a round,*” she said, “*and I am going to answer it in the only way it can be answered, which is in numbers, and then I am going to open that door, and then you are going to go away and stand somewhere else.*”

She turned the chair round, which is what she does when she is going to do arithmetic.

“*About thirty doors a fortnight. That is what this book has always been. There are nine hundred doors in this ward. A fortnight is fourteen days, so thirty doors in fourteen days is about two doors a day, and two doors a day is the whole of it. That is the rate. Two doors a day, nine hundred doors, so two doors a day for nine hundred doors is four hundred and twenty days to go round the ward once.*”

“*Four hundred and twenty days.*”

“*A year in this country is fifty-two weeks of seven days, which is three hundred and sixty-four days,*” said the woman at the table. “*So a round is four hundred and twenty days and three hundred and sixty-four days is a year, and the difference between them is fifty-six days. A round is a year and fifty-six days. I have been doing it since I was thirty-three and I have got round it nine times and the last time I finished the whole of it was in the autumn before the water came, and when the water came, eleven weeks back, I was about a fifth of the way through this one.*”

“*And the cost.*”

“*That is the cost,*” she said. “*A door costs a visit. That is the unit. Not four shillings, not a farthing, a visit. And there is no other price on it in this country, and anybody who tells you there is has not been to a door.*”

---

He waited, because there was more of it, and she had said there was.

“*I am paid four shillings a year,*” she said, “*which is forty-eight pence, and I have said that to you once and I am saying it again because it is now part of the answer instead of being a fact about me. Forty-eight pence is a year. The round is a year and fifty-six days. So the fifty-six days are the part of it that nobody has ever paid anybody for, and I have never been able to find out what a day of that is worth, and I have looked for eleven years, and there is nobody in this city who could tell me, and I am not going to be a person who asks.*”

“*That is a small thing to say.*”

“*It is the whole of what I have,*” said the woman at the table. “*Two doors a day. A visit. Nine hundred doors. And the part of me that is not a wage and is not a task and is a woman of forty-four with a book, and if you write that down anywhere you will have written down the only number in this city that cannot be bought.*”

She opened the door, and eight people went in, and a man she did not know stood at the back of them and did not go in and did not go away.

“*Him,*” she said. “*He has been at that door since the first hour and he is not going to tell me and I am not going to ask him in front of eight people. Go and ask him whatever it is, and do not do it in this room.*”

---

He asked her, standing in the passage behind the shop, and the man was about forty and had a bad cough that was not the fever, and he had been in that house eleven years and it was the only house he has had in this city, and his wife was on the books of the low hall at the end of Tallowgate since the second week of the fever and he could not pay for a bed and could not do the other thing either.

“*I have been at the door since the first hour,*” he said, “*because the woman who keeps the book comes round about once a year and she has not come round since the autumn and I have had my name ready since the water came and there is nowhere to put it, and I am not going to be a man who says a thing like that in a shop door in front of eight people, and I have said it to you because you are a stranger with a leg and a stranger with a leg is the only kind of person anybody in this ward will say anything to.*”

“*Say it at the door next time.*”

“*There is no next time until somebody calls a meeting of this ward,*” said the man of forty. “*And then it is not you asking me and it is not me at a door. It is a thing on a form, and a form has somebody's name at the top of it, and I have got four in my house and I am not one of the four and I am not going to be.*”

---

And in the afternoon he walked out with her.

That is the part of the day that nobody in this city is going to know about and the part of the day that is the whole of what she is doing, and he was not useful in it and he was there for four hours.

She knocked. She did not knock the way a man knocks, and he asked her about it once, on the fourth door, and she told him and that is the only instruction he has been given in this city.

“*Two fingers and the knuckle of the middle one, low, and you do it three times and then you stop and you stand there,*” she said. “*A man knocks like he is owed something. Two knocks and a wait is a debt. Three low ones and a stand is a person at a door, and if somebody in there is owed money by anybody in this street they do not come to the door, and if somebody in there is hiding they do not come to the door, and if somebody in there has the fever they do not come to the door. So you stand there, and after a while you go away, and you come back, and it is not a knock that gets a door. It is being the same person at the same door three times.*”

He stood there eleven times that afternoon.

Twice the door came and it was a woman who said there was nobody home and it was not true, and she chalked the mark and she did not argue. Once it was a man who asked what it was about and she said it was about the roll and he said the roll was right and shut the door, and she chalked the mark and said *right* back at the door, which he did not understand until the eleventh. Once the door came and there were two children in it and nobody else, and she asked them a question and they answered her, and she chalked the mark and did not write anything down, and he saw her look at the two of them for a moment longer than the work required.

Once there was a boy of about sixteen at a door in the row above the plate-yard's, and she said one sentence to him and he said one back and neither of them looked at the stranger at all.

“*What was that.*”

“*That was a boy who lives in a house with his aunt and works in a yard and I told him to have his aunt answer her own door,*” she said, “*and he told me he has asked her twice and she says she will get round to it, and I have believed him, and I am not going to put that in a book anywhere.*”

---

At about the seventh hour she stopped in a doorway and turned round and looked at him, and he understood that he was going to be asked a question, and it was the fourth of the day.

“*You have been holding doors and standing in cold passages for four hours,*” she said, “*and you have not asked me one thing about the work, and I do not believe you for a second and I have not believed a stranger in nineteen years. So here is the question, and I am going to ask it now, and I want the true answer and not the one that makes you a good man.*”

“*Ask it.*”

“*Why are you doing this.*”

And he thought about it for a long time, in a doorway, with a bad leg and a cold hand.

“*Because there is a book at the back of a chandler's shop that two hundred people are not in,*” he said, “*and there is a form in this city that nobody has ever filled in, and the form needs a name at the top of it, and I am not going to put a name at the top of anything, and so the only instrument I have is to say a true thing out loud in front of other people, and that makes a person into a person in a record, and I have used it three times in twelve days and it has cost three people something and it has not put a single name into that book.*”

“*So you are here for the book.*”

“*I am here for the book,*” he said, “*and I have been useless to you for four hours and I will be useless to you for however many days you have left of that chalk, and I would like you to put me on a door and not to put me anywhere else, because the moment anybody in this city finds out that I am any use at this, I will be a man with a job, and a man with a job is a man whose name is on a form.*”

She looked at him for four seconds and then she went and knocked on the next door.

“*Then hold the door,*” she said.

He held the door.

The four bells went that night at the low end of Tallowgate, and nine hundred doors in that ward had four hundred and twenty days in them and four days' worth of chalk, and the rate was two a day, and two doors a day is not a wage and is not a task and is a woman.

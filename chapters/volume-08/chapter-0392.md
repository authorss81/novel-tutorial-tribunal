# Chapter 392: Seven Things Read Out, And A Bench With Nothing To Rule On

The second day of the seventy-eighth week was his hundred and forty-second morning and his forty-first in a city he came to on foot, and the fever was fifteen weeks and four days old, and a bench of four came down the hill at the second hour and sat down at the end of a long table in a public room at the foot of it, which had never happened.

There are four of them and they came together, and the one who had the chair nearest the door had been in the chamber above the nine bridges for twenty-two years and had been in that room four times in twenty-two years, every one of the four in answer to a thing that had been sent up, and he said so himself, standing, before he sat down, because nobody had asked him and he wanted it in the room that he was not a stranger to it.

“*Twenty-two years,*” he said, “*and four times in this room, and three of those were about a boundary and one of them was about a drain, and I have never in twenty-two years read an order in the room it is on the wall of, and I am not going to pretend to you that I know how this morning goes, because nobody in this room knows how this morning goes and that is the first honest thing anybody has said to me about it in a year.*”

---

There were forty-one people in the room at the second hour and the clerk of thirty counted them himself and said the number out loud, because that is a thing a clerk of thirty does four hundred times a day, and the room holds about sixty, and there was nobody at the door after the fourth hour.

The order went first, because the man with the bad ear had asked for the order and not for anything else, and the one with the chair nearest the door read it himself, standing at the end of the long table, all six parts, in the voice of a man who has read a thing out in a room nine times in twenty-two years and is not proud of any of them. He read the five parts and then he came to the sixth and read a date, and then read the word in the margin.

Nobody said anything when he had finished, which is what a room in this city does at the end of an order, and there were nine inches of blank under it on that wall, and the blank was still blank.

Then he said: “*We are four men and we are not able to be asked to do one single thing with what we have just read. If anybody in this room has a question about a part of it, put the question, and we will answer it, and that is all the work there is for us today.*”

---

So the seven things were read out, and the clerk of forty wrote them down because a room that writes things down was in the room, and every one of them was read by the person it belonged to, in their own mouth, and not one of them was joined to any of the others by anybody in that room, including the four men at the end of the table, who were there to be asked about them and not to be told what they meant together.

“*One,*” said the woman on the stool, who had come up the row at the first hour and had been on her stool since the eighth morning of the fever and had not sat down in the room. “*Eleven beds in the low hall at the end of this row and twenty-one names on the books of that hall, and the ten that are not in beds are in a yard and a shed at the Ninth Weir and they are better, and every morning I put up a number that says nothing is free, and the number is true, and I have known for a long time that the number is the part of it that is wrong, and I have no way of being less honest than I am. That is mine and I will answer for it and I have said that boy is worse than the other nine every morning since the eighth morning of the fever and I am not going to say anything else about him in this room.*”

The man with the bad ear read the four boards he had charge of and gave thirty beds free across four halls, and the man of fifty-two gave his four numbers off four walls and named the two doors that had not opened to him, and did not say why they had not opened to him, because he had said that already and to somebody else.

---

“*Two,*” said the woman with the book, who had walked a mile and a half and had not opened the door for it. “*Nine hundred doors, thirty of them a fortnight, and thirty lots of fourteen days is four hundred and twenty days, and a year in this city is three hundred and sixty-four, and a round is a year and fifty-six days, and the fifty-six days over that are the part nobody has ever paid anybody for. That is a figure and I have been saying it since the morning a man with a bad leg sat on the other side of this table and asked me for it, and I say it now because a bench of four is in the room and there has never been one in the room while I said it.*”

“*Three,*” said the man with the bad ear, and he put two fingers on the table and did not have anything to put them on, because the boards were at the top of this city and the halls were in three of the four low wards and one of them was in Cresset Street, which nobody in this room can reach this morning. “*A line in chalk at the foot of a board at the end of a run of boards, and a second line on a board at the top of this city, and the man who wrote the second one is me, and I did not rub out the first one, and I have said that out loud in a room before anybody asked me and I am saying it again because there are four of you there and I have never said it to any of you. There is no discharge paper in this city and no office that issues one and I have been asked to rub it out and I said no and I was right to say no.*”

“*Four,*” he said, “*is nineteen lines in chalk in a column in this ward with nothing over the top of it and nothing at the foot of it, and I have been keeping it since the second week of this fever, and I am paid nothing, and I have wanted a heading over it every day of that, and I asked for one in a room on the last day of last week and I did not get one.*”

He turned round to the end of the table then.

“*You are four men and a chair each and you have been in that chamber twenty-two years. Can one of you write words over a thing in a ward.*”

The one with the chair nearest the door said: “*No. We cannot put a word over anything in a ward. There is one document we produce and it is called a return, and a return is what we send back down the hill when there is something to send back, and a return is written by a clerk and not by us, and a return has to be about a thing that came up, and nothing has come up this morning except a form with a name on it.*”

“*Then I have not got a heading,*” said the man with the bad ear, “*and I have not got anything else, and I am not going to ask a second time in a room.*”

---

“*Five,*” said the woman with the book, in the voice of a woman reading a thing out of a book. “*A roll of a ward with a lamp on it, and two names in it that cannot be put right. One belongs to a person who is alive in a bed at the top of this city and is at a shut door in a row above the plate yard's, and one belongs to a person who died in the fourth week of the fever, and the name is correct and he is dead. And three hundred names in four low wards that are on a paper and not on a plate. The three hundred are not a gap in anything and they are not a thing anybody has to pay for, because a name said by a person of a ward goes in without a fee, and that goes in without a plate, and that is a second way of keeping a record and there is nothing for a second way of keeping a record to be a mistake about.*”

“*Six,*” said the man at the end of the table with a plate under his arm, who had walked a mile and a half with it because he could not leave a plate. “*A plate, a paper, a woman with a lamp, and a block in a gatepost at the end of a passage with a mark on its face that nobody in this ward can read, and the four of them are what makes a ward's record a record, and they do not agree with each other, and they have not agreed with each other for as long as anybody in this room has been in this ward. The paper is the account and the plate is the working and the block says what it said before either of those two was cut, and the block gets nothing, because there is no meeting of a ward that can weigh it and there has not been one in Tallowgate since the autumn before the water came.*”

“*And seven,*” said the one with the chair nearest the door, standing, and looking at the wall rather than at the room. “*That is the order, and we hold it, and it is a bench's document the way a candle is a chandler's, which is to say it is on a shelf and it belongs to him. We can be asked what it says and who wrote it. We cannot be asked to do one single thing with it, and no form in this city lets anybody ask us to, and that is not an excuse and it is not modesty. It is the arrangement, and it is the whole of the arrangement.*”

---

Nobody joined any two of them. He watched it from the second hour to the change, which is longer than the seven things took to read, because people kept coming to the end of the table and then going and standing at the wall, and he kept waiting for somebody to put two of them side by side and say *and*, and it did not come, and at the change the clerk of thirty put his hand on the book he had been writing in and said what a room in this city says at the end of an order, and it stopped.

And then the one with the chair nearest the door said the thing, and he said it slowly, and he said it as a finding and not as a favour, and he said it to the room and not to Ilyan, and Ilyan understood afterwards that it had not been given to him either.

“*We have been sent things for nine years,*” he said, “*and there are nine lines in the column on the wall of that chamber and every one of them is a thing somebody wanted done to a document. This morning is the first morning a thing has come up that is not a document. It is a form with a name at the top of it and seven things said out loud in a room, and we can be asked about all seven of them. Here is what we have to say about it before anybody asks.*”

He put both hands flat on the table, and Ilyan watched a man who had never been in that room do the thing he did every time he finished a piece of work, and did not know that he was doing it.

“*There is no form in this city in front of us, and there has not been one all morning, and there is not going to be one before the change. A row of things laid out in a room is nothing. It is a morning. People will remember it or they will not. It is not a finding and it cannot be enforced and it will be contradicted in a fortnight by anybody with a reason.*”

He looked up.

“*And a bench of four with no form in front of it is also nothing. That is not a thing anybody in this city has had to say before, because it has never been in a room with anybody. That is the thing we have been for four hundred years, and it is not hidden, and it is not a lie, and nobody in this city has ever been able to say it out loud, and we are saying it now because a man of thirty-one with a bad ear asked us a question in front of a clerk and we could not write words over his nineteen and we are not going to pretend that is the same as being able to do nothing.*”

Nobody in the room thanked him.

“*It goes back down the hill on a return,*” he said, “*because that is the only form we have, and a clerk writes it and not us, and whatever is on it can be sent back down and read out in this room, and a thing that is read out in this room is the only kind of finding there is in this city. I am not going to promise what will be on it.*”

---

The clerk of thirty wrote nothing down for a minute after that, which is the only time anybody who has watched him for nine years has seen him do it, and then he filled the tray and the tray went up the hill a little before the noon bell with a runner, and nobody sent for the runner.

The man with the bad ear put his hat on the table again and did not say anything and was not thanked and did not expect it, and the fever was fifteen weeks and four days old and it did not stop for a finding, and the blank under the order on that wall was still blank, and the four bells went over the low end of Tallowgate, and a woman of twenty-eight went past the door with a slate against her hip and no chalk in her apron, and she looked in, and she did not come in, and she went on down the row.

# Chapter 381: The Bill For Candles, Folded Into Four, And A Form With A Name At The Top Of It

It was his hundred and thirty-first morning and the fifth day of the seventy-sixth week, and he had been in this city thirty days, and he took the paper out of his coat in the yard at the low end of Tallowgate.

It was folded in four. The creases had gone so far into it that the pencil had broken through the paper in two places, and where it had broken through there was nothing behind it, and he could see the bench through the holes.

He had carried it across a mile and a half of low ground every morning for twenty-three days and had not looked at it since the morning after he wrote it.

Then he unfolded it on the bench where plates are cut and read it out loud, all of it, in a shed, to a man of fifty-nine at a wheel, because a thing that stays in a coat is worth nothing to anybody and he had nothing else.

---

There were five clauses and a half and there was a line struck through before the fifth, and the striking-through was his own and he had done it in about the fortieth minute of the morning he wrote it.

The first clause said that everybody sleeping in the four low wards is inside the order from the moment the order stands and not from the moment a roll has their name in it.

The second said the paper and the plate of a ward are one record, and that where they differ the paper is the account and the plate is the working, until a meeting of the ward has weighed the block again.

The third said that a person of the ward who can say a name may put it in, and that a name said by a person of the ward goes in without a fee.

The fourth was four words. *Somebody must go to the doors.*

And the fifth was the half, and it was the only one with anybody in it, and it said that the somebody in the fourth is the keeper of the roll of that ward.

Underneath the fifth there was one line more, and it was in the same hand and it was not a question, and he had written it at the end and he had not been able to work out what it was for, and it was still there and it was still not a question.

Bevin Tarr did not stop cutting while he read it. He went on cutting for a good while and then the wheel stopped, which in that yard is a louder thing than a person speaking.

---

“*Who is that,*” said the plate-cutter, to the bench.

“*The woman who keeps the roll of this ward.*”

“*Have you asked her.*”

“*I read it to her. On the morning I wrote it. She heard all five and she did not interrupt me once, and then she put her pen down and said I had put her in it, and she was right, and she said it was not a clause, it was a document, and there is a difference, and I have not got an answer to the difference, and I am not going to get one in this city.*”

He turned the wheel a quarter round and stopped it again.

“*And what did she say about the fifth.*”

“*She said she was not going to tell me what to put in it, and she wanted it written down that that was not modesty and not a refusal to help a stranger with a bad leg, and then she told me the whole of what was the matter with it, which was that I had written the person and left out the bill.*”

“*And you have come down here and read it to me.*”

“*I have come down here and read it to a man who is in this yard and has been in this yard before I came into this city, and you asked me who she is, and you did not have to.*”

---

“*Say the fourth one again,*” said Bevin Tarr.

He said it again.

“*And the fifth.*”

He said it again, and the plate-cutter looked at the two holes the pencil had gone through in the paper, and then he went back to the wheel, and he worked for about a minute and a half, and neither of them said anything.

Then he said: “*Eleven years she has been the woman that comes when the water comes, and thirty doors a fortnight in the four hundred years before that, and you have written a piece of paper that says she is the one who has to walk them, and you have carried it in your coat for twenty-three days, and you have come down a lane and opened it out on my bench and read it to me before you had shown it to a single person who is not paid nothing.*”

“*I showed it to her.*”

“*You showed it to her before you had decided to do it,*” said Bevin Tarr. “*That is not the same as showing it to her after, and I do not know what the difference is worth and I am not going to work it out in my own yard this morning.*”

He cut for the rest of the hour and did not ask him anything else, and when he left the shed the paper was still open on the bench, and it rained a little on it, and he took it with him, which he had not intended to do.

---

He was at the black building at the middle of the nine bridges at the third hour, and the clerk of thirty keeps the tray at the end of the long table on the ground floor, and Ilyan asked him a question, and he did not ask it well and that turned out to be the right way to ask it.

“*What happens to a piece of paper in this city.*”

“*Depends which paper.*”

“*A piece of paper with a thing in it that somebody wants kept, and it is not a form, and it is not a ruling, and it has nobody's name on it and a man has asked for it to be kept.*”

The clerk of thirty put his pen down, which is what he does at the end of a morning and not at the beginning of one, and he looked at the sheet standing on its corner on the end of the table with a bill for candles printed on the back of it and four clauses and a half in pencil.

“*Then it goes to the chamber on the second floor,*” he said, “*and it is copied in a fair hand, and the fair hand writes the thing down, and the fair hand is a woman of forty at a table and she is not in this room and you will not be able to stop her and neither can I.*”

“*And the copy.*”

“*The copy has the thing on it and the date on it and a line at the top of it for the name of the person who is sending it, and there is no way to send one of those up this hill with nothing at the top of it, and the reason there is no way is that it would be a thing nobody could be asked about, and then it is not a record, it is a piece of paper in a room.*”

---

He stood at the end of the table for a while with the rain coming through the door onto the floor by the step and the clerk of thirty not saying one word more than he had been asked for, which is the ordinary way of that building and the whole of what he has been able to get out of it in thirty days.

“*There is a form,*” he said.

“*There is a form for sending a thing up the hill and there is a line at the top of the form, and they are the same line, and I fill those lines from the paper in front of me, and I have filled that line a great many times in nine years and every one of them had a name on the paper it came off.*”

“*And this one has not.*”

“*This one has a name on it in your own hand and it is not at the top of it and it is not in the middle of it and it is nowhere on it, and the word at the bottom of your fourth clause is *doors* and the word under the fifth is a name, and I am telling you what is in front of me and not what I would like to be in front of me.*”

The clerk of thirty picked his pen up again.

“*I have not asked you for it and I am not going to,*” he said. “*You have been in this city thirty days and you have never once had to give me a name and I have never once had to ask you for one, and I would like that to go on being the arrangement. That is not a kindness to you and it is not a kindness to me. It is that nobody has ever stood in this room and handed me a thing with nothing at the top of it and asked me to send it up, and if you do, I am going to ask you for the name, and you are going to have to either say it or not say it, and there is no third thing, and I have not said that to you because you have not asked me yet.*”

He went back to his tray.

“*Close the door on your way out. There is a draught at this end that gets at the fair hand on the second floor and she will be telling me about it for a month.*”

---

He stood in the lane outside the black building for a while with the paper in his hand, unfolded, in the rain, and this is the whole of what he had at the fourth hour on the fifth day of the seventy-sixth week, thirty days into a city he came to on foot, and it is not a decision and it is not a resolution.

He came into this city with nothing at the top of him. He has refused a name at a gate and in a room and he has had a clerk do it for him twice, and it has been the best protection he has ever had, and it is not a right, and it is not the law, and it is the ordinary practice of a man with a tray who does not want to be the person who put a name on a thing.

And the practice ends the moment a piece of paper is put in front of him with nothing at the top of it, because then there is only one person in the room it can be filled from, and that person is the man holding it, and that man is him, and there is nobody to stop him and nobody who will refuse for him and nobody who is even going to mention it.

A thing in a coat is a thing he is carrying. A thing in a room is a thing somebody can be asked about, and he has had that in his head for a month and it has never once been a reason not to do a thing, and today it is the only reason he has for anything at all.

He folded it in four again, and the creases went along the old ones, and the two holes went in at the same places, and he put it back in the inside pocket of a coat that has cost him nothing to keep for thirty days, and the four bells went over the low end of Tallowgate, and the fever was fourteen weeks old, and it did not stop for him, and in the morning he was going to be in a room at the back of a chandler's shop, and the name at the foot of the fifth clause was the woman who would be at the table.

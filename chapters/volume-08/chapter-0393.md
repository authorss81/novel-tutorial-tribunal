# Chapter 393: The Same Rule In Two Kinds Of Ward, And A Door

The third day of the seventy-eighth week was his hundred and forty-third morning and his forty-second in a city he came to on foot, and the fever was fifteen weeks and five days old, and the part of the order had stood nineteen days, and nobody had called that room for anything, and it was full at the second hour anyway, because it is a room and the door is open and in this city that has turned out to be the whole of how a room gets used.

The woman on the stool came up the row at the first hour and stood by the end of the long table and did not sit down, and she had a slate off a man who keeps a column of nineteen and a stub of chalk off the man at the wheel, and she had asked for the chalk and he had not asked her why and had put it on the table by his own hand, and she was going to give it back and neither of them was going to write it anywhere, and this was the first time in fifteen weeks and five days that she had been in a room with a slate in it.

“*I am going to do it in front of you,*” she said, “*because I have been on a stool since the eighth morning of this fever saying one short thing every morning to everybody who comes through a door, and I have got nothing else, and a slate is the only thing in this city that anybody can be shown a number on and be argued with.*”

---

The six halls in this city are three in the four low wards and three in the four rich ones, and the three in the low wards are the low hall at the end of Tallowgate and two in wards this morning nobody in the room can reach, and the three in the rich wards are in Cresset Street, which is four miles up the hill and which nobody in this room can reach either, and that is the whole of the difference between them this morning.

“*Six boards,*” she said. “*Six numbers of beds free, chalked and rubbed out every morning by a person whose job it is, and the job is honest, and the numbers are true, and the numbers are the only thing about the six halls that anybody in this city has ever written down.*”

She put a mark on the slate and turned it round so that the room could see it, which took her a while, because there were forty people in it and the slate is small.

“*The low hall at the end of this row has eleven beds and twenty-one names on its books and has had the same shape since the eighth morning. The other two in the low wards are the same and I have not been in either of them, and the man of fifty-two has been in neither of them, and he has said so in this room.*”

“*That is four figures and two doors,*” said the man of fifty-two, from the end of the table. “*Two, four, eleven and thirteen, and it is thirty, and I have not got the other two halls, and I am not going to make that number into a number about a rule, and I said it at a door on this row and I am not going to say it again.*”

“*I know why,*” said the woman on the stool, “*and I am not asking you for it. I want the other thing.*”

---

“*Here is the arithmetic and it took me all night and it is not a feeling,*” she said. “*Nobody has been moved out of a hall in Cresset Street. Not one person, in fifteen weeks and five days, and there are three halls up there, and I am not in them and I cannot get to them and neither can anybody in this room.*”

“*Then you have not proved it.*”

“*I have not proved it and I cannot prove it and that is what I am telling you,*” she said. “*I have proved the other half, and the other half is the whole of it. The rule moves whoever is worse. Being worse is not a person and it is not a group and it is not a ward, and a ward is not a person and cannot be asked anything, so the rule does not have a group in it. It moves the worst person in a hall. And the worst person in a hall at the end of this row is in a hall at the end of this row because the houses are here, and the houses are here because this is where the people are, and the people are here because this is where they have always been, and the roll of this ward has their names in it because the roll of a ward has the names of the people in that ward in it.*”

She chalked something else and did not turn the slate round.

“*So a rule that moves the worst goes after the people whose names are on a roll of their own ward, and it does it every time, and it will do it every time there is a fever, and no person wrote that and no person chose it. That is the part I have got.*”

“*And the rest of it.*”

“*The rest of it is a door,*” said the woman on the stool. “*And a person moved out of a hall at the end of this row is at no door at the end of this row, and his line is still at his door, and there is no form in this city that moves a name from one place to another, and a woman in a hall up the hill who is better can go home in the morning because there is somebody at the door for her to go to, and she goes, and nobody writes a line about it because a hall has a line for every person who comes in it and no line at all for anybody who goes out.*”

---

Nobody said anything for a while, and the man with the bad ear said it.

“*I have looked,*” he said, “*and I have said in a room that nobody hid anything from me and I am saying it again because it is going in a room today and somebody is going to write it down. Nobody in this city has hidden anything. The book says what the book says. The board says what the board says. The order says what the order says. There is nothing in any of them that is not true, and I have been keeping a column of nineteen dead with nothing over the top of it and nothing at the foot of it, and those nineteen are the truest thing in this ward, and they are true because of a door.*”

“*And nobody can be asked,*” said Tarin Keel.

“*Nobody can be asked,*” said the woman on the stool. “*That is the last part and it is the one I have been saying one sentence about every morning for fifteen weeks and five days without knowing it. The people that rule moves are the worst today. There is no meeting of them and there is no name for them and there is nobody at the top of anything, and you cannot ask a group whether it agrees, because there is nothing to ask, and you cannot tell one of them that anything has happened, because you do not know this morning who it will be.*”

The woman of twenty-eight came in at that, and she was not asked, and she came in because the room was full and she is a person who goes to doors and a full room is a place where people are.

“*Say the number,*” said the boy of sixteen from the end of the table, which is a thing a boy of sixteen does at a door and in a room.

“*No,*” she said, and she put the slate against her hip.

---

“*Say the number.*”

“*You do not need me to say a number, and I am not going to, and it is not modesty and I would have said it a month ago. A number of mine is a number about how many doors I have been to, and a door I have been to is a visit, and that is the only thing in this city that is not a shilling, and a visit is not a thing a person can be held to. You cannot ask a room how many doors a person has gone to and get anything out of it that means a person agreed to something.*”

“*Then what can you be asked about.*”

“*Whether the doors are being gone to,*” she said. “*That is it. That is the whole of what I am for, and I did not agree to it and nobody asked me, and I cannot say no, because saying no is doing the doors again. So if you want to know whether the people your rule moves agree, you cannot ask them, and you cannot ask me how many, and you cannot ask the book, because the book has got no line at all for anybody who goes out, and you cannot ask a ward, because a ward is not a person.*”

She looked at the end of the table where the four of them were sitting.

“*That is the whole of the exposure and it has taken me thirty-two days and it is arithmetic, and nobody in it has done anything to anybody, and the man with the bad ear is right that it does not go in a book, and the reason it does not go in a book is that a book is the room and the street and the ward are not the room, and the ward is not the street, and the three of them have never once in four hundred years been in the same place, and the only one of the three that has the beds in it is the one nobody goes and look at.*”

---

And then the clerk of thirty said the thing that made the next four days possible, and he said it in the flat voice and he did not enjoy it, and everybody in that room could tell the difference and nobody said so.

“*Then it goes up the hill,*” he said, “*on the tray, and it goes up because a return is the only form a bench of four has got and I write it and they do not, and what is on it is what was asked this morning. And a return goes back to where the thing came from, and the thing that came up at the start of this week was a form with a name on the top of it, so it goes back to a room at the foot of a hill and a man with a bad ear.*”

“*That is a room and not a person who can be asked to do anything,*” said Tarin Keel.

“*It is,*” said the clerk of thirty, “*and that is the end of the road and I have been in this room nine years and I know where every road in this city ends. There is one more thing and I am going to say it and then I am going to write the tray.*”

Nobody said anything.

“*A bench of four can be asked about a book, and the order is a book, and the order was written by a magistrate, and there is no form in this city that lets anybody ask a magistrate to do one single thing. A magistrate cannot be sent for. There is no runner and no tray and no line on any form in this building that will put a magistrate in a room at the foot of a hill. I have looked for nine years and that is not an opinion, it is a press.*”

He put his hand flat on the tray.

“*A magistrate can be asked. That is a different thing and it is an ordinary thing, and a person asks another person out loud in a room, and the person asked can come or not come, and nobody is sent and nothing is required. If a bench of four asks a magistrate to come to the room the order is on the wall of, and a magistrate comes, then that is not a form either. It is four men and four stools and a woman with a pen, and a question, out loud, in a room, with forty people in it.*”

“*Will she come.*”

“*I do not know and it is not my employment to know,*” said the clerk of thirty, “*and I am going to write the tray and send it up before the change, and there is no way of finding out whether she will come except waiting, and I have been waiting nine years and it is a thing you get good at and it is not a thing you get to like.*”

---

The tray went up the hill with a runner at the change and nobody sent for the runner, and the woman on the stool put the borrowed slate and the borrowed chalk on the end of the long table and thanked nobody, because there was nobody to thank, and the man of fifty-two went back down the row to a door at the end of it because he had been told to stop counting and had not stopped, and the fever was fifteen weeks and five days old and did not stop for arithmetic either, and the four bells went over the low end of Tallowgate, and a bed at the top of this city was free that had not been free for a fortnight, and the man with the bad ear was told about it at the change by a boy of sixteen who had heard it in a doorway, and he wrote it on a board and it did not come off.

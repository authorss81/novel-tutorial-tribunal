# Chapter 372: The Sixth Part Of An Order, And A Word In A Margin

It was his hundred and twenty-second morning and the third day of the seventy-fifth week, and he had been in this city twenty-one days, and there were four people at the door of the room behind the chandler's shop, and he went in and sat down opposite the woman at the table and asked her a question about a piece of paper on a wall at the foot of a hill that he had never had read to him in full.

“*Then we are going to do this the long way,*” said the woman with the book. “*I have four people at that door and none of them is going to stand at a door for an hour because a stranger is asking about a wall.*”

---

He said it plainly, because he had learned in three weeks that there is no arrangement in this city for saying a thing twice.

“*I know the order by its shape. I have been under it for twenty-one days and I know that it has five parts and a half and I know that the half is the part where it ends, and I know that the word in the margin says standing, and I cannot act on any of that, and I am not asking you to act on it. I am asking you what it says.*”

The woman with the book put her pen down, which is a thing she does before she answers a thing and does not do before she answers a thing she is going to refuse.

“*Say the question again and say it properly.*”

“*What does the sixth part say.*”

“*It says a date and it says standing,*” she said. “*That is all it says. There is a line in the order with a day in it, and beside the day there is a word in the margin in the same hand, and the word is standing, and the effect of the two of them together is that the day does not arrive.*”

---

And then she gave him the rest of it, and she gave it in the voice of a woman reading out a thing she has read a great many times, and he understood before she had got to the end of it that he had asked the right person, and before she had got to the second half of it that he was about to be told something he had not gone there for.

“*A covenant has six parts and this city has known that for longer than I have been alive,*” said the woman with the book. “*The first is who it is about. The second is when it applies. The third is what it costs. The fourth is who is to be asked about it. The fifth is what is to be done. And the sixth is where it stops.*”

“*You read that last week off a form.*”

“*I read it off a form in a book in a room over a lock-and-ledger shop near the courts seven days ago, and you said the first five of them out loud in this room before I ever saw the form, and I went and got the form, and the form says what you said and what I am saying now, and that is the whole of my authority and it is a form in a room and not a person.*”

She turned the chair round, which is what she does when she is going to do something with her hands.

“*So that paper on the wall at the foot of the hill has the first five of the six and the sixth one is a date that does not arrive and a word in the margin that says so. And that is not a rule that has no end. That is a rule that has an end in it and has been told not to come, and there is a difference between those two things and it is the whole of what I know about it and I have been in this city thirty-one years.*”

---

“*Say the difference.*”

And she said it, and it took her a moment to find it, and he understood that she had been carrying it for twelve weeks without knowing she was carrying it.

“*A refusal is a way of not being in it,*” she said. “*You are not in it. You never were. It is not yours and you do not have to get out of it. And an exit is a way out of a thing you are in, and you have to be in it first, and the way out has to be a door and the door has to be somewhere you can get to. Only one of those can be written by a magistrate in a week. The one that is written is the one where the rule says it stops, and that is a refusal in everything but the ink and there is not one of them in this city.*”

“*Nobody in this city has one.*”

“*Nobody in this city has one and nobody has ever asked for one in my hearing, and I have been in this room eleven years and I have read things for people in this city for thirty-one years,*” said the woman with the book, “*and when somebody asks me what a rule is for I have always been able to say the first five parts, and I have never once been able to say the sixth, and it has been sitting on a wall in this city for twelve weeks with a word in the margin and I have walked past it.*”

---

“*Why is that paper the only one in this city with a sixth part in it.*”

“*I do not know,*” she said. “*I have not looked. I do not look at that wall. I look at a book with names in it and I do not have any business with a piece of paper on a wall and you have just told me that I have been reading it in my head for twelve weeks without knowing I was reading it, so I will tell you the honest answer, which is that I do not know, and go and ask somebody whose job it is.*”

“*Whose job is it.*”

“*That is the whole of the problem in your city and you have been in it twenty-one days,*” said the woman with the book. “*There is no post in this city that has the care of a piece of paper on a wall. A rule is a thing that is read out to the people it is about, and a rule that is not read out is a thing that hangs on a wall, and I have never been given it to read out and neither has the woman at this door and neither has the man of twenty-eight at the end of the row.*”

---

He sat with that for a while, and then he asked the question he had come for, and he said it in the plainest words he had.

“*What happens to that half of it when the person who wrote it goes out of office.*”

The woman with the book did not answer for a long time, and the four people at the door did not come in, and the lamp was the only thing burning in the room.

Then she said: “*A rule that does not end is a permanent rule.*”

And then she stopped, and he understood that she had not planned to say it, and that she had said it because he had asked her a question and a person in this city answers a question when it is put to them, and that this was the first thing in eleven years she had said in this room that nobody had asked her for.

---

“*Say the rest of that.*”

“*There is no rest of it,*” she said. “*That is the whole of the sentence and I have not said it to anybody. Say the date in that line is never reached, and the rule has no end, and a rule with no end is not a law, it is a thing one person wrote in a week because she was right, and the whole of what is holding it in force is that she is still in the room. If she goes out of office that line comes alive on a morning and everybody in four wards finds out at the same time, and there is nothing in between and that is not a thing anybody has ever had to think about because nobody has ever written one.*”

“*You have thought about it.*”

“*I have thought about it since the morning you asked me what the round of this ward costs,*” said the woman with the book, “*because that is the same sum, and a round is four hundred and twenty days and my year is forty-eight pence, and a thing that has no end is a thing nobody is holding. That is all. That is a book-keeper's arithmetic and I have not said it to anybody and there is no form in this city that lets a person unsay a thing in her own room.*”

She looked at the door and there were four people at it and she did not open it.

“*You have said a price out loud in this city four times, and every time you said it you said it to a room and not to me,*” she said. “*And now I have said a thing about a wall to a man with a leg in my own room, and I cannot take it back, and you are not going to tell anybody I said it, and if anybody comes to this door in the next year and says a magistrate's clerk says a magistrate's clerk says that the woman in this room has an opinion about the order, then that is on me, and it was on me the moment I opened my mouth, and you did not do it. I did it, because I answered a question, which is what people do.*”

“*I am not going to tell anybody.*”

“*I know you are not,*” she said. “*Go and open the door, because there are four people at it and one of them has been there since the second hour.*”

---

He was in the row at about the sixth hour and the woman of twenty-eight was at the end of it with a string and a slate and a paper of chalk, and the chalk was new and it was in a twist of paper with a string round it, and she was making a mark on a door with it, and he stood and watched her do two before he spoke, which is a thing he has learned to do in this city.

“*That is chalk.*”

“*That is chalk,*” she said. “*And I have not asked you about it and I am not going to, and I have told you once at the end of this row two days ago that the next nine days of chalk is a penny and that nobody in this city has it as a job to buy it, and I am not going to say that again.*”

He did not ask her who had bought it. He had made up his mind about that on the way down the row, and the reason he had made it up is that there are four people in this city who could have done it and three of them would have wanted something.

“*Who bought it.*”

“*A woman at the standpipe,*” said the woman of twenty-eight, “*and I am going to tell you one thing about her and then I am going to knock on this door, because I have a house at the end of this row and there are two people in it who are not in the book and I am not going to be late for them on account of a conversation.*”

---

“*Say it.*”

“*She is sixty and she has lived in that street fifty-eight years and she holds four names she has never said out loud to anybody, and she is the only person in this ward who has ever said a true thing to me that I did not ask her for, and she gave me the chalk at the standpipe at the first hour and asked me for nothing.*”

“*That is a good thing that happened to you.*”

“*That is a thing that has happened to me and I have been standing at this door for four minutes trying to work out what it is and I have got it and I am going to say it and then I am going to knock,*” said the woman of twenty-eight. “*In a ward where a thing costs money, a thing that costs nothing is a favour, and a favour is a line in a list, and the list is not written down anywhere, and the line is in it. She has made me a woman who is owed a thing by a person. She has not asked me for anything and she is not going to, and she is going to be at that standpipe at the first hour of every morning until the day one of us is not there, and I am not going to be able to say no to the second one. That is a rota with one line in it and the line is not mine and I did not draw it, and I have told you that I would not be asked for it and I have been asked for it by a woman who is being kind, and I would like to know which of the two things this city is.*”

“*Both.*”

“*Both,*” she said, “*and I have nineteen years of the both of it and I am going to knock on this door now.*”

And he stood in the cold at the end of a row in a low ward and did not say a price out loud, and he noticed that he had not, and he could not tell whether that was discipline or cowardice, and there was nobody in four hundred yards to ask.

The four bells went. In a room at the back of a chandler's shop a woman of forty-four had told a stranger a thing about a wall that she has not said to anybody in eleven years, and in a low ward a woman of twenty-eight had a paper of chalk she had not asked for, and a boy of eight at the end of a run of boards in a hall at the end of this ward was watching a pail, and nobody in any of those three places had been asked anything at all.

# Chapter 370: What Came Out Of It, And What Did Not

It was his hundred and twentieth morning and the first day of the seventy-fifth week, and the fourteenth had been the day before, and he was in a room over a lock-and-ledger shop near the courts at about the third hour because there are two things a man can do in a city with a day after a hearing and he had done the other one already.

He had a sheet in his coat with four clauses and a half on it. He had not filed it. He was not going to, and there was a reason and the reason was a woman of sixty with a bundle who had walked out of a room.

He had come to say one fact and he said it standing up, because she was working and had four documents open and a lamp on and had been at it since before the light.

“*Nine lines went into a book on your second floor yesterday,*” he said, “*and the word the map uses is in it, and a bench of four wrote down that it cannot answer the question on the slate, and it is entered and kept, and that is all of it.*”

“*And the question.*”

“*Not answered. Not asked again. Entered.*”

“*Right,*” said Sera Quill, and turned back to her table, and then turned round again, which she does perhaps twice in a year. “*That is the fourth time since you came into this city that a document has been received and put in a book and nobody has read it, and I have been in this room for all four of them, and one of them was yours, and I am not going to say anything about the other three because I do not know whose they are and I have not asked and you should not tell me.*”

He put the fact down on her table and she gave him nothing back except the fact that she had noticed, and he did not stay, and there was nothing else said in that room that morning, and neither of them mentioned bread.

---

Here is what came out of the fourteenth, and it is a short list, and the whole of it is that the list is shorter than the day was.

Four things, and he can name the four without looking at anything.

A document received by the city of Orison from a county four hundred miles off, saying that a thing is under this city and is not found, and a bench of four entering and keeping the fact that it has no power to open anything and no form to be asked to open anything. The question on the slate was not answered and was not asked again. And a stranger's own sentence about a document he did not make, on a sheet that has his name on it and no form that takes it off, with the price of being on that sheet said out loud in the room before he answered it, and the clerk wrote the price down too, which is the part of it that is in the book.

The word *anchor*, in the middle of a sentence, read aloud in the ordinary way by a clerk of thirty, meaning nine things and explained by nobody, which is the only one of the nine answers that anybody in that building now has.

A sentence from a man of fifty-nine about a line of cut glass, taken down in a fair hand and closed with the words a bench says at the end of a thing, which he declined.

And a woman of forty-four who was asked eleven questions on a sheet and then some more than were on the sheet, over a whole day, and answered all of them, and two of them were about her, and the answer to one of the two is in a book in a city of ninety thousand people.

And nothing else came out of it, and no other thing in this city is going to come out of it, and there is no office that would be told.

---

Here is what did not come out of it, and this is the longer list, and nobody is going to do anything about any of it.

Three hundred names across four wards are still outside a protection that a magistrate put there on purpose. Two hundred names in Tallowgate are still on a paper and not on a plate, and the plate is four shillings the hundred, and there is no money in that ward and has not been for two years. The block in the gatepost still says what it said two years ago, and no meeting has been called, and no form calls one, and there has not been a meeting of Tallowgate since the autumn before the water came.

There is no discharge paper in this city and no office that issues one, and a woman of sixty-one in a hall at Weir Lane wants to go home and has asked for a sheet of paper instead of a voice and nobody has brought her one.

A man of forty-four at the top of nine steps in Weaver's Row is not on the roll and there is no paper in this city that would put him in one, and his light is better than it was.

There is a name in the roll of Tallowgate belonging to a man who died in the fourth week of the fever, and the name is correct, and nobody has said so, and nobody can say so, because the only person in this city who could be asked to decide that a thing in a book is not true has no duty to decide it and no form to be asked by.

A boy of eleven is the head of a house with three names in it and there are six people in the house, and three names were said out loud in a street to a stranger at the second hour, and there is nowhere to put them, and nobody is going to put them anywhere, and that is the correct decision and it is not a good one.

The order still has five parts and a half, and the sixth line is a line with a date in it, and the word in the margin is still standing, and it is an exit and not a refusal, and nobody in this city has one of either.

About a hundred and forty people are dead in four wards in four columns that have not been added up and cannot be, and they stay dead, and there is no figure anybody in this city has got that makes that smaller.

And a man of fifty-two said in a doorway on the second day of the seventy-fourth week that he would go up the hill and say a thing to a magistrate in ninety seconds and that she would write a notice. It is six days since. The notice is not written. The man is at a rail outside the black building counting what comes through the door. The stranger is in a low ward holding doors open. Neither of them has mentioned it, and that is the whole of the disagreement between them, and neither of them is going to win it, and both of them are right.

---

And here is the count, and it is the only count in it, and it came out of a slate in a hand that is not his.

He was at the low end of Tallowgate at about the sixth hour and the woman of twenty-eight was at the end of the row with a string and a slate and no chalk.

“*You have run out.*”

“*I have run out,*” she said. “*The last of it went on three days ago and I did not find out until there was nothing left to make a mark with. It is nine days of chalk and a day is three doors, and I have done nine days of it, and there are twenty-seven marks on this slate, and I have been going since the day you found me at the end of a row with a bundle of string, and that is ten days, and three of them were today and yesterday and the day before, with nothing to mark with.*”

“*Twenty-seven doors.*”

“*Twenty-seven doors out of nine hundred,*” she said. “*And I want to say the rest of it out loud because you are the only person in this city that has ever asked me a question about the work and I am going to say it once. Three a day is faster than the book. The woman with the book does thirty doors in a fortnight and a round is a year and fifty-six days and I am doing three a day and I have got a fifth of a ward in a fortnight and a penny's worth of chalk.*”

“*A fifth.*”

“*I am going to correct myself and then I am going to stop,*” said the woman of twenty-eight, “*at the first hour I would have told you that was a fifth of a ward. It is not a fifth of anything. I have tried it four ways this morning and I do not get a figure I am willing to say out loud, and the only measure anybody in this ward has got is the round, and the round is the woman with the book’s, and she is not mine to quote at a door.*”

“*How much of the round.*”

“*The round is thirty doors a fortnight,*” she said, “*and the round is four hundred and twenty days, and I have done twenty-seven doors, and at thirty doors in a fortnight twenty-seven doors is twelve days and three-fifths of a day of that round, and twelve days and three-fifths of a day out of four hundred and twenty is not a number anybody can do anything with, and I have been going ten days, and the rest of it is not mine and is not anybody’s.*”

“*And the chalk.*”

“*The next nine days of chalk is a penny, and there is nobody in this city whose job it is to buy it, and I am not going to be asked for it, and so either somebody buys it off me at a standpipe without making a list or I stop, and those are the same thing as a rota nobody has written down, and I have told you that and I am not going to tell you again.*”

She put the slate against her hip.

“*You are going to ask me who buys it.*”

“*I am not going to ask you anything.*”

“*Good,*” said the woman of twenty-eight. “*Then there is one thing left to say and it is not about the chalk. There is a child in the low hall at the end of this ward who is worse than he was yesterday, and he is not worse than the fever, he is worse than the other nine in that hall, and the woman on the stool says so out loud to anybody who stands there, and his mother is at the standpipe and she is on the roll and she says his name at a door, and his line is on the board at the end of the beds and it is in chalk and it will be in chalk when the order ends because there is no office that issues a discharge, and the number on the board went up by one this morning and it has not come down by one since the eighth morning of the fever.*”

She stopped, and she waited, and he did not say anything, because there was nothing in him that was any use in a room or out of one.

“*You have not said his name,*” she said.

“*I have not said his name.*”

“*Right,*” she said, and took the slate, and went down the row, and there was no chalk in her pocket and there were nine hundred doors in that ward and four hundred and twenty days in the round and a child at the end of it who was worse than the other nine, and the four bells went, and the fever did not stop, and the door was shut.

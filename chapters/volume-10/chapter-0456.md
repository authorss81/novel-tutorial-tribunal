# Chapter 456: The Board Comes Down Off The Beam Onto A Table In Four Minutes With A Fire Lit And Nine Men In The Room And Four People From The Fenrow In The Doorway Because They Are Not Of The Town, And A Woman Of Thirty-Four Turns The Board Over Because Nobody Told Her Not To, And On The Back Of It In A Fifth Hand Is A Date A Hundred And Thirty-Eight Years Old

Fourth day of the eighty-seventh week. His two hundred and sixth morning. Fifty-six days after the settlement. The fever twenty-four weeks and five days. Ten days since the division.

The Book came down off the beam at about the first hour, and it took four minutes, and two people did it, and neither of them was asked whether they wanted to.

The steward took the two top nails and the clerk took the two bottom ones because the bottom ones are older and the head of that board is four hundred years old and the feet of it are about ninety. The lead tag swung on the vellum the whole way down and it made a sound nobody in that room had heard before, because nobody in that room had ever heard it before.

“*Say how long it was up,*” said Wenna Crale, to nobody, and then answered herself, which she has done twice in nine years. “*Since the last audit, and that is ten days.*”

The board was on the long table and the fire was lit and the room had nine men of the water board in it, and the steward, and the clerk, and four people from the Fenrow in the doorway, and about four hundred people in the square outside who had come because it was a Thursday.

The four people in the doorway were the only ones there who could not be charged for the water.

---

Clause four is the water clause and Wenna Crale read it out standing at the end of the table with her hand on the board, in the voice she uses for the minutes, and it took about nine seconds and nobody interrupted her.

“*Four. The water of the Hallow from the sluice downward is common to all households of this town, and the water board is set up to keep it, and no household is to be put out of the water for any cause but the causes set up in this Book, and the causes set up in this Book are set out at clause five, and there are four of them and no person has caused one of them in living memory.*”

“*Say the rest of it and do not stop in the middle of a sentence,*” said the chair of the water board, who was forty-seven and whose name was Perdy Sallow and who had not been in the room with Ilyan for longer than about nine minutes.

“*That is all of clause four. And the objection of the nineteen houses at the Fen, which was carried up to this door on the Sunday and is a yard long, asks the House to say that a notice that the water is to be scoured is a notice that takes the water off the households it is common to. And the objection is right in a form. And the objection does not say the frame is sound, and the frame is not sound, and the man who says it is not sound is here and he is a man of the county.*”

“*Say the four causes at clause five,*” said Sallow. “*In order, and say whether anybody in this room knows one.*”

“*One, a house that is not a dwelling. Two, water standing above the sill of a house for eleven days. Three, a household that has not kept its own part of the race. Four, the water board, by order, for a cause set out in writing.*”

Nobody in the room knew of one.

“*Then say the whole of the business,*” said Perdy Sallow, “*and get it down. We have a fire lit, a board on a table, and the fenrow in the doorway, and this is the second time in nine years the Book has been in this room, and it will not be here in an hour.*”

---

The vote was six to three and it took about eleven minutes, and the three against it were not wrong, and their names went in the minute.

“*Say the finding and the cost and do not leave the cost out,*” said Sallow.

“*That the race from the sluice to the tail of the mill is scoured, in nine days beginning on Friday. That the cost of fourteen pounds is leviable on the eighty lots upon the Four Hundred at three and a halfpence a lot, charged in the roll of this year and not in next. And that no household is charged anything for the water below the sluice during the nine days.*”

“*Say that last part again and do not stop in the middle of it.*”

“*No household is charged anything for the water below the sluice during the nine days. So the nineteen houses get nothing, because a thing that costs nothing is not money. Compensation is at clause five, cause four, and cause four is the water board by order. The board has just ordered it, without a cause set out in writing by a household.*”

“*Say the three against it and let them say it themselves.*”

---

The three said it themselves, and the second of them was a man of fifty-four who had a lot on the Four Hundred and had paid two pounds for it in the week of the division, and his name is in the minute.

“*I am against,*” he said, “*because three and a halfpence a lot is three and a halfpence a lot. The frame is the board’s frame and the frame is for the town. The fenrow paid nothing into the common and are getting the frame like everybody else, and I do not say a word against them, and I have been in that row every week of my life.*”

“*Say the half of that which is the other half,*” said Sefa Lund from the doorway.

“*The other half is that if the fenrow had bought a lot they would be paying it. They did not buy a lot because the ground they are on was not being sold, it was being flooded. And I have just been told that the ground they are on is not being sold because it will not be sold, and I would like somebody in this room to tell me how that came about, and I have asked the steward and he says it came about because of the water.*”

“*Say the rest of that and do not stop in the middle of a sentence,*” said Amos Trill from the end of the table, and he said it slowly.

“*It came about because of the water. The Fen was not divided and it was not sold because a man would not buy nine feet of standing water in the wet season, and that was the sale, and the sale was legal, and the sale was right, and I made the arguments for it in front of a commission and I would make them again.*”

Nobody in that room said anything at all for a while.

“*Say the thing that is underneath that,*” said Sefa Lund, “*because I have been up that row for nine years and I have known every one of those things, and I have never once heard the two of them said one after the other.*”

“*Say it, then,*” said Ilyan, from the wall, where he had been for the whole of it.

“*The Book is four hundred years old,*” said Amos Trill, “*and clause one is the common and clause four is the water, and the whole of this town’s argument for nine weeks has been about two clauses, and the Book has been nailed nine feet up over that fire since before anybody in this room had a grandmother, and it has not been read out in a room in ninety years, and the only reason it has not been read out in a room in ninety years is that the audit is an audit of a common and there has been no common since the division.*”

---

It was the seventh time, and the first time outside that city, and about a minute after Sefa Lund had said the thing about the two clauses.

**CLAIM: A clause in a book that has never been read out in a room is not a rule. It is a piece of writing, and the difference between the two is not in the book. It is decided by whoever gets a table, and a fire, and nine men, and says it out loud.**

**EVIDENCE: The face of the board is four hundred years old in four hands and the whole of the front is about a common. The back is in a fifth hand, in a different ink, and is dated a hundred and thirty-eight years ago. The Book is opened twice a year in a hurry by two people and has not been opened in ten days. Nobody in this town has read the back.**

**BURDEN: One woman of thirty-four in this room is the only person who can read it, and she is the copyist, and she is paid two pounds a year, and the burden of the reading falls on her and on nobody else, and there is no line anywhere in four hundred years of this town that says the reading may be done by somebody else.**

**CHOICE: Read the ninth clause into this room, or put the board back up the beam and let it come down at the next sitting.**

It did not say what would happen. It did not say that it was true, and it did not say that it was the right of anybody to anything.

He chose nothing out of it, then or afterwards, and he has never chosen anything out of one, and he has never told anybody that he had been asked and had not answered.

And then a woman of thirty-four, who had been reading the front of it for twenty minutes while nine men argued above her, turned it over without asking anybody, because nobody had told her not to. The back went over the front with a sound like a shutter.

---

“*Say the whole of it out loud,*” said Sefa Lund from the doorway, “*and read it in your own hand’s order and not in the order of the clauses, and take as long as you like, because the fire is lit and there are nine men here and none of us is going anywhere.*”

It took her about two minutes and it is four sentences long and about a hundred and ten words, and it is the oldest thing anybody in that room had ever heard, and it is a hundred and thirty-eight years old.

“*Nine. No household of this town shall be required to take water, or to be entered, or to answer, against its will.*”

Perdy Sallow said, in about the fourth second of the silence after it: “*Say the date again.*”

“*The nineteenth day of the second week of the spring of the year one hundred and thirty-eight. And it is signed with a name and there is a witness line under the name and there are two names in the witness line and one of them is a name of this town.*”

“*Say the other one.*”

“*I cannot,*” said Wenna Crale. “*I can read the hand. That one is not this town’s hand and it is not the old hand of the Book and it is a small hand and it is written by somebody who was not taught this town’s letters. It is four letters and a stroke and I do not know it.*”

---

And then Ilyan did the thing that is going to cost him for as long as he is in this town, and he did it because it was true, and he did it without stopping, and about sixteen people heard it.

“*Say the rest of what I am about to say out loud and go on to the end of it,*” said Wenna Crale, and she did not know what he was about to say, and she asked for it anyway, because that is what you do in a room.

“*That clause is a right of refusal. That is what it says. That no household of this town can be required to take water, or to be entered, or to answer, against its will, and that is the shape of it in about six words and it has been that shape for a hundred and thirty-eight years.*”

He stopped there, which was correct, and then he did not stop there.

“*And the right of refusal was taken out of the law of that country about a hundred and forty years ago, and it has not been put back. I know that because I was in a room forty-one miles off on the seventh day of the eighty-fifth week, where a man of sixty-one said it out loud, and there is a book at the seat with the date in it, and I was in the room.*”

Nobody in that room said anything.

Then Mab Harrow, who had come into the room about four minutes earlier and had been standing by the fire, asked the second question of her week.

“*Say how you know,*” she said. “*Say how you know, and say what is in this town that backs it, and say it in this room and not to him.*”

“*Say the half of that which is the other half,*” said Ilyan.

“*The half of it is that he knows it and that it is a true thing,*” said Mab Harrow, “*and the other half of it is that there are sixteen people in this room and not one of them can check it. There is a date on a board that says one hundred and thirty-eight. The two dates are a hundred and forty and one hundred and thirty-eight, and a stranger has told us the gap is two years, and we are forty-one miles from the room where the other half of it is true.*”

“*Then I have put a figure in this room that is not on anything,*” Ilyan said, “*and I did it without stopping, and I would like it said out loud that I did it without stopping and not after asking anybody.*”

Amos Trill stood up from the end of the table. He was sixty-one and he had been the steward of that town for forty years and he did not say anything for about twenty seconds, and the fire moved.

“*Say the rest of that and do not stop in the middle of a sentence,*” said Sallow.

“*I have been a steward for forty years and I have been right about the common for forty years, and I was right in the week I sold the common, and I would be right again. And I have never once in forty years had to know a thing about a law, because a steward of this town is a man who keeps what is here, and what is here is a common and a water and a rate.*”

He put his hand flat on the table beside the board.

“*And there is a sentence in this room tonight that nobody in this town can check, and the sentence is that somebody in this country took a thing out of the law about a hundred and forty years ago. And I am not able to do anything about that sentence, and I am not going to pretend I do, and I am not going to let it sit in my town being a fact because a stranger said it.*”

“*Say what you are going to do about it.*”

“*I am going to enter the ninth clause in the minute of this sitting, in the words on the board, with the date on the board. And I am going to enter the stranger’s sentence in the same minute, under the words *said by a person not of this town, unverified*, and I am going to enter that this House has not the means to verify it and has not been asked to.*”

“*Say the half of that which is the other half,*” said Sefa Lund.

“*The other half is that whoever wrote the ninth clause, somebody in this country wrote it a hundred and thirty-eight years ago, which is a hundred and thirty-eight years after the Book was made, and two years after your stranger’s law. And that is the whole of what I have. It is not a right anybody in this room has. It is two years somebody in a town nobody here has heard of had something they wanted to write down.*”

The fever was twenty-four weeks and five days old. A sentence a hundred and thirty-eight years old was in the minute of a water sitting, and a date a hundred and forty years old was in the same minute under the word *unverified*.

The board went back up the beam at about the third hour with two old nails and a lead tag that swung. The Book was the same book it had been on Monday. And four people from the Fenrow went back down to the low ground in the rain, knowing something about their own water that nobody in that town had known for a hundred and thirty-eight years.

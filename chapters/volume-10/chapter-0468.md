# Chapter 468: A Letter Goes Four Hundred Miles With A Witness Line In It And A Woman Of Twenty-Eight Reads It And Does Not Answer It And Writes Down That She Has Not Answered It, And Writes Back In A Hand He Asked Her Not To Write In, And Says He Is Half Right And Is Right About The Other Half Of It

First day of the eighty-ninth week. His two hundred and eighteenth morning. Sixty-eight days after the settlement. The fever twenty-six weeks and three days. Twenty-two days since the division. Sixteen days in this town.

He had it out of the inside pocket of his coat at about the sixth hour and put it on the table, and it was not folded any more than it had been folded eight days before, because a thing that has been in a pocket for eight days has been taken out and put back four times and not once refolded.

The tea was cold and bitter. The return was on the table with its face down, where it had been since the Saturday before last, and it was thirty days old, and a woman had stood in a room over a nail shop and told him to send it.

He read the letter from the top, which he had not done since he wrote it, and got to the middle of it and stopped.

---

There were two paragraphs in the middle of the letter that were not about the ninth clause, and he had not shown them to anybody, and there was nobody here to show them to.

The first of them asked her to write her answer in a particular hand.

*Write it in the hand you make your fair copies in, the small upright one, and not in any other, and I will tell you why, and the why is the whole of what I have learned in sixteen days.*

*A hand is a thing a person can be asked about. You have taught me that and I do not think you were wrong, and a thing a person can be asked about is the only kind of thing there is.*

*If this is in the small upright hand then anything you say about a witness line is a thing a clerk in your office can be taken to a room about. If it is in any other hand then it is a letter from a person, and a letter from a person is a thing that a person is asked about.*

The second of the two was the one he should not have written. He had written it in about forty seconds on the Monday of a week he could now count, and he had crossed it out, and then he had written it again underneath the line.

*I ought to tell you what I am, since you are going to be asked to look at a witness line on my account.*

*I have signed a return in respect of a defect in a town of ninety thousand people. I have not sent the return. There is a figure in the fourth line of the margin of it, and the figure is the only one of its kind in ninety thousand people, and the office that asked for it does not have it.*

*I am telling you the figure because it is the only thing about me that is a fact. I am not telling you the office, because if you have the figure then you have the office, and that office keeps a list of the persons who have not sent one, and your name would not be on that list and mine would be. I would rather you had that from me than worked it out in a building of four hundred people.*

He put the heel of his hand flat on the table, which is the thing he does, and it did not help, and he had noticed that in about the fifty-second week.

“*Say it out loud and do not stop in the middle of it,*” said nobody, because there was nobody in the room.

That was the first thing he had noticed about the town in sixteen days and he had not written it down. In four hundred yards of lane and in a stone room and in a shop with the rain coming under the door, people said that to one another, ten times a day, to a man who had not asked to be asked. In a room over a nail shop there was nobody to say it to, and the tea was cold.

---

Do not move a card, and do not print a rail: the road out of Halloway has a milestone on one side of it and nothing on the other, and the carrier was a man of about fifty-three who had driven it for thirty-one years and had a bad chest in the wet.

“*It goes to the counter at Ostrey,*” said Doff Anstey. “*And from the counter it goes on, and I have never carried it the second part and I could not tell you what happens to it, and I would not, because you would only write it down.*”

“*Say what the counter does with it.*”

“*It puts a day on it. And then a man with a head on a book writes the day and where it came from, and after that it is a thing a person can be asked about, and you will not be able to take it back out and neither will I.*”

“*Say the rest of it and do not stop in the middle of it.*”

“*I have been a carrier thirty-one years and I have never in thirty-one years sent a letter out of this town that anybody came back for. I am not saying that to frighten you. I am saying it because you have been carrying that paper in a coat for eight days and it is the only object in this country that is yours, and after today it is a line in a book four hundred miles off with your county at the top of it.*”

Nobody in that lane said anything.

Then Ilyan said the sentence he had been building since the sixth hour, and it was the right sentence, and it made everything about that morning worse.

“*Then that is the correct way to send it and I have been holding it for the wrong reason for eight days.*”

“*Then what was the reason.*”

“*That I liked having it.*”

---

So it went at about the ninth hour.

The thing that went up the road was a sheet with the ninth clause on it in the words on the board, and two dates a hundred and forty years apart, and a witness line with two names in it. One of those is a name of a town of four thousand people. The other is four letters and a stroke that nobody in that town can read, including the only person there who can read the Book, and including him.

And it went with a paragraph in the middle of it that named a register and gave a number.

There was no version of that letter without the witness line. He had gone over it three times on the Monday of the week before last and again at the fourth hour that morning, and the arithmetic of it was four letters and a stroke and one person in a county who might be able to say what they were.

The whole of what he had built in sixteen days came down to whether that one person existed.

He had wanted to cut the paragraph about the register. He had it on the table with the pen uncapped at about the seventh hour and he wrote one line under it and read the whole thing over, and the line he wrote was that the register was kept at an office that did not keep refusals, which was true, and which is the kind of sentence a man writes when he has already decided.

---

Four hundred miles off, on a day that is not this town’s day, a woman of twenty-eight read a letter in a room with a desk in it and a press against the wall and about nine hundred books in the press, of which she was the second in command and the only one who wrote.

Sera Quill kept a day book. It was a quarto book with a printed head on the first leaf. It was the only book in that office in which anything was written that the office had not asked for, and it had been kept for six years and a half.

She read it twice on the day it came and once on the day after, and she did not go to the press on either day.

Not because of the letter. Because of the shelf.

The register office keeps its refusals and its returns in one press, on one shelf, in two ranges, and the ranges are eleven inches apart.

The clerk who works that press is a woman of fifty-eight who has worked it for twenty-six years. She does not read the days that go past her. She writes down the days that go past her in a book, because that is what clerks are for.

To find out what four letters and a stroke are, a person has to open the range that holds the refusals. A refusal is entered in that book by the name of the person who witnessed it, and the name is a name, and a name in that range is a name a clerk will see has been looked at.

Sera Quill’s own name is not in that range and has never been, and there was no reason on earth why it should begin.

She wrote in the day book, in the small upright hand, because that is the only hand she has for the day book.

*Letter from I. Vester, received this day. Not answered. He asks one question and I have not answered it and I am not going to.*

*I am entering that here because I have noticed in six years that the questions I do not answer are the ones I remember.*

And then she turned to a clean leaf and wrote the answer, and she wrote it in the round hand of the counters, which is the hand of about eleven clerks in a building of four hundred people, and which Ilyan Vester had never seen and had asked her in a letter not to use.

---

*You are half right and you are right about the half that is easy.*

*You are right that a hand is a thing a person can be asked about. You are right that the small upright hand is the only hand in that building a clerk can be taken to a room about. You are right about a witness line: a name in a witness line is a thing a person can be asked about, and if four letters and a stroke are a person then they are a person who can be asked about.*

*That is the whole of the case for opening the book. I have read your letter three times and I agree with all of it.*

*And I am not going to open the range, and you did not ask me to, which is the part of the letter I would have gone on about if you had.*

*Here is the other half. I have been a recordwright for six years and a half and I have read about nine hundred exits, and every one of them is a form. An exit is a form, and a form has a day on it, and a form has to be in before another day, and the days between the two are the days in which a person who needed to leave was still there.*

*I have never once in six years read an exit and thought: that is a right. I have thought, every time, in a slightly different voice: that is a privilege, and a good one, and better than nothing, and it belongs to whoever could reach the counter before the day.*

*Your ninth clause is a hundred and thirty-eight years old and it says no household of this town shall be required to take water, or to be entered, or to answer, against its will.*

*You had been in that town sixteen days when you wrote to me. In sixteen days you watched nineteen houses get onto a roll that had them on it as land, and I want you to write down how.*

*A steward opened a drawer in his own office that he had had for forty years. A stranger had asked him about that drawer in a market square twelve days before and he had not opened it. Nobody in that row asked. A man of sixty-one asked on their behalf, in a room, having done a sum in a lane.*

*And the roll was corrected in a morning, in four minutes, by nine words. That is the whole of the machinery.*

*There is no form in that county that says a household may enter itself. There is no form that says a woman may leave. There is no form that says a person who cannot be asked about anything may be entered on anything. Every one of the three things that happened in that row in sixteen days happened because somebody with a form in his hand decided that the form could be used, and the people in that row who would have got there on their own are the people who are not in a position to ask, and there is no form in that county that a person in their position can fill in.*

*A right that you have to knock on nineteen doors for is not a right. It is a favour, and the favour is made out of the character of the person who asks, and every one of the four of you is a person I would trust, and that is not the argument.*

*The argument is this. In four hundred years nobody had to knock on anything, because the clause was there, and it did nothing, because the word in it was *household*, and the word in it shut the door, and the door was not shut by a person. A door that shuts itself is not the decision of anybody and can be argued with for a hundred and thirty-nine years.*

*A door that opens when a man of forty-seven is frightened of his own minute is a decision. And in about four years somebody in that town will make a different one, and the clause will be a hundred and thirty-eight years old, and it will have been in and out of that door twice.*

*You asked me for a favour and I am not going to do it, and I want the reason in the same letter. It is not the range. It is the shelf.*

*The range is eleven inches from where I sat. The clerk who works the press is a woman of fifty-eight who has worked it for twenty-six years. And a person who is entered in that range, and who is also entered in a register of defects, and who has not sent his return, is a person whose name would be in two books in one building, and the second book is the only copy of the figure in it and the first book is the only copy of him.*

*I have been entered in a book in this building four times in six years and I have not asked for one of them. I know exactly what the fifth would cost and it would not be money.*

*And I will tell you the thing about your paragraph in the middle, because you wrote it twice and you crossed it out once, and you were right to write it and I would have been frightened if you had not.*

*You told me a figure. You did not tell me the office that wants it, and I have not worked it out, and I am not going to, and you did not ask me to, and that is the first thing in six weeks of your letters that you have not asked me for.*

*I am writing this in the round hand of the counters because you asked me not to. A hand is a thing a person can be asked about. So is the absence of one.*

*If this is in the small upright hand it is a letter from a woman to a man, and it can be used, and it can be used against either of us, and there is no form in that building that says what may not be used. If it is in this hand then it is the office that has said it, and an office can be cited by a clerk, and a clerk can be cited by a person, and I would rather be cited than be used.*

*You are half right. I have been right about this for six years and you had it for sixteen days, and you have been in the room with it since, and I do not think you have come out of the room.*

*Do not send the return to me. Send it to a counter with a head on a book. You have been told that by a better person than I am.*

*And I have not answered the question. And I have written down above that I have not answered it. And I am not going to tell you which of the two halves of that I am sorry about.*

She signed it with the round hand and did not sign it with her own name, and folded it, and put it in the bag, and the bag went out of that building, which is to say that it went. Nobody watched it go and nobody could have stopped it.

She did not write a second letter. She wrote in the day book again on the day after, and the four lines were that the letter had gone, and that a question had been asked and not answered, and that the person who had asked it had been right about the range and wrong about every other part of it, and that the day book was not going anywhere.

The fever was twenty-six weeks and three days old in a town of four thousand people on a Tuesday, and the inside pocket of a coat over a nail shop was empty of the letter and full of a return that was thirty days old, and a woman had told him nine days before to send the second thing to a book that has a head on it, and he had not done it, and the reason he had not done it was the reason he had not sent the first.

Four hundred miles off, a woman put a sheet into a bag in a round hand and would not put her own name to it. Eleven inches from where she sat was a range of a book she had not opened, with about nine hundred refusals entered in it, and one of those nine hundred may be the four letters and a stroke, and she is not going to find out, and she has written down that she is not going to find out.

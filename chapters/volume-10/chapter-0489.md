# Chapter 489: The First Day Of The Ninety-Second Week, And A Tuesday On Which A Copyist Of Thirty-Four Comes To A Room Over A Nail Shop And Asks For The Other Three Pages Of A Letter That Is Addressed To A Water Board And Not To A Man, And They Are About A Hundred And Eighty Forms And A Shelf Eleven Inches Wide, And The First Disagreement Of This Week Is Not A Disagreement And The Second One Is, And Four Hundred Miles Off A Woman Of Twenty-Eight Has Written In The Round Hand Of The Counters That Neither Of Them Can Build The Thing The Other One Needs, And She Has Not Signed It With Any Name At All

First day of the ninety-second week, and the first day of it. His two hundred and thirty-ninth morning. Eighty-nine days after the settlement. The fever twenty-nine weeks and three days. Forty-three days since the division. Thirty-seven days in this town.

The room over a nail shop at the south end of the market square has a bed, a table, a chair and a fire that is lit in the cold part of the year.

There were about two people in it on the Tuesday morning of the forty-third day after the division, and one of the two was a copyist of thirty-four. She had walked down from a stone room at the other end of that square, because there is one thing in this town that she cannot do without being told about by somebody, and it is not on a press.

The four pages came into Halloway on the Tuesday of the twenty-ninth day after the division in a bag off a carrier who has driven that road for thirty-one years. He read them on the Wednesday night and read out four lines of them in a market square on the Thursday of the thirty-first, and about thirty people heard those four lines, and the other three pages had been folded in the inside of a coat for thirteen days.

“*Say what you have come down here for and say why it is in this room and not in the Stone House,*” said Ilyan Vester.

“*Because the Stone House has a press in it, and anything I say in the Stone House is a thing anybody in this town may read. I have come down here to ask you a question and not to have it entered, and that is the first time in eleven years I have come out of that building to ask a question.*”

“*Go on and say the rest of it.*”

“*And I would like the reason for it in this room and not afterwards in a square.*”

“*Say the reason and say it in your own mouth.*”

“*Because I copied a sheet of eleven lines into a press on the twenty-fifth day after the division, and I have entered eleven things since that day that no body of this town asked me to enter, and about nine people in this town have said so to me in a square.*”

“*Go on and say the rest of it and do not make it a speech.*”

“*I have never once had a document handed to me by a person who did not make it. A thing brought to me by a body I have been doing for eleven years, and a thing brought to me by a person is a different trade, and I have not been trained in it, and I am the only person in this county who could be.*”
---

And he read out the other three pages, and it took about nine minutes, and a copyist of thirty-four stood at the end of a table and listened to a man of thirty-one read a stranger’s argument about doors in a room with a fire in it.

The first of the three pages was about forms.

“*Say it in the order she wrote it and do not put your own things in it,*” said Wenna Crale.

“*A hundred and eighty forms in six years and a half. Nine buildings. Every exit she has read in that time is a form, and a form is a door, and a door is opened by whoever is nearest the hinge, and the hinge in a hundred and eighty forms is always the same person and that person has never once been the person the form is about.*”

“*Go on and say the rest of that and do not make it a speech.*”

“*And she has looked for six years and a half, every working day, in a building of four hundred people and in eight others, for a thing a person can open without asking anybody’s character, and it is not in any of the nine.*”

“*Go on and say the rest of that and do not make it a speech.*”

“*She has looked for it so long that she has a shelf eleven inches wide in the room where she sits and about nine hundred refusals entered in it, and the shelf is eleven inches from where she sits. She has not opened it, and she has written down in a book of her own that she has not opened it, and that is the eleventh time she has written that sentence in six years, and nobody has ever asked her why.*”

Nobody in that room said anything for about a minute.

“*Say the second page and say it in the order she wrote it.*”

And the second page was about the lane, and it took him about four minutes, and about a third of it was not what he expected.

“*A lane works.*”

“*Say the rest of that and do not make it a speech.*”

“*That is the first line of it. A lane works. In six years and a half she has seen exactly one thing open without asking anybody’s character, and it was in a lane in a market town of four thousand people.*”

“*Say the rest of it and do not make it a speech.*”

“*About eleven people were standing in it, and a woman of thirty-four refused a form that had been put in four sentences in front of about nine people, and no office in that county had anything to do with it, and nobody in that lane could be sent for.*”

“*Say the rest of it and do not make it a speech.*”

“*And a man of thirty-one read out a notice in a square in the same town on the first day he was there, and nobody asked him to. The whole of what that town has done for six weeks was done by about eleven people in a lane, and a woman refusing a form, and a copyist who would not read a man out of a room.*”

“*Go on and say the rest of that and do not make it a speech.*”

“*She has put all three of those in a paragraph in a round hand and she has not put his name in any of them, and that is the second time in twelve days that a person she has never met has left his own name out of a thing she wrote about him.*”

“*Go on and say the third page and say it as she wrote it and do not make it a speech.*”

And the third page was the one that took the longest to read out, because he had read it nine times and it was four lines long and it was the page he had been carrying since the Wednesday of the thirtieth day.

“*You have a shelf eleven inches wide and I have a lane. I can build you a shelf. I can build it out of a year and a half of my own work, and it will be in a room that anybody may come into, and it will be a thing a person can be asked about. It will be eleven inches wide and it will be full, and it will take about nine years, and it will be built by a person and not by a body, and you cannot be asked about it and neither can I.*”

“*Go on and say the rest of that and do not make it a speech.*”

“*You can build me a lane. You can do it in an afternoon and you have done eleven of them in six weeks, and they are the only place I have ever stood where nobody’s character was asked, and I want that and I cannot have it, because a lane is not held. A lane is a place where people stand while they are saying a thing that dies with them, and a shelf is a place where a thing is said once and does not die.*”

“*Go on and say the rest of it and do not make it a speech.*”

“*Neither of us can build the other one’s thing, and there is no form in this county and none in the nine buildings I have worked in that would let either of us try.*”

“*Say the rest of that and do not make it a speech.*”

“*And this is written to a body and you are not the body, and I have not signed it with my name, and you will not find my name on it.*”

“*Say the rest of that and do not make it a speech.*”

“*If you are asked who wrote it you will have to say that a recordwright in a building of four hundred people wrote it, and you will not be able to say whether she is twenty-eight, and you will not be able to say that I have never seen her.*”

---

And the first disagreement of that week was not a disagreement, and it was the one he had been carrying for six weeks.

“*Say the part you came down here to say and say it in about four sentences,*” said Wenna Crale.

“*I came down here to say that a thing said in a lane dies with the people saying it, and that I have been building a town out of sentences for six weeks, and that it does not work.*”

“*Go on and say the rest of it and do not make it a speech.*”

“*I have been right about that in nine rooms, and I have not been right about anything else since the sixth day after the division.*”

“*Say the rest of that and do not make it a speech.*”

“*It is on the second page, in a round hand, in about four hundred lines, and it is said there as a fact and not as a finding.*”

“*Go on and say the rest of that and do not make it a speech.*”

“*It is said there by a person who has not been in this town and who has no reason to be kind to me. I have been arguing with a woman of thirty-four in this town for six weeks about the opposite of it, and both of those women are right.*”

Nobody in that room said anything for about a minute.

“*Say the rest of it and do not make it a speech,*” said Wenna Crale.

“*And that is the first disagreement I did not have. The second one is on the third page, and it is real, and I am going to be on the wrong side of it in about four years, and I can hear that from here.*”

“*Go on and say the rest of that and do not make it a speech.*”

“*And I would like it in a book in this town that I heard it on the forty-third day after the division and did not answer it.*”

“*Say what the second one is, in your own mouth, and do not stop in the middle of it.*”

“*That she is right about the shelf and I am right about the lane, and neither of us can build the thing the other one needs. She has written that in four pages in the round hand of eleven clerks in a building of four hundred people, and she has not signed it with any name at all.*”

“*Go on and say the rest of that and do not make it a speech.*”

“*A copyist of this House has just read out the whole of it in a room over a nail shop and is not going to enter a line of it, because there is no body behind it, and because I did not ask her to.*”

“*Go on and say the rest of it and do not make it a speech.*”

“*A person not of this town cannot be entered in a book of this town, and there is no form in the county of Ostrey that says he can be asked about a letter instead.*”

---

And then he wrote back, and it took him about four hours, and it is the only thing he has done in this town in six weeks that cannot be undone by anybody.

She had told him, in the first letter, on the twenty-second day after the division, in about nine lines, that if a letter is written in the small upright hand then anything said in it is a thing a clerk in an office can be taken to a room about.

She had told him that if it is in any other hand then it is a letter from a person, and a thing a person can be asked about. He had written in that hand before and she was right about it, and he has told her so in one of them.

He wrote this one in it as well, because it is the hand that makes a letter usable, and because what he wanted to send her was a usable thing.

He wrote it in that hand because she had used that hand for a thing about a person and not for a thing about an office, and he had been wrong about that for nine days in a letter four hundred miles off.

He wrote nine hundred words in a hand that is hers, and folded it in four, and put nothing on the outside of it, and gave it to a carrier who has driven that road for thirty-one years.

There is no way on this earth for a person in a building of four hundred people to tell a fair copy in that hand from a letter in it. A hand is a thing a person can be asked about, and he has given her a thing in her own hand, and has made no mark on it that says where it came from.

That was the mistake of Ilyan Vester on the forty-third day after the division, and it is the fourth one he has made in six weeks that was about being able to be asked about. The other three of them he can name. He could not name this one for about nine days.

The fever was twenty-nine weeks and three days old. A room over a nail shop at the south end of a market square had about two people in it and one fire in it, and a copyist of thirty-four had read out three pages of a letter that was addressed to a water board and not to a man.

Four hundred miles off, a woman of twenty-eight had not opened a range of about nine hundred refusals and had written down eleven times that she had not, and had put neither of those facts into the round hand of the counters, and had not signed the four pages with any name at all.

A sheet folded in four with nothing on the outside of it went out of this town on a carrier’s cart, in a hand that is not the hand of the man who wrote it and is the hand of the woman who did not sign.

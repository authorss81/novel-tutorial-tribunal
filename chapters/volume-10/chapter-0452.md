# Chapter 452: The Last Nine Miles Of That Road Have A Milestone On One Side Of It And Nothing On The Other, And He Reads A Notice Out In An Empty Market Square That Nobody Asked Him To Read, And By The Time He Has Finished Somebody In The Square Knows His Name

Sixth day of the eighty-sixth week. His two hundred and second morning. Fifty-two days after the settlement. The fever twenty-four weeks and one day. Six days since the division.

The milestones stop nine miles from Halloway.

Doff Anstey had driven that road for thirty-one years and did not think there was anything in it, and when Ilyan asked him about it at about the fourth hour on the Sunday morning he answered in the tone of a man explaining the price of hay.

“*They put one up at the last house and took the other one off about nineteen years back, and the one that is standing has a letter on the north face and nothing on the south face.*”

“*Say the letter.*”

“*It is the letter of the county. It is not the letter of anything else, and there is nothing on the other side of it, and that is because the county bought the stone and not the road.*”

“*Go on.*”

“*After that stone you are off every list I know of, and I have never had a reason to be on one, and I have been a carrier thirty-one years.*”

Ilyan had a bad leg and had been in the seat of that cart for four hours. He asked eleven more questions before noon. He got four answers that were useful and about nine that were not. He wrote nothing down, and then noticed that he had written nothing down, and understood that it was the first time in six weeks that writing had not seemed to him like the only thing to do with a thing.

---

Halloway came up out of a fold in the ground with the river on the left of it and a wood on the right, and about two hundred years of somebody having made a decision about where to put a market. The square was cobbled, and there were no stalls on it, because it was Sunday, and the stone at the north end of it had a fire in it and about forty people standing outside the building it was in.

Not walking past. Standing, in the wet, in the way people stand who have come to a place and are waiting for it to be open and are not going to go home.

“*Say what that is,*” he said. “*You have a house and a fire and a door, and those people are stood outside it on a Sunday night in the rain and there is no sign on the door at all.*”

“*That is the Stone House,*” said Doff Anstey, “*and that is the House of Halloway, and they have been stood there since about two hours back. They will be there till the steward lets them in, and he will not, and they know he will not, and that is the whole of what is happening on my Sunday.*”

“*Say who they are.*”

“*I do not know and I am not going to guess at a man I drive for in a square. I will say there is a knot of them, and that there is a girl of nineteen at the front of it with a roll of paper under her coat.*”

---

He got the room first, over a shop that sold nails and did not want the rent, and he put the bag down and did not unpack, and then he went back down into the square because he could not be in a room in a strange town for one hour without knowing what was in it.

They were still there. He counted twice because he counts.

There were forty-one of them, which he wrote down afterwards, and nineteen houses, which he worked out on the second count. There was a girl of nineteen at the front with a paper under her coat and her hat in her hand and the rain coming into her face.

“*Say your name,*” said a man of about sixty at the back, and he had not asked him anything else first, which was the first decent thing anybody had done to him in nine weeks. “*You are standing in a square that is not yours and you are looking at my neighbours as though they were a count.*”

“*Ilyan Vester. I am thirty-one. I am not of this county and I am not upon a road of the Shelf and I have been in this square four minutes. I am counting because I count, and I am sorry for it, and I am not going to stop counting.*”

“*Say what you are going to do with the number.*”

“*Nothing,*” said Ilyan. “*I have no use for it here and I have not been asked for it.*”

The man of sixty looked at him for a while and then said the other half of it himself, which is a thing people in this book do.

“*Then you are the first one who has said that to me in nine weeks and I do not believe a word of it.*”

---

The notice was nailed inside the door of the Stone House and had been there since the Wednesday.

Forty-one people were standing outside a shut door who could not read what was on it, because there was nobody there to read it. The man of sixty explained that much in about four sentences without being asked to.

“*We are not stood out here because we cannot read it. Most of us can read. We are stood out here because the notice went up on the Wednesday. It says the water below the sluice is to be scoured on a Thursday nine days off. The water below the sluice is ours. And it is the first notice anybody has nailed to that door in about forty years, and the steward put it up himself.*”

“*And your objection is on a paper.*”

“*Our objection is on a paper and the paper is the ninth clause and the fourth clause and it has been on that board in this room for four hundred years.*”

“*Say the paper.*”

The girl of nineteen got it out of under her coat and did not open it, and there were forty-one people in a wet square and nobody told her to.

“*I am Cullen Skay,*” she said. “*I am nineteen. This is the objection of nineteen houses and it is a roll and it is a yard long and my grandmother cannot come out in the rain so I have got it.*”

“*Say what is on it and do not do it the way a paper is done.*”

“*It says the water of the Hallow from the sluice downward is common to all households of this town. It says nobody has scoured it in ninety years. It says the people who keep the sluice have kept it free. And it says a notice that it is to be scouring is a notice that takes the water off the households it is common to, and it asks the House to say that it is not.*”

Nobody in that square said anything for a moment, and then the man of sixty turned round to Ilyan.

“*You have read a notice,*” he said. “*You are not of this town. Read it out.*”

---

He read it out. That is the thing he did and it is the thing he has not been able to put down since.

It was about two hundred words, and it was nailed inside a door at about the sixth hour of the evening in the wet, and he read it with somebody else’s rain in his face, standing on a step, in a square with forty-one people in it who had been standing there since two hours.

He read the whole of it. He read the date at the top, the nine days, the name of the race and the four lines underneath.

He read it as though it were a sheet coming down in a tray forty-one miles off, which is what it was not. By the fourth line he could hear his own voice doing the thing it does in rooms, and he did it for two hundred more words anyway.

When he had finished, the girl of nineteen said one thing.

“*Say it again and put my house in it,*” she said. “*I am the sixth house of nineteen and I have not been in it once, and I would like somebody to say what the objection of six houses is.*”

And Ilyan said no, and said it fast, and said it in front of forty-one people, and that was the second time in two days that a woman had told him where the limit of his mouth was.

“*I cannot do that. I have read a notice and I have not read a roll and I cannot put a house in an objection I have not seen. If I do it, then the notice in your square and the roll under your coat are the same document, and they are not, and one of them is nailed to a door and the other one is in your hand.*”

“*Say the rest of that and do not stop in the middle of it,*” said the man of sixty, and the girl of nineteen looked at him as though she had wanted to say that for a week and had not known how.

“*And if I read your roll I will be the man who read the objection of nineteen houses, tonight, and in nine days. And you cannot have that either. There is nobody in this town who does not know who is dealing with a thing, and there is no form in this county that says what a man who reads a thing out is.*”

Nobody argued with him about it, and about nine of the forty-one looked at him differently, and the man of sixty said one sentence and it went into the evening.

“*Then you have told us that reading a notice makes you our man, and you have said it out loud. I am sixty-one and I have been the steward of this town forty years, and I have watched men do that in this square for forty years, and I have never once seen a man do it before he was asked.*”

That was Amos Trill, and Ilyan found out his name two days later, and the name was on the notice and he had read the notice and had not looked at the foot of it.

---

What he wanted at about the eighth hour of that Sunday, standing in a wet square in a town of four thousand people, was to be a man standing near something.

He had been in this country two hundred and two mornings, and on two hundred and one of them he had been the man with the sentence, and in nine weeks in that city he had been produced four times, and every one of the four had been somebody else’s document. In that square there was a notice on a door, put up by a man of sixty-one with his own name at the foot of it, about water. There was a roll under a girl’s coat that nineteen houses had agreed on. Not one thing in the whole of it had his name anywhere near it.

And he read it out. He read a two-hundred-word notice in a market square to forty-one people in the rain because a man he had met ninety seconds earlier asked him to, and he could not afterwards find the moment where he decided to.

That was the shape of it and he said so out loud, later, to a person who had come forty-one miles to ask him a question, and she wrote it down.

“*I came to a town to be a man who is on nothing, and the first thing I did in it was make myself the person who reads, and I did that because I am good at it and because nobody here had asked me to be good at it.*”

---

The room over the shop had a bed, a table, a chair and a cup with a chip out of the rim of it. There was a tap behind the shop and it was free, and the tea was cold and bitter, which is how it comes.

He sat down on the bed with a bad leg at about the ninth hour. He wrote out the four useful things he had been told. He could not make a fifth out of the whole of the day.

Then somebody knocked on the door, and it was a boy of about eleven with a shilling in his hand and a piece of paper, and the paper had four words on it in a hand he knew, because he had been given one sheet by that hand and told it was a question.

The boy said a name at the door.

“*Sefa Lund keeps the sluice and she says you are in the room over the nails, and she says the woman from the Saturday has got in on a cart at eight and she has told nine people, and your name is one of the nine.*”

Ilyan had been in that town four hours. He had told nobody his name except one man in a square, in front of nobody, in the first minute.

“*Say who the nine are,*” he said.

“*I do not know. She said the man who read the notice out and eight others, and she said the man who read the notice out is the one everybody is talking about, and she said it like it was a weather.*”

“*And what did she say about the water.*”

The boy looked at the shilling in his own hand, which he had not been given for saying anything.

“*She said the water is to be scoured on the Thursday and there are nine days of it. She has not told the nineteen houses that yet. She has not told them because she does not know whether to say it out loud or say it to somebody. And she said a man who is good at reading notices would know.*”

The fever was twenty-four weeks and one day old, and it had not stopped for a notice or for a square or for a roll under a coat.

In a room over a nail shop a woman of thirty-eight was waiting to find out whether to say a thing out loud. She had kept the sluice for nine years and had never been paid for it, and nobody in that town had ever asked her whether she wanted to.

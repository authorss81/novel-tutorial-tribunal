# Chapter 453: The Board Is Four Feet By Two And A Half And It Hangs Over A Fire That Has Been Lit Every Winter For Four Hundred Years, And It Comes Down Twice A Year Onto A Table In A Hurry, And The Last Time It Was Due It Was Ten Days Ago And Nobody Touched It

Seventh day of the eighty-sixth week. His two hundred and third morning. Fifty-three days after the settlement. The fever twenty-four weeks and two days. Seven days since the division.

She came at about the seventh hour with her hat in her hand and did not come in out of the wet, and stood in the doorway of the room over the nails with the rain going down the step behind her.

“*Say your name,*” said Ilyan. “*And then say why you sent a boy with a shilling before you came yourself.*”

“*Sefa Lund. I am thirty-eight. I have kept the sluice at the Fen nine years and I have never been paid for it, and I sent the boy because I have not got to the bottom of what I was going to say and I wanted to see whether I would say it or not.*”

“*Go on.*”

“*There are nine days of water. It is to be scoured on the Thursday. And I have not told the nineteen houses that yet. I have not told them because I do not know whether to say it out loud in the square, where about two hundred people would hear it, or to say it to the steward, whom I have argued with twice and lost twice.*”

She said the rest of it standing in the doorway in the rain.

“*And you read a notice out in this square on Sunday that nobody asked you to read, and I heard about it before I heard it, which means there are about nine people in this town who know your name and about nine of them have told somebody else.*”

“*Say what you actually came to ask me.*”

“*Whether I should say it out loud.*”

---

He gave her the wrong answer, and he gave it in about two seconds, and it was the answer he has given in rooms in nine weeks.

“*Say it out loud. Say it in the square, at the market, on the ground, in front of people, and do not say it to the steward, because a thing said to one man is a thing one man has, and a thing said in a square is a thing about nine hundred people have.*”

“*Say the half of that which is the other half.*”

“*The other half is that a thing said in a square can be said by anybody afterward, and in nine days it will be said by the steward, and by the water board, and by a man from the county, and the first time is the only time it is yours. I have said six prices out loud in six rooms in six weeks and the last four of them are still going round this city, and I would like somebody to have the first one.*”

Sefa Lund listened to all of it and then shook her head, and she was not being rude, and she had the better of him inside about nine seconds and both of them knew it.

“*No,*” she said. “*I am not going to say it in the square. And I will tell you why, and then I am going to go and do the thing you did not tell me to do.*”

“*Say it and do not stop in the middle of it.*”

“*Because if I say it in that square then about two hundred people have heard that the water is to be scoured. And in nine days, when the water stops, there will be a hundred and forty people at the sluice, and I have to wind it twice a day for an hour with a bad hip, and I have not got a hundred and forty people’s worth of anything in me on a Thursday.*”

She turned the hat over in her hands.

“*And because I have kept that sluice for nine years and nobody has ever asked me a single question about it, and I am not going to be the woman in the square that shouts. I am going to do the ordinary thing, which is go and see the man who scours it and ask him what he is going to do, in a room, with a witness, and write down what he says.*”

“*That is right,*” said Ilyan. “*And it is better than what I said, and I would like it said out loud in a room that the woman who told me I was wrong is right and that I answered in about two seconds.*”

“*I will do that,*” said Sefa Lund, “*on the Wednesday, with the fenrow woman in it, and you can come if you are the sort of man who comes.*”

“*I am not.*”

“*I know,*” she said. “*You are the sort that gets asked, and then stands in the room. There is a difference and I have watched men not see it for nine years.*”

---

The Stone House is not much of a building and it has been doing one job for four hundred years, which is being the coldest room in Halloway in the winter, which is exactly the job a room where forty-one men have to sit and listen needs doing.

The board is on the north beam. It is about four feet by two and a half, and there is a vellum on the face of it, and a lead tag at the foot of the vellum with four seals in it that have gone the colour of a wet stone.

In the corner underneath it is a hearth, and the hearth has a fire in it in the winter, and it has had one in that corner for four hundred years.

The board is nailed nine feet up and about five feet in from the wall. That is the highest place a man can reach with a chair and the warmest place a board can hang in a building with one fire in it.

Wenna Crale said all of that standing underneath it, and then she said the part that mattered, and she said it looking up.

“*It comes down twice a year.*”

“*Go on.*”

“*On the audit day and on the water sitting day. It comes down onto the long table, and two people take it down. It is a heavy thing and the nails are old, and there is a fire lit, because this is where the House sits in the winter and the cold is the reason the House sits in a stone house at all.*”

“*And who are the two people.*”

“*The steward and the clerk of the House. That is the custom and it is ninety years old and nobody writes it down anywhere.*”

---

Ilyan did the thing he does, in a building he had been in for about eleven minutes.

“*Say how many people in this town can read that hand.*”

“*I have never once been asked that.*”

“*Say it, and I am asking it before I have sat down.*”

“*Two,*” said Wenna Crale. “*And you have just asked the question that four people asked me on Saturday and I have answered it four times, and you are the fifth and you asked it in a different order again.*”

“*Say the other order.*”

“*The other order is that nobody asked how many people can read it. They asked how many people can read the copy. There are four of those. I am one. One of the other three is sixty-one and deaf in one ear. One is twenty-four and has been in this office nine weeks. And the fourth is a man of eighty-one who was taught it by his mother and has not used it since his mother died.*”

“*And the two who can read the Board itself.*”

“*Me and my grandmother, and my grandmother has been dead nine years.*”

---

Then she said the other half of it, and she said it to the board and not to him, and it took her a while.

“*And I learned the old hand off the copy. Not off that. I have never read that on my own and neither has anybody in this town in nine years.*”

“*Say that again and do not make it smaller.*”

“*The copy is a fair copy made ninety years ago by a woman of twenty-four in this office, and her name is on the foot of the fourth leaf, and it is Isabel Tuke. She was dead sixty years ago, and my grandmother was born in that year. My grandmother learned the hand off the copy, and I learned it off my grandmother, and there are three people between the hand and anybody in this town who can use it.*”

“*Say the other half of that and go on to the end of it.*”

“*And the Book is the oldest thing in this county and the copy is the only thing anybody in this county can read, and the two do not agree, and nobody has been able to put them together since the year the copy was made.*”

---

The audit day was in the week of the division and it had come and gone, and the Book had not come down, and there was a reason and the reason was in a book in the room and took four lines to say.

“*The audit is an audit of the common. That is what the word means. There was no common on the audit day because it had been divided and sold in the same week, and so there was nothing to audit, and so the Board was not taken down, and so the whole of the thing took four lines and about two minutes.*”

“*And the next time it comes down.*”

“*The water sitting. The Thursday of this week, which is the third day of the third week after the division, which is in three days.*”

“*Say what happens when it comes down in four days.*”

“*Two people take it off the beam and put it on that table, and the fire is lit, and there is not room in here for a fire and nine men and a board. The whole thing is done in about a quarter of an hour because it has to be done in a hurry, and then it goes back up.*”

She looked at him.

“*And you cannot be one of the two.*”

---

“*Say why not and do not be kind about it.*”

“*Because you are not asked,*” said Wenna Crale. “*Nobody in this town is asked. The two are the steward and the clerk, and the clerk is me. I have been the clerk for eleven years and nobody has ever once asked me whether I would rather it was somebody else, and I would like that written down, and I have just said it in front of a board.*”

“*Say the rest of it and do not stop in the middle of a sentence.*”

“*And you are a man who read a notice out in a market square on Sunday that nobody asked him to read, and there are about nine people in this town who know your name, and the House does not put its hands on a stranger’s board, and that is not an insult to you. It is what the House is for. The House is forty-one men who have lived here, and their whole power is that they can be asked things, and you cannot be asked a single thing about the water of the Hallow, and that is why you cannot have the board.*”

“*Say the half of that which is the other half,*” said Ilyan, and it came out of him before he had decided to say it, which is how the things that cost him get said.

“*The other half is that if I am not on the table then the only person in this room who can read the ninth clause is the person who has to write the minutes, and the minutes are hers, and the nine of you will be told what she writes. I am the only man here who has spent six weeks watching a person be turned into a column, and I am telling you that a copyist standing over a board is a copyist standing over a board.*”

Wenna Crale did not answer him straight away, which she had not done to anybody since Saturday.

“*Say that again in front of the board,*” she said, “*and I will let you, and then you can be a man who said it in front of the board on Monday, and I will write it down, and it will be the only thing in my book this year that is not about water.*”

He said it again in front of the board.

And then she told him the thing she had come back to tell him, and she told it standing under the board with her hand not touching it, and it took her about a minute.

“*You asked me on Sunday what happens to a book when there is no office to keep it in.*”

“*I did not ask you. A woman asked me, and I told you, and you have brought it back.*”

“*I brought it back because it is the only reason I answered your letter and I did not answer your letter.*”

“*Go on.*”

“*It goes in the press in this room, and the press stays in this room, and the next House finds it in this room. The four hundred years of this town are in this room. There is one book, one copy of it, and one woman who can read the hand, and she is paid two pounds a year and has been for eleven years, and she is the only public officer in this town that is paid anything at all.*”

She put her hand flat on the case of the press against the west wall.

“*And that is the answer, and I have had it ready since Saturday, and it is a good answer. And the other half of it is that not one line in four hundred years of this town says the next House has to open that press. I have looked. There is a form for the audit, a form for the water, a form for the poor, and there is nothing whatever for a press.*”

“*Say the rest of that and do not make it larger than it is.*”

“*So a book in this town is kept by the town for as long as the town is feeling like it. And I am paid two pounds a year out of forty-one households, and I have never asked for it to be more. You may write that down as a virtue or as the other thing. I have not decided which it is, and I am not going to be told by a man who has been here one day.*”

“*I am not going to write anything down about you,*” said Ilyan. “*I have not got a book and I have not got a right, and there is a woman in the room over the nails who has not been told a thing yet. I have been in this town one day. I have already read a notice out and asked to be let near a board, and both of those were mine.*”

The fever was twenty-four weeks and two days old. A board about four feet by two and a half hung nine feet up over a fire in a cold room in a town of four thousand people, and in three days it was coming down onto a table in a hurry with a fire lit and nine men in the room.

One woman was going to be standing over it, and she was the only person in the world who could read what was on the other side of it.

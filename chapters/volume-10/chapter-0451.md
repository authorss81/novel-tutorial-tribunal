# Chapter 451: A Woman Stands Up In A Room At The Foot Of A Hill And Gives Her Name And Her County Before Anybody Asks Her, And He Says Out Loud In Front Of Thirty People That He Wants To Live In A Town Where His Name Is Not Printed On Anything

Fifth day of the eighty-sixth week. His two hundred and first morning. Fifty-one days after the settlement. The fever twenty-four weeks old to the day.

She had been in the town eleven days and she had not asked for anything in any of them, and on the Saturday morning she stood up at the back of about thirty people and said four sentences before anybody had got to her feet.

“*My name is Wenna Crale. I am thirty-four. I am the copyist to the House of Halloway and I have been in that office eleven years. I have come forty-one miles and I have slept nine of them in a hayloft above a saddler because I could not get a bed and I did not want to be a person somebody had to find a bed for.*”

Skell put one hand up flat, which the room knew what it meant, and the room stopped.

“*Go on,*” said Fenna Rusk, from the second chair along.

“*And I have got one question and it is not a political one, and I have put it down in writing and I can put it down again if anybody wants it in a book.*”

Nobody in that room had asked her county. She had given it anyway. Ilyan worked out what she had done with the first two sentences while she was still standing up, and he was right about it. A name and an age and an office and a year, in that order, before a single one of them could be asked about. She had been made a thing in a column in a town forty-one miles off once. Fenna Rusk had been made one twice.

“*Say the question,*” said Skell, “*and then say what it is about, and do not make the two of them into one thing.*”

“*It is: who is the hand on the ninth clause of the Hollow Book. And it is about a date.*”

---

She had the paper out and she did not read it, and Ilyan noticed that, and understood a half of what it was doing, and did not understand the other half for about nine days.

“*The Book is said to be four hundred years old,*” she said. “*That is on the front of it, in the hand of the first man, and the front of it is four hundred years old, and everybody in Halloway including the man who owns the shop I buy ink from will tell you four hundred years.*”

“*And the other half of that,*” said Fenna Rusk.

“*And the ninth clause is not four hundred years old. The ninth clause is in a different ink and it is in a hand none of the eight above it is written in, and it is dated on the back of it, and the date is a hundred and thirty-eight years ago.*”

The room did a thing rooms do, and Darrin Skell, who had been at the wall since the second hour, said the half of it out loud before anybody could stop him.

“*Then the Book is not four hundred years old. The Book is four hundred years old with a hundred and thirty-eight-year-old clause at the back of it. And this town has been reading a date off the front of a thing for four hundred years.*”

“*I am glad you are here,*” said Wenna Crale. “*I did not know anybody in this room would do that and I have said it in four rooms in my own town and in two of them a man laughed at me about the ink.*”

---

Fenna Rusk wanted the paper read into the book, and Cope had his book open, and it was read.

“*Say the whole of it,*” she said, “*including the bit about where the paper is.*”

“*The Hollow Book, being the oldest covenant of the town of Halloway, kept in the Stone House upon the north beam, in the county of Ostrey, eleven miles from the counter at Ostrey and not upon a road of the Shelf. Clause nine, in the fifth hand: no household of this town shall be required to take water, or to be entered, or to answer, against its will. Dated a hundred and thirty-eight years ago. And struck through in a sixth hand, in a fair copy made ninety years ago, and the copy is in a press in the Stone House in four leaves.*”

The room did not make a sound for a moment.

“*Say the rest of it and do not stop half way through a sentence,*” said Skell.

“*There is no rest of it. That is all of it, and I have carried it forty-one miles, and there is a boy at my lodgings in this city who took eleven days to get a cart for me and I paid him out of a year’s money, and I would like somebody to tell me what a man does with it.*”

She put the paper on the long table, squared, which is not a thing a country woman does in a room, and which Ilyan also noticed.

---

What he wanted, at about the fourth hour on that Saturday, was to be in a town where nothing he had done was printed on anything.

He had said the price of a want out loud in that room five times in six weeks. It had not stopped him once. On the sixth he said the want instead, which was worse, and he knew it was worse while he was doing it, and about thirty people heard the whole of it.

“*I have wanted one thing out loud in this room in six weeks and I have not got it,*” he said, “*and what I have wanted this week is not a road and not a bar and not a sheet in a tray. It is a town where my name is not on anything.*”

Skell did not say *go on*, and did not say anything for about nine seconds, and the room waited.

“*Say the rest of that in your own mouth and not the room’s,*” said Fenna Rusk.

“*My name is on four hundred and sixty-three forms in a district eleven miles off. There is a word at the top of every one of them that says the whole of it is temporary. And my name is at the foot of the fifth page of a form in the window of a press, and a woman’s name is under mine, and there is no line anywhere to take me off it. And there is a declaration on a table at the top of this room that governs about a hundred and forty people who are dead. I do not know of one street in ninety thousand people where I could stand in it and be nobody.*”

He put his hand flat on the table, and it was the thing he did, and he had been doing it for six weeks. It did not help. He had noticed that in about the fifty-second week and had carried on.

“*And I have said the price of six wants out loud in this room and the price of the sixth one is that I am not going to get it, and I have not said that before, and I would like it in the book without a name on it.*”

“*It goes in without a name on it,*” said Cope, and wrote it, and did not read it out, which was the first time in six weeks that anybody had let one of those sit in a book without reading it into a room.

---

Then he did the thing that made the whole of the next nine days, and he did it before he had decided to, and he did it because he is a man who cannot leave a useful sentence alone.

“*I am going to tell you something you did not ask me for,*” he said, “*and I am going to tell you now, in this room, and I would like it noticed that I have not made you pay for it.*”

“*Say it out loud and let us decide what it costs,*” said Wenna Crale.

“*The declaration that came into this city on the third day of this week governs every person entered in a register of the Shelf, in every town and city upon a road of the Shelf, alive or not. Halloway is not upon a road of the Shelf.*”

He watched it go round the room, and it went round the room. Fenna Rusk put her hand flat on the long table and copied it, which she does not do.

“*Say the half of that which is the other half,*” she said.

“*The other half is that the declaration has never touched your town, and that nothing else that has come down that hill in nine weeks has touched it either, and that is not a mercy. A thing that cannot govern you cannot protect you. There is a woman in this room with a slate under her arm who has made five pounds two shillings and sixpence in fifteen days and there is no office to keep the book in. And you have a copyist, and a press, and forty-one heads of household, and I have got none of the three and you have got all of them.*”

Nobody said anything for a moment.

“*You are telling me,* said Wenna Crale, “*that there is nothing that can send anybody to my town, in about nine weeks, and that I have come forty-one miles anyway, and that you knew it before I sat down.*”

“*Yes.*”

“*Then you should not have said it in a room.*”

“*No,*” said Ilyan. “*I have said it in a room because I have said six things in a room in six weeks and it has not stopped me once. And because you asked me a question about a hand and I have not answered it. And because it is the only thing I have had that could be of any use to anybody in nine days.*”

“*And what has it cost you,*” said Fenna Crale, “*and do not do me the courtesy of a small answer.*”

“*That you will now be able to leave, at any hour, without telling me. And I will not hear from you again. And I would have got eleven days of it out of the ask, and I gave it away in four seconds, because I like being the man with the thing nobody else has.*”

Hessa Marl said one sentence from the back, with the slate under her arm, and she said it in the voice she used about nine doors a day.

“*Say the rest of that,*” she said, “*because you have just told a woman forty-one miles from her own town that nobody can make anybody come and find her, and I have been the woman in a box for fourteen days, and I would like somebody to say the other half of it out loud.*”

“*I have not said the other half of it. The other half is that a woman forty-one miles from her own town cannot get anybody to come and find her either. And that if her town has a fire, or a flood, or a bailiff, there is nothing between her and it but forty-one miles of road, and a copyist, and a board on a beam. I have not said that. I have said a thing that sounds better because it is shorter.*”

He put his hand flat on the table again.

“*Say that in the book,*” said Fenna Rusk. “*All of it, and the other half, and the day.*”

---

He refused her at about the fifth hour, in front of the room, and he was right to, and he gave the reason in the order it should be given.

“*I cannot come,*” he said, “*and I am going to tell you why in the order it is actually true, and not in the order that sounds best. First, because I have said yes to a case four times in six weeks and every one of the four was somebody else’s matter. None of the four is finished. Second, because I am not of your town and there is nothing in your town that can enter me. A man who can be sent for by nobody is not a thing you want in a room in a week when your House is sitting.*”

“*And the third,*” said Fenna Crale.

“*And the third is that I want to go, and I have not said so, and if I go it will be for my own reason and it will be dressed as yours, and you will go home and tell your House that a man came, and I will have taken a thing off you and given you the credit for it.*”

“*Write that down too,*” said Skell, and Ferrow wrote it down, and it went in without a name on it as well.

Then Wenna Crale asked the only question she asked in that room, and she asked it to Skell and not to him, and she had it ready.

“*Is there a town in this country where a book is kept by the town itself and not by an office?*”

“*Say the whole of that and do not narrow it,*” said Fenna Rusk.

“*Is there one, and how would a person find out, and how long does it take.*”

Skell looked at her for a while.

“*There is one in this county,*” he said, “*and I will not tell you which, and it is not my town and I have not been in it since I was thirty. And it is four miles off the near road and it has a hall and a counter and a plate on it with a name at the head and nothing at the foot, and the person there has been in the room four times since the eighty-second week.*”

“*That is not what I asked,*” said Wenna Crale.

“*No. It is what I have.*”

---

On the Sunday morning he was on a cart going the other way, and it was not her reason, and he said so out loud to four people in a yard at about the sixth hour before he got on it.

“*I am going forty-one miles to a town that is not upon a road of the Shelf, and I am not going for the question, and I am not going because of the ink on the ninth clause.*”

“*Then say what for, in your own mouth, and do not make it noble,*” said Fenna Rusk, who had walked down to the yard and had not been asked to.

“*On Friday Hessa Marl asked me what happens to a book when there is no office to keep it in. I said I did not know. I have said I did not know in two rooms in six weeks and both times I was right. And there is one answer in this world that I have not seen. It is a town with a press of its own and a woman who writes the minutes. I am going to look at it. And if it is not any good I shall come back and say so.*”

“*And if it is any good,*” said Fenna Rusk, “*you will be a man in a town of four thousand people with a slate and a board and no register, and your name will not be on anything at all, and you will have gone forty-one miles to find that out.*”

“*Yes,*” Ilyan said.

“*Then get on the cart,*” she said, “*and I am going to say the other half, because you will not. You will find something that is not on any paper, and you will be the only person in that town who has ever been on any paper, and you will be asked about it in nine days, and you will have brought it with you.*”

The fever was twenty-four weeks old. The office of Regent of the Shelf was not held and the second seat at the council of the Shelf was vacant.

The four hurdles and the nine links of chain and the plate with nothing cut in the corner of it were still up on the north side of the ninth marker. About two hundred crossings a day went up four yards of mud on a side that had no name in any office document in the world.

A woman of twenty-eight was in a box on the north side with a slate and a book of the visits that had no office to be kept in, and he was not going to be there for any of it.

# Chapter 496: The First Day Of The Ninety-Third Week, And A Tuesday On Which A Request For A Charter Is Drawn Up With A Head And Two Lines At A Copyist’s Own Table With No Body Behind It, And The Fourth Heading Is Objected To And The Objection Is Entered, And A Man Of Thirty-One Tries To Make The Seventh A Clause And Is Refused

First day of the ninety-third week, and the day before was the last of the ninety-second. His two hundred and forty-sixth morning. Ninety-six days after the settlement. The fever thirty weeks and three days. Fifty days since the division. Forty-four days in this town.

The room over the taproom held about nine people round a table scarred with the rings of about a hundred years of cups. The door stood open to the stair and the stair stood open to the square.

Somebody had brought a broken chair up and put it against the wall, and two men were sitting on the frame of it, because there were not enough stools.

A bar four feet long at the head of the sluice had not been turned at the sixth hour or at the twelfth hour for twenty days. The wheel at the mill had been slow nineteen days completed.

Wenna Crale had her own table in the corner, four feet from the long one, and her own paper on it, and her bag on the floor at her feet. She is thirty-four and has been copyist to the House of this town for eleven years at two pounds a year.

Ilyan Vester sat down at her table without being asked, which he has done twice in eleven years of other people’s tables and which is a thing to be written down, and he had a sheet squared for headings and a steel pen and about four ideas about what a document is for.

“*I am going to say the six,*” Wenna said, “*and then I am going to stop, and anybody in this room may object to any one of the six and the objection goes in under the head with the day on it, and I will read the objection out before the sheet goes into the press.*”

Ordwin Cape told her to read the six.

She read them off a sheet of four pages in a round hand that had come into this room on the forty-third day, folded in four with nothing on the outside of it, out of a building of four hundred people on a road four hundred miles off. It was addressed to a water board and not to a man, and it carried no name at the foot of it, and the writer is a woman of twenty-eight who is not a house and has never been in this town.

A subject. A condition. A burden. A witness. A remedy. And an exit.

“*Six,*” said Wenna Crale, “*and the seventh is the one nobody in this county has ever had.*”

She wrote the head, and then two lines under the head, and she did it at her own table with no body behind it. That is the way a copyist of this town has drawn up everything anybody here has ever asked for: two lines on the thirty-sixth day, two lines on the fortieth, two lines on the forty-second. There are about nine of those in a press against a west wall, and not one of them has a body behind it, and nobody in this town has yet worked out that this is why they keep.

---

A man at the long table objected to the fourth one, and the objection is in the sheet, and it is the reason that one of the six is different from what anybody brought into that room.

“*A witness,*” he said. He is a head of household in the sixth ward and he has been a miller’s man for twenty-two years. “*A witness is a person who is stood up in a room and asked whether he saw it. I have been a witness twice in my life and once I was asked what I had seen and I had seen a thing at four in the afternoon and I was asked about it at nine the next morning and by the second question I had stopped being sure. I will not carry the word.*”

“*What do you want instead of it,*” Wenna said, and she had the pen up and had not written anything.

“*I want the name of the person who was in the room. Not a promise that there was a witness. The name.*”

A man of the third ward said that a name was a thing a man could be turned out of a town for, and the man of the sixth ward said that was the point.

They argued about it for about eleven minutes. Two people who had not spoken all morning said something. A cup went over on the long table and was wiped up with somebody’s sleeve, and the man whose it was said it did not matter and then said it did.

Wenna wrote the objection into the sheet under the head and read it out standing, in the voice she uses for a minute. It says: *a witness — the name of the person in the room, and no promise of any kind; entered on the objection of a head of household of the sixth ward, on the fiftieth day after the division.*

“*That is in,*” she said. “*Now the exit.*”

---

Ilyan set his squared sheet on the corner of her table.

That was his mistake, and it came in with the paper, and the mistake was a want. His want was that this be a form. He had wanted it since the Friday. A form is a thing a person can be sent for, and a form is a thing that can be asked about, and if this charter came out of the room as a form then there would be a thing in the county that it had to answer to, and he wanted that more than he wanted the water shut.

He tried to make the seventh heading a clause.

“*Write it as a clause under the six,*” Ilyan Vester said.

He put his finger on the seventh line as he said it, on her table, over her paper.

“*A clause can be kept. A clause can be read out in a room twice in ninety years and it will still be there. That is what the ninth clause of this town did. It is a hundred and thirty-eight years old, it is on the back of a board nine feet up over a fire, and it is the only thing in this county that anybody has ever been able to say no with. Put the exit in as a clause and it will be the second one.*”

Mab Harrow stood by the window with her arms folded. She rents the second room in the fourth house and is not a head of household and has never been in the House of this town in her life. She has refused nothing in six weeks.

“*Then we will have had it,*” she said, “*and it will have done what the ninth clause did.*”

A man at the table said that the ninth clause is on the back of the covenant and protected this town for a hundred and thirty-eight years.

Mab Harrow turned round and looked at him for a moment before she answered.

Nobody at that table picked up anything. A man who had been writing put his pen across the sheet to keep it from curling, a woman put both hands flat on the oak, and a man by the door stayed where he was with his hand on the frame of it, and that is what a room looks like when a sentence it had agreed with for six weeks is being argued with.

“*It has been read out in a room twice in ninety years and given to nobody twice,*” she said. “*It is used once, on the forty-first day, when it stopped nine men of a majority and shielded the man who would have done the compelling, and not the person who would have been compelled. It binds households. The row are not households. About nine people in this room would use it again this afternoon if it were offered, and it would be the same day and the same nine, and the row would be exactly where they are.*”

She came away from the window.

“*A clause is a thing a person can be given. An exit is a thing a person can open.*” She put one finger on the paper. “*That is the whole of the difference. It is the whole of what a woman of twenty-eight has been writing about for six years and a half in a building four hundred miles off.*”

She put her hand flat on the two lines.

“*She has been writing it to nobody. There is not one person in this county who can give her an answer. This morning we are writing her sentence on a sheet in a room over a taproom, and we are not going to improve on it, and we are going to sign it with her unasked, which is a different thing and not a better one.*”

About nine people in that room understood the difference.

A man at the long table wrote the word exit on a scrap and underlined it twice, spelt it wrong, and turned the scrap over. A woman went out to the stair for air and came back in with rain on her shoulders.

A man of the fifth ward said *so it is a door then*, in the tone of a man who has understood a thing and is annoyed about it. Nobody answered him. He said it again more quietly and then said that he had been in nine rooms in five weeks and that in six of them somebody had used a long word where a short one would have done, and that he was the one who had to go home and live with it, and about four people in that room laughed, and a copyist of thirty-four did not.

Then a man of the second ward asked who was going to hold it, and that turned out to be the question that had been underneath the other one all along.

Wenna asked him what he meant by hold.

“*The charter. You have asked a county for a thing. A county is eleven miles off and it has a counter, and the counter has a clerk, and the clerk will want to know which room in this town keeps it.*”

About nine people in that room looked at one another, and the room went through the thing it had been doing for six weeks in about four seconds, which is the shortest it has ever taken, and then somebody said the thing out loud before anybody had decided to.

There is a press against a west wall in a stone room in this town and any person of the town may read it. There is a copyist at two pounds a year. There is a second book of about nine hundred names under a miller’s counter. There is a water list that goes by tenement and a minute of a water board in a room over a saddler’s shop. And there is a woman of thirty-eight at the head of a sluice with a key to gates that stand open.

“*It goes in the press,*” Wenna said. “*With the day on it. And I will keep a copy of it, and the copy is mine, and if I am gone the copy goes in the press, and if the press is gone the whole of it is nine words and a heading and anybody who has read it can write it out again.*”

The man of the second ward said that was not holding it.

“*No. I have said so. There is no office in this county that could hold it, and there is no clerk that could take it, and anybody who tells you otherwise is selling you a room.*”

She wrote the seventh heading then, and the room watched her do it, and it is the only line of the seven that is not a heading.

---

She folded the request with the head and two lines on it and put her hand at the foot of it, and it went into the press against the west wall of the Stone House, where any person of the town may read it, and where it is the fifth thing in eleven days.

A copyist of this town is paid two pounds a year. She is the only public officer in Halloway that is paid anything at all, and the two pounds have not moved in eleven years, and nobody has asked for a rise and there is no body in this county that could give her one.

He walked down to the square in the evening with the squared sheet still folded in his coat and the feeling of a thing gone out of his hands.

The cost was that the charter would not be his shape. It would be a thing with six headings and a seventh, and the seventh would be a line that a person could open, and not a clause that protected the strong the way the ninth clause had protected a chair of forty-seven from himself on the forty-first day after the division.

A boy was shutting the taproom below and had to come up for his coat because the room over the shop was locked. He asked the man of thirty-one whether the paper in the press was for everybody or for the House, and then said that everybody was the whole town and the House was forty-one men, and then said that the man would know that better than he did and went down.

A woman of about fifty who had come up the stairs behind him said that the boy was nine and asked good questions, and that this town had stopped asking him that kind about a hundred days ago, and went back down.

The fever was thirty weeks and three days old. A request for a charter with a subject, a condition, a burden, a witness, a remedy and an exit had been drawn up with a head on it and two lines under it, at a copyist’s own table, and the fourth of the six had been objected to in a room with about nine people in it and the objection had been entered under the head with the day on it.

The seventh heading was the one nobody in the county of Ostrey can supply.

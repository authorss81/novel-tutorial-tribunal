# Chapter 496: The First Day Of The Ninety-Third Week, And A Tuesday On Which A Request For A Charter Is Drawn Up With A Head And Two Lines, With No Body Behind It

First day of the ninety-third week. His two hundred and forty-sixth morning. Ninety-six days after the settlement. The fever thirty weeks and three days. Fifty days since the division. Forty-four days in this town.

The room over a taproom held about nine people round a table scarred with rings. The door stood open to the stair. A bar four feet long at the head of the sluice had not been turned for twenty days. The wheel at the mill had been slow nineteen days completed.

Ilyan Vester wanted the charter to be a form. He had his steel pen in his hand and a sheet squared for headings. He wanted boxes that would hold, lines that would bind, a shape any clerk would know at sight.

Wenna Crale sat at her own table in the corner with her own paper. She is thirty-four and copyist to the House eleven years. About nine people in that room knew the six headings out of a letter written four hundred miles off in a round hand by a woman of twenty-eight who is not a house and has never been in their town. The six were a subject, a condition, a burden, a witness, a remedy and an exit. The seventh heading was the one nobody in that county has ever had, and the seventh was the exit.

“*Put down what is asked and put it short,*” said Ordwin Cape.

Wenna wrote with her head bent. When she lifted the pen, she read out standing, the way she reads minutes.

“*To the House of Halloway and to any town that will hear it,*” said Wenna Crale. “*A thing is asked with a subject, a condition, a burden, a witness, a remedy and an exit. The exit is the one nobody in that county can supply. Anybody in the room may object to any of the six and the objection will be entered.*”

Ilyan set his squared sheet on the table. His mistake entered with it. He tried to make the exit a clause, with words that would protect and limit.

“*Write it as a clause under the six,*” said Ilyan Vester. “*A clause can be kept by a board and read out twice in ninety years. It will hold.*”

Mab Harrow stood by the window with her arms folded. She rents the second room in the fourth house and is not a head of household. She spoke in front of about nine people without lifting her voice.

“*A clause is a thing a person can be given,*” said Mab Harrow. “*An exit is a thing a person can open. The difference between them is the whole of what a woman four hundred miles off has been writing about for six years and a half.*”

A man at the table put his pen down and pushed his stool back. A woman went out to the stair for air and came back with rain on her shoulders. A boy wrote the word exit on a scrap and underlined it twice, wrongly spelled, and hid it under his hand.

Ilyan looked at his squared sheet and saw it for what it was. About nine people in that room understood the difference and about nine did not. A form would have let him keep hold of the work. An exit meant letting go of it.

Wenna folded the request with the head and two lines and put her hand at the foot of it. It was drawn in the same way the request for the arbitration was drawn, at her own table, with no body behind it. It would go into the press where any person of the town might read it.

He walked down to the square in the evening with the unsquared feeling still in him. The cost was that the charter would not be his shape. It would be a thing with six headings and a seventh, and the seventh would be a line anybody could open, and not a clause that protected the strong the way the ninth clause had protected a chair of forty-seven on the forty-first day.

The fever was thirty weeks and three days old. A request for a charter with a subject, a condition, a burden, a witness, a remedy and an exit had been drawn up. The exit was the one nobody in that county could supply.

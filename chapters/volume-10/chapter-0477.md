# Chapter 477: The Answer Of A Woman Of Twenty-Eight Four Hundred Miles Off Comes Back In A Round Hand And Is Addressed To A Water Board And Not To A Man, And There Is A Second Sheet Folded In Four With No Name On It, And The Whole Of What She Said Eight Days Ago Is Demonstrable In A Town Of Four Thousand People In Four Days, And She Is Right, And The Answer To Right Or Privilege Is Not A Sentence

Third day of the ninetieth week. His two hundred and twenty-seventh morning. Seventy-seven days after the settlement. The fever twenty-seven weeks and five days. Thirty-one days since the division. Twenty-five days in this town.

The bar at the head of the sluice is four feet long and takes two people and about an hour, and it is wound at the sixth hour and at the twelfth hour, and it has been wound at both on every day for nine years whether the gates were shut or open.

It was not wound at the sixth hour on the Thursday morning of the thirty-first day after the division, and it was not wound at the twelfth hour, and it was not wound at the sixth hour on the Friday.

There were about eleven people at the head of it on the Friday morning and about twenty came up the lane in the half hour after, because the sound of a bar carries four hundred yards in this town and everybody in it knows what the bar sounds like.

Nobody turned it. Nobody was asked to turn it. The board had put nineteen houses out of the water at about the sixth hour on the Wednesday. The houses are not households, and a household is the only thing in this town that anybody has ever been able to require anything of.

A form saying that a household may be required has never been found in this county, and it has now been made unnecessary.

---

The bag came into Halloway on the Tuesday morning of the twenty-ninth day after the division, off a carrier who has driven that road for thirty-one years, and there were about nine things in it and one of them was a sheet of paper in a hand Ilyan Vester had never seen in his life.

He did not open it in a room. He opened it on the Wednesday night in the room over a nail shop with a cup of tea that had gone cold in it, and he read it twice, and he read a second sheet out of it, and the second sheet was folded in four and had nothing on the outside of it.

He read both again on the Thursday morning and he wrote for four hours on the Thursday and he has not said one word about either of them to anybody, and there is no minute in this town in which either of them appears.

The sheet was in a round hand. It is the hand of about eleven clerks in a building of four hundred people, and he had asked her in a letter not to write in it.

It was not addressed to him.

---

“*Say what you have got there and say who it is from,*” said Wenna Crale, at about the ninth hour on the Friday, in the Stone House, with about nine people in it and the door open.

“*Go on, and do not stop in the middle of a sentence,*” said Ilyan.

“*It is from a woman of twenty-eight who is four hundred miles off, and she is a recordwright, and she has been a recordwright for six years and a half, and she is the only person in that building who writes anything the building has not asked for.*”

“*Go on and say the rest of it and do not make it smaller than it is.*”

“*She is also my partner in the only thing I do that anybody else calls work, and she is not a friend of mine and she is not anything else, and there is no arrangement between us of any kind that is not about a form.*”

Nobody in that stone room said anything.

“*Say where it is addressed,*” said Wenna Crale.

“*To the water board of Halloway, in the county of Ostrey. In a round hand, and not signed with her own name.*”

About nine people in a stone room with a hearth in it did not make a sound.

“*Say the other half of that and say it in your own mouth,*” said Perdy Sallow, from the end of the fourth stool’s row.

“*She is not writing to me. She is writing to a body, and a body can be asked a question and can be cited by a person, and I cannot.*”

“*And the board.*”

“*And the board cannot answer it either, because the answer is not a matter the board has any power over, and I know that because I have been in this town twenty-five days and it is the only thing I have learned that is worth the price of it.*”

---

He read it out in the square at about the eleventh hour on the Friday, standing under the awning of the mill, to about thirty people, and he read out four lines of it and then stopped, and the four lines were these.

*You asked me in your letter of the twenty-second day whether four letters and a stroke are a person. I have not opened the range and I am not going to, and I have written down again on the day I wrote to you that I have not opened it.*

*That is the eleventh time I have written that sentence in a book of my own in six years, and nobody has ever asked me why I do it.*

*You are right that a hand is a thing a person can be asked about.*

*Every exit I have read in six years and a half is a form, and a form is a door, and a door can be opened by whoever is nearest the hinge.*

*An exit is a right when the person it is for can open it without asking anybody’s character. That is not a sentence. That is a thing with hinges on it, and there is no such thing in the building I sit in, or in any of the nine I have worked in, and I have been looking for one every working day for six years and a half and it is not there.*

Nobody in that market square said anything for about nine seconds.

“*Say the rest of it,*” said Ordwin Cape, from the edge of the awning.

“*That is the whole of the four lines I am going to read out,*” said Ilyan Vester. “*There are four pages, and I am not going to read the other three in a square.*”

“*Go on and say the rest of that and do not make it a speech.*”

“*She wrote it to a body and not to a man. If a body gets it out of a man’s mouth in a market then it is a sentence and not a document, and she has spent six years and a half telling me that is the whole of what is wrong with the both of us.*”

---

Then he said the thing about right and privilege, and it was the first time in twenty-five days that anybody in Halloway had said the words right and privilege out loud, and he said them to about thirty people in a market square and about nine of them repeated them correctly by the evening.

“*Say the rest of that and do not make it a speech,*” said Sefa Lund, who had come down the lane.

“*She says it is neither. She says a right and a privilege are the same shape on a piece of paper, and that the whole of the difference between them is whether the person it is for can open the door without asking anybody’s character.*”

“*Say the other half of that and say it in this square,*” said Ivo Cray.

“*The other half is that in eight days a town of four thousand people has demonstrated every word of it, and that I have been in that town twenty-five days and I have built the demonstration.*”

Nobody in that square made a sound.

“*Say the whole of that out loud,*” said Mab Harrow, “*because there are about thirty of us here and none of us has been in a room about it, and I have been in nine rooms in three weeks and I know what a room does to a thing.*”

So he said it, standing under an awning, at about the eleventh hour on the Friday, in the order it happened.

“*One. A right you have to knock on nineteen doors for is a favour and not a right. She wrote that down eight days ago.*”

“*Go on.*”

“*The row at the Fen knocked on nineteen doors for three weeks and got onto the roll in four minutes on a Sunday morning, because a steward of forty years decided that a form in his own drawer could be used.*”

“*Go on to the second one.*”

“*Two. A form is a door, and a door can be opened by whoever is nearest the hinge. There are three forms in this county of Ostrey that anybody knows of and all three have been used in four days. The steward’s form has been used three times, on the nineteenth tenements on the Sunday, on eleven houses nobody asked on the Monday, and on those same nineteen tenements back again on the Wednesday afternoon in about four minutes.*”

“*Say the rest of that and go on to the end of the sentence,*” said Ordwin Cape.

“*Three. And a thing in a book is a thing a person can be asked about, and that is the third thing I have taught this town in three weeks.*”

“*Say the rest of it and do not stop in the middle of a sentence.*”

“*The third thing has turned out to be the handle on the door. I did not know that when I taught it and I would not have taught it if I had.*”

About thirty people in a market square in a town of four thousand people did not make a sound.

---

And then he said the last of it, and it was the part about the four instruments, and he had been building it since the sixth hour and he had never said it out loud anywhere.

“*Say what you have been building since the sixth hour,*” said Wenna Crale, who had come out of the Stone House and was standing at the end of the awning, and she had not been asked to come and had come.

“*That the answer to her question is not a sentence. She says that and she is right.*”

“*Go on and say the rest of it and do not make it a speech.*”

“*I have been saying for twenty-five days that a sentence is the answer, and I have said it in nine rooms and in three squares, and a sentence is a thing that dies with the person who said it, and I have known that since the twelfth day after the division, and I have been using it anyway.*”

“*Say the rest of that and do not make it a speech,*” said Sefa Lund.

“*An instrument is not a sentence. An instrument is a thing a person can be asked about, and it can be opened by the person it is for, and it does not need anybody’s character to work, and there are four in this town.*”

“*The four, then.*”

“*A miller’s second book at the bottom of the market square, with about nine hundred names in it and a mark against each, and he will not let it out of his hands. A copyist in a stone room, who is paid two pounds a year and is the only person in this county who can read the old hand. A minute of the water board, in a room over a saddler’s shop, with a day on it.*”

“*Go on and say the rest of that and do not make it a speech.*”

“*And a woman of thirty-eight who keeps a sluice and refuses things in lanes, and who has refused this board in a lane in front of about nine people, and who has been right every time, and who is out of the water as of Wednesday because a man of sixty-one wrote a flood down on a bill.*”

Nobody in that square thanked him for any of it.

“*Say the other half of that and say it in your own mouth,*” said Wenna Crale.

“*And I am on none of them. There is no form in the county of Ostrey that would let me be on any of them.*”

“*Go on and say the rest of it and do not make it a speech.*”

“*There is no form in this county that would let anybody put my name in a book in this town, and I have been proud of that for nine weeks, and this week it has stopped being a thing to be proud of.*”

“*Say the rest of that and go on to the end of the sentence.*”

“*She is right that a right you have to knock on nineteen doors for is a favour, and I have been the man at the door for twenty-five days.*”

---

And then he said the other thing, and it was the thing he had never once asked her about, and he had had it in his coat since the Wednesday night and there was nowhere in this county to put it.

“*Say what you have not been saying since Tuesday,*” said Ordwin Cape, “*because you have had that coat across your arm for three days and about nine people in this square have watched you not say what is in it.*”

Nobody in that square said anything.

“*Say it out loud or do not say it at all,*” said Sefa Lund, “*and if you say it then say all of it, because a thing that is half said in a market belongs to the market.*”

“*There was a second sheet,*” said Ilyan Vester. “*Folded in four. Nothing on the outside of it. And it was in a different hand from the other one, and the other hand is the small upright one that she makes her fair copies in, and I asked her in a letter not to write in it.*”

“*Go on, and do not stop in the middle of a sentence,*” said Wenna Crale.

“*And the second hand is the one that a letter from a person is in. She told me that. In the first letter. She said: if it is in the small upright hand then anything you say is a thing a clerk in your office can be taken to a room about, and if it is in any other hand then it is a letter from a person and a letter from a person is a thing that a person is asked about.*”

“*Go on and say the rest of that and do not make it a speech,*” said Sefa Lund.

“*She has used the hand of the office for the thing about an office and the hand of a person for the thing about a person, and I told her which to use.*”

“*Say the rest of that and do not stop in the middle of a sentence.*”

“*She has used them the other way round, and she is right about that as well, and I have been wrong about it in a letter four hundred miles off for eight days.*”

About thirty people in a market square did not make a sound.

“*Say the rest of that and do not make it smaller than it is,*” said Ordwin Cape, “*because you have not said what is on it.*”

“*It is four lines long. It is about six years. It is about a refusal that was given six years ago by a household that is dead except one person, and a claim that the refusal be entered upon a register of refusals, and about why it has not been entered.*”

“*Go on, and do not make that a speech,*” said Sefa Lund.

“*Because a refusal is entered by the name of the person who witnessed it, and the person who witnessed it is not findable, and she has been told at a counter that it is not findable four times in six years, and she has not gone and looked for it herself. And if she looks for it and the name is in the range then the refusal was witnessed, and then the claim can be filed, and then her name goes on the claim as the person presenting it.*”

Nobody in that square said anything for about a minute.

“*Say the rest of it and go on to the end of the sentence,*” said Wenna Crale, “*and do not make it into a thing about a stranger in a market.*”

“*And she is the only person in the world who can sign it, and she has not signed it for six years, and she has told nobody, and she has told me, on a road, on the second of two sheets, in a hand I asked her not to use.*”

“*Go on and say the rest of that and go on to the end of the sentence.*”

“*And I am a man who is on nobody’s book four hundred miles off, and there is no form in her building that says what may not be used.*”

The fever was twenty-seven weeks and five days old. A bar four feet long was not turned for the second day at the head of a sluice in a town of four thousand people.

A sheet of paper in a round hand was lying in a room over a nail shop with the water board of Halloway written on it and no person’s name at the foot of it. A second sheet, folded in four with nothing on the outside of it, had come out of a bag in a market town and been read out in a market square in about four minutes.

There is no form in the county of Ostrey that will let anybody give a thing back.

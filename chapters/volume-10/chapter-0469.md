# Chapter 469: The Man From The County Was Due Back On A Thursday And Came On The Wednesday Before It With A Bill For The Frame At Nine Pounds And A Page Out Of A Book That Has Been Sitting In An Office Twenty-Two Miles Off Since The Year Before Last, And The Office Is Not The Bench And Has No Power Over Anything Here

Second day of the eighty-ninth week. His two hundred and nineteenth morning. Sixty-nine days after the settlement. The fever twenty-six weeks and four days. Twenty-three days since the division. Seventeen days in this town.

The stools were put away in the Stone House and the fire was lit anyway, because it is a stone room and a hearth in a stone room is the only warm thing in Halloway. The copy of the roll was on the west wall with eleven new lines on it in a copyist’s hand, and about nine people were in the room at about the eighth hour.

He was let in before anybody had said he was coming, which the steward of forty years has not done for a stranger in forty years and which he mentioned out loud before the door was finished opening.

“*Say the whole of it and do not stop in the middle of it,*” said Amos Trill.

“*I was asked to say it first, before I said anything else. In about four years the only thing anybody in this town will remember about that morning is that I let a man of the county into this room at eight in the morning.*”

“*Go on.*”

“*I had let him in at a market on the eighth day after the division and in a lane at midnight on the seventeenth, and both of those were on somebody else’s ground, and this one is mine, and I have not done it since I was thirty-one.*”

Garrick Nye was forty-four and had a bad coat and a broad face and he came in with a sheet of paper folded in four and did not sit down.

“*I was due back on the Thursday,*” he said. “*I have come on the Wednesday, and I have brought two things, and one of them is a bill and one of them is a page, and I have had the page in my coat since the Monday.*”

“*Say why the Wednesday and not the Thursday.*”

“*Because on the Friday I went back to Kerby and got it copied, and if I had gone home and slept and come on the Thursday I would have had to look at it on the Thursday morning, and I did not want to do that. I have been in this county a long time and I have never once been a man who comes in with a thing, and I would like it written down that a page in a coat is a thing and I carried it here instead of going home.*”

Nobody wrote it down, because the clerk was not in the room yet.

---

He put the bill on the table first. It was the back of a form of query, with a printed head on the other side of it, and the head said that the opinion of the officer named was asked upon a return numbered. The number was on it, and the day on it was the day of two years ago.

“*Say what the form is and do not make it smaller than it is,*” said Perdy Sallow.

“*It is a form of query of the office of the county surveyor at Kerby, which is twenty-two miles off and on a road. I answered it two years ago. My answer is on this side, in my own hand. The return is on the other side, and the return is not my hand and is not mine.*”

He turned it round.

“*Read the return out in this room and do not do it in an office voice, because it is a return and not a book, and a return is written by a man with a rule in about a day.*”

“*It says: Halloway sluice. Frame of oak, reported at sixty years. North heel sounded to a depth of nine inches; the timber is gone at the bottom and is not to be relied upon. To be re-bedded on stone on the north side. Estimated cost of a new heel at the county figures, nine pounds.*”

He put a finger on the last line and stopped there, and about nine people in a stone room waited.

“*Say the rest of it and do not leave the cost out of it in a room where the cost is the whole of it,*” said Ilyan.

“*That is all of it. It is eleven lines and it is the whole of what the county has said about the north heel of that frame in two years, and it is the whole of what the county has ever said about it, and there is not one other page in that book about Halloway.*”

---

And then Ordwin Cape, who was sixty-one and who had done a sum of his own in a lane twice in a fortnight and been short both times, said the sentence that ended the argument before it started.

“*Say the whole of it and say it in this room, because I have done it four times now and the fourth time is the one I would like somebody else to check.*”

He had the account in his own hand and he had had it since the second hour.

“*Say the other half of it as well, and say the first half slowly, because I have done this sum four times in a fortnight and I was wrong in front of a stranger on one of them.*”

“*Go on.*”

“*The water fund of this town is thirty-four pounds and fourteen shillings a year, and it has been that for eleven years. It is thirty-five pounds and six shillings and eightpence since Sunday morning, when a steward of forty years corrected a roll in four minutes.*”

“*Say the rest of it and do not make the fourteen pounds do any work it cannot do.*”

“*The fourteen pounds of the scouring is not out of that. It was put on the eighty lots at three and a halfpence a lot and charged in the roll of this year, and I have known since the Friday before this one that it was on the lots and I have said since the Friday the week before that it was out of the water, and a man from the county was in the room when I said it.*”

“*Then say the sum, and say it in this room and let nine people check it.*”

“*The water rate is twopence a quarter, and the board’s money is the quarter’s. This quarter’s money, on one thousand and sixty households, is eight pounds and sixteen shillings and eightpence. The heel is nine pounds.*”

Nobody in that room said anything for about nine seconds.

“*Say the other half of that and say the nineteen in it.*”

“*The nineteen houses put on the roll on Sunday bring in twelve shillings and eightpence between them for the whole year, at eight pence a household, and the heel is nine pounds.*”

“*Say the rest of that, and do not leave the twenty-eight pence a quarter out of it, because I have had that figure wrong twice this morning.*”

“*The money in the board’s hand is a year’s money divided into four, and the figure a woman of thirty-four proved on Monday is a year’s money divided into four as well, and the two of them differ by what the roll takes out and not by anything to do with the nineteen. Twenty-two households stand in that row and not nineteen, and eleven rows in this town are entered as land and are not households at all, and that is twenty-four pence on three doors and eighty-eight pence on eleven rows, which is a hundred and twelve pence a year, which is twenty-eight pence a quarter.*”

“*Say the rest of it and go on to the end of it.*”

“*So the true quarter is twenty-eight pence more than the one in my hand and is eight pounds and nineteen shillings, and the heel is nine pounds, and it is short by a shilling instead of by three and fourpence, and there is no way of writing that column in which the heel comes out of it.*”

“*Say the other half of that,*” said Sefa Lund.

“*The other half is that the heel is not for the nineteen and it is not for the row and it is not for the water. I have stood behind that since a room over a taproom fourteen days ago, where a woman of thirty-eight said it out loud and the rest of us said nothing and let it go round the room. The heel is for this town, and this town has eight pounds and sixteen shillings and eightpence in its hand, and a frame that is going, and if the board does the other thing it does not do this thing.*”

“*And if the board does this thing,*” said Perdy Sallow.

“*Then it does not do the other thing until the autumn, and I am not going to stand in a room and say which of the two a man of sixty-one should choose about a town of four thousand people in the worst flood.*”

---

The page was the second thing, and he did not have it out for another eleven minutes, and he made nine people wait for it.

“*Say why,*” said Amos Trill. “*You have had it in your coat since the Monday and you have been in this room a quarter of an hour.*”

“*Because the bill is mine. I drew it up out of a return and I put my hand to it. If a man of the county puts a figure on a table in a stone room then the county has come in with a thing, and I have spent this whole fortnight telling nine people that I am not that man, and I would like to be allowed to do it in the right order.*”

“*Then do it in the right order, and say what the order is, because I have been the steward of this town for forty years and I do not know it.*”

“*The order is this. The county made a return two years ago. The return is in a book. The book is twenty-two miles off in a room, and any person in this county may walk to that room and ask to see that page.*”

“*Say the rest of it.*”

“*I have not shown you the page because I have not been asked for the page. I have been asked for the frame, and I have been asked for nine pounds, and I have been asked for the second time that it is a season. I have answered all three. I have been keeping the page for the fourth thing, and the fourth thing is somebody asking me what the county has.*”

Nobody in that room had said it, and then Ilyan Vester said it.

“*Say the rest of that and do not stop in the middle of it,*” said Garrick Nye. “*Say it to the room and not to me, because I have been in this room about a quarter of an hour and you have not once asked me what the county has, and I want everybody to hear that said before the page comes out.*”

Ilyan said it, and it cost him about four seconds to find and about a year to have earned.

“*What the county has.*”

He put his hand flat on the stone of the table, which is the thing he does, and it did not help.

“*I had you in a lane on the eleventh day after the division with the gates shut and about two hundred people in it, and I asked you for a minute. I had you in a lane at midnight on the seventeenth with a straight edge and a nail and about twenty people in it, and I asked you for a minute. I asked you twice for a minute, and both times I had a man in front of me who worked for a county, and neither time did I ask him what the county had.*”

“*Say why you did not, and do not make it a virtue.*”

“*Because you are a man with a county, and asking a man with a county what his county has is asking him to be a second thing as well as the first one. I have spent seventeen days in this town refusing to make anybody carry a thing for me, and I have been so careful about it that I have never once said out loud, in a room, in four hundred yards of lane, ask him what he has got.*”

“*Say the half of that which is the other half.*”

“*The other half is that a frame is going. I had it in a lane twice, and a foot of rain in it, and I spent both times asking for a book instead of asking a man. There is a page in a room twenty-two miles off that says nine pounds and it has been saying it for two years. The reason it has not been said in this room is me.*”

---

The clerk came in at about the ninth hour, and the page came out after she came in, because a man of the county had worked out in four seconds that a thing nobody can be asked about is not worth the paper, and he was right about it and had been right about it for two years.

The page was a copy and not the return, and the copy had a head on it that the return had not had.

*Copy of a return of the office of the county surveyor at Kerby, made in the year before last, on the sluice at Halloway. Certified a true copy at Kerby in the year before last by the clerk of that office, and the clerk’s name is at the foot of it, and it is not a hand that anybody in this room has ever seen.*

“*Say the rest of that and go on to the end of it,*” said Wenna Crale, with her pen up.

“*That is all the head on it. There is no seal. There is a number and a day and the word *copied* and the name of a clerk I have never spoken to.*”

“*And what does the office do.*”

“*It makes a return and puts it in a book. That is the whole of what the office is, and I have been its man for two years and I have never once been asked to do anything else.*”

“*Say the other half of that and say it in this room, and say it in your own mouth, because a man of forty-four has been a very careful man for seventeen days in this town and I would like to know what he is.*”

Garrick Nye looked at the fire for a moment.

“*The office of the county surveyor at Kerby is not a bench. It cannot order your board to do a thing. It cannot make anybody in this town answer a question. It cannot fine you, and it cannot rate you, and it cannot put a name in a book of yours, and it has no power over the frame, over the roll, over the sluice, or over any person in this room.*”

“*Say the rest of it.*”

“*It has a book. And the book has been in a room twenty-two miles off for two years with a page in it. The page says the north heel of a board’s frame is not to be relied upon, and that a new heel is nine pounds.*”

“*Say the rest of it, because that is not the end of the sentence.*”

“*No law in that county makes anybody show you the page. The reason nobody in this town has seen it is that nobody in this town has walked twenty-two miles and asked.*”

The fever was twenty-six weeks and four days old, and about nine people were in a cold stone room with the stools put away, and on the table in front of them lay a sheet of paper with a printed head on it, and the head said the return it was a copy of had been made in the year before last, and the sheet itself was in a press in a town twenty-two miles off where any person in this county might ask for it on any working day of any week.

Nobody in that room had asked, and a man of thirty-nine at the bottom of a market square had about nine hundred names in a book of his own that nobody had asked him about either, and both of those facts were now in books, and a frame at the top of a lane was still being wound twice a day by a woman of thirty-eight with a bad hip.

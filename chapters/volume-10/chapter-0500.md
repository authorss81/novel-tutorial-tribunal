# Chapter 500: The Fifth Day Of The Ninety-Third Week, And A Saturday Which Is The Hundredth Day After The Settlement, And A Page Comes Back With Two Names In A Witness Line

Fifth day of the ninety-third week. His two hundred and fiftieth morning. One hundred days after the settlement. The fever thirty-one weeks and no days. Fifty-four days since the division. Forty-eight days in this town.

The Stone House stood with the door open from the ninth hour. About nine people of the town sat within, and about forty-one stools stood ready for the House if called, and about two hundred people moved in the square outside. The charter lay in the press with six headings and a seventh that was an exit, and the seventh heading was a line anybody can open. A bar four feet long at the head of the sluice had not been turned for twenty-four days. The wheel at the mill had been slow twenty-three days completed.

Two things came true on this day and one of them was about a man of thirty-one in a market town.

The first was the compact. Five towns held to six lines. The town remained outside central control and that was a fact about a market town of four thousand people on a river and not a victory. The charter was read out in the stone room with the door open. Its exit was the seventh heading. Nobody in that room was grateful and nobody was ever going to be.

Wenna Crale read it standing in the voice she uses for minutes. Perdy Sallow sat with his hands on his knees. Sefa Lund stood by the wall. Mab Harrow sat by the window. Amos Trill stood by the press with his bag at his feet. Ilyan Vester sat at the end of the long table about four feet from where Wenna stood.

“*Read the seventh and read it plain,*” said Ordwin Cape.

“*Any household or person of this town, being entered or not entered, may leave the water covenant on giving a day notice at the Stone House, without forfeit of dwelling, and no question shall be put to character, rate or household,*” said Wenna.

A man in the square shouted a question through the door. A woman on a stool answered it. A child ran across the light and back. The reading ended and the room went back to its work.

The second thing came at the eleventh hour. A carrier came into the square off the road that runs four hundred miles to the building where a woman of twenty-eight sits. He carried a page folded in four with nothing on the outside of it. It was a page from a register in the building four hundred miles off where a woman of twenty-eight sits. It carried an entry of a refusal. The refusal was one a household in that county gave six years ago that is dead except one person.

Wenna took it to the table and opened it where all could see. About nine people in that room looked at the page. Four of them can be asked about it and he cannot.

At the foot of the entry stood a witness line with two names in it. One of the two was a name of this town. The other was a hand that was not of this town. A man of thirty-one had seen that hand before because he put it there himself on a form about a town of ninety thousand people. He tells nobody what it is and there is no form in the county of Ostrey that says he shall.

Ilyan looked at the line and felt the blood leave his face. His want had been to know, and now knowing sat before him in two names, and he could not speak either of them. The cost was to carry the sight alone among nine people, four of whom could be asked and would be.

Perdy bent over the page. Ordwin leaned in beside him. Sefa kept her eyes on Ilyan for a long moment, then looked away.

“*Name what it is and name only that,*” said Perdy Sallow.

“*It is a page from a register,*” said Wenna Crale. “*It carries an entry with a witness line. The line holds two names. There is no way of telling from this town whether the name of the person who witnessed a refusal six years ago is in that range.*”

The Book hung nine feet up over a fire on the north beam and had not been off that beam and was not read out again. It was not the witness line of the Book and it was not read out of the Book.

The page was put in the press beside the charter, where any person of the town might read it. The room emptied slowly. The square thinned. The gate at the head of the sluice stood open on two hinges with water going through it.

Ilyan walked out last with the line burned behind his eyes. A town of four thousand people had learned in six weeks that a book is a door and had not opened one.

The fever was thirty-one weeks and no days old. One hundred days after the settlement had come on a Saturday. The witness line held two names.

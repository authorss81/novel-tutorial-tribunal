# Chapter 463: The Sleeper On The Frame Has Gone At The North Heel And A Man Of The County Brings A Rule To A Lane At Eleven O’clock At Night And Cannot Get A Word Of It Into A Minute And Says So Out Loud, And A Woman Of Thirty-Eight Will Not Have It Read In A Lane, And It Goes On The Back Of A Paper She Struck A Line Out Of On The Wednesday

Third day of the eighty-eighth week. His two hundred and thirteenth morning. Sixty-three days after the settlement. The fever twenty-five weeks and five days. Seventeen days since the division. Eleven days in this town.

The sluice is wound at the sixth hour and at the twelfth, and it is wound whether the gates are shut or open, because the frame has to be wound and the frame is the board’s.

The wheel is nine feet across and the bar is four feet long and it takes two people and about an hour, and on the Thursday night of the third day of rain it was wound by a woman of thirty-eight with a bad hip and by a man of sixty-one, in the wet, on a frame with a rule laid across it.

There were about nine of the row on the head of the sluice and about eleven from the town who had come up the lane in the dark because the sound of the bar carries, and about twenty people in that lane in the rain at the eleventh hour at night.

Garrick Nye was one of the eleven and he had a rule in his coat, which is not a thing a man of the county carries, and he had carried it three miles.

---

“*Say what you came up here for and do not stand in front of it,*” said Sefa Lund, from the bar.

“*To look at the north heel in the dark with a straight edge on it, because a thing I have measured in daylight twice is worth one more look at night, and because a man who has said a thing is a year away and cannot find a way of stopping it has got nothing else to do on a Thursday.*”

“*Say the rest of it and do not stop in the middle of it,*” said Mab Harrow, from the second doorway.

“*The rest of it is that I have brought a rule and I have come eleven miles on a cart that was going anyway and I would like to be asked a question, because I have not been asked one by anybody in this county for two years and I have got four questions of my own.*”

Nobody in that lane asked him one, and he went and laid the rule on the frame and worked for about twenty minutes in the rain without saying anything, which is the thing he does.

---

The frame is the board at the head of the sluice. It has two posts and a beam and a bed of timber called the sleeper that the two posts stand on, and the sleeper is the oldest timber in it and it is sixty years old and it is bedded in the ground at the north side.

He put the straight edge across the top of the beam and ran his thumb down it. Then he did it again from the other end. Then he got a nail out of his coat, put the head of it in the joint where the beam sits on the north post, and showed it to about twenty people in the rain.

“*Say what that is and say how you know,*” said Sefa Lund.

“*That is a nail head and it is three-eighths of an inch proud of the timber it was driven into nine years ago, and I drove it, and the timber has gone down under it.*”

“*Say the rest of that and say it out loud in this lane, because there are about twenty people in it and four of them are going to be asked about it.*”

“*The north side of that frame has dropped three-eighths of an inch since I was here nine years ago and the south side has not moved at all, because the south side is bedded on stone and the north side is bedded on wood, and the wood is in the ground and the ground is the Fen.*”

The bar stopped for about four seconds and then went on.

“*Say how long,*” said Ordwin Cape, from the rail.

“*On the Friday before last I told you it was two years. I am not standing in that lane to un-say a number in front of twenty people. I am telling you that I have measured it and the number was two years and it is a season, and the reason I am able to say that out loud tonight is a nail and a straight edge and no arguing at all.*”

---

Then Ilyan did the thing he does, and about twenty people in that lane heard him get a shape ready for a room.

“*Say it and say who it is for,*” said Sefa Lund, without turning round from the bar.

“*Two years is a season and three-eighths of an inch is a nail head, and neither of those is a thing a person can be asked about. If you are going to say a number about a board’s frame in a lane at eleven o’clock at night, then it wants to be in the minute of the water board, in the hand of the chair, with the day on it, and I have said for nine weeks that a number in a book is the only kind of number that survives a Monday.*”

“*Say the other half of that and say it now, because I have been turning the bar for fifty minutes.*”

“*The other half is that you have been turning a bar for fifty minutes in the rain on a frame that has dropped three-eighths of an inch in nine years, and there is no minute in this county that can make anybody mend it, and I have known that since a lane nine days ago and I have said it once in a lane and not twice.*”

Ordwin Cape said the rest of it, and he said it without stopping, and it was the sentence the man from the county had said in that same lane nine days before.

“*The board does not take paper from the county. And the only person who can get a thing into a minute is a clerk, and she is paid two pounds a year, and she does not want it. He said that in this lane on the Friday before last and he has not said it since and that is the one thing in this row he has done right.*”

---

And then Sefa Lund took her hands off the bar, which she does not do, and let it stand where it was with the weight of it on the two rungs, and turned round into the lane.

“*Say what you are about to do and do not do it,*” said Ilyan.

“*I am going to say no to you in front of about twenty people and I am going to say why, and I have never once in nine years had to.*”

She put her wet hat on her head and took it off again.

“*I will not have the frame read out in a lane. Not tonight and not by you.*”

“*Say the reason and say it in your own mouth and not in mine.*”

“*Because a thing said in a square belongs to whoever is standing in the square, and I have watched three of them go out of that square with my sentence in it and not my mouth on it, and I have had enough of that this month to last me a year.*”

She was about four feet from him and she was not loud, and about twenty people in that lane could hear both of them.

“*And a lane is not better. A lane is only different, because there is a steward stood in the square and there is nobody stood in a lane. Everything I have said in that square has been said twice since. Everything I have said in a lane has been said once, and then it was not mine any more, it was just out.*”

“*Then say where it is yours and stop.*”

“*Here. In a lane, at night, with the people who were in the room. That is the whole of it and I have known it for nine years and I have never had a reason to say it to anybody who could use it.*”

---

Nobody in that lane said anything for about nine seconds, and the rain came off the wood above the sluice, and the bar stood with the weight of the water on it.

Then the man from the county said the sentence that turned the whole night, and he said it to the bar and not to either of them.

“*Say the other half of that and say it in your own mouth,*” said Garrick Nye, “*because a woman of thirty-eight has just told you the half of the law of this town and the half of the law of this town is on a board nine feet up over a fire, and there is nobody in this lane who can read the other side of it.*”

“*Say it and do not stop in the middle of it,*” said Sefa Lund.

“*The other half is that a lane is a room with the roof off, and the reason a thing said in a lane is yours is not that nobody stands in it. It is that nobody stands in it who can be asked about it afterwards. And that is the whole of the difference, and it is a difference about the book and not about the lane, and it has been a difference about the book since the audit nine years back.*”

---

So they had twenty minutes and a rain and a board’s frame, and they had nowhere to put the number.

The county could not put it in a minute. The House would not touch the water, and had said so in four lines on the Monday, and the House’s copyist had refused to carry a thing off this row’s account on the Wednesday and had said why in a room and had been right.

And Ilyan asked for the one thing he has been able to ask for in eleven days, and he asked it in about four seconds, and it is the shape of the whole of his life in this town.

“*Say that to the minute,*” he said, “*not mine. Hers. I am asking her to enter that a man of the county measured the north heel of a board’s frame at the eleventh hour on the third night of the rain with a straight edge and a nail, and found it three-eighths of an inch down in nine years and says it is a season instead of two years. And I am asking her to enter it because a thing in a minute is a thing a person can be asked about, and I will not have that number in a lane and in twenty mouths.*”

Sefa Lund looked at him for a while with the rain running off the brim of the hat in her hand.

“*No,*” she said.

“*Say the reason.*”

“*Because in a minute it is the board’s minute. And the board is nine men, and the board has not asked, and the board will be asked on the twenty-fifth day, and a number in a board’s minute on the sixth day is a number the board owns on the seventh. And I have watched a thing get into a book in this town and come out of it in nine weeks as somebody else’s sentence and I am not doing that to a frame at eleven o’clock at night.*”

“*Then where does it go,*” said Ilyan, “*and say it fast, because it is going to rain harder and there are about twenty people standing here.*”

And Sefa Lund, who has kept a sluice for nine years in a town with one press and one woman who can read an old hand and no office whatever in this county, looked at the man from the county and then at a stranger against a wall, and said the only thing there was.

“*It goes on the back of a paper,*” she said, “*and there is exactly one paper in this county with a day and a room and two witnesses on it. It is the one you wrote on a Wednesday in a room over this taproom. It is in a press in the Stone House against the west wall, and it is the only paper in this town anybody can be asked about.*”

---

So Garrick Nye wrote it on the back of the statement of the Wednesday at about the twelfth hour, standing in the lane with a blanket over his coat.

He wrote the day and the hour and the rule and the nail and the three-eighths of an inch and the nine years. He wrote that in his judgment the north heel of the frame at the sluice at Halloway is a season and not two years. He signed it. It is the second thing that man has written in this town and the first one anybody has kept.

And then Sefa Lund took the paper off him and turned it over and read the front of it, all of it, standing in the rain with about twenty people watching a woman read a document in a lane at midnight, and it took her about four minutes.

The fourth paragraph was the one she had struck out with two strokes on the Wednesday morning and signed across with her own hand. It said that in his judgment the board ought to carry the whole of the cost of the scouring out of the water fund, and that the households of the Fen ought to be recompensed for the nine days.

Nobody in that lane had read it since the Wednesday, because it is a document and documents are read by clerks, and the clerk was standing in the lane.

She wrote two lines under Garrick Nye’s hand, and they are the two lines that cost her.

“*Attested as to the measuring: Sefa Lund, at the sluice, at the twelfth hour, in the lane, being in the room when it was said.*”

And that is the name of a woman who is not a household, at the foot of a page that says in the other half of it that the board ought to carry the whole cost out of the water fund. It is in a press against a west wall, in a stone room, in a town of four thousand people, unlocked, which any person of the town may read.

And in four hundred years of that building, nobody has ever read the back of anything.

The fever was twenty-five weeks and five days old, and the third night of rain was going on a roof over a lane, and a fact about a board’s frame was in this county in exactly one place, and that place was the wrong side of a leaf.

Nobody had thanked her. She had a key to gates that were open, and a bad hip, and a bar that takes an hour to turn twice a day, and her own name at the foot of a thing she had refused nine days before.

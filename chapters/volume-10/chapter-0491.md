# Chapter 491: The Third Day Of The Ninety-Second Week, And A Thursday On Which About Nine People In A Stone Room With The Door Open Work Out That An Instrument And A Decision Are Two Different Things, And A Woman Of Thirty-Four Who Is Not A Head Of Household Says It Out Loud

Third day of the ninety-second week. His two hundred and forty-first morning. Ninety-one days after the settlement. The fever twenty-nine weeks and five days. Forty-five days since the division. Thirty-nine days in this town.

The Stone House has a hearth in the corner and a press against the west wall, and the door of the building is open and has been open since the fourth hour. About nine people were in the room at the ninth hour and about twenty more were in the square doing nothing in particular. The flagstones inside the door were wet to the depth of a hand’s breadth where they had been tracked in and dried and tracked in again, and there was a rim of it dried white along the line where the door stops swinging.

The fire was not lit. It is the wrong hour for a fire in a room that size with the door open on a wet morning, and every person in it knew that, and two of them had said so out loud before the ninth hour.

A bar four feet long at the head of the sluice had not been turned at the sixth hour or at the twelfth hour for fifteen days. The wheel at the mill had been slow fourteen days completed. The two of those are different counts and the difference between them is the whole of it, and it is now on the roll in a minute, and about nine people in this room knew the difference and about four of them could have said which of the two numbers was the larger.

The roll for the autumn is made in two days, on the forty-seventh day after the division, in an office over a shop at the north side of the square, by a man of sixty-one working alone.

Wenna Crale stood at the long table under the north beam with the instrument of the fortieth day spread flat in front of her. The head of it carries the day on it. Her hand is at the foot of it. Under the head there are two parts, and the first is the four instruments of this town with the holder of each named, and the second is the account of what the row at the Fen was told and by whom and on what day, given by a woman who is not a household.

Nobody had turned it over since it went into the press. A man on the second stool put his palm flat on the corner of it to turn it, and she put her own hand on the same corner first, and he took his away and wiped the damp off it on his breeches.

Ilyan Vester sat at the end of the long table with his bad leg set out to one side. He had come down the lane in the rain and the water had got into the seam of the boot at the ankle and he had not taken the boot off. He had been carrying one want since the Saturday and had not said it to anybody. The want was that the instrument of the fortieth day should turn out to have been a mistake, so that he could go back and make it again in a form that binds.

Perdy Sallow took the fourth stool and set his reed down square with the edge of the table before he sat down at all, which he does.

He asked for the head and the day on it to be read, and then both parts, and the second part not to be hurried, because the second part is the one a person gave away.

She read it standing, in the voice she uses for the minutes, and it took about four minutes, and the room let her get all the way through it. The second part is nine lines long and it took the woman it describes nine years to keep, and about nine people in that stone room heard themselves in it, and about four of the nine had known her a long time. The rain came up the step and stopped and started again while she was reading.

Ilyan had read that second part twice before. He had heard it in the square on the thirty-ninth day to about two hundred people, and once in a room over a taproom, and he was the reason it existed in the shape it existed in, because a woman of thirty-four had said in that room that a book is a door and had gone and found out what the only book in this town was.

---

The question in that room on the Thursday morning was not asked by anybody for the better part of an hour. It sat in the room the way water sits in a race. Three of the men who came in had brought it with them. At the tenth hour a man of about thirty-four came in off the square with a bill of sale inside his coat, and he put it on the table face down and kept his hand flat on it, and that is how anybody in that room knew what he had come with.

He holds the ninth lot upon the Four Hundred. The sale carried the water of the Hallow from the sluice downward along with the land, and he has had the paper in that coat for six weeks and has not known what it is for.

“*Put down what the room is for,*” Ordwin Cape said from the rail. He is sixty-one and had come in out of the wet with his bar-hook over his shoulder and had not put it down. “*Not what it is about. What it is for.*”

Perdy laid his hands flat on the wood.

“*To find out what forty-one men can do about a bar four feet long,*” he said.

Ordwin Cape did not let it stand.

“*That is a thing they cannot do, and you know it, and they know it. Name the thing in front of it.*”

Perdy did not answer for a while. A woman near the door put the sleeve of her coat over her mouth and left it there.

“*To find out what this room can do with a piece of paper,*” he said at last.

Wenna turned the instrument a quarter round without lifting it off the table. “*Then the first thing this room can do with it is read it, and the second thing is find out what a piece of paper is.*”

The hearth had gone cold and somebody put a log on it anyway, and it smoked.

---

Ivo Cray turned the bill of sale over and pushed it along the table toward the middle, and he did not take his hand off it until it was out of his own reach, which is what a man does with a paper he has not finished deciding about.

“*I have been up to the steward twice about this paper and I want to say why, and then I want it lying in front of nine people instead of in my coat.*”

Nobody interrupted him. That is worth recording, because about nine people were in that room, a man had the floor, and not one of them took it off him.

“*I bought a lot in the week the common was sold. The paper says the water of the Hallow from the sluice downward goes with the land. I did not know that when I bought it and I would not have bought it if I had known, and that does not make the paper wrong, because the paper is right.*”

He put two fingers on the bill of sale and turned it so that the room could see the seal, and then took his hand away and put it in his coat, which is a man deciding to stop touching a thing.

“*I have carried it about for six weeks on the assumption that a man would come along and tell me it was not true. No man came. And I have had six weeks to work out the answer to that, and here it is: nobody in this county is going to come and tell me that paper is untrue, because the paper being true is the whole of what a man who owns five acres wants.*”

Perdy Sallow had picked up his reed and put it down again without having written anything.

“*If the water does not go with the land then my lot is worth two pounds less than I gave for it, and there is no man in the world who wants my lot two pounds cheaper than that.*”

A man on the third stool laughed once, and stopped, and put his hand over his mouth afterwards.

“*So I have brought it down here,*” Ivo Cray said, “*and I would like somebody to tell me what a lot is.*”

The steward of forty years had been standing at the back of the room since the ninth hour without sitting down. He is sixty-one and he caused clause one to be struck in the week of the division and he was right about it and has never once pretended otherwise.

“*A lot is five acres and a rate on it,*” Amos Trill said. “*That is what the county calls it. It is not what I call it. I called it a field when I signed the division, and I have been wrong to use the other word every day for nine years, and I have used it every day for nine years.*”

He said it with no weight on it whatever, and went out into the square through the same open door everybody had come in by, and left his hat on the bench.

---

The want came out of the man at the end of the table about half an hour after that, and it came out of him because the room had arrived at the place where somebody was going to say something and he had been waiting for it since the Saturday.

“*I would like it to have been a mistake,*” Ilyan Vester said.

About nine people turned round on their stools.

Ordwin Cape asked him to say what he would like it to have been, which is the question a room asks when it has worked out that a man is about to ask it for something.

“*I would like the instrument of the fortieth day to have been a mistake, so that we can go back and make it in a form that binds. It was made in a room with no procedure and no standing and no chair with power. There was nothing behind it. If it had been a mistake we could have had it right in an hour, and we should have had it right on the Monday, and instead we have got a sheet of paper that nine people in a market town are applauding.*”

He was not looking at Wenna. He was looking at the middle of the table where a bill of sale lay face up for the first time in six weeks.

“*It was not a mistake,*” Ivo Cray said. He said it flat and without raising his voice, and about four people in that room moved as though somebody had taken hold of them. “*A thing asked for by everybody is not nothing. About thirty people asked for that hearing. About twenty of them stayed four hours. A man who needs a thing of his own to be wrong has not understood what the thing was for.*”

Ilyan asked him to say what the instrument was for, because he had been in nine rooms in this town and nobody had told him, and he put the paper out of his own reach while he said it, which is what a man does with a thing he is about to be told the price of.

“*It was for this town to see itself in a thing. That is all. You want me to tell you the sheet is nothing so that you may go and make another one. Make it. It will be a better sheet, and it will be the eighth piece of paper in a town of four thousand people, and there is no body in this county that has to answer any of them, including the one you would make, and including the one that is lying on this table with my name in it.*”

---

A woman of thirty-four came in out of the rain while that was going on and had to be given a stool, and the only one free was a bench, and a man on the bench had to get up and stand against the wall to make room. She rents the second room in the fourth house and is not a head of household and has never been in the House of this town in her life. Her sleeves were damp to the elbow, because she had washed at the sluice that morning before the water came.

She had refused nothing in six weeks.

“*An instrument and a decision are two different things,*” Mab Harrow said.

Nobody had asked her. She said it to the middle of the table, in the ordinary voice, the way a person gives back an answer that has been asked on somebody else’s behalf.

Perdy told her to put the nine years in it, and she did, and it took her about ninety seconds, and the room let her get all the way through it without a sound.

It is worth setting down what she said, because it is the sentence the whole of the last six weeks of this town turns on, and it was said once, by a person with no standing, in a room with a door open.

“*For nine years this board has made decisions. It set the scouring at fourteen pounds on the tenth day. It set the rate. On the thirtieth day it put nineteen houses out of the water, in the words of this covenant, correctly, and four of the nine said out loud in that room that they wished it were not so.*”

The man at the end of the table, who had voted on the thirtieth day, moved his leg out of the way of a man going past and then did not put it back.

“*Those were decisions. They bound. I have washed this morning at a sluice that four of those men decided about, and there is water in it, and no person in this town has the standing to say that is unfair.*”

She put her damp sleeves flat on her knees and left them there.

“*The sheet of the fortieth day binds nothing and names everything. That is what an instrument is. One of them tells people what to do. The other one tells people what was done. This town has had the first for nine years and has made the second in six weeks, and it will go on making the second for as long as it is allowed to, and the water will do what it does in the meantime.*”

The man at the end of the table put both hands on the wood and left them there.

Wenna said the other half, which is that there is no form in the county of Ostrey that says a body must produce a decision, and that a board can sit and answer correctly and still leave the matter exactly where it found it.

Both of the answers of the thirty-seventh day were right. One was that the board has no power to appoint a person. The other was that it cannot promise a person will be asked nothing touching his character or his rate or his household, and the reason it cannot promise that is what the board itself did on the thirtieth day.

Ordwin Cape had not moved from the rail in an hour.

“*Then the four instruments are not a poor substitute for an office,*” he said. “*They are what a town has instead of one, until somebody makes one.*”

“*And the somebody is the whole of the next nine days,*” Mab Harrow said, “*and not one of us knows what he is called, and I have been in this town nine years and I have never seen one.*”

---

About four people round that table said out loud that that was right. A man with a wet sack said aye and meant it. A woman by the press said it was the plainest thing anybody had said in the room all morning. Two more gave a short word each, one of them an old man who had come in for the fire and stayed four hours for the argument.

About five did not. One man looked at the beam the whole time. One kept his eyes on the bill of sale in the middle of the table. One got up and went and stood in the doorway where the rain blew in, and stayed there.

One refolded a list he had brought with him and put it away in his coat. And one said out loud that a woman who was not a head of household had told that room what a woman who was a head of household could not, that this was a bad sign and not a good one, and that he would like it written down that he had said so. His name is at the foot of the instrument of the fortieth day and nobody has taken it out.

No minute was taken on the Thursday morning. No body sat. Nobody in that room can be asked what they thought, and that is not because anybody refused to answer. It is because there was nothing to ask them under.

---

He went back up the lane in the rain with his bad leg going badly on wet flags and the water finding the seam of the boot again, and by the time he had climbed the stair above the nail shop he had the whole of the morning worked out and none of it was any use to him.

He had asked nine people in a stone room to call his own work wrong so that he could be useful a second time.

A man of thirty-four had refused him in front of witnesses, without cruelty, and had been exactly right, and being right at a man in front of nine people is the hardest kind of being right there is.

The cost of the Thursday morning was that he would have to work from what stood, and not from a second attempt, and that a woman of thirty-four with no standing in this town had said out loud in a room with a door open the one sentence that six weeks of his work had been walking round, and that he had not said it, and that he was not going to be able to say it later either.

The instrument stood. The decisions stood apart from it, nine years of them, binding, correct, and four of the men who voted on the thirtieth day were in that room, and one of them had not slept well since and had said so out loud in a room on the fortieth day and had been thanked for nothing.

A copyist of thirty-four is paid two pounds a year and is the only person in that town whose name goes at the foot of what she does. A man of thirty-nine keeps a second book of about nine hundred names shut to a hand’s width under his own counter. A woman of thirty-eight keeps a sluice and has a key to gates that stand open and no authority over anything. A man of fifty-four holds the water list that goes by tenement. Four instruments, and not one of them can be sent for, and not one of them can be made to do a thing by wanting it.

He would have to work from that, and not from a second attempt. There is no form in the county of Ostrey that says a man may go back and do a thing again, and there is no form that says he may not, and both of those facts were in the room and neither of them helped.

The fever was twenty-nine weeks and five days old. The copy of the roll came off its bench beside the door and went out to the middle of the long table, flat, into the light coming in at the open door, put there by a man who could not read a thing standing up and would not say so in front of a room.

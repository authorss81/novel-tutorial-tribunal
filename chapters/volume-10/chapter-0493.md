# Chapter 493: The Fifth Day Of The Ninety-Second Week, And A Saturday On Which The Roll Of This Town Is Made In An Office Over A Shop By A Steward Of Sixty-One Working Alone, And The Figure Is One Thousand And Seventy-Four, And Nobody In The Town Is To Know It For A Year

Fifth day of the ninety-second week. His two hundred and forty-third morning. Ninety-three days after the settlement. The fever thirty weeks and no days. Forty-seven days since the division. Forty-one days in this town.

The market of Halloway is on a Tuesday and this was a Saturday, so the square stood empty in the morning light with the barrows put up at the north side and the stones washed in a broad sweep down the middle where they had been swept every Tuesday for a hundred years. The office over the shop at the north side had its own stair and its own door, and the door was shut, and it stayed shut from the seventh hour until noon.

About nine people waited in the square at a distance from the stair. They did not wait near it. Two of them had brought stools and one had brought a basket with something in it that nobody wanted to talk about. A person not of this town cannot be in a room where a roll is made. That is not a rule anybody made. It is a thing that is true.

He went up the four steps at about the eighth hour and tried the door, and it was locked, and the key was in the steward’s own coat and the steward was behind it.

A woman of about fifty came out of the shop underneath with her sleeves rolled and stood at the bottom of the stair with her arms folded, and she did not ask him who he was, and he did not tell her.

“*He has been in there since the seventh hour and he has not asked for anything,*” she said. “*Not a cup and not the stool from your own shop. You can stand on the step as long as you like and it will be the same at the twelfth hour as it is now.*”

“*I know a person not of this town cannot be in there. I have said that out loud in nine rooms in this town.*”

“*Then you know it and you have come up the steps anyway, and I am not going to say anything about that, and I am going back inside because there is bread coming out of that oven.*”

She went in and shut the lower door and left him on the steps with the square behind him and about nine people watching him not be in a room.

A bar four feet long at the head of the sluice had not been turned at the sixth hour or at the twelfth hour for seventeen days. The wheel at the mill had been slow sixteen days completed. This was the day the roll had been waiting for since the twelfth day after the division, and it had been put off twice, and the first time it had been put off by a man of sixty-one because the list of works was in front of him, and the second time because of rain.

Ilyan Vester stood in the square with his hands in his pockets and watched the shut door. He had come down at the seventh hour on the chance of the door being open and it was shut, and he had stood at the foot of the stair for about two hours. His want that morning was to be in the room. He wanted it the way a man wants a thing he has been told he cannot have, which is more than a man wants a thing nobody has mentioned.

---

Amos Trill went up the stair at the seventh hour with his forms in a leather bag and shut the door behind him.

He laid them out on the desk in the order he has used since he was thirty-one. The rate roll of the year, and the water list that goes by tenement and not by name, and the minute of the forty-second day with a levy in it and a hand at the foot of it, and a sheet of clean paper, and the pen he has used for this for forty years with a nib that has been re-ground so often it is shorter than it was.

He worked alone. That is how a steward works and it is why nobody in that town has ever seen the inside of a roll being made.

He set down each house in the order of the wards, and he checked the ninth ward first because it has been the ninth ward for a long time. He checked each of the nineteen tenements of the Fen as land of the common and not as households, as they were corrected on the thirtieth day. The water list has them rated as property in respect of water. That is a different book, it does not agree with this one, and it was never meant to.

He added the eleven rows that were corrected on the twenty-eighth day. He added the twelfth row with a dead man’s name against it, because the form asks for the head of household and there is a head of household and he is dead, and the steward has never yet struck out a name for being dead and there is nothing in the county of Ostrey that requires him to.

Then he went back to the minute of the forty-second day and entered the levy of three and a halfpence on each of the eighty lots upon the Four Hundred, to be charged in the roll for the autumn and in no other roll. That is the form the board used, and the form is the form.

---

He came down the stair at noon with the bag over his shoulder and the door shut behind him, and about nine people were in the square, and the sun had got as far as the third gutter.

He said it out loud on the step, in the ordinary voice, to nobody in particular, which is a thing a steward of forty years does when he has finished a thing he cannot un-say.

“*It is done, and it is in the forms I have used three times, and the day is on it.*” He put the bag down on the step and did not take his hands off it. “*I have counted houses and I have entered the levy. I have done it correctly. I will not know what else is on that roll until the collection, and the collection is thirteen weeks off, and I could not tell you this morning what I had found if you had asked me at the seventh hour instead of the twelfth.*”

A woman asked him for the number.

He shook his head, and he did it without any unkindness, and she took it well enough.

“*A roll is read from the press when it is copied,*” Amos Trill said. “*Not from my mouth on a stair.*”

Two of the nine followed him part of the way toward the Stone House and then stopped, and one of them said to the other that there was no point, and the other agreed. One went off toward the mill. The rest stood in an empty square on a Saturday morning with nothing at all to carry home.

---

The figures were not in the square and were not in the market, and this is the part of it that is worth setting down, because about nine people stood in that square all morning and one of them was a man of thirty-one who had spent five weeks making sentences about a number.

He learned it standing there, and the learning was the mistake and the cost together and it took the whole of the afternoon to sit down properly.

The one day in five weeks on which the whole of this business was decided had been a day he was not in the town for.

A person not of the town cannot be where a roll is made. He had made about nine sentences a day for thirty-eight days about what the town should do with a number.

The number had been made in a room over a shop between the seventh hour and the twelfth, by a man working alone, on a desk he could not have got to. The door had been shut, and there is no form in the county of Ostrey that says a man not of a town may stand in a room where a roll of that town is made.

And nobody had lied to him. That was the part that was hard to get hold of. Not one person in that square had withheld the figure from him. It had simply never occurred to the steward that the man in the square was a person the figure was owed to, and it would not have occurred to him if it had, because the figure is not his and has never been his and he gave it into a roll and into a press and not into anybody’s mouth.

---

What was on the roll, and the working of every figure on it, in the order the steward wrote them. It is set out here in the same hand as the fifteenth item of the forty-fourth day, and it is the first time in eleven years that the whole of the arithmetic of this town has stood in one place where a person can look at it.

*One thousand and seventy-four houses were counted at the seventh hour of the forty-seventh day after the division, in an office over a shop in a market town of four thousand people.*

*The water rate is twopence a quarter. That is eightpence a household for the year. One thousand and seventy-four times eightpence is eight thousand five hundred and ninety-two pence, which is thirty-five pounds and sixteen shillings for the year, and no pence.*

*A quarter of that is one thousand and seventy-four times twopence, which is two thousand one hundred and forty-eight pence. That is eight pounds and nineteen shillings for the quarter, and no pence.*

*The heel of the frame at the head of the sluice is nine pounds, and the money for it is thirteen weeks off, and the quarter was short of nine pounds before the levy went on at all.*

*The levy is three and a halfpence on each of the eighty lots upon the Four Hundred. Three and a halfpence times eighty is two hundred and eighty pence a year, which is one pound three shillings and fourpence.*

*That is the figure the board entered on the forty-second day. It goes in the roll for the autumn and in no other roll. And a board in this county has always counted it against the quarter it holds in its hand, which is true about the way this town counts and is not a thing anybody has ever said out loud in a room.*

*The quarter on the true count with the levy on it is two thousand one hundred and forty-eight pence plus two hundred and eighty pence, and that is two thousand four hundred and twenty-eight pence. That is ten pounds two shillings and fourpence a quarter, which is two hundred and sixty-eight pence more than the nine pounds the heel costs, and two hundred and sixty-eight pence is one pound two shillings and fourpence.*

*The roll as the town has held it stands at one thousand and fifty-two, which is twenty-two houses short of what was counted. One thousand and fifty-two at the same rate is thirty-five pounds and one shilling and fourpence for the year, and eight pounds fifteen shillings and fourpence for the quarter. That quarter with the same levy on it is nine pounds eighteen shillings and eightpence, which is two hundred and twenty-four pence more than the nine pounds the heel costs, and two hundred and twenty-four pence is eighteen shillings and eightpence.*

*Ten pounds two shillings and fourpence against nine pounds eighteen shillings and eightpence is forty-four pence a quarter.*

*Forty-four pence a quarter is the whole of what this business was about. It is twenty-two houses at eightpence a year, and it is the difference between a figure a man counted at a desk on a Saturday morning and a figure a town has been arguing with since the thirtieth day after the division.*

*The levy that is on the roll to raise it is one pound three shillings and fourpence a year on the eighty lots, and nobody in Halloway will be able to feel any of it until a collection that is thirteen weeks off, which is after the season it was for has come and gone.*

The out-of-roll book was not opened at any point on that Saturday. It is a line, a name, a day, and the water board may at any time enter a household that is not on the roll, and it has never been written in in this town, and it was not written in on the day the roll was made, and there was nobody in that office to write it in.

---

He went up the lane in the afternoon and looked at the sluice, where the water was going through the open gate in a long flat sheet and coming over the low sill on the north side.

Ilyan Vester had wanted to be in a room that morning, and he had spent five weeks in about thirty rooms, and this was the first one he had wanted to be in that he could not be in. The difference between this and the other thirty is that in the other thirty he had been let in.

Sefa Lund was there with her bar-hook over her shoulder. She is thirty-eight and keeps the sluice and is a household of this town. She did not ask him what had passed in the square, and he did not offer it, and they stood about four feet apart looking at the water for a while.

A boy of about fifteen came up the lane at a run with a hoop and went past both of them. Behind him a man of the county came walking slowly with a straight edge in his hand under his coat, going up to look at the north heel in the evening because a man who has measured a thing once will measure it again, and he said good evening to both of them and went on.

The fever was thirty weeks and no days old. The office door over the shop stood shut with the roll inside it and a day on the roll and a hand at the foot of the day, and the empty square held nothing at all except sun on wet stone and a number that was nine weeks too late to be of any use to anybody standing in it.

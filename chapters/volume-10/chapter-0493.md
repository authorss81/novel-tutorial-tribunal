# Chapter 493: The Fifth Day Of The Ninety-Second Week, And A Saturday On Which The Roll For The Autumn Is Made In An Office Over A Shop, And A Steward Of Forty Years Counts Houses

Fifth day of the ninety-second week. His two hundred and forty-third morning. Ninety-three days after the settlement. The fever thirty weeks and no days. Forty-seven days since the division. Forty-one days in this town.

The market is on a Tuesday and this was a Saturday, so the square stood empty in the morning light. About nine people waited by the office over a shop at the north side, talking low. The door of the office stayed shut. A person not of this town cannot be in a room where a roll is made.

A bar four feet long at the head of the sluice had not been turned for seventeen days. The wheel at the mill had been slow sixteen days completed. This was the day the roll had been waiting for since the twelfth day after the division.

Ilyan Vester stood in the square with his hands in his pockets and watched the shut door. His want that morning was to be in the room. He had counted nothing and no character in this town had counted anything out loud, and still he wanted to see the counting done.

Amos Trill went up the stair at the seventh hour with his forms in a bag. He is sixty-one. He set the bag on the desk and laid out the rate roll of the year, the water list that goes by tenement, and the minute of the forty-second day with a levy in it, entered with a day and a hand at the foot of it.

He worked alone, the way a steward works. He set down each house in order of the wards. He checked each tenement of the Fen as land of the common and not as households, as corrected on the thirtieth day. He added the eleven rows corrected on the twenty-eighth day. He entered the levy of three and a halfpence on each of the eighty lots upon the Four Hundred, to be charged in the roll for the autumn and not in any other.

By the eleventh hour the counting was done. The figure was one thousand and seventy-four houses. The year money on it was thirty-five pounds and sixteen shillings. The quarter on it was eight pounds and nineteen shillings. With the levy of three and a halfpence a lot on the eighty lots the quarter was ten pounds two shillings and fourpence. On the roll as the town holds it at one thousand and fifty-two the quarter with the levy is nine pounds eighteen shillings and eightpence. The whole of what the matter had been about was forty-four pence a quarter.

Amos sat back and looked at the figures a long time. He did it correctly and he would not know what was on it, and he said so when he came down the stair at noon.

“*It is done, and it is in the forms I have used three times,*” said Amos Trill in the square to the nine waiting there. “*I have counted houses and entered the levy. I have done it correctly. I will not know what else is on it until the collection, and that is thirteen weeks off.*”

A woman asked him for the number. He shook his head.

“*A roll is read from the press when it is copied,*” said Amos. “*Not from my mouth on a stair.*”

He walked away toward the Stone House with the bag over his shoulder. Two of the nine followed him partway and then stopped. One went off toward the mill. The rest stood in the empty square with nothing to carry home.

Ilyan learned it standing there, and the learning was the mistake and the cost together. The one day in five weeks on which the whole of the business was decided had been a day he was not in the town for, because a person not of the town cannot be where a roll is made. He had spent thirty-eight days making sentences about a number, and the number had been made behind a shut door while he stood outside it.

He went up the lane in the afternoon and looked at the sluice where the water ran through the open gate. Sefa Lund was there with her bar-hook over her shoulder. She did not ask him what had passed in the square, and he did not offer it.

The fever was thirty weeks and no days old. The office door stood shut over the shop, and the empty square held nothing but sun on stone and a number made behind wood.

# Chapter 455: She Asks For A Room And A Witness And Somebody To Write It Down And He Offers To Be The Somebody, And A Woman Of Thirty-Eight Reads His Own Words Back To Him And Strikes One Line Out Of Them In Her Own Hand, And Nobody In That Room Can Say Whether The Nineteen Houses Own The Water Or Are Only Keeping It

Second day of the eighty-seventh week. His two hundred and fifth morning. Fifty-five days after the settlement. The fever twenty-four weeks and four days. Nine days since the division.

He said on Monday morning that he was not the sort of man who comes, and he came at about the ninth hour on the Wednesday and stood at the back of a room over a taproom in a house in the Fenrow and did not say one word for fifty-one minutes.

The room had a table, three chairs, a fire that was out, and a window with the shutters open. It was over a taproom because there is nowhere else in the Fenrow with a table in it that is not somebody’s kitchen.

“*Say your name,*” said Sefa Lund, to the man from the county, before anything else happened, and the man said it before she had finished, which told her something.

“*Garrick Nye. I am forty-four. I am of the county and I have scoured the Hallow twice, once nine years ago and once about twenty years before that, and I have come this morning because the steward wrote to the county and the county sent me.*”

“*Go on.*”

“*I have got a paper and I have got my own figures and I will give them to anybody who writes them down.*”

---

He was right about the race. About the whole of it, in the way a man is right about a thing he has measured.

“*There is a race from the sluice down to the tail of the mill, and it is a mile and a bit, and it has not been scoured in nine years. A scouring means the gates are shut at the sluice and the water is let down slow from above and the bed of the race is walked and the silt is dug out and the gates are opened again. It takes nine days.*”

“*Say the half of that which is the other half.*”

“*The other half is that a race that is not scoured silts up, and a silted race is a race that is higher than the land it is meant to drain, and the land it is meant to drain is the Fen. Nine years back, in the worst flood anybody in this row can remember, the water came over the top of the Fen at Coldheath and put four feet of water into nine houses.*”

Nobody in that room said anything.

“*Say that last part again and say the year and do not stop in the middle of it.*”

“*Nine years ago. Four feet. Nine houses, and the nine houses are the second to the tenth of this row, and there is not a mark of it anywhere because the water went out in a day and nobody wrote it down.*”

“*And the frame.*”

“*The frame is the board at the head of the sluice and the frame has rotted at the heel on the north side. You can see it if you go and look. I have looked. In another two years the north heel goes and the sluice drops a foot, and when the sluice drops a foot this town is finished in the worst flood.*”

---

Sefa Lund had asked for a room and a witness and somebody to write it down, and the somebody was supposed to be her granddaughter, who is fourteen and writes a fair hand, and the granddaughter was at a taproom and could not leave it.

“*Say what you can do,*” said Sefa Lund, “*and if you cannot do it say that, and I will send for the boy at the mill.*”

Ilyan had the back of a bill in his hand before he had decided, and he wrote the date at the top of it, and he said the price out loud before he did it, which is what he does and it has not worked once.

“*I can write it. And the price is that it becomes a thing somebody can be asked about, and I know that, and I am going to do it anyway, because you have asked for it to be written and I have never in nine weeks seen a person ask for that and then not be believed.*”

“*Say the whole of that and do not stop in the middle of it.*”

“*A record is a handrail. It is also a rail a person can be pushed against. I would rather have the rail and the pushing both, than have you standing in a room saying a true thing that was in the air on Tuesday and gone by Wednesday.*”

“*Then write it,*” she said, and he wrote it, and it took him about eleven minutes, and it is the only document Ilyan Vester has ever written in a town of four thousand people.

It was four hundred and ten words. It had the date, the name of the man, the name of the room, the two witnesses, and the figures.

Then Sefa Lund read it back to him, out loud, all of it, standing over his shoulder, and she did it in about six minutes and she did not hurry any of it.

At the end of the fourth paragraph she stopped.

“*Say that again.*”

“*And the board ought in my judgment to carry the whole of the cost of the scouring out of the water fund and the households of the Fen ought to be recompensed for the nine days.*”

“*Whose judgment.*”

“*Mine.*”

“*Say that again in the room and hear it.*”

He looked at the woman from the taproom in the doorway, who was thirty-four and rented and had come down because there was a fire out and something being said, and who had not been asked and had not offered.

“*Say your name,*” Sefa Lund said to her, and the woman said it, and it was Mab Harrow, and she had not been asked to be a witness and was one by standing in a doorway.

Sefa Lund took the paper and struck out the fourth paragraph with two strokes, and she did not tear it, and she signed across the strike with her own hand, and she put the paper down on the table in front of him.

“*That is the one,*” she said. “*Everything else in that is a man from the county saying what a man from the county has measured. And that line is a stranger telling nineteen houses what they are owed, in a room with two women in it, and I have not asked nineteen houses whether that is what they think, and neither have you.*”

“*Say the half of that which is the other half.*”

“*The other half is that you have been in this town three days and you have already written the sentence that makes me a person with a claim, and I do not want to be a person with a claim this week. I want to be a woman who keeps a sluice who is not a household. I want that to be the end of it. If somebody writes down that I am owed, then somebody has to answer me, and then I am a woman with a grievance in a town where about nine people know you.*”

He read the paper back with the fourth paragraph struck and her signature across the strike, and it was a better document, and he had not made it, and he said so.

“*Say it as a fact and do not make it smaller.*”

“*That is the third thing I have got wrong in four days and it is the one I shall keep. On Sunday I made myself the man who reads. On Tuesday I put a figure into a market. And on Wednesday I wrote out for nineteen houses a thing that they had not said they wanted, because it is a better shape than the thing they have said, and I would have got them a hearing with it.*”

“*And you still think the hearing is right,*” said Sefa Lund.

“*I think a hearing is right. I think I am the wrong end of it. Both of those are about me and I am trying to make them about me, and I have noticed myself doing it four times in four days, and I would like somebody to tell me to stop.*”

“*I am not going to tell you to stop,*” she said. “*I am going to tell you to be nine days late. That is the only thing I know how to say.*”

---

Then Mab Harrow, from the doorway, in the voice of a woman who has been washing at a sluice for eleven years and has not been asked anything in any of them, asked the one question.

“*Say the rest of that and do not stop in the middle of it. If the frame goes, who is it for.*”

Nobody answered her straight away.

“*Say the half of that which is the other half,*” said Ilyan, because he could not think of it and asked for it the way he asks for it.

“*Say the whole of that,*” said Sefa Lund. “*Ask her again, and ask Garrick Nye, and ask me, and get three answers and see whether they are the same.*”

Garrick Nye answered first, and he was right again.

“*The frame holds the water up for the Fen. The Fen takes the water off this town when there is a great deal of it. The frame is the board’s frame and it is a good frame and it was put in by a man’s grandfather about sixty years ago, and it holds a million gallons and I have not got the sum.*”

Mab Harrow said the sum, and nobody had asked her for it.

“*A hundred and forty acres of Fen, and when the river is up an inch the whole river is up, and I do not know the sum and neither does he.*”

Then Sefa Lund said it, and it was the answer that was in the room, and it was the answer nobody wanted.

“*It is for this town. Not for the nineteen houses, and not for the water, and not for the reed. The frame is the board’s and it keeps the ground floors of Halloway out of a river in the worst flood, and the whole of the reason there is a sluice is that there is a town under it.*”

“*Then the Board will scour it,*” said Garrick Nye, “*and the Board is not going to be asked, and the nine days are going to happen, and the objection of nineteen houses is going to lose on Thursday, and I would like everybody in this room to have heard me say it today and not on Thursday.*”

“*Say the cost and do not stop in the middle of it.*”

“*Fourteen pounds. The county does not pay for water and the Board does, and the Board has thirty-four pounds and fourteen shillings in the fund for the year, and fourteen of that is two fifths, and the Board will find it.*”

“*Say the rest of that and do not make it larger than it is.*”

“*And the nine days are the nine days. And in the nine days there is no water below the sluice. There are twenty-three people living in this row, and there are about nine hundred people in this town who have got used to having water out of that race. Nineteen of the households of this town are in this row, and the other nine hundred are not going to be in this row.*”

Nobody in that room put it into a sentence. It went round the room on its own for about half a minute, and the woman from the taproom said it out loud before anybody else, which is what she had done once already.

“*Then the objection of nineteen houses is the only thing anybody has said in a week about the water, and what it says is that nobody has ever told us it is ours.*”

The fever was twenty-four weeks and four days old. Four hundred and ten words had been written in a room over a taproom, and a fourth paragraph had been struck out of it with two strokes in a woman’s own hand.

The scouring was going to be done on Thursday for fourteen pounds out of thirty-four pounds and fourteen shillings. The nineteen houses that had kept the sluice free for nine years were going to lose the water for nine days, and were going to be owed nothing, in writing, by a man who had been in the town three days.

# Chapter 460: The House Of Halloway Sits In The Stone House On Forty-One Stools With The Fire Lit And The Door Open, And About Two Hundred People Stand In The Square Outside It, And The Question Is Whether The Nineteen Houses At The Fen Shall Be Entered As Households Upon The Roll, And A Steward Of Forty Years Declines It In Their Own Name In Front Of All Of Them, And They Did Not Ask Him To, And Nobody Says Out Loud That It Is A Refusal

Seventh day of the eighty-seventh week. His two hundred and tenth morning. Sixty days after the settlement. The fever twenty-five weeks and two days. Fourteen days since the division.

The House sits on forty-one stools in the Stone House with the fire lit and the door open, and the cold is the reason it sits in a stone building at all. The stools go out at the seventh hour and are put away at the eleventh.

The order of the stools is the order of the wards. There are nine wards and a head of household is elected in each of them, and a head of household is not a household, and the number of households in a ward has never been the number of seats in it, and there is no form in this county that says a ward has a number of seats at all. About two hundred people could not get into a stone room in the wet and were in the square at the door, and the door was open, and everything said inside it was said out loud into the square about four times before it was finished.

About two hundred people stood at the edges of the square. There were about nine of the fenrow at the front and about forty of the four hundred who had been carrying water four hundred yards twice a day for four days, and the gates of the sluice had been shut for four days and came off at the sixth hour that morning, five days early.

There is no form in the county of Ostrey that says a person not of this town may stand in this room. He stood at the end of the fourth stool’s row with a bad leg and was not moved and was not asked twice.

---

The question had been on the table since the Sunday, and it had a head on it, and Wenna Crale had written the head herself because nobody else in that town would write a head on it.

*Upon the minute of the water sitting of the third day of this week: whether the nineteen tenements at the Fen, being entered in the roll of this year as land of the common and not as households, shall be entered as households of this town upon the roll to be made in the autumn, or shall not.*

“*Say the rest of the head,*” said Ilyan, from the end of the fourth stool’s row. He had asked for it four times already. He asked a fifth time because he could not stop, and she looked at him and said it a fifth time anyway.

Nobody had told him to stand there and nobody had told him to leave. Ilyan had been in that square for the whole of it and had said four words in it, and about two hundred people in a town of four thousand had worked out by the fourth stool that the man at the end of the row was the one who had read the notice.

“*Or shall not. And the water board has entered no petition, and there is no petition, and the question is on the table because the steward put it there.*”

“*Say the rest of that,*” said Amos Trill, “*and say who is to answer it.*”

“*The House. Forty-one heads of household, and nobody else, and there is nobody in this room who can answer it and is not a head of household of this town, and that is not an insult to anybody in it, it is what the House is.*”

---

And then he asked him, in the open air, in front of about two hundred people, and he had to, and he said so out loud first.

“*I am going to ask a man in this square for his view, and I am going to say out loud that I am asking because he is the only person in it who has been wrong and said so in a room. I am not asking because he is the man who read the notice.*”

Nobody in that square said anything.

“*Say it a fifth time and do not stop in the middle of a sentence,*” said Sefa Lund, from the front.

“*I am going to ask a man in this square for his view, and I am going to say out loud that I am asking because he is the only person in it who has been wrong and said so in a room.*”

Amos Trill stood up off his stool, and he stood up slowly, and he was sixty-one and he had been the steward of that town for forty years.

“*I have got an answer and I have had it since the Friday,*” he said, “*and I have had it since the Friday and I have not said it to anybody, and I want it in the book that I held it for two days.*”

---

“*The House declines,*” said Amos Trill. “*The nineteen tenements at the Fen are not entered as households of this town upon the roll to be made in the autumn. The out-of-roll book is not written in. And this is at the request of the persons concerned, made to me on the fourth day of this week in the lane at the sluice.*”

About two hundred people in a market square did not make a sound.

“*Say the rest of that and do not stop in the middle of a sentence,*” said a man on the third stool.

“*The rest of it is that the woman who keeps the sluice asked me for that, in a lane, in front of about nine people. She said the words *I want to be a woman who keeps a sluice who is not a household*. She said *I want that to be the end of it*. And she said it in front of a stranger who had been in this town eight days.*”

He turned round on the stool and looked at the front of the square.

“*And I have taken a woman of thirty-eight at her word in a public square, in her own name, about her own standing, in a town where she has never once been asked for it, and I have done it in about nine seconds.*”

Sefa Lund stood up at the front of the square. She was at the fourth house of nineteen and she had come up the lane that morning with four other people and she had not been asked to come and she had not been asked to sit.

“*Say the whole of that again and do not stop in the middle of it,*” she said, “*and then say the next four lines, because I know what the next four lines are and I have been waiting since Saturday to be able to say so in a square.*”

“*I have not got four lines.*”

“*Then write them now and read them out, and I will tell you if they are true, and I will tell you in this square, and about two hundred people will hear you say a thing about nineteen houses that I have not agreed to.*”

Amos Trill stood there with the sun on him and the pen not moving.

“*Say the rest of that and do not stop in the middle of it,*” said Ilyan, from the end of the fourth stool’s row, and then he said the thing that has been the shape of nine weeks of his life, and he said it at the wrong moment for the ninth time.

“*And I am going to say that I have no standing in this town and no vote and no part in this decision, and I am not going to say one word about it, and I would like the minute to say that I said nothing.*”

“*Go on,*” said Sefa Lund.

“*And I have said the price of six wants out loud in a room in a city forty-one miles off. I said the want out loud in front of about thirty people. I said the price of that want out loud in the same room. And every one of those six was me deciding in about four seconds to be the man with the thing nobody else had. I have not changed, and this is what it looks like.*”

Nobody in that square thanked him and nobody in that square agreed with him.

---

“*Say the minute,*” said Sefa Lund. “*Say all of it, and say the part I have not agreed to.*”

Wenna Crale read it out standing at the head of the table, and it is four lines, and three of them are the ordinary minute of forty-one men who have run a town for a long time.

“*One. That the House has heard the question, and has heard both of the margins at the back of the fourth leaf of the copy, which are a strike by order upon a paper not of this town and a woman of twenty-four writing that she could not learn whether the striking was of that clause alone, and both of them entered by the steward on the sixth day of this week.*”

“*Two. That the House declines to enter the nineteen tenements at the Fen as households upon the roll to be made in the autumn, and that the out-of-roll book is not written in.*”

“*Three. That the declining is at the request of the persons concerned, made on the fourth day of this week; and that the persons concerned are not households of this town, have no standing in this House, and are not before it; and that the House has proceeded on a request made to the steward and not to the House.*”

She stopped there and looked at Sefa Lund, and about two hundred people in a market square looked at Sefa Lund.

“*Say the fourth line,*” said Sefa Lund.

“*Four. That nothing in this minute is a finding about the water of the Hallow, and the water board may not charge any household for the water below the sluice while the gates are shut, and the compensation at clause five cause four is not made out.*”

Then Sefa Lund said one thing, standing at the front of the square, and it was nine words, and she did not say it as a sentence and she did not say it to him.

“*Say that back to me,*” she said. “*Say the third line back to me, all of it.*”

Wenna Crale said the third line back to her.

“*Then it is in a book in a town of four thousand people that nineteen houses are not of this town and have no standing in this House and are not before it, and that the House has declined to make them households at their own request,*” said Sefa Lund. “*And I have asked nine days for somebody to tell me what the water is. And there is a sentence on a board in this town that is a hundred and thirty-eight years old, and it says the water is common to all households. And I have not got a household, and I did not agree to not have one.*”

---

The gates came off the water at the sixth hour that same Monday, five days early. The county man decided on the first morning that the silt was worse than he had thought, and worked through a Sunday.

About two hundred people walked four hundred yards to watch the water come back down the race. They stood on the bank above the sluice and did not go in.

The fever was twenty-five weeks and two days old.

That afternoon, in a room over a nail shop, a man with a cracked steel pen and a bad leg wrote a letter to a recordwright four hundred miles off. It took him most of the afternoon.

He wrote that a board in a stone room in a town of four thousand people has eleven clauses and a lead tag and four hundred years on the front of it. He wrote that the ninth clause is in a fifth hand and is dated a hundred and thirty-eight years ago, and that the right of refusal was taken out of that country’s law about a hundred and forty years ago and has not been put back.

And then he wrote the last part of the letter, and it was this.

*And under the ninth clause there is a witness line, and there are two names in it, and one of them is a name of that town and I cannot place it. The other is four letters and a stroke in a hand that is not this town’s hand and is not the old hand of the Book, and nobody in that town can read it, including the only person there who can read the Book, and I have not read it either.*

*I have read everything else in that town in eight days. I have read a Book on a beam, four leaves in a press, a roll made in the week a common was sold, and a minute of forty-one men. I have not read those four letters and a stroke, and I am not going to guess at them. I am writing to you because you are the only person I know who would tell me that a name in a witness line is a thing a person can be asked about, and because I have not sent this.*

He read it over twice, and he did not send it, and the paper was the only thing in that room with his name on it that anybody had asked for.

A steward of forty years had put nineteen houses out of his own town on a Monday morning, in their own name, in front of about two hundred people, and the whole of the instrument was one line of a minute that said *at the request of the persons concerned* and then went on, in the same line, to say that the persons concerned are not before it.

Nobody had thanked him for the fifth of those words. The water was back in the race below the sluice, the roll for the autumn was a long way off, and the man who had read a notice out in a market square on a Sunday had been in the town eight days and had his name on nothing except a letter to a recordwright that he had not sent.

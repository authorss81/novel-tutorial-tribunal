# Chapter 499: The Fourth Day Of The Ninety-Third Week, And A Friday On Which A Head And Two Lines Are Drawn Up Asking For A Refusal Given In A Lane In This Town To Be Entered Upon A Register Four Hundred Miles Off, And A Man Of Thirty-One Writes The Fair Copy In A Hand That Is Not His

Fourth day of the ninety-third week. His two hundred and forty-ninth morning. Ninety-nine days after the settlement. The fever thirty weeks and six days. Fifty-three days since the division. Forty-seven days in this town.

About nine people gathered in the room over a nail shop in the afternoon. It is two rooms, and there is a bed in the one end pushed to the wall, and a table, a chair and a fire that has not been let out properly since the first week he came to this town. The table and the chair and the fire were as they had been on the forty-third day, and about nine people had been in this room twice in six weeks and both times it had been about a letter.

A bar four feet long at the head of the sluice had not been turned for twenty-three days. The wheel at the mill had been slow twenty-two days completed.

Wenna Crale came at the fourth hour with her bag and put her paper on the table and sat down on the side of it with the light. She is thirty-four, and she is the only public officer in this town that is paid anything, and what she is paid is two pounds a year, and the two pounds have not moved in eleven years.

Ilyan Vester had been in the room since the second hour with a sheet squared for headings, and he had not said anything in it for two hours, and there was a cup of cold tea on the table that he had not drunk. He wanted a refusal of this town entered on a register of refusals, and he wanted it the way a man wants a thing that would mean he had been here for a reason, and he had come to this room at the second hour to be in it when it happened and had not been asked to.

She brought the four pages with her. They have been in a press against a west wall since the forty-third day, unsigned, addressed to a water board and not to a man, and she has not read all of them in this room.

“*I am going to draw this up the same way I draw up everything in this county,*” she said. “*A head and two lines under it, at my own table, with no body behind it.*”

She squared the paper.

“*I am asking for one thing and one thing only. Anybody in this room may object to the asking and the objection goes in.*”

“*Ask for it,*” said Perdy Sallow.

“*Say the thing you are asking for first,*” said Ordwin Cape. “*Not the words. The thing.*”

So she said the thing, and it took her a while, and she got it in the wrong order twice and started again, and the second time is the one that went in.

“*There is a register of refusals in a building of four hundred people on a road four hundred miles off. A range of about nine hundred of them stands on a shelf eleven inches wide in a room eleven inches from where a woman of twenty-eight sits. She has written down eleven times in six years that she has not opened it, and the eleventh of those eleven was in the four pages that were read out in a market square in this town on the thirty-first day after the division and to about two hundred people on the thirty-ninth.*”

She put her hand flat on the table.

“*I am asking that a refusal given in a lane in this town be entered upon that register. That is all. One entry, one refusal, one day on it.*”

---

Then somebody asked how a refusal is entered, and the answer is a form, and the form is four lines: the day, the name of the person who refused, the name of the person who witnessed it, and the place. Everybody in that room knew the four lines except the two people who had never had to fill one in.

A refusal is entered by the name of the person who refused it, the day it was given, and the name of the person who witnessed it.

The person who refused was in the lane and is in the room. She is thirty-eight and she keeps the sluice at the Fen and she is a household of this town.

The form that a chair of this board put in front of her in that lane on the eleventh day after the division was written out for her by name. It asked her to put her name to it and to say she did not consent. She said no, out loud, in a lane, in front of about nine people.

There was no form for what she did, nobody gave her anything for it afterwards, and she has never asked.

“*Put my name in it,*” Sefa Lund said.

It was the first thing she had said in about half an hour. She had been sitting by the fire with her hands folded and her back to the wall the way a person sits who intends to be there a long time.

Nobody wrote it down for about four seconds and then Wenna Crale wrote it, because she had asked for a room and she had said that anybody in a room could stop her and she was not going to be a person who takes a refusal out of a room without the day on it.

“*Name of the person who refused: Sefa Lund. Day: the eleventh day after the division. Lane, at the head of the sluice, in front of about nine people.*”

“*Put in there that I had nothing to refuse with,*” Sefa Lund said. “*Say that in there as well, because I have read the four pages and there is a sentence in them about a person who refuses without having it and I would like it to be written that I am one of those.*”

“*It goes in under your name or it does not go in at all,*” said Wenna. “*I will not put a thing in a register about you that you have not said yourself. Say it and I will write it in the two lines and you may read it before it is folded.*”

“*Then it goes in.*”

“*Say the second line,*” said Perdy, “*because you have both of you been at this for ten minutes and the second line is not written.*”

---

It is about twenty-two words and it is the whole of the request, and the witness line is the other nine, and the nine words are the whole of what this town is actually asking for.

*That a refusal given in a lane in this town on the eleventh day after the division, by Sefa Lund, who had no right of refusal to give, be entered upon the register of refusals.*

That is about twenty-two words. The witness line is the other nine, and the nine words are the whole of it, and the nine words are these.

*And the line for the witness stands open, and no name is put to it, and no hand is set to it in this room, and a copyist of this town does not set her hand to a witness line that is empty.*

She wrote the head first and then the two lines, and she read them out standing, in the voice she uses for minutes, in a room over a nail shop with the fire not drawing and about nine people in it.

“*To the keepers of the register of refusals, and to nobody else. A refusal given in a lane in this town on the eleventh day after the division is asked to be entered upon a register of refusals. The name of the person who refused it is Sefa Lund.*”

“*Leave the witness line open,*” Perdy Sallow said. “*That is the whole of it. A refusal is entered by the name of the person who witnessed it. There is no witness line in a lane, and there was nobody in that lane whose job it was to be one.

The whole of what this town can do about a thing it did six weeks ago is to say so in nine words and put them in a hand a person four hundred miles off can read.*”

She read the two lines back before she folded them, which is what she does.

Sefa Lund read them over her shoulder, put her finger on the name, and said that it was spelt right. About four people in that room watched a woman of thirty-eight check the spelling of her own name in a document going four hundred miles, and not one of them said a word about it.

Nobody objected. About nine people in that room were asked and none of them said anything against it.

One man of the fourth ward, who was not of this town and had come eleven miles, said that an open line was the most honest thing he had seen in six weeks.

He then said that honest was the wrong word for it and that he would not use it again. About four people in that room laughed and one of them was the copyist.

---

Ilyan took up the pen then, and his mistake was the hand, and it was the same hand as on the forty-third day and it was the third time in six weeks and it is the thing he does.

He wrote the fair copy in the small upright hand, which is a hand that is not the writer’s, and which is a hand a person can be asked about. The small upright hand is the hand of a recordwright, and a fair copy in it and a letter in it are the same thing on paper, and nobody in that room could have told the difference except the four people who could.

About four people in that room knew why. About nine did not. The copyist was one of the four, and she had asked him for it, and she had asked him for it in a way that left him nowhere to put his own.

She did not say anything about the hand after he had written it. That is worth setting down, because a person of thirty-four could have said a great deal about it, and she said nothing, and the nothing lasted the rest of the afternoon.

He folded it in four and put nothing on the outside of it, because that is what she does with everything.

A man of the fourth ward, who had come eleven miles, asked the only question anybody asked in that room that afternoon that was about the answer instead of the asking.

“*What happens when it gets there.*”

Nobody in that room could tell him, and about nine people in that room worked out at about the same moment that this was the first question of the whole six weeks that nobody in it had an answer to, and that the reason was that the whole of the six weeks had been spent learning what a thing this county cannot do.

Wenna said that it was a fair question and that she was not going to pretend it had an answer.

She knew one thing about it. The four pages that came out of that building say that the woman who sits there has written down eleven times in six years that she has not opened a range of about nine hundred refusals on a shelf eleven inches wide. Whether she opens it on the strength of nine words from a town of four thousand people is not a thing anybody in that room can do anything about, and she would rather say that in a room than not say it.

“*Then it may be nothing,*” the man of the fourth ward said.

“*It may be nothing. It is nine words and a shilling and fourpence, and the best thing a person in this town can do this year is nine words, and I would do it again on Monday.*”

Sefa Lund said that she would, and that the copyist was the only person in that room who got two pounds a year, and that it was not enough.

Wenna looked at her for a moment and then wrote two lines at the foot of the sheet, in the margin, which said that the carrier’s wage of one shilling and fourpence had been paid out of the copyist’s own two pounds a year, and read them out, and entered them under the head with the day on it, because a thing anybody in that room could check on ought not to depend on a person being asked about it later.

A carrier took it from the square at dusk. The carrier is a man of about thirty. He goes to a town on a road eleven miles off twice a week and takes whatever anybody gives him for a shilling and fourpence.

He took it without asking what it was and put it inside his coat. Nobody in that room was thanked for the fourpence and nobody thanked the copyist for the rest of it.

The town had asked for a thing nobody in Halloway can be sent for to do, and nobody in Halloway can be asked about it after.

He sat late by the fire with the cost of it. The leg was bad and he had not eaten since the middle of the day.

The want had been the whole of the reason he had come, and there is no form in the county of Ostrey that says a man may be a reason. A woman of thirty-eight had a refusal in a book in a press in a stone room and a name at the foot of a second part of an instrument.

A man of thirty-one had been in this town forty-seven days and had about nine sentences, and this morning one of them had been worth a shilling and fourpence and four hundred miles of somebody else’s hand, and he could not be asked about any of it.

He had written in her hand again and made a thing of hers with no mark on it that said where it came from.

It was the only way the request could travel four hundred miles and be read when it got there. It was also a thing he could never answer for as long as he lived, and a woman of thirty-four was sitting four feet away from him and had not mentioned it once.

The fever was thirty weeks and six days old. A request had gone out of this town in the small upright hand of a recordwright four hundred miles off, folded in four with nothing on the outside of it, and the witness line on it stood open.

It is the first thing anybody in Halloway has ever sent anywhere. There is nothing in this county that can say whether it will be answered, and nothing in this county that can ask.

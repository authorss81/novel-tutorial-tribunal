# Chapter 465: A Board Of Nine Sits On A Saturday Six Days Before The Day It Wrote Down, And A Question Goes In That Nobody Asked For, And Four Of The Nine Say The Same Three Sentences Word For Word, And The Answer Is Nine To Nothing Against Nineteen Houses, And The Nine Are Right, And Then A Man Of Forty-Seven Goes Down A Lane And Gets A Request Out Of A Row That Has Refused One

Fifth day of the eighty-eighth week. His two hundred and fifteenth morning. Sixty-five days after the settlement. The fever twenty-six weeks and no days. Nineteen days since the division. Thirteen days in this town.

The water board of Halloway is nine men and it sits when the chair calls it, and the chair called it for the Saturday, which was six days before the day he had written at the foot of his own four lines on the Wednesday.

The room is over a saddler’s shop at the north end of the square and it has a table, nine stools, one window and a fire that was not lit. There were about forty people in the square in the wet outside it, and eleven of them were from the Fen, and a woman of nineteen of the Fen was at the front of them with a roll of paper under her coat, which she has now carried for three weeks in three kinds of weather.

Wenna Crale was in the room with a book, because everything that happens in this room ends up in a book somewhere, and she is the only person in this county whose job is that.

---

“*Say what is on the table before anybody says anything else,*” said Perdy Sallow, “*and I am going to say the harder half of it first, because I have had it in a drawer since Wednesday and there is a man of thirty-four at the bottom of that lane who has been refused by me and would like to know why he is sitting here.*”

“*Go on.*”

“*Nobody in this room asked for this question. I put it in. I put it in on the Wednesday in the shape I wrote down, and I have brought this sitting forward six days to hear it, and the day I wrote at the foot of it is the twenty-fifth day after the division, and that is a day I chose myself and nobody made me choose it.*”

He put a single sheet of paper on the table and squared it with two fingers, and it is the same gesture a copyist makes and nobody in that room said so.

“*It says: whether the water board will enter the nineteen tenements at the Fen in the out-of-roll book, and if so in whose name, and on what day, and at whose request.*”

“*Say the rest of it and do not make it a speech,*” said a man of forty-four on the end stool.

“*That is all of it. I have brought the room together six days early to answer a question nobody asked, and I would like it in the book that I said so before I asked it.*”

---

The three sentences came from a man of fifty-four with a lot on the Four Hundred who had paid two pounds for it in the week of the division, and who had been the second of the three against the scouring nine days earlier, and whose name is in two minutes already.

“*Chair. Three things, and I have said two of them twice in this room and I am saying all three of them now, and four of us have them in the same words.*”

“*Say them.*”

“*One: we did not lose the water. We sold it. We were paid. Eighty lots at two pounds and a levy of three and a halfpence and the water went with the land because the sale said the land and the water, and nobody wrote a line anywhere saying the water did not go with the land.*”

“*Go on.*”

“*Two: we are asked now to carry nineteen houses in a roll we did not make, in a year we did not choose, in respect of a water that a board of nine has had the whole of the money for, and the money is thirty-four pounds and fourteen shillings a year and it has been the same for eleven years.*”

“*And the third one,*” said Sallow.

“*Three: the fenrow have not asked.*”

He looked round the table and four men nodded, and one of them was about fifty and one of them was about thirty and two of them were not looking at anybody.

“*Say why the third one is the one,*” said Sefa Lund, from the doorway, where she had not sat down.

“*Because one and two can be argued with for forty years, and the third cannot be argued with at all. And if the third one goes, the other two go with it inside a year, and then this room is not a water board, it is nine men deciding what a row of nineteen are.*”

---

Nobody said anything for about a minute, and then Perdy Sallow put it.

“*All those against. All those for.*”

Nine against. None for. It took about eleven seconds and it was the shortest vote anybody in that room had ever taken and it was unanimous and nobody was surprised and that was the thing about it.

“*Say it in the words you have got,*” said Wenna Crale, with her pen up.

“*That the board will not enter the nineteen tenements at the Fen in the out-of-roll book on the question as it is put. Nine in favour of nothing being done. None against.*”

“*And the day you wrote at the foot of it?*”

“*Stands. I have not touched it. I shall consider it on the twenty-fifth day, and I shall consider it against nothing that happened today, and I should like that written as well, because I know what I am.*”

---

And then he did the thing, and he did it in about four minutes, and he was wrong, and he was the only man in that room who was not entitled to be wrong.

“*Say what you are doing,*” said Sefa Lund, and he said it to the window and not to her.

“*I am going down the lane and I am going to ask that row whether they want to be entered, and I am going to do it in a room over a taproom with the door shut, and if they say no then the twenty-fifth day will come and nothing will happen and I shall have spent a fortnight on a question nobody asked.*”

“*Then say why you are doing it, because you have just written in a minute that they have not asked, and a man of fifty-four has told this room that the third sentence is the one that cannot be argued with.*”

“*Because I have not been able to sleep on the third line of a minute of the House of this town since the Monday,*” said Perdy Sallow, “*and I will read it out, and it is the only thing in this room I have not been able to put down.*”

And he read it out, standing, in a room over a saddler’s shop on a wet Saturday.

“*That the declining is at the request of the persons concerned, made on the fourth day of this week; and that the persons concerned are not households of this town, have no standing in this House, and are not before it; and that the House has proceeded on a request made to the steward and not to the House.*”

He put the paper down.

“*A steward of forty years got a sentence out of a woman in a lane in about nine seconds and did a thing to nineteen houses with it in front of two hundred people, and every word of that minute is correct. I have been the chair of this board for nine years and I have never once been able to say that sentence about anybody. So I am not going to be the man who comes in with a thing, and the only way I have found of not being that man is to make the row say it themselves.*”

---

Ilyan had it written before Sallow was at the bottom of the stairs.

It was a good piece of work. It was a form of request, with a head, and a first line that said the persons concerned asked, and a second that said at what, and a third that said on what day, and it was four hundred and thirty words and he had been building it in his head since the Sunday in the square and he had it ready in about ninety seconds on the back of a bill.

“*Take it,*” he said, “*and do not take my name on it. Take the words. I wrote the fourth paragraph of a different paper ten days ago in that taproom and a woman of thirty-eight struck it out and signed across the strike, and I would like the whole of this week to be better than that.*”

Perdy Sallow read it standing on the bottom stair with about forty people in the square in front of him.

“*Say the reason and do not be kind about it,*” said Ilyan.

“*Because it is four hundred and thirty words and it is in the mouth of a man who has been in this town thirteen days, and because there is not one line in it that a person of the Fenrow would have said.*”

He folded it in four and gave it back.

“*Say the other half of that and say it on the stair, in front of these forty people.*”

“*The other half is that you have written a thing the row will say no to, in words so careful that it will take a man of thirty-four half a morning to find the one that is wrong. And I have watched you do that three times in thirteen days and every time it has been for them and never with them.*”

---

The row said no for about an hour.

Before anybody said anything else the woman who keeps the sluice said how many of them there were, which is the first thing a row says when it is about to agree to something, and she said it standing, and there were nineteen of them and she had said so twice that week to a man in a lane and she said it a third time, and about eleven people heard it, and the door was shut.

Sefa Lund said it in four sentences and it was the same four sentences she had said in a lane on the Friday of last week and she said them standing, and about eleven people heard them, and the door was shut.

Cullen Skay said no in eleven words.

Mab Harrow said no and said why, and the why was that she was not a household and had never been one and had been refused credit at a counter a week ago by a man who was right.

And then Ordwin Cape, who was sixty-one, and who had kept the sluice for twenty-two years before the woman who keeps it now, and who had cut reed for thirty years before that, and who had eleven shillings a year, said yes, and he said it standing up, and it took him nine sentences and about four of them were a sum he had done twice already in a lane.

“*Say the rest of it and do not stop in the middle of it,*” said Sefa Lund, to him, and not to the chair.

“*The rest of it is that I have eleven shillings a year and I have had it nine years and I have never once been asked to hand any of it to nineteen houses, and I said so in a lane a week ago and I meant it and I still mean it. And I cannot do it. There is no form in this county that lets me hand eleven shillings to nineteen houses, and there is no form in this county that lets anybody in that row do anything at all with anything.*”

“*Go on and finish it.*”

“*And a household on the roll can be charged. I have known that since the fifth day of last week and I worked it out in the lane with a stranger in it. So I am not on the roll I am not a household and I cannot pay, and I cannot be charged, and I cannot be in the House, and I cannot be in the room where the minute is written, and I cannot be asked about any of it, ever, by anybody, and that is not a wrong. That is a word.*”

He put his hand on the table of a room over a taproom.

“*And there is a sentence on a board in this town that is a hundred and thirty-eight years old and it says no household of this town shall be required to be entered against its will. I have not been required. I am asking, in front of nine people, out loud, in a room, and I would like it in a book before I am sixty-two.*”

---

Nobody in that room said anything for about nine seconds, and Ilyan Vester, who had been right about eleven things in thirteen days and wrong about all of the other ones, said the one true sentence he had been holding since the Wednesday, and it was the wrong moment for the tenth time in thirteen days.

“*Chair. That is the ninth clause. That is what it says, word for word, and the copy of it is alive and it is four leaves in a press in the Stone House, and he has just said the whole of it in your own room without knowing that there is a copy of it four hundred yards away with his own words in it.*”

“*Say the rest of that and say it in your own mouth,*” said Perdy Sallow.

“*And the ninth clause is what he said because the ninth clause is the only thing in this town that a man of sixty-one can hold in his hand. And I have been saying it in rooms for thirteen days as though it were an answer, and it is not an answer, it is a floor, and I have just watched a row find that out in four minutes and I did not see it coming.*”

---

So the row wrote it, and a girl of nineteen wrote it, and it is in the minute of the board of Halloway, and it is five lines, and Wenna Crale entered it and had to read it out in a room over a taproom to about thirty people because she said a thing that is in a book in a room where nobody else can hear it is not a thing anybody can be asked about.

“*One. That the nineteen tenements at the Fen ask to be entered as households of this town upon the rate roll to be made in the autumn.*”

“*Two. That the households of the Fen be charged for the water of the Hallow from the sluice downward, in respect of water only, and at one and the same rate as the households of this town.*”

“*Say the rest of it,*” said Ordwin Cape, “*and read it as it is written and not as you would write it.*”

“*Three. That they be charged for it knowing that the water of the Hallow from the sluice downward is common to all households of this town, and that they ask to be charged because they have had the water free for nine years and there is a frame at the sluice that holds it up and a man of the county has said in writing that the north heel of it is a season instead of two years.*”

“*Say the last line and say it as it is written, because it is the one I would like a book for.*”

“*Four. That no household entered under this request be struck off the roll of this town for any debt under two years.*”

The fever was twenty-six weeks and no days old. Nine men had voted on a Saturday against nineteen houses in about eleven seconds, and the nine were right, and then a man of forty-seven had gone down four hundred yards of lane and come back up it with a request in his own hand that he had not wanted and could not refuse and had not been authorised to ask for.

There is no form in the county of Ostrey that says a row may agree to anything, and the clerk of the House of Halloway entered the agreement of nineteen houses that are not of the town, into the minute of a water board, on a Saturday, at the sixth hour of the evening, in the rain.

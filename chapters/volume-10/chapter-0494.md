# Chapter 494: The Sixth Day Of The Ninety-Second Week, And A Sunday On Which The Season Comes Up The Lane At The Seventh Hour And The North Heel Goes With A Sound And Not A Finding, And A Board Of Nine Enters The Fact With A Day On It And Calls It Nothing, And A Woman Of Thirty-Eight Tells A Man Of Thirty-One In A Lane That A Sound Is Not A Sentence

Sixth day of the ninety-second week. His two hundred and forty-fourth morning. Ninety-four days after the settlement. The fever thirty weeks and one day. Forty-eight days since the division. Forty-two days in this town.

Rain had fallen in the night and stopped about the fourth hour. The lane to the Fen runs with water to the ankle for four hundred yards and it is the worst four hundred yards in the county, and at the seventh hour on that Sunday it had a skin on it that took the light off in three places.

About eleven people were at the head of the sluice by the sixth hour. They had been coming up that lane on and off for eighteen days, in the rain and out of it, waiting for a frame that was not coming back this year.

The bar four feet long lay in the mud where two people had put it down on the Wednesday of the thirtieth day. It had not been turned at the sixth hour or at the twelfth hour on any of eighteen days, and the wheel at the mill had been slow seventeen days completed.

The gates had been standing open since the sixth hour of the fourteenth day after the division, five days early. Shutting them needs the bar. The bar is in the mud, and there is no form in the county of Ostrey that lets anybody require a person who is not a household to pick a thing up out of one.

A man of the county had measured the north heel of the frame in nine years with a straight edge and a nail at the eleventh hour of a Friday in the rain. Three-eighths of an inch, and he has a copy of the figure because a man who measures a thing writes it down.

In his judgment the heel was a season and not two years. He would not un-say a number in front of twenty people to say so. A man of thirty-four with a lot on the Four Hundred had said in a square on the thirtieth day that if the bar is not turned then it is not a season, it is a year, and nobody has been able to settle the two, because the only thing that would settle it is a bar turned twice a day for four weeks, and there is no form that lets anybody require that.

The money for it was thirteen weeks off.

---

Ilyan Vester came up the lane at the sixth hour with Ordwin Cape and stopped a little apart from the row, and he had a sentence ready, and he had had it ready since the Friday.

His goal that morning was to get somebody in that row to turn a bar four feet long. He knew the whole of what stood in the way. The row are not households, so the ninth clause does not touch them and there is no other form in this county that does. A board of nine had already required it once, on the forty-first day, and the answer was one word and the one word is in a minute. He wanted it anyway, and about nine people had watched him want things in this town for six weeks.

He said it to the three nearest first, and then to the rest, and he said it well.

“*Put your hands to it with me and we will shut the gate before the tide of the race. Two people and an hour. Nineteen of you have done it every morning of your lives and eleven of you are standing here now. Nobody is asking anybody to do it twice. One hour, and the water goes where it went for nine years, and the sills stay dry.*”

Sefa Lund stood by the gate with both hands on the head of her bar-hook.

She is thirty-eight and keeps the sluice at the Fen and is a household of this town. She has never been paid for it, because a common is not a service, and she has a key to gates that stand open and no authority over anything at all.

She looked at him without heat for about four seconds. The water went past the open gate and over the low sill, and something under the frame shifted and settled and made a noise, and eleven people heard the noise and about nine of them looked at her and did not say anything, because they have been hearing it for nine years and it is not a thing anybody has ever asked her about.

Coll Sarn, who is twenty-two and lives in the sixth house of nineteen, put his hand on the frame and then took it off again. A man of about fifty came up the bank from below with a spade over his shoulder and stopped about ten feet short and did not come the rest of the way, and has not come the rest of the way on any of the eighteen days.

“*A sound is not a sentence,*” Sefa Lund said. “*I have been making that noise for nine years and nobody in this county has ever once asked me to explain it. Not you, not the man of fifty-four with the list, not the nine men in the room over a saddler’s shop. You are making it legible. That is the thing you do, and I have watched you do it nine times in five weeks and it has not once moved a hand.*”

Ilyan felt his face go hot in the cold. He opened his mouth, and she put one hand up flat before he could get the shape of a word out of it.

“*There is no form in the county of Ostrey that says a man may apologise,*” Sefa Lund said. “*Stand clear of the gate.*”

He stood clear of the gate.

About nine people in that lane watched him step back. Coll Sarn moved a bucket out of his way without looking at either of them. An older woman pulled her shawl up over her head and kept her eyes on the water. A boy of about nine held a stick and had been holding it before the man of thirty-one arrived and was still holding it after.

And then Sefa Lund said the other half, which she had not been going to say and said anyway, and she said it to the water and not to him.

“*Nine years. Twice a morning. Two people and an hour, and it is me and whichever of them comes, and I have had a child on my hip at the sixth hour four times in nine years and gone up with the child.*”

Coll Sarn put the bucket down on the right side of the gate instead of the left, which is where it goes, and wiped his hands on his coat, and did not look at either of them.

“*I did not tell you that to make you say something. I am telling you because you have just stood in a lane and asked nine people to do a thing I have asked them for in a different voice for eighteen days, and I would like it on the record that nobody asked me to.*”

There is no form in the county of Ostrey that says a person may put a thing on the record in a lane, and there is no form that says she may not, and about nine people in that lane heard it and none of them can be asked about it afterwards.

And then the eleven of them did the work of a room that has nothing to say: a woman got a second child out of the wet under her coat, a man walked up the lane to see whether the water was coming, and the boy of about nine put his stick down in the mud and left it there.

He has said since, to nobody at all, that it was the worst four minutes of the six weeks. He has also said, to nobody, that about nine people in a lane carried on doing what they had been doing for eighteen days, and that he was the only one in that lane who had come to do something else.

---

The water came up the lane about a quarter past the sixth hour and went over the low sill of the sluice in a long flat sheet and stood there. About eleven people began moving the buckets and the boards they had brought. Nobody asked the man of thirty-one to help and nobody told him not to. He picked up a board and carried it because it was there.

Then at the seventh hour the north heel went.

It was a sound and not a finding. Timber cracked low and deep, once, and then twice in a different place, and the water took the gap with a rush that made a noise like a door being shut in another county. The frame at the head of the sluice settled half a hand to the north with a lurch you could feel through the soles of your boots from the bank.

Spray came up over the boots of those nearest. A child cried out and was lifted clear by her mother and did not cry again. A dog went along the bank at a trot and did not bark. The bar, where it lay in the mud, jumped about a foot as the ground under it went.

Sefa Lund put her hand flat on the frame of the gate and took it off again, which is what she does.

Nobody turned the bar. Nobody could be required to turn it. The row are not households, and the one household among them had answered a requirement of the forty-first day with one word, and that word is in a minute, and there has never been such a thing in four hundred years of that town.

The man of the county came up the lane at a fast walk with the straight edge still in his hand and did not say a word about it.

About half an hour later he came back with a nail, knocked it into the frame at a new place, wrote three figures on the back of his own hand, wiped them off, and wrote them a second time.

---

The board of nine met in the afternoon in the room over the saddler’s shop. About nine men sat round the table with wet coats steaming on the backs of their stools and the window open the whole time, and the room smelled of wet leather again, and the broken chair was still against the wall with nobody on it.

Nobody had called the meeting. It had assembled the way a room in this county assembles, which is that nine men who are all aware of the same thing turn up at the same hour and nobody afterwards can say who called it, and the steward’s minute will not say so either.

Perdy Sallow wrote the fact with a day on it and did not call it anything. He is forty-seven and he is the chair of this board and he has been the mouth of a thousand people for nine years.

“*On this day, at about the seventh hour, at the head of the sluice, the north heel of the frame settled,*” he said. “*The season has come. The frame is gone. The town loses a season on a frame and is right about the loss.*”

A man asked whether the minute ought to call it a failure.

Perdy put the pen down on the table and left it there.

“*It calls what happened,*” he said. “*Nothing more.*”

“*It is a failure of the board if the frame goes while the board has money for the levy.*”

“*Then write that at the foot of it with your name against it and it will be a true sentence and it will be in a minute that a person can be asked about, and I will copy it out myself. But I am not going to put a word in the head of it that the men of this board did not see with their own eyes on the bank at a quarter past seven this morning.*”

The man did not write it. He is on the board and his name is in the minute of the fortieth day and he sat with the reed in his hand for about a minute and then put it in his pocket.

Wenna Crale copied the minute out in the room, standing, and read it back with the day on it, and set her hand at the foot of it. The copy went into the press against the west wall of the Stone House, and any person of the town may read it.

Nobody in that room was thanked. A man asked whether the tail bar was going to be any use now, and Perdy said it would, and did not say why, and about four men in that room worked out the reason on their own and about five did not.

---

Ilyan sat on the stair outside the room and listened to it through the open door, and he had been on that stair for about four hours, and his leg had gone from bad to worse in the first hour and the rest of it he had not noticed.

The mistake had been made at ten past six in the morning in a lane and it was the one he makes every time: he had taken a thing nine people had been living with for eighteen days and had tried to turn it into a sentence, as though a sentence were a handle.

The cost was that about nine people had watched him do it, and that a woman of thirty-eight had told him out loud in front of all of them that it was not wanted. There is no form in the county of Ostrey that says a man may apologise. He had about four seconds in which he could have said he was sorry and used them on not saying it.

A thing he had wanted for six weeks had come and gone in that lane with about eleven people standing round it, and the frame had gone at the seventh hour.

A frame at the head of the sluice is nine pounds. A quarter of the fund with no frame on it was eight pounds fifteen shillings and fourpence. A levy of one pound three shillings and fourpence a year on the eighty lots had been entered on the forty-second day with a day on it, and the collection was thirteen weeks off. The season had arrived on a Sunday with about eleven people in a lane.

He walked home in the wet with mud to the knees and said nothing on the way, and there was nobody on the way to say it to.

The fever was thirty weeks and one day old. The board minute of the forty-eighth day called the going of the heel what happened and nothing more, and it had the day on it and a copy of it in a press against a west wall.

The mud at the head of that sluice held the print of about eleven pairs of boots all the way round a bar four feet long that nobody turned.

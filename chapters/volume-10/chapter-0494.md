# Chapter 494: The Sixth Day Of The Ninety-Second Week, And A Sunday On Which The Season Arrives And A Board Of Nine Enters A Fact With A Day On It And Calls It Nothing

Sixth day of the ninety-second week. His two hundred and forty-fourth morning. Ninety-four days after the settlement. The fever thirty weeks and one day. Forty-eight days since the division. Forty-two days in this town.

Rain had fallen in the night and the lane to the Fen ran with water to the ankle for four hundred yards. About eleven people stood at the head of the sluice by the sixth hour of the Sunday morning. The bar four feet long lay in the mud where two people had put it down on the Wednesday of the thirtieth day. It had not been turned for eighteen days. The wheel at the mill had been slow seventeen days completed.

A man of the county had measured three-eighths of an inch in nine years with a straight edge and a nail at the eleventh hour of a Friday in the rain. He would not un-say a number in front of twenty people to say so. In his judgment the north heel was a season and not two years. The money for it was thirteen weeks off.

Ilyan Vester came up the lane with Ordwin Cape and stood a little apart from the row. His goal that morning was to get somebody in that row to turn the bar. He knew there was no form that lets anybody require a person who is not a household, and that a board of nine had already required it once and the answer was entered in a minute. He wanted it all the same.

Sefa Lund stood by the gate with her hands on her bar-hook. She is thirty-eight and keeps the sluice at the Fen and is a household of this town. About eleven people at the head of the sluice had been standing in the rain for eighteen days, on and off, waiting for the frame.

“*Put your hands to it with me and we will shut the gate before the tide of the race,*” said Ilyan Vester. “*Two people and an hour. The row knows the work.*”

Sefa looked at him steady. Water ran past the open gate and over the low sill. A plank shifted somewhere under the frame and settled.

“*A sound is not a sentence,*” said Sefa Lund. “*I have been making that noise for nine years and nobody has ever once asked me to explain it. You are making it legible, and that is the thing you do.*”

Ilyan felt his face heat. He opened his mouth, and she lifted one hand before he could shape the words.

“*There is no form in the county of Ostrey that says a man may apologise,*” said Sefa. “*Stand clear of the gate.*”

About nine people in that lane watched him step back. Coll Sarn, who is twenty-two, moved a bucket out of the way. An older woman pulled her shawl tight and kept her eyes on the water.

At the seventh hour the north heel went. It was a sound and not a finding. Timber cracked low and deep, water took the gap with a rush, and the frame at the head of the sluice settled half a hand to the north. Spray came up over the boots of those nearest. A child cried out and was lifted clear by her mother.

Nobody turned the bar. Nobody could be required to turn it. The row are not households, and the one household among them had answered a requirement on the forty-first day with one word entered in a minute.

The board of nine met in the afternoon in the room over a saddler shop. About nine men sat round the table with wet coats steaming. Perdy Sallow wrote the fact with a day on it and did not call it anything.

“*On this day the north heel of the frame settled,*” said Perdy Sallow. “*The season has come. The frame is gone. The town loses a season on a frame and is right about the loss.*”

A man asked if the minute should call it a failure. Perdy shook his head and put the pen down.

“*It calls it what happened,*” said Perdy. “*Nothing more.*”

Ilyan sat on the stair outside the room and listened through the open door. His mistake had been to make the morning legible, as if naming the work would move hands to it. The cost was that about nine people had watched him do the thing he does, and a woman of thirty-eight had told him out loud that it was not wanted. He walked home in the wet with mud to his knees and said nothing on the way.

The fever was thirty weeks and one day old. The board minute called the going of the heel what happened and nothing more, with a day on it, and the mud held the print of boots round an unturned bar.

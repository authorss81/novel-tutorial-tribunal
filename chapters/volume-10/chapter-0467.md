# Chapter 467: Two Of The Nineteen Are Found To Be Two Households And One Of Those Is A Man Who Has Been Paying A Rent To A Woman Who Is Not On The Roll Either, And The Correction Of A Roll Turns Into An Audit Of A Whole Row, And Nobody In That Square Is Glad

Seventh day of the eighty-eighth week. His two hundred and seventeenth morning. Sixty-seven days after the settlement. The fever twenty-six weeks and two days. Twenty-one days since the division. Fifteen days in this town.

The rain had stopped at about the second hour on Saturday night and the lane above the sluice was still the worst of its four hundred yards and the frost was a long way off, and mud is not a thing anybody gets used to, it is a thing everybody stops mentioning by the fourth day.

She was in the doorway of the fourth house at about the eighth hour with her sleeves up and a bill in her hand, and she had been knocking on doors since the sixth.

“*Say your name,*” said Ilyan, “*and say what you are doing, because you have been in this town fifteen days and you have not knocked on a door.*

“*Mab Harrow. And I have knocked on nineteen doors since six this morning and I have asked one question at each of them, and the question is who sleeps there.*”

“*Say why that question and not any other.*”

“*Because on Sunday morning the roll was corrected and it says there are nineteen households at the Fen, and I have been in the fourth of these houses for eleven years, and I have a room in it, and I am not in the roll, and on Saturday I said in a lane that there are nineteen of us.*”

Nobody in that doorway said anything for a moment.

“*Say the rest of that in your own mouth and do not make it smaller than it is,*” said Sefa Lund, from behind her, where she had been listening with a bar-hook in her hand.

“*I said it in a lane on Saturday in front of about eleven people, and I said there are nineteen of us, and I have heard that number come back at me twice in two days, and I do not know how to be more careful than saying it once.*”

---

So he did the thing he does, and he did it in about three seconds, and he was not the first person at the door that morning.

“*I can write it,*” he said. “*I have written a thing for this row before and it came out better than I made it. Let me go round with you and take the names and I will have it in a hand by noon and it can go into the press.*”

“*No,*” said Mab Harrow.

“*Say the reason.*”

“*Because I have been round the market four times in a fortnight asking strangers questions and I have got nothing. If a man who is not of this town comes round nineteen doors with a bill, then nineteen doors have been asked by a stranger and not by the row.*”

“*Go on.*”

“*And in about four days this will be a story about a stranger in the lane instead of a count in the row.*”

“*Say the other half of that.*”

“*The other half is that you are the only person in this county who can be asked nothing, and I have thought about that for two days and I have not decided whether it is a gift or a hole.*”

She gave him the bill and took the pen off him, and she wrote on it herself, in a hand that was not a copyist’s hand and had never been in a press.

---

The count was done by three people and a stranger holding a bill.

Mab Harrow knocked. Coll Sarn, twenty-two, wrote the number against the door. Cullen Skay, nineteen, of the sixth house of nineteen, went back up the lane and knocked the ones they had skipped. And Ilyan Vester stood in the middle of four hundred yards of mud with a bad leg and a sheet of paper and did not say one word for an hour and eleven minutes, which is the longest he has gone in nine weeks.

Fourteen of the nineteen doors answered in the ordinary way and gave a number that was one, and the woman writing the numbers asked the same question at every one of them and got the same answer fourteen times.

Two of them did not give the same answer, and both of those two were opened by somebody who was not expecting to be asked anything.

The first of the two was the second house of nineteen.

“*Say your name,*” said Mab Harrow, to a man of about thirty-four who opened the door about a hand’s width and did not open it any further.

“*Wat Priddy.*”

“*Say how long you have been in that back room.*”

“*Two years, and I pay a shilling a week for it, and I pay it in coin to the old woman and not to anybody else, and I have asked her twice how often, and she says it is what she says it is.*”

Nobody in the lane said anything.

“*Say her name,*” said Cull Sarn.

“*Iven Cape. Eighty-one. And she is her own person and there is a form in this county for a woman being her own person and I have not seen it, and she is not on the roll, and neither am I, and I am in a house that is on the roll.*”

---

And that is where he said the number, and he said it standing in the middle of a lane in front of about eleven people, and it was wrong.

“*Twenty,*” he said. “*That is nineteen doors and the second one of them is two, so twenty.*”

“*Say what you have just done,*” said Ordwin Cape, who was sixty-one and who had a bar-hook over his shoulder.

“*I have added up nineteen doors and one of them is two.*

“*Then say it again slowly, and say how many of the nineteen you have been to.*”

That was the mistake, and it was his own, and it was not the thing that went wrong at the door of the second house, and it cost him the only thing he had, which is that nobody can put a number back.

He has never said in any room in this town that it was a small one.

---

The second of the two was the fourth house, and it took them about a minute, and the two houses that were done afterwards both gave a number that was one, which is nineteen houses and twenty-two households.

Coll Sarn, who had been born in the ninth house of nineteen and had come up off the low ground at twenty, wrote it down. He wrote it in the wrong column and ruled a line under the whole of it, which is a thing a man does when he cannot make a sum come out.

“*What have you got there,*” said Ordwin Cape.

“*Nineteen doors. The second house is two, because there is a man in the back room of it paying a shilling a week to a woman of eighty-one.*”

“*Go on.*”

“*And this house is three. There are three people in it and there is no form in this county that has ever asked whether any of the three of them belongs to another.*”

The fourth house held Ordwin Cape, who had kept the sluice for twenty-two years and cut reed for thirty years before that, and Sefa Lund, who had kept it for nine years, and Mab Harrow, who has rented the second room of it for eleven years. Not one of the three is the other’s.

“*Say the number out loud,*” said Ordwin Cape, “*and say it once, and then put it on the paper and let the paper be the thing that has it.*”

“*Twenty-two,*” said Coll Sarn.

Nobody in that lane said anything for about nine seconds.

Then Sefa Lund said the thing she was wrong about, and she said it in her own mouth, and she gave the reason.

“*Then it does not go on the paper.*”

“*Say what you mean by that, and say it in this lane and not in a room.*”

“*It goes in the row. It stays in the row until somebody asks for it out loud in a place where it cannot be said back to me differently.*”

“*That is a rule and not a reason. Give me the reason.*”

“*The reason is that a thing said in a lane is mine, and a thing said in a square belongs to whoever is standing in the square. I have watched three of my own sentences go out of that square with my mouth not on them. I will not hand this row two numbers about itself to a market in one week.*”

Eleven people were in that lane. A man from the county was not, and there was nobody in that lane who could be asked about it afterwards.

About four days earlier she had said out loud in a lane at midnight that a thing is not yours because of where you said it. She had been told the other half of it by a man with a straight edge, and she had not taken it, and it was in her own mouth now in front of eleven people that it was the half she had not taken.

“*Then say what you did on Saturday,*” said Mab Harrow.

Nobody made her do it. She did it herself, in the lane, with eleven people in it, and she did not take a moment over it.

“*I said nineteen on Saturday. In a lane. And there were about eleven people in it and one of them was the old man with the pension, who has done a sum of his own out loud in this lane twice in a fortnight, and who is now holding a bill with a number on it that is not the number he has been saying.*”

“*Say the rest of it and do not stop there.*”

“*And I was wrong, and I was wrong in a lane, and the reason I was wrong in a lane is that I would not say it in a square. That is the whole of it and I have been carrying it about for a day and a half.*”

---

And then the woman of thirty-four who washes at a sluice and rents a room in a house she is not a household of said the thing that settled it, and it was not Sefa’s rule and it was not a rule at all.

“*I did not count the row,*” said Mab Harrow. “*I counted it because the steward corrected a roll on Sunday morning in four minutes and I could not sleep on it.*”

“*Go on, because that is the half of it.*”

“*And I have been to the mill before I came up here. There is a man at the bottom of this lane with nine hundred names in a book who has been keeping a second one since the Saturday before last, and he will not show it to me, and he read me nine names off the counter out loud with his hand on the book.*”

“*Say the names, or say the number of them.*”

“*He told me the eight of them that are living and the one that is not.*”

“*Say his name and say what he told you.*”

“*Ott Vernay. Thirty-nine. He has nine blanks, and eight of the nine are on the copy of the roll of this town as land, and they are houses, and there are people asleep in them, and he has known that for four years and has never told anybody because a man with a counter is not the office.*”

“*And the ninth.*”

“*The ninth is a man called Pyle Garraw who owed him four shillings and died two years ago, and he has carried the four shillings on the book ever since, and he said it the way a man says a thing he has said to himself a great deal.*”

---

They got to the copy of the roll at about the eleventh hour, in the Stone House, and any person of the town may read it.

Wenna Crale stood at the press and let them read it and did not copy anything, and said why.

“*Because I have been asked three times in two days to enter a number about the Fen. Twice it was a man with a bill and once it was the woman who keeps the sluice. And in all the days since a stranger read a notice out in that square I have entered nothing at all about the Fen into my own book.*”

The eleven took the rest of the day and most of the evening.

Eight of them were the eight blanks Ott Vernay had read out from behind his counter, and they were rows in the town entered as land in some year, and there were people asleep in all eight. Three more were found by Coll Sarn and Cullen Skay off the west wall between the seventh hour and the eleventh, and they are rows entered as land in some year in a hand nobody living can account for.

“*Say the number out loud and do not do it in a market,*” said Ordwin Cape, in the lane, at about the sixth hour.

“*Say the other half of that as well,*” said Sefa Lund, and she said it in the lane, with about nine of them left in it by then, and it was the last time that day she said anything in a lane that anybody wrote down.

Eleven rows in the town are entered as land and are not households, and there is a twelfth row that is entered as land and has a dead man against it and cannot be got at by anybody.

Twenty-two households at eight pence is a hundred and seventy-six pence. Nineteen at eight pence is a hundred and fifty-two. So the roll that was corrected in four minutes on Sunday morning under-charges this town by twenty-four pence on the row, and by a hundred and twelve pence on the row and the eleven together, which is twenty-four pence on three doors and eighty-eight pence on eleven rows.

And there is nothing in the county of Ostrey that can be done about any of it before the roll for the autumn is made, on a day out of a day, by a man in an office over a shop who will do it correctly and will not know.

“*Say the rest of that and do not make it smaller than it is,*” said Coll Sarn.

“*Say it slower. It is not a small wrong. Nineteen doors are twenty-two households and eleven more rows in this town are households, and every one of them was in a book all the time.*”

“*Go on.*”

“*And the number of households in this town is a figure that has been found rather than a count, and there is no form in this county of Ostrey that says a person may find one.*”

---

There were about two hundred people in the market square at the eighth hour that evening, out of habit, from a week of standing about in it in the wet, and it had not begun again.

Nobody was glad.

The steward of forty years stood under the awning of the saddler’s shop with the copy of the roll in his hand, because he had carried it in out of the wet, and read out the eleven lines and the one line of the dead man.

Then he read out the four lines of the correction he had made in his own office on Sunday morning, and about two hundred people heard a man of sixty-one read his own work and a row of nineteen read themselves out loud in a lane that morning.

“*Say the half of that which is the other half,*” said Sefa Lund, from the edge of the square, where she had not been asked to come and had come.

“*The half of it is that all of it is true, and the other half is that the number of households in this town has been the number of households in this town for four hundred years, and it has never once been anybody’s job to be wrong about on purpose.*”

“*Go on.*”

“*And now it is anybody’s. A miller has given nine names away behind his own counter and a woman of thirty-four has knocked on nineteen doors and a man of twenty-two has ruled a line under a sum, and none of the three of them is the office.*”

“*Go on and say the rest of it, because that is not the end of the sentence and you know it is not.*”

“*And a man of thirty-one stood in this square on Sunday morning and said that a corrected roll is a corrected constituency. It was true. I have had two days to turn it over and it is the answer and it is not the question, and I do not think this town will be able to have it.*”

The fever was twenty-six weeks and two days old, and about two hundred people were in a square in a town of four thousand, and eleven lines on a copy of a roll that anybody in that town might read were land with people asleep behind them.

Nobody said one word about a number being in a mouth instead of in a square, because every person in that square had done the same thing with a different figure in about a year.

A man of thirty-one stood at the edge of it with nineteen doors on a sheet of paper and two numbers on the sheet, and the one that had been said out loud in a lane that morning was the one that was wrong.

There was no form anywhere in the county of Ostrey for taking a number back out of eleven mouths.

# Chapter 602: The Second Day Of The Hundred And Eighth Week, And A Wednesday, And On Which A Man Of Forty-Nine Named ORIEL SAYER Has Been Dead Since Before The Last Flood Came Up And Nobody Wrote The Day Down, And On Which The Chair He Held Is Stood Up Against The Side Of A Lighter With His Name Against It In A Book And Eight Of The Eleven In That Book Are Not Dead And Not Away, And On Which A Woman Of Seventy-One Who Kept His House Says Out Loud In About Nine People’s Hearing That This Is Not A Refusal And Is Not To Be Written Down As One, And On Which A Man Of Thirty-One Is Told In About Nine People’s Hearing That He Is The Only Person In That Room Nobody Can Be Sent For And Says Nothing About It

Second day of the hundred and eighth week. His three hundred and fifty-second morning. Two hundred and two days after the settlement. The fever forty-five weeks and four days. One hundred and fifty-six days since the division. One hundred and two days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

The day before was the first day of the hundred and eighth week.

---

A chair in Hask is named for the ground and not for the person, and there are twenty-nine of them. The names of the ground are on the inside of the lids in a hand older than the book. Eleven of the twenty-nine have a person against them in a place called the taking column, because the person taking a thing on ground is the person who may speak for it.

Oriel Sayer held the reed. He was forty-nine when he stopped.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Orla Fennimore. “*There is a man of thirty-one on this deck who has been in this town one night, and he is asking about a man who has been dead a year, and about nine of us are here. The reed is the ground under the landing, and the man who held it cut it for thirty years, and there is nobody in this town who can be sent for about him except me. I am standing in front of about nine people saying his name out loud for the first time this week.*”

Nobody on that deck said anything.

“*Then say the rest of that and do not be sorry for me,*” said Garrin Tolley, who had his pole under his arm and had not sat down. “*Because you said eleven on Tuesday and you have said two of them dead, and a man of thirty-one has heard the number twice in a day and about nine of us would rather he heard the name.*”

“*Oriel Sayer, forty-nine, of the lower ground, and it is the reed and not the gravel and the difference between them is about two feet of water in a bad week,*” said Orla Fennimore. “*He has been dead since before the last flood came up. Nobody wrote the day down. There is no form in this town that asks anybody to write a day down.*”

---

Then somebody else was put into the room, and nobody had sent for her, and she came because she keeps a house that is not hers and has kept it for eleven years.

She was seventy-one years old, and she was washing her hair in a bowl in a kitchen at the back of a house on the low side of the landing. She came up to the deck with wet sleeves and a towel over her shoulder. About four people on that deck knew her and about nine of the people in that town knew her.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Orla Fennimore, because she says it to everybody and it is the whole of what a book is for. “*Because there is a woman on this deck with wet sleeves and she is not in this book and there is no line for her and about four of you know her and about nine of you do not.*”

“*Eliza Lark, seventy-one, and I have kept the house at the low side since the year before the flood came up last,*” she said, “*and I am not of his family and I was never of his family and I have not had his name on anything in eleven years and I did not ask for it.*”

About four people on that deck said something at once. About nine did not.

“*Then say the rest of that and say what you want,*” said Garrin Tolley.

“*I want to be asked one thing in front of about nine people and I want it asked and answered in my hearing and then I want to go back to my bowl,*,” said Eliza Lark.

---

Then she was asked, and it was the whole of the morning.

The question was not who would sit in Oriel Sayer’s chair. It was whether anybody in Hask was entitled to answer for him at all, and that was asked first and it took about an hour and a half and nobody answered it.

“*He had no wife,*” said Eliza Lark. “*He had no children. His people are in a yard at Sallowcross four days off, and there is a brother of his in that yard. That brother went away in a lighter about eleven years ago and has not been heard of since. He has a chair at the other end of that book with the same name on it and nobody in it either, and the two of them are the two of the eleven who are not in this town and not dead.*”

“*Then there is nobody to ask,*” said Ilyan.

“*Then there is nobody to ask,*” said Eliza Lark.

About four seconds went past.

“*Then say what we do about it,*” said Garrin Tolley.

“*And I would like to say one thing about that before anybody does,*” said Eliza Lark. “*I would like it said out loud in front of about nine people and I would like it said by whoever writes it down, and what I would like said is that this is not a refusal.*”

Nobody on that deck moved.

“*Then say the rest of that and do not be sorry for me,*” said Ilyan.

“*A refusal is a person saying no,*” said Eliza Lark. “*I have been in this town seventy-one years and I have heard a great many noes, and I have heard them from people who were in a position to say them. Oriel Sayer is not in a position to say no. He is in a hole in the ground above the flood line, and there is nobody left who can put a question to him and get an answer.*”

About four seconds went past.

“*If you write that down in a book as a refusal, then in about four years there will be a form in this county that says a man who does not answer has refused,*” said Eliza Lark. “*And I will not have that in a book I have kept a house beside for eleven years.*”

“*Then say what it is,*” said Orla Fennimore, with the pencil behind her ear and her hand not going to it.

“*It is a person who cannot be asked,*” said Eliza Lark. “*That is all it is and it is a smaller thing and it is an uglier thing and it is not anybody’s no. Do not put my no on his line either, because I have not got a no for him either. I will not stand in a chair and say no for a man who is dead and did not ask me and would not have wanted me to.*”

About four people on that deck had that. About nine did not.

---

Then the morning tried to make a rule out of what was left, and there was no rule, and three people told the man of thirty-one so in about nine sentences each and he did not like it.

“*There are three ways a chair gets filled in this town and I have written all three down twice and there is no fourth,*” said Orla Fennimore. “*A son takes his father’s chair. That is one, and it is not a rule, it is what about nine people have done about nine times.*”

“*The other two,*” said Garrin Tolley.

“*The man who does the work sits in the chair of the ground he does the work on, and that has happened about four times and every one of the four times he was the son of somebody on the list, so we do not know whether it is a second way or the same way twice.*”

“*And the third,*” said Ilyan.

“*And anybody who stands on this deck and says yes in front of about nine people has the chair until somebody says no,*” said Orla Fennimore. “*That has happened about nine times in nineteen years and I have the days on all of them and there are only two in this book that came about that way and the two are the only two chairs anybody here can tell you were not a son and not the work.*”

“*Then one of the three covers him,*” said Ilyan.

“*A son, and he had none. The man who does the work on the reed, and there is a man on the reed and he is thirty-eight and he is standing on this deck with a pole and he is not a son of Oriel Sayer and he has not said yes.*”

“*Then the third way is open to anybody,*” said Ilyan.

“*The third way is open to anybody,*” said Orla Fennimore, “*and a man of thirty-one has been on this deck about a day and a half and is the only person in this room nobody can be sent for. Not his turn, not his ground, no household of his in this county, not a son of anybody on this list, and nobody nine miles off can be sent for to ask him whether he would like to be on it.*”

About four people on that deck turned round and looked at him. About nine did not.

Nobody on that deck thanked anybody.

---

Then a woman of seventy-one went back to a bowl of water, and the man with the pole went to work, and a woman of forty-four said out loud what her year was, because he had asked her twice what a chair was and she was going to tell him what she was paid for it.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Orla Fennimore, “*because I have not said a figure out loud in front of a stranger in nineteen years and about four of you have never heard it and about nine of you have.*”

Nobody on that deck said anything.

“*A penny a head on this landing and a penny a cart. About eight hundred heads and about two hundred and forty carts in a year, which is a thousand and forty pence, which is four pounds six shillings and eightpence. The book takes three pounds of that, which is ruling, binding, lamp oil and copying it out. What is left is three hundred and twenty pence, which is one pound six shillings and eightpence, and that is between the two of us. I have about half of it, which is a hundred and sixty pence, which is thirteen shillings and fourpence.*”

About four people on that deck checked that. About nine did not.

“*Then say what thirteen shillings and fourpence is a year of,*” said Ilyan.

“*It is a year of me,*” said Orla Fennimore, “*and a bed and bread in this town is a penny and a penny, which is two pence a day, which is seven hundred and thirty pence in three hundred and sixty-five days, which is three pounds and tenpence. So the year of the woman who keeps this book costs a person less than a year of being bedded and fed, and I have a house, and I have said that out loud once and I am not going to say it again.*”

Nobody on that deck thanked anybody and nobody on that deck was sorry for anybody.

The fever was forty-five weeks and four days old. A man of forty-nine named ORIEL SAYER has been dead since before the last flood came up and nobody wrote the day down, and the chair he held is the reed, and it is stood up against the side of a lighter in two rows of twenty-nine with his name against it in a book. Eleven of the twenty-nine have a name against them and nobody in them, and eight of the eleven are not dead and not away. They are people who have not come, and the book does not say why, and there is no column in it that could.

A woman of seventy-one said out loud in about nine people’s hearing that this is not a refusal, and that a person who cannot be asked is a smaller thing than a person who says no, and that she will not stand in a chair and say no for a man who is dead.

And a man of thirty-one was told in about nine people’s hearing that he is the only person in that room nobody can be sent for, and he said nothing about it, and what he said nothing about was a block of limestone with a line cut round it nine miles up the water.

And there is a house on the low side of that landing that is not his and has not been his for eleven years, and the woman of seventy-one who keeps it has gone back to a bowl. There is a chair stood up in the front room of it with nobody in it, and there has been since before the last flood.

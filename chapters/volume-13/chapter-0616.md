# Chapter 616: The Second Day Of The Hundred And Tenth Week, And A Wednesday, And On Which A Woman Of Twenty-Nine Who Said Yes To A Chair In The Open On The Thursday Of The Week Before Last Stands Up Out Of It And Says So In Front Of About Nine People, And On Which It Fails In The Open And Is Not Anybody’s Fault And Is Not Settled, And On Which A Man Of Thirty-One Asks For A Column In A Book And Is Told Why There Is Not Going To Be One, And On Which A Mother Comes Back From A Fair And Comes Out Of The Number Without Anybody Being Told

Second day of the hundred and tenth week. His three hundred and sixty-sixth morning. Two hundred and sixteen days after the settlement. The fever forty-seven weeks and four days. One hundred and seventy days since the division. One hundred and sixteen days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

Sefa Callow came back down the cart road on the Saturday of last week with what she had not sold in a basket on her arm and a length of oilcloth over her head, and she came up the plank at about the fourth hour and she was sixty-eight and she walked up eleven feet of it without stopping.

Nobody on that plank thanked her for the two days.

The count came down from ten to nine on the Saturday, and about four people on that deck knew exactly why it had moved, and about nine did not, and the woman who keeps the book was not on the plank and was not told until the Wednesday.

---

Then on the Wednesday a woman of twenty-nine stood up out of a chair in the open air, in front of about nine people, and said out loud that she was taking it back, and nobody interrupted her, and the count came back up to ten while she was standing there.

She had said yes on the Thursday of the week before last, in about nine seconds, because nobody had asked her whether she wanted it, and she had said so out loud on the same day and had asked for it to be written down somewhere that was not the book, and it had not been written down anywhere.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Garrin Tolley, from the after end. “*Because a woman has just stood up out of the reed in the middle of a Wednesday morning in front of about nine people, and about four of us understand why and about nine do not, and I would like it said out loud that nobody on this deck stopped her and that nobody on this deck could.*”

Nobody on that plank moved.

“*I am standing in front of nine people and the reed, and I said yes to it on the Thursday of the week before last about nine seconds after the question was finished being asked, because nobody asked me, and I have had thirteen days to find out what is on it. I have found out that the ground the chair is for is not the ground I am on. I have not stopped cutting withies and I am not going to, and I have not found out one single thing that makes me want the chair less, and that is the trouble.*”

About four seconds went past.

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Ilyan.

“*I am taking the yes back in the open, out loud, in front of about nine people, because a no in the open is the only door out of a chair that anybody in this town has. If I do it quietly, then in about four years anybody can do it quietly, and about nine of the ten of us would do it quietly. The day after that there would be a reason for it.*”

Nobody on that plank said anything to that.

Then she stood up, which took her about four seconds and which nobody helped her with, and she was nine feet off a boat in flat shoes and the deck moved under her once while she was doing it, and the man of thirty-eight put a hand out and did not touch her.

Nobody on that plank said anything to that either.

---

Then a man of thirty-one said the thing he had come to say, and it was out loud, and it was in front of about nine people, and it was a request for a column in a book.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said the woman of twenty-six from the ash, where she had been sitting for nine years, and she had not stood up. “*Because a man of thirty-one is about to say the fourth version of a thing he has said in four counties, and I have been in a chair for nine years and I would like to say this before he does.*”

“*I am standing in front of nine people and twenty-nine chairs, and I have just watched a woman stand up out of one of them out loud, and I am going to say that the book should have a column.*”

Nobody on that plank moved.

“*Say the rest of that and do not be sorry for me,*” said the woman of twenty-six.

“*You are going to say that the book should have a column for a reason, so that a person who stands up out of a chair is not the same as a person who has not come. It is the best thing anybody has said in this town for a fortnight. I am going to say no to it in about nine seconds, and I would like you to let me.*”

About four seconds went past.

“*Then say it,*” said Ilyan.

“*A column for a reason is a column for a reason. You put it in and about nine people in this town have got a place to put the true reason, and about nine people have got a place to put the reason they would rather have. About four of us find out about it in about four years, and after that the true reasons are the ones people doubt first. That is not a thing I have read anywhere. It is a thing I have been in a chair for nine years and watched happen twice.*”

About four people on that plank understood that. About nine did not.

“*And say the second half of it, and the second half is the one that matters. If there is a column for a reason, then the woman who stands up out of the reed on a Wednesday has to write a reason in it, in the open, in front of about nine people, and she has to give one that will be believed in about four years. And she has not got one. She has got a fortnight, and a withy knife, and the truth, which is that she cannot say why. About eight of the people who have been in that book could not say why.*”

About four seconds went past.

Nobody on that plank said anything to it.

“*And I would like it said out loud that I have been in a chair for nine years and I said yes to it in the open, and I have never once been asked whether I wanted it, and I am not standing up. I want it said in front of about nine people that I am not standing up, and I am not going to, and I am not going to pretend to anybody that the answer to her question is that everybody gets up.*”

---

Then a woman of forty said out loud what the standing had done, and she was on the bank, and her mother had come back on the Saturday and was at that moment in the fourth chair from the after end with a basket at her feet.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Wenna Callow, “*because a woman has just gone back to the gravel and a woman of sixty-eight came back up that plank on the Saturday, and the two of those are the same thing and about four of us can see it and about nine cannot.*”

“*I am standing in front of about nine people and my mother, who was at the fair at the crossroads for two days and is sitting in the fourth chair with a basket at her feet. She went into the number on the Saturday and did not know it, and nobody told her until the Wednesday, when a woman of twenty-nine stood up and went in as well.*”

Nobody on that bank moved.

“*Say the rest of that and do not be sorry for me,*” said Ilyan.

“*The count is ten again. It was eleven on the Tuesday of the week before last and it came down to nine on the Saturday and it is ten since about four seconds ago, and about nine people in this town have been carrying it for a fortnight.*”

Nobody on that bank moved.

“*And I would like it said out loud in front of about nine people that a count of a chair is not a count of a person. This town has known that since the Friday and has gone on using it as one.*”

About four people on that bank had that. About nine did not.

“*My mother was in that number for the two days she was at the crossroads, and she was not in it for the nine years before them, and the book took her out of it because she got into a chair. That woman was not in it while she was in a chair, and she is in it now because she got out of one. The count follows the chair. It has never once followed the person, and about nine of us have been reading it the other way round for nineteen years.*”

About four seconds went past.

“*And nobody is at fault. I would like that said out loud in front of about nine people before anybody in this town goes looking for who did it. My mother is not at fault for going. That woman is not at fault for standing up. The book is not at fault because it has never once pretended to know. The only person in this town who is standing nine feet off holding a boat-hook and who asked for a column about a minute ago is not at fault either, and about nine of us know that and about nine do not.*”

About four seconds went past.

“*And it is not settled. I would like that said too, in the same breath, and I would like it said by somebody who is not going to be thanked for saying it.*”

---

The fever was forty-seven weeks and four days old. He had been in that county sixty-two days. A woman of twenty-nine stood up out of a chair in the open and said so out loud, and nobody on that deck could stop her and nobody in that town could either. The count came back up to ten, and it is ten. A woman of sixty-eight had gone into a chair and out of the number on the Saturday without being told, and the two of them are the same line in a book and there is no column that tells them apart.

And a woman of twenty-six who has been in a chair for nine years said no to a column for about nine seconds and then said the better half of it, which is that the woman who stands up has not got a reason that will be believed in about four years.

And a man of thirty-one asked for the column and was told why there is not going to be one, and the reason is that a column for a reason is a column for a reason, and about eight of the people who have been in that book could not say why.

# Chapter 601: The First Day Of The Hundred And Eighth Week, And A Tuesday, And Which Is The First Day Of A Town Nine Miles Down A Cart Road That Is Under Water For Six Weeks Of The Year, And On Which A Lighter Is Lying At A Landing With Twenty-Nine Chairs On Her Deck And Nobody In Them, And On Which A Woman Of Forty-Four Who Keeps A Book Says Out Loud That She Is The Only Person In That Town Anybody Can Be Sent For About A Chair, And On Which A Man Of Thirty-One Is Nine Miles From A Block Of Limestone With A Line Cut Round It And About Nine People In That Room Have Not Been Told Why

First day of the hundred and eighth week. His three hundred and fifty-first morning. Two hundred and one days after the settlement. The fever forty-five weeks and three days. One hundred and fifty-five days since the division. One hundred and one days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

The day before was the seventh and last day of the hundred and seventh week.

---

The last quarter of a mile of the cart road is a plank, and the plank is eleven feet long, and a lighter is lying against the bank at the end of it with her pole under her fore thwart and a stone on the pole so it will not walk.

The town of Hask is in the county of Kell, nine miles down the water from a weigh-house where a man of thirty-one stood on Monday morning in about nine people’s hearing, and it is in the county, which is the whole of what is true about where he is standing.

He came it in two days because of the leg. A cart at a crossroads put him four miles on the road and brought the cart back. Four shillings is what that man asked and four shillings is what he paid, and the man did not ask him a single question. Ilyan Vester had under two pounds on Monday morning and has a pound and fifteen shillings and eightpence of it now. That is two nights of a bed and bread at a penny and a penny, and four shillings of cart, and that is the whole of what he has spent and he is not going to say the cart’s again.

Nobody on that plank thanked anybody for the two days.

He slept two hours on a bank above the water with the mooring rope round his wrist, because a man lying loose on a bank above a river in this country wakes up in it. The rope was cold and he could feel the pull of it going slack and taut in his sleep. Every time it did that he knew where the water was without looking at it, and that has been true of him since a Thursday morning in a room over a stable. It is not going to stop at a river nine miles from the one it started on.

He wanted to know what a chair was.

Nobody on that landing had anything to say to that.

---

There are about eleven hundred people in Hask. About nine of them were standing on the deck of a lighter called the Gull at about the seventh hour, and the Gull is about thirty feet from her stem to her rudder and she lies low in the water with a cart already half into her.

Twenty-nine chairs are stood up along her starboard side in two rows. They are deal, and about half of them have been out in the weather long enough to go silver at the joints, and one of them has a split in the seat that has been mended with a piece of withy gone round and twisted.

Nobody in any of the twenty-nine chairs was sitting in it.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said the woman who keeps the book. “*Because there are about nine of us on this deck and twenty-nine chairs on her, and every one of them is a line in a book. I am the only person in this town anybody can be sent for about a chair, and you are a stranger on a deck at the seventh hour, and I am going to write down that you stood here.*”

She was forty-four years old and her name was Orla Fennimore and she had kept the book nineteen years and she was standing on a boat in flat shoes with a pencil behind her ear and no slate under her arm.

“*I am standing in front of twenty-nine chairs and about nine people and a book with a lamp on it, and I have nine miles of cart road behind me and a bad leg, and I have not been in this town before Thursday.*”

Nobody on that deck moved.

“*Then say the rest of that and do not tell me you have not been in this town before Thursday as though it were an answer to my question,*” said Orla Fennimore. “*There are about eleven hundred people in Hask. Nine of them are standing on this deck. The other eleven hundred and ninety-one are not, and I have no way of saying anything to them that is not a book, and the book goes to the two of us and no further.*”

“*Then say why you are on my deck,*” said Orla Fennimore.

Nobody on that deck moved.

“*I am asked about a chair and I have been asked about a chair four times in two days, and I do not have an answer that is a whole one,*” said Ilyan. “*A man of thirty-eight in a weigh-house nine miles up this water asked me a question on Monday morning of last week and I gave him a true thing, and I came down here on a cart in the night, and I am telling you the whole of it, and the whole of it is that I do not know what a chair is and that nobody nine miles off thought I did when he asked.*”

About four people on that deck had that. About nine did not.

He could not leave the county and he was standing in that room. There is no form in the county of Kell that says a man may go out of it before the water goes over that block of limestone again, and about nine people on that deck were standing in the open air within nine feet of the fact and not one of them had been told it, and about nine of them never were.

“*Then say what a chair is,*” said Ilyan.

“*Say what a chair is out loud to a man in flat shoes, and say it as a person of this town and not as a man of a record,*” said a man of thirty-eight with a pole in his hand and gravel to the knee. He was called Garrin Tolley and Orla Fennimore said his name for him as though she had said it several times already. “*And say it plain, because about four of us will take it and about nine of you will not.*”

---

It is a chair, and it is on a boat, and the record of Hask is not a book of names and not a wall and not a cupboard.

It is twenty-nine chairs. A person holds one. Holding one means you sit in it when the town sits, and what you say in it is said for the ground the chair is on, and when you are not in it the chair is stood up against the side of a lighter and it is nobody’s.

There is no roll. There is no form. There is no line of descent and no clerk and no seal and no counter, and there has never been any of them, and the only person anybody in that town can be sent for about a chair is the woman with the pencil behind her ear.

“*Nineteen years,*” said Orla Fennimore. “*And there were twenty-nine the year before me. And the woman I learned it off said there had been thirty-one and could not tell me which two had gone, and I have looked for the two in every book in this town for nineteen years and I have not found them, and I am not going to invent them now.*”

About four people on that deck checked that against each other. About nine did not.

“*Then say what happens to a chair when the person in it dies,*” said Ilyan.

“*Nothing,*” said Orla Fennimore. “*The chair stands up against the side of this boat with a name against it in a book. There is no form in this town that says a dead man’s chair is struck and there has never been one, and I have asked twice in nineteen years and the answer both times came from a man who said there was no hurry.*”

---

Then somebody asked the question that was underneath all of it, and he asked it in about nine people’s hearing, and about nine of the people in that town cannot be asked it at all.

“*Say the rest of that and say the number,*” said the man with the pole. “*I have been on this deck since I was nine and there is a thing I have never once counted out loud, and a man of thirty-one has been standing on it for about an hour asking about chairs the way a man asks about a rate, and I would like to know how many of them are standing up.*”

“*Eleven,*” said Orla Fennimore.

“*Say how you know that,*” said Ilyan.

“*I counted them on Tuesday and I counted them on Tuesday afternoon and I got eleven both times. And I am not a person who can be checked, so the man with the pole counted them on Wednesday morning and got eleven. Two people, two counts, the same number, and I have put the day on it in the margin of the book and not in the book, because the book has no place to put a day except against a name.*”

Nobody on that deck said anything.

“*Then say the rest of that and do not end it at eleven,*” said the man with the pole. “*Say what the eleven are. Because I have known four of them all my life and there is not one of the four I could tell you died.*”

“*That is the whole of it and it is the reason the number is eleven and not four,*” said Orla Fennimore. “*Two of the eleven are dead. One of them has been dead since before the last flood came up and nobody wrote the day down, because there is no form in this town that asks anybody to write a day down. One of them went away in a lighter about eleven years ago and has not been heard of since and his name is on a chair at the other end of the book.*”

“*Then the other eight,*” said Ilyan.

“*The other eight are not dead and not away,*” said Orla Fennimore. “*They are people who have not come. That is all I know and it is all the book knows, and I have never once written the word for it. If I write the word for it, then in about four years every person in this book who goes to the fair for a week will have it written against them, and I will have made a list of the people of this town who were not in a room.*”

About four people on that deck said something at once. About nine did not.

---

Then the man with the pole said the half of it that the man of thirty-one had come nine miles for, and he said it in about nine people’s hearing.

“*Say what you are standing in front of,*” said Garrin Tolley. “*Because you have been on this deck about an hour and you have asked me about a chair twice and the woman about a chair four times, and I am going to say the thing under it, and about four of you will have it by the fourth hour and about nine of you will not.*”

Nobody on that deck thanked anybody.

“*Then say it,*” said Ilyan.

“*A chair is a person who says something for ground he is not standing on,*” said Garrin Tolley. “*That is the whole of it. There is no other thing about it. A man in a chair can say a thing about nine hundred yards of gravel that he has never walked, and about nine people will say he has said it. That is a use, and there is no form anywhere in this county that lets a person do it except by being asked and saying yes in front of about nine people on a boat in the open air.*”

“*And the eleven,*” said Ilyan.

“*And the eleven are eleven people this town has not been able to ask,*” said Garrin Tolley.

---

Then a thing was said on that deck that he was told and cannot pass on, and it was said in about four people’s hearing, and about nine of the people on that deck did not hear it, and the four who did are not going to say it again.

It was said late, when the cart was in and the water had begun to move under her.

Nobody on that deck thanked anybody.

Orla Fennimore did not say it on the deck. She said it at the after end of the boat, with her back to the two of them and the water going past. She said it to him and not to the man with the pole, and she said it because he had asked her four times about a chair and she had decided he was going to ask a fifth time.

“*I have sat in one of the eleven.*”

About four seconds went past.

“*Then say the rest of that,*” said Ilyan.

“*Twice. The first time was about nine years ago and the second was about four. Both of them were on a Tuesday afternoon on this deck in front of about nine people, and I got out of it both times before the sitting was done. I did not write anything down either time, and there is no day anywhere in this book against those two afternoons.*”

“*Then why did you do it twice,*” said Ilyan.

“*Because somebody has to be in it or a chair is not a chair,*” said Orla Fennimore. “*And do not say that out loud in that room or in a market or on a cart road. If it is said, then it is a thing a person can do. And if it is a thing a person can do then any of the eleven can be done by anybody who says they are minding it, and there will be no telling which of the nine hundred in this book is holding a chair for a man and which of them is holding one for themselves.*”

“*I am not going to say it,*” said Ilyan.

“*You are not going to say it because you are in this county for a year and you cannot be sent for after that,*” said Orla Fennimore. “*I have had a book for nineteen years and I have had you on this deck for an hour and I can tell which of us is going to be here. Do not say it. I have said it to you and not to him, and I will know which of the two of us you are by the fourth hour.*”

---

The fever was forty-five weeks and three days old. A town of about eleven hundred people stands a lighter at a plank and keeps its record in twenty-nine chairs stood up along her side. Eleven of those chairs have a name against them in a book and nobody in them. Two of the eleven are dead, one is away, and eight are people who have not come.

A man of thirty-one came nine miles of a cart road that is under water for six weeks of the year, in two days, with a bad leg. He had a pound and fifteen shillings and eightpence left of under two pounds. He asked four times what a chair was and was told, and was given one true thing he cannot pass on, because passing it on would turn a thing two people did on a Tuesday afternoon into a thing anybody can do.

About nine people on that deck have twenty-nine chairs and eleven names. About nine of them have a stranger on the boat, and about four of those nine have the four shillings of a cart, and the two nines have not asked each other.

And there is a page in that book that has been opened twice and written on neither time, and the only person alive who has been told so is standing at the after end of a lighter with his hands behind him, and he cannot pass it on.

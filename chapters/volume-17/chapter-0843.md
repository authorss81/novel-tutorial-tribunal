# Chapter 843: Fifth Day Of The Hundred And Forty-Second Week, And A Saturday, And Four Inches And One Day Of The Coming Back And The Next Time Is In Seven Days, And A Man Of Sixty-One Reads The First Of Nine Charters Aloud And A Seat Is Challenged And The Rule Is Spent In A Mouth And Not In A Panel, And Six Stones Are Named As Staying Where They Are

Fifth day of the hundred and forty-second week. His five hundred and ninety-third morning. Four hundred and forty-three days after the settlement. The fever eighty weeks old. Three hundred and ninety-seven days since the division. Three hundred and forty-three days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

The bank was about four inches over the top step at about the seventh hour, and it was one day of the coming back. The man of forty-three gave the reading and the working and the figure for the next time, and then said the fourth thing had been nothing for eleven days, and that he was not going to apologise for it in the same breath as the inches.

Corvin Tace is forty-three and has kept that landing nineteen years, and gives three things every morning. The fourth thing is a figure of how many of those ninety steps are under, and it has been nothing for eleven days.

“*About four inches at the seventh hour, and it is one day of the coming back, and it came off yesterday at about the sixth hour, and it is standing at the figure it stood at eight days ago, and I am not going to count the steps for you this morning.*”

“*And the next time about ninety steps can be walked is in seven days. That is the fifth day of the hundred and forty-third week, and I can give you that now because the bank is not full, and I know the rule, and I have not looked at the water-stone.*”

At about the ninth hour a satchel came open on the dry stone at the top of that landing, and a man of sixty-one read nine charters out of it, one at a time. He would not summarise the first one, and he said why before anybody had asked him.

Nevin Trask is sixty-one and has a book of heads and carts and two numbers in it in eleven years, and has refused since the second day of the hundred and thirty-sixth week to write a bench’s notice in it. The satchel came up the cart road on Friday evening after the light had gone, and was left at the top step by a person who did not give a name and did not stay.

“*A person left this at my step last night and went down the cart road and did not look back. I have thought about that for an hour and I have decided not to decide what it means.*”

“*Nine charters, one for each of the nine positions, and a bundle I am not opening this morning. A woman of sixty-four spent nineteen years on a summary once and I am not spending anybody’s morning on one.*”

“*Here is the first of them, whole, and it is about a stone, and I am going to read it as it is written, and not as I would say it.*”

He read the first charter whole, and it was about one of the six restored stones. It said the stone stays where it is, and the people who live nearest it keep it, and a woman of forty heard her own lane named in it and said a charter that names her lane had better be read twice.

“*One: the first of the six stones is the stone at the low end of that lane, and it stays where it is. It is not moved up the bank and it is not moved down it.*”

“*Two: the people who live nearest it keep it, and the keeping is theirs and not the seat’s. A seat has no power over a stone that a person is holding with their own hands.*”

“*Three: the seat for that position is held by the person who keeps the stone, and another person who lives nearest it may take that seat instead, and no person outside that lane may hold it.*”

“*Four: the terms on which that lane keeps the stone are published, and a term that is not published is not a term. The publishing is the keeping’s own work, and is not a duty laid on it from elsewhere.*”

The woman said the charter had her lane in it, and then said what she thought of the fourth line, and refused to have it read as a compliment.

Wenna Callow is forty and has asked for a day against a name for eleven years, and has kept the first of the six stones for those eleven years. She will not name a room. She has never published anything in her life, and she has been on that landing four mornings this week.

“*That is my lane.*”

“*And I want one thing said before anything else is said about me this morning. I have asked for a day against a name for eleven years. I am not going to stop asking on a Saturday because there is a charter lying on a table, and I am not going to be thanked for keeping a stone that I was already keeping.*”

“*And the first three of those lines are what I have been doing for eleven years, and I want that said in about nine of your ears before anybody puts it in a book. I did it with a barrow and my own back, and not with a charter.*”

“*The fourth one is the trouble. It says the terms are published, and it says publishing is my own work, and it is, and I have never published anything, and that is not modesty. It is that there has never been anything of mine worth the paper.*”

“*And I am not saying no to it, I am saying I want it read twice, and I want somebody to tell me where it goes. A line that says a term is not a term until it is published has not told me.*”

Then a woman of forty-one who holds a different seat asked the question the charter had left open. She asked it as a question and not as a refusal, and she said which of the two it was going to be.

Ivet Quillon is forty-one and holds a seat that has no stone behind it yet, and came up the cart road on Friday night with the person who left the satchel. She has asked to be asked things in public since she arrived, and nobody has yet done it.

“*I am not refusing that charter, and I want that said first, in about nine of your ears. About four of you have been waiting for somebody to say a hard no, and I have not come up here to be the hard no.*”

“*Here is what I cannot do, and I am not being clever about it. That fourth line says a term is not a term until it is published, and I have been in this county one night, and I have not seen the terms of that lane published, because they are not published, and I am not the lane and I cannot publish them for it.*”

“*So if I hold a seat on Monday, and the rule I am under is a rule I have not seen, then I am in exactly the place the arrangement puts people every day. I have been here one night and I can already feel how that feels, and I am not going to pretend I need longer than that.*”

“*Ask me the question properly, and ask all of it: who publishes, to whom, and in what words, and when. Ask it in the ordinary voice and I will answer it in the ordinary voice.*”

Then a man of about fifty-two read the clause out of the printed edits, and said he could check it and could not check two other things. He named his ninth thing, and a woman of sixty-four said what the clause was worth and what it was not worth.

Simon Rook is about fifty-two and cannot see well, and has a palm and a forearm, and has named eight things he cannot check and a ninth on this morning. A boy of thirteen has been keeping a count of how many he names.

“*I have opened the bundle, and it is one sheet and eleven lines, and I am going to read the seventh, because a woman of forty-one has just asked the question it answers, and I would rather put it in front of her than in front of a room four hundred miles off.*”

“*Seven: a seat is not bound by a rule it has not published and reviewed. A rule that binds a seat without that is not a rule of this common archive. It is an instruction, and an instruction is not a seat’s rule, however old it is.*”

“*That is the seventh of eleven lines and I have read it twice, and I can put a line under it, and that is why I am saying it out loud instead of letting about four of you assume that a man who cannot see well has not understood a thing.*”

“*And here is my ninth thing I cannot check, and I am naming it because a boy of thirteen is counting. It says a seat, and it does not say what a seat is made of: a person, a council, or a street. I can read the line and I cannot tell which of those three it is talking about.*”

Ada Renk is sixty-four and has a name in ink on the ninth line of a book four hundred miles off and has never had a day against it. She heard the seventh line read out, and she said what it was worth and what it was not worth, and she said it in that order.

“*That line is the whole of what I have wanted to hear said out loud in nineteen years. I am not going to be thanked for saying so, and I am not going to be brave about it, and I want both of those said in about nine of your ears before I go on.*”

“*Here is what it is worth, and it is a great deal more than about nine of you are expecting. For nineteen years I could not tell the difference between a rule and an instruction, because a rule arrives looking exactly like an instruction, and both of them are in ink, and both of them are said in a room with the door shut.*”

“*And here is what it is not worth, and this is the part the woman of forty-one has felt for one night and I have felt for nineteen years. A seat that may say no is worth nothing on a Saturday morning on a landing, if the rest of the charter can be taken away from it on the Tuesday.*”

“*So the seventh line is the first half of a thing, and the first half of a thing is a very expensive half. I am not going to stand on this bank this morning and let about nine of you call it a settlement because a man who cannot see well read it out.*”

Then a boy of thirteen said what the seventh line has to be attached to before it is a settlement, and said it in the ordinary voice. He said he had been keeping a count, and that he was giving the count and not the argument.

Wat Marshe is thirteen and has been in this county a hundred and ninety days, and the figure is one more than the morning before, and he counted it in the lane before the light came up.

“*A hundred and ninety days, and it is one more than the morning before, and I counted it in the lane before the light came up.*”

“*A woman of sixty-four just said the seventh line is half a thing. I am not going to argue with her, and I am not going to pretend I worked it out first.*”

“*Here is what I have got, and it is a count and not an argument, and I am giving it whole. That bundle has eleven lines in it, and a man of about fifty-two has read the seventh, and that is one of eleven, and it is a figure of lines and not a figure of anybody.*”

“*And I am keeping a count of how many of those lines a person on this landing can check, and it is one, and I will say which one. He can check the seventh and I cannot check the other ten, and I am not going to say I have read them because a man of sixty-one has a bundle in his hands.*”

“*And that is all I have got, and I would rather say a small thing that is true on a Saturday than a large thing that is a boy being pleased with himself in front of nine people. That is the exact shape of the thing a woman of sixty-four has handed away three times.*”

Then a man of thirty-one said what he was and was not on that landing, and gave the figure for the standing offer out loud with the subtraction in it. He said what a quorum of nine seats and six active seats is and is not, and he named the three of the nine positions he still cannot account for.

Ilyan Vester is thirty-one and is nobody’s, and has been in this county two hundred and eighty-nine days, two hundred and eighty-nine being eight hundred and forty-three less five hundred and fifty-four. The cut across that palm is a hundred and fifty-eight days old, a hundred and fifty-eight being eight hundred and forty-three less six hundred and eighty-five.

“*I am going to say four things and then stop, and I am going to say them in this order, because a woman of forty-one asked a fair question and it would be a poor sort of man who answered her out of order.*”

“*One: I hold no seat, and I am not going to be given one by anybody on this landing this morning. If about four of you have been waiting three days for me to be handed one, then you are going to keep waiting, and I would rather say that than have it turn up in a charter on Thursday.*”

“*Two: nine seats, and six of them have to be active to keep the shared archive going, and both of those are figures of seats. Nine is a figure of seats and six is a figure of seats, and neither of them is a figure of a person, and I am not going to stand here and let them turn into one.*”

“*Three: the third, the eighth and the ninth of the nine positions are not stones, and I have known that for eleven days. They are a bell network in one place, a works arrangement in another, and a building where paper is kept in the third. None of those three is a person, and I do not know how a bell network holds a seat, and neither does the man of about fifty-two.*”

“*And four, and it is the one I am not allowed to be reminded of. The offer asked at the seven-hundredth is a hundred and forty-three days old, and a hundred and forty-three is eight hundred and forty-three less seven hundred. None of this morning is an answer to it, and I am not going to let a charter read out in about nine of your ears be taken for one.*”

Then a man of sixty-one said the first charter could not be accepted, and said what he would accept, and named the thing that was missing out loud. About nine of them stood on a landing with a bank four inches over the top step and a charter that had been read twice and not taken.

Nevin Trask is sixty-one and read the first charter twice and did not take it. He set it face down on the dry stone and said what he would take it with.

“*I am not going to write it in the book this morning, and I am not going to write the sixth line of it either. I have two numbers in eleven years and I am not starting a third with a thing I have not understood.*”

“*Here is what is missing, and it is not a feeling, and here it is in the ordinary voice. That fourth line says the terms are published, and it does not say to whom. To a clerk, to a room, to a page read out with the door shut, which is what the arrangement has been doing to me for nineteen years, and which a woman of sixty-four has just told you is the whole of the difference between a rule and an instruction.*”

“*And a man of thirty-one has said he will not be given a seat, and I am writing that down in about nine of your ears, because in about three days somebody is going to say he was offered one, and I do not want to be the only man on this landing holding that the offer was refused.*”

About nine of them were on that landing at about the eleventh hour of a Saturday, with one day of the water coming back, and one of nine charters read twice and not taken, and a seventh line out of eleven that a woman of sixty-four said was half a thing.

The fever was eighty weeks old. He had been in that county two hundred and eighty-nine days, and the cut across that palm was a hundred and fifty-eight days old, and a hundred and forty-three days after an offer that was asked at the seven-hundredth and has not been answered.

The first of nine charters was read aloud twice and was not accepted, and the sixth line of it is still blank, and a woman of forty said her own lane had better be read twice, said she had asked for a day against a name for eleven years and was not going to stop, and then said she was not refusing.

A man of about fifty-two read the seventh of eleven lines out of the arrangement’s own printed edits: a seat is not bound by a rule it has not published and reviewed, and a rule that binds a seat without that is an instruction and not a seat’s rule.
A woman of sixty-four said that was the first half of a thing and that the first half of a thing is a very expensive half, and she was not thanked for it and was not brave about it.

A boy of thirteen gave a count and not an argument: one of eleven lines can be checked by a person on that landing, and he said he would rather say a small thing that was true.
And a man of thirty-one said he holds no seat and will not be given one, that nine and six are figures of seats and not of persons, and that none of the morning was an answer to the standing offer, which is a hundred and forty-three days old and unanswered.

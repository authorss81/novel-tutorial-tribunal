# Chapter 698: Seventh And Last Day Of The Hundred And Twenty-First Week, And A Monday, And On Which The Water Comes Off The Top Step In The Night For The Fourth Time Since The Saturday Of The Hundred And Nineteenth Week, And The Coalition Puts What It Has Into Something Other People Can Read, And A Woman Of Twenty-Eight Who Is Not One Of The Nine Decides What A Copy Is For And Says Out Loud Before She Starts That Nobody Is Going To Ask Her

Seventh and last day of the hundred and twenty-first week. His four hundred and forty-eighth morning. Two hundred and ninety-eight days after the settlement. The fever fifty-nine weeks and two days. Two hundred and fifty-two days since the division. One hundred and ninety-eight days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

The water was off all ninety of the steps by the sixth hour. The man of forty-three gave the reading and said the other half of it, and about four people on that bank had been waiting nine days to hear him say it.

Corvin Tace is forty-three and has kept a landing nineteen years. A full bank is about sixteen inches over the top step, and he has said he has nothing to say about a night four times.

“*Off all ninety at the sixth hour. It came off the top step in the night, and that is the fourth time since the Saturday of the hundred and nineteenth week, and I was on that landing at the second hour because there is nothing else to do at the second hour.*”

“*And I am not going to tell you how many days that is. I gave a reading every morning this fortnight and I stopped after the reading on the first of them and I am not starting again on a Monday.*”

About four people on that bank had the four times and about nine of them had ninety steps out of the water.

Then a woman of twenty-eight said what she was going to do before she did it. She said it standing in the wind with nothing in her hands, and about nine people were on that bank.

Sera Quill is twenty-eight, came up that cart road on the Monday of the hundred and thirteenth week, and cannot be sent for about in this county or in the one she came from. She keeps a day book with two numbers a day in it in her own hand.

“*I am going to make nine copies. I am going to do it in about four days, and it is going to cost me the whole of every one of those days.*”

“*Nobody is going to thank me and I am not going to be thanked.*”

“*And here is the part I want said in this order, and I want it said before I start and not afterwards.*”

“*Nobody is going to ask me whether I will do it. Nobody is going to be able to ask me, because there is no form in this county that says a person may be asked, and there is no form that says a person may not, and about nine of you know both of those.*”

“*And nobody on this bank can be sent for about me. That is why it is mine. It is not because anybody thought it should be mine.*”

“*And I have not been asked, and I have not refused, and a person who has not refused has not said no either.*”

About four people on that bank heard the order of those sentences and about nine of them heard the words.

Then a man of forty-one said what a copy in this county is made of, and he said it before anybody asked. He brought nine boards up the track on the back of one of his carts and set them down in a row.

The man is forty-one and keeps two carts, and the near wheel is making a noise. He has a fire for warming the grease on a cart axle and he has had it about nineteen years.

“*Boards. About the length of my own forearm and the width of two hands. I have cut nine of them out of the bottom of a load of stone crates I was never going to get back.*”

“*And a stick burned at the end for the writing. You cannot write on a board in this county with anything anybody has got, and you cannot rub anything off one either, and I am not going to stand here and tell you that is the same as being right.*”

Then a woman of forty-four said the thing about the second half of that. She said it flat, and about four people on that bank had been thinking it.

Orla Fennimore is forty-four and has kept a book of twenty-nine chairs nineteen years for thirteen shillings and fourpence a year. She has said a list is a column and has not said it out loud since the Saturday of the hundred and twentieth week.

“*Nine boards is a list. I have said that sentence to a man of sixty-one three times in a fortnight.*”

“*And I am not going to say it a fourth time this morning, because this morning I have not got anything new.*”

“*And a burn in a board cannot be rubbed out in about four seconds the way a thing on a wall can. I am not going to be thanked for noticing that either.*”

“*Because not being rubbed out is not the same as being right, and a woman of forty-nine has been telling this bank the difference between a blank and an empty for about four days.*”

Nobody on that bank improved on her and about nine of them had the four seconds.

Then the man of forty-one gave the count of what was going on nine of those boards, and he gave the working out loud, and he refused to finish it, and the refusing is the fourth time he has refused a sum on that bank this fortnight.

“*Nine. That is nine openings counted twice by two men walking opposite ways, and nine jobs cut over them, and nine of the nine put back together out of what is left in them.*”

“*And I have watched eight of them get done since the Wednesday of this week in about four days.*”

“*And not one of the nine has a name on it. Nine and nothing is not a sum and I am not going to give you a total.*”

“*And I am not going to add the nines to the nines the way a man of thirty-one did on the Saturday of the hundred and twentieth week before anybody stopped him.*”

“*And not one name is not a smaller number than one name. It is the other kind of number. I have said that once this week already and I am saying it again because the second time is the one that counts.*”

About four people on that bank had the nine and about nine of them had the other kind of number.

Then a man of thirty-one tried to keep one of the nine. A woman of twenty-eight stopped him in six sentences, and a man of fifty-four who cannot read stopped him in eight more, and nobody improved on either of them.

Ilyan Vester is thirty-one and is nobody’s, and there is no column in the county of Kell with his name at the top of it. There is no form that says a man may hold a thing.

“*I will hold the ninth. I am nobody’s anyway, and if a thing nine people have gets lost then a man nobody can be sent for about is the right person to—*”

“*You are not going to hold anything.*” She did not raise her voice. “*I am not going to be a keeper of a thing nine people have. That is the same sentence I have been saying since the Saturday of the hundred and nineteenth week, and it is the fifth time this fortnight.*”

“*And you are nobody’s, and there is no form that says a man may hold a thing. And you have just said the reason out loud in the wrong order, and the wrong order is the reason nobody can be sent for about you.*”

Then a man of fifty-four said the other half of it, and he cannot read, and he said it before he had been asked anything.

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four and holds the gravel at the head of that drain. He is the only person anybody on that bank can be sent for about, and he has said no four times in nine days.

“*And I will tell him the other half, because he has not thought of it and about four of you have.*”

“*A thing nine people have is a thing nine people can be sent for about. I have been saying that in a different shape for nine days.*”

“*And if nine people have it, then any one of the nine can be asked what the other eight did with it. There is not one form in this county that stops that.*”

“*And I cannot read a word of a burn in a board. I am going to take one of these nine boards and I am not going to know what is on it, and I am going to have it anyway.*”

“*And I am not going to be thanked for that, and I am not going to be the man who says he was right first about the fifth thing in nine days.*”

Then he took one, and about four of them saw him take it, and he turned it over twice and put it inside his coat and did not ask anybody to read it to him.

And the man of thirty-one gave his board to a man of thirty-eight, and said why, and the man of thirty-eight took it and did not thank him and did not say sorry.

“*You have got a lamp and you are the only person on this bank who can put a light on a thing in the dark, and I cannot hold it, and I am not going to pretend I gave it to you for any other reason than that.*”

Then a man of sixty-one was offered one and refused it, and he gave the reason in seven sentences, and it was the fourth refusal of that kind in about a fortnight and the first one about a thing he could actually have carried.

Nevin Trask is sixty-one and has kept a book of heads and carts eleven years. There are five lines on the leaf after the last head, and the last of the five is a number he wrote on the Saturday of the hundred and twentieth week.

“*No. And I want the reason said out loud, because I have been saying no nine times this fortnight and about four of you have stopped believing me.*”

“*I have refused to write nine spaces in this book. I have refused to write two names. I have refused to write a first of the nine.*”

“*That is a book with things in it that people have told me about in a place I have not been.*”

“*And I am not going to carry a copy of it in the inside of a coat, where I would be a man in a coat with a thing in him, and there is no form in this county that says what happens to a man like that.*”

Nobody on that bank argued with him and about nine of them had the inside of a coat.

Then about nine boards went out of that county that week, one to each of about nine people. There was no original and there was no keeper, and a man of forty-three had a burnt board under his own coat with about four inches of one end gone in it.

And a lamp was burning on a hook in the ninth of nine rooms about a hundred and thirty feet under that bank, about a quarter full of oil, and nobody lit it and nobody put it out.

And a stone about the size of two hands laid flat had nine lines in it, in nine hands that cannot write. The ninth of the nine lines was cut on the Thursday of the hundred and eighteenth week, and not one word of any of them has been read by anybody in four counties.

---

The fever was fifty-nine weeks and two days old. He had been in that county a hundred and forty-four days.

Nine of the nine were put back together out of what was left in them and not one of the nine has a name on it. On the last day of the hundred and twenty-first week that was burned into nine boards and carried out of the county by about nine people, and there is no original and there is no keeper.

And a woman of twenty-eight made them, and said out loud before she started that nobody was going to ask her, and that nobody on that bank could be sent for about her, and that a person who has not refused has not said no.

And a man of sixty-one refused the ninth board and gave his reason. A man of fifty-four who cannot read took the eighth one and has not asked anybody to read it to him.

# Chapter 672: Second Day Of The Hundred And Eighteenth Week, And A Wednesday, And On Which Nine Go Down Past The Last Of The Beams And Past The Place Where The Water Stops And Find Nine Openings, And A Man Of Thirty-One Says A Fourth Name And Is Stopped By A Man Who Cannot Read

Second day of the hundred and eighteenth week. His four hundred and twenty-second morning. Two hundred and seventy-two days after the settlement. The fever fifty-five weeks and four days. Two hundred and twenty-six days since the division. One hundred and seventy-two days since a page was read out in a room with the door shut, in a town in another county.

---

They were on the water at about the fifth hour and the man of forty-one was in about four feet of it by about the sixth, and it took him about two hours, and he said the reason out loud before anybody asked him for it.

“*A man of forty-one emptied nine steps yesterday in about two hours, and the drain has put back about two feet of it in a day, and about two hours takes two feet back off again.*”

“*And I am going to say the other half so that nobody puts it in a book wrong. About two hours is enough for nine people to stand in. It is not a figure of those nine steps and it is not a figure of that flight and I am not going to let it be one.*”

Nobody on that bank thanked him for the two hours and he did not wait.

Then the nine went down, and the man of thirty-one went down fifth with his hands and nothing else in them. The lamp was the man of thirty-eight’s, and the two who lift the board had been found at about the seventh hour by a boy of thirteen who was not going down and who nobody had sent.

The passage is about as tall as a man and about as wide as two men for about nine feet, and then it narrows, and there is water in the narrowing, and then it opens out.

The gallery is about nine feet high and about as wide as a room with a hearth in it. And there are about nine beams in the roof of it set about four feet apart. And eight of them are still, and the ninth goes along about two inches and comes back, and there was no person in the room.

About four people in that gallery said the word out loud before anybody had asked them anything, the way they had said it on the Monday of the hundred and fifteenth week and on the Tuesday and on the Wednesday of last week. And nobody in that gallery argued with them and nobody in that gallery told them they were wrong. A man of thirty-one said out loud that he was not going to supply a fifth thing about it, and that he has had five in him since the Monday of the hundred and fifteenth week.

Then the man of thirty-one went past the last of the beams, and about nine feet past it the roof came down and the walls came in and the floor came up. There is a line about nine inches up the wall that goes back all the way to the bottom of the ninety steps, level, the whole way.

And below the line the wall is dry and the gravel is dry and about a foot deep, and it is walked.

Not trodden. Walked, by somebody going to a place and coming back from it, for a long time. And there is no groove in the gravel and not one in the stone under it.

“*About nine feet, and I have paced it, and a pace of a man of thirty-eight is about two feet and that is four and a half paces.*”

“*And I have measured it with my own rope twice and got about nine feet both times, and the rope is mine and I did not lend it to anybody and I am not going to.*”

“*And I have laid my hand along that wall nine times going along it, and it is nine hands. And I cannot read and I can do that.*”

About nine people in that passage were looking at the gravel. Nobody in that passage said a word about the absence of a groove.

Then the man of thirty-one went past that place as well, which nobody had done, and the passage went on.

It did not turn and it did not narrow further and it did not go up. It went on at the same fall, dry, with the same line about nine inches up the wall going back past them all the way.

The man of thirty-eight went first with the lamp and the man of forty-one went second, and the man of thirty-one went fifth again.

“*About sixty-five paces. A pace of mine is about two feet, and sixty-five times two is a hundred and thirty.*”

“*And that is a figure of my pace and not of the passage, and I have said so before about nine feet and I am going to keep saying so as long as there are figures.*”

“*And a man of forty-one says a hundred and thirty feet is about fourteen of the nine-foot grooves he pushes a stone along. That is fourteen and he is not going to round it.*”

“*I would like about four of you to have two figures that do not agree rather than one that has been rounded.*”

Then the passage came up out of itself about a hundred and twenty feet on, and there was a step, and there was a wall.

And in the wall there were openings.

Nine of them, and they were about as tall as a man and about as wide as a man, and they were not opposite one another and they were not the same distance apart, and over every one of them the same thing was cut into the stone.

Two hands.

Not a hand and a bar and a numeral. Two hands, side by side, cut once. The cut is the same cut in all nine of them.

A man of sixty-six stood under the ninth one and put a hand flat on it. He said the mark in the lintel ninety steps back is this mark, and that he has been under about ninety steps of bank six times in about eleven days and knows one cut from another.

“*I have said the guild mark is a different mark and I have said it twice and I am not going to take it back for a room with a lamp in it.*”

“*The guild mark has a bar under it and a numeral on the bar and that is what a guild mark is for. This has no bar and no numeral. And a mark with no numeral on it is not a mark a builder puts on a thing he made.*”

“*And I will tell you what it is. It is a mark a person puts on a thing they have to come back to. And I have not got a figure for that and I am sixty-six and I am a sinker and I know what a hole is.*”

About four people in that passage had the bar and the numeral. About nine of them had two hands cut in stone nine times.

Then they counted the openings, because nobody could write them down, and a man of thirty-eight counted them going along the passage and a man of fifty-four counted them going back, and both of them got nine.

“*Nine going along and nine coming back and neither of us has a hand that can write and the man of sixty-one is on a bank with a book he will not put this in.*”

Then the man of thirty-one said a name for it, and it was the fourth name anybody had said for anything down there in about four days, and he said it in one sentence in a passage with a lamp in it.

“*It is a row.*”

“*No. I cannot read and I have said so seven times now, and I am not going to say how long ago I said the first of them.*”

“*And you have said three names in three sentences on Wednesday and a fourth one in one sentence on this morning. That is four names in four days.*”

Barnaby Crove is fifty-four and holds the gravel at the head of that drain and has not walked a quarter mile he holds in nine years.

“*And the count of me stopping you is four in four days and I would like about four of you to have that figure instead of the name.*”

“*And I am not going to do it twice. There is nothing in that word and a man of thirty-eight has told you there is nothing in it and I am the hand in this room that cannot write and there is nobody else to stop the fifth one.*”

“*And a row is a thing you put things into. I have not seen anybody put anything into one of them and I have not been in one of them and I am not going to say what I think is in them from a doorway.*”

About nine people in that passage heard a man who cannot read keep a fourth place out of a list in one sentence, and nobody in that passage said a word about it.

Then a woman of forty-nine said a figure out loud, and nobody had asked her for one, and she has come down that road twice now and does not know what a copy is.

“*One hundred and eighteen days. Six hundred and seventy-two less five hundred and fifty-four.*”

“*I count days because I count loaves and I count loaves because if I do not count them I bake the wrong number and about nine houses find out at about the sixth hour. So a day is a thing I can do without anybody asking me and that is the only sort of number I have ever had.*”

“*And I counted nine of them going along and I counted nine of them coming back and neither of those is a number I would put on a door, because I do not know what a copy is and I do not know what you would copy.*”

Nobody in that passage explained anything to her.

Then the lamp guttered, and about four people in that passage could not see anything. The man of fifty-four was led out by a hand on the coat by a man of thirty-eight, and he said nothing at all on the way out.

And the man of thirty-eight said afterwards that the man of thirty-one was not going to be asked a question about any of the nine openings on the Wednesday. He said the reason was that the man of thirty-one had not been asked a question on the Wednesday of last week either.

Then they came up, and the board went on its stick by two people because that is what it takes, and the boy of thirteen was at the head of the flight with a barrow and a coat and did not go down.

Nobody on that bank thanked anybody for coming up, and the man of thirty-one said out loud that the coming up is the part nobody thanks anybody for.

---

The fever was fifty-five weeks and four days old. He had been in that county one hundred and eighteen days.

Nine people went down past the last of about nine beams, and about nine feet past that place. The passage went on dry for about a hundred and thirty feet by the paces of a man of thirty-eight, and it was about fourteen of the nine-foot grooves of the stone a man of forty-one pushes, and the two figures do not agree and nobody on that bank made them agree.

And in a wall at the end of it there were nine openings with the same mark cut over all nine of them. A man of fifty-four who cannot read counted nine and a man of thirty-eight counted nine, and neither of them has a hand that can write it down.

And a man of thirty-one said a fourth name for it, and was stopped by the same man who stopped the third one. The count of that man stopping him is four in four days, and about nine people in that passage heard a person who cannot read keep a place out of a list for the fourth time in four days.

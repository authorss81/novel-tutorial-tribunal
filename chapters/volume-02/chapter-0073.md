# Chapter 73: What They Actually Wanted

The bar was at the end of the lane and it was not a gate. That is the first thing and it took the bench of four about a fortnight to stop describing it as one.

It was a stone, about waist high, on the boundary where the lane met the drovers' road, and a slot had been cut in the top of it about the width of a thumb, and an iron chain lay across the road beside it with a ring on the end of it, and the ring was the size of a cart wheel. There was a board on a post above the stone and the board had been painted once, a long time ago, by somebody in a guild's colours, and what was on it could be read if you knew what you were looking at.

> **LOWGILL WARD. TOLL ON DRAUGHT. ONE PENNY THE LOAD AND NO PASSAGE FREE TO ANY MAN ALIVE.**

Nobody had worked that bar in living memory. The chain came up when the first cart of the day came, and the first cart of the day lifted it, and if nobody came for a week the chain lay across the road and carts went around it or through it or somebody broke it, and last year a drover had taken a corner off his own shaft to get round it and had said nothing and gone.

Hester Vole kept the tally of the yards, not the toll. What she had for eleven years was the count of carts, by day, in a book, in a hand that was nearly a fair hand because she had been taught the psalms and numerals come with psalms. She had never had the money out of the slot. The slot had a stone in it and the stone had been put there by her husband's father, and the penny a load went into a common fund that paid for the lane's two bridges and the chapel candles and a share of a schoolmaster who came four times a month, and the whole of the arrangement worked, and the whole of the arrangement worked because about sixty people lived in nine yards within a mile of it and every one of them knew the arrangement was a good one and none of them had ever asked what happened on a week when nobody passed.

Then the Orison fen road came through, and the drovers went up the canal, and carts came off the main road for the grass and the hedges and the water and did not stop at the bar, because the bar was a penny a load and a waggon of hedge is not a waggon of draught and the drovers' own clerk had said so in Orison in a year nobody in the lane could name.

Which is the whole of the trouble at Lowgill, and it is not a trouble anybody in this case has ever had to look at, because a bar with a penny in it and nobody standing at it is not a gate and a gate is not a bar and this case has spent nine weeks on gates.

“It wants about a pound a week off the carts,” said Hollis Dune, on the first of the month, in a cold room with a candlestick on the table. “That is not a great deal. I have had this lane measured both ways. It is not about the money. It is that there is nowhere to put a cart that has been caught. I have had a man put a load of stone on the flat by the chapel and the chapel has had a fortnight of nothing and the man has never been back and there is a stone on the flat that is not the chapel's.”

“You have a bar,” said Ilyan.

“We have a stone and a chain and a slot and a post and a board that says ward on it,” said Dune, “and that is four things, and none of the four is a man.”

Nobody had said the word out loud in that district in living memory. He had read it off the board to them and he had said what it meant in a guild's words, and he had said out loud that the word on that board is a guild's word and not a bench's word and it means a duty and not a place, and he had said that a court has no power to make a seat, a name, or a body out of a piece of work and has said so on the face of a record twice. He had said all of that in eleven minutes in a cold room and it had made no difference whatsoever, because the thing that is wrong at Lowgill is not a word on a board.

---

They did not want a charter. He established that in about forty minutes, and it took forty minutes because nobody said it, and he established it by being given four answers that were all about a *bar*, each of which turned into an argument about a horse trough.

What they wanted, and it came out in the last of the four answers, from Jonas Pyle, who was sixty-three and had farmed eleven acres his son did not want and who had driven the second reading of the nine sheets for the benefit of a man who could not read them, was a person to come and stand in the room.

“Listen to me,” he said. “I have been the convenor of this bench nineteen years and we have had a matter before the First Verdict once in my life and it was a boundary and it went against us and I did not know what had happened to me until a year afterwards. And I have read — she read it — nine sheets about a gate and a pump and a woman who cannot appeal, and I have understood about one word in four, and I am telling you that the sheets are not what we are after.

“What we are after is for somebody to come up this lane and sit in this room and be somebody we can ask. Once. So that this bench knows what it is looking at. I am sixty-three and I have four sheets of my own about the toll and about a month of carts and about a schoolmaster and I have shown them to two magistrates’ clerks and both of them have been kind to me and neither of them could tell me a thing, because there is nobody in between me and them. There is no page. There is no counter. There is a bar and a stone and eleven years of a book in a kitchen and a woman who can read and cannot write, and we have been a district for a hundred and forty years and we have never once had anybody come up the lane and sit down.”

And then he said the other thing, and he said it with a candlestick between them.

“You are the first one to come up the lane in nineteen years,” he said. “So the bench of four of Nethergill is going to have to decide this afternoon what it is looking at, and I am going to ask you and you are going to answer, and then I am going to minute it. Who are you.”

He gave them the true answer and it took him about nine sentences and it was the worst thing he has said in this case, and it was true.

“I am not a bench and I am not a party and I am not a body,” he said. “I am not a district and I cannot be sworn and I cannot be produced and I have no office here and none in the nine sheets. My standing in this city is confined to one matter about a bridge eleven miles from here and in that matter I may file and be heard and I may not be cited as a permit or a schedule or a levy or an acknowledgment or a bearer of labor. There is no word in the charter for what I am. Harrow wrote the fifth term so that there would be a person in it and I am not the person in it and there is a woman four miles from here who is.

“What I am for is this: the fourth term of that charter says the terms are carried to any district that asks, and it names me, and nobody else may carry them, and if I refuse or I am unable or I am more than four miles off, then the terms are not carried and the appeal is stayed until another carrier is sworn. I am the reason that appeal is not stayed. That is my whole use. I am a man that a door depends on, and I walked eleven miles to bring you nine sheets, and past the fourth mile nobody in this city could have made me do it, and I want that in your minute, because it is the truest thing I know about what a carrier is and it is not a comfort to be one.”

Jonas Pyle wrote it down. He wrote it slowly, in the hand of a man who farmed, and he got the spelling of *sworn* wrong and did not correct it, and he read it back at the end and said, “That is a bad minute to hand a man.”

“It is the true one.”

“It is the true one,” said Pyle, “and I have had thirty years of being a bench and the true one is always the bad one, and I am sixty-three and I would like it minuted that I have been told by a stranger in my own room what the stranger is for.”

---

And then the copy.

Hester Vole had gone down the lane at the third bell to get the nine sheets she was keeping, and she came back at the fifth with them rolled in a cloth and a boy holding the other end of the string, and she put them on the scrubbed table in the order they were sworn in, which is the order the string has them in, which is the only order there is.

“Let nobody speak for two minutes,” she said, and she was in the room, and nobody had asked her, and the bench let her.

He began at the first sheet. He got through the first three and a half of the fourth and then he stopped, in the middle of a line, and read the same line again, and the room did what rooms do.

“Go on,” said Hollis Dune.

“This is the copy the court file carries,” he said, “and it is the same nine sheets as the one in a room over a pump four miles from here and the same as the one on a table in a weighhouse office in this city where it is not binding anybody, and the three of them were sworn the same afternoon. And this is the second half of the fourth term, and the fourth term ends here, and the fifth term begins on the next sheet.”

“That is the end of it,” said Dune.

“That is the end of it,” he agreed, “in all three.”

And it went quiet in the room, and Hester Vole said, “Then somebody in this district has been holding a fourth one.”

She went out of the room and came back with a folded sheet inside a cloth, and she had had it in a dresser drawer since the twenty-seventh of the month before, because a drover had brought it down the lane on his way back from Orison and left it at the bar for the reader, and there was no asking and no payment and no clerk and no seal on the string, and nobody in the district had known what it was until the twenty-eighth, when Marguerite Skeath looked at it and said out loud in a room that it was the new rule.

“No,” said Hester Vole, coming round the table with it open, “not *a* difference. The string is the same string and the number is the same number and the fourth sheet is a half a sheet long in this one as it is in yours, and the half that is missing is the half that has a man’s name in it. I have had both of them in my hands, in this room, at this table, and I am the only person alive who has laid them on top of each other, and I have not laid them on top of each other before today because the other one is in a room four miles away and I have never once been in it and I am not a party and I may not appear in support of one.”

She said it in the flat voice she reads notices out in, and the bench of four looked at the two sheets, and neither the bench of four nor a girl of twenty-nine had ever seen the two of them together, because the copy that had got here on its own was the one that had come off a cart, and the copy that had come off a cart is the copy a clerk makes when a clerk is copying a thing for a carrier to walk away with.

“The clerk stopped,” said Corva Silt’s entry, which he had in a strap, and read out of it. “She wrote at the foot of the fifth sheet that the fourth was copied in full. She wrote what was true about the sheet and it was not true about the terms. A clerk does not copy a man’s name into a district’s paper when a district has not asked for a man. It is not a fault. It is a habit. Every copy of a term in this city stops where a person begins, because a person is not a district and a district cannot be served on one. And that is why a form ends up with a word at the top of it. The thing that gets copied is the rule. The thing that gets left off is the person, and the thing that gets left off is always the part with a consequence in it.”

Nobody said anything. The candle in the bottle made a small sound and went on making it.

Then a man in the room said, “I’ll take them.”

He was about fifty. He was not one of the four; he had come in at the fourth bell to hear about a cart that had gone round his gate, and he was a drover, or said he was, and he had four miles of road between him and anywhere.

“I’ll take them on to whoever asks,” he said. “I go the road twice a week. That’s the whole of it, that’s nothing.”

And the entire room turned and looked at a stranger in a lane, because a stranger had just said the sentence that four hundred people’s door depends on, and nobody in the room knew that, and he knew it, and he was the only one in the room who knew it.

“No,” he said.

“I have not said anything wrong.”

“You have said the right thing,” he said, “and you have said it about a thing you have not read, and the reason you have not read it is that it is not in the paper you have seen. In the paper you have seen it ends and then the next term starts. And the rest of it says that I am the only one who may carry these terms anywhere, and that if I do not, the appeal in that charter is stopped — not for you, not for this district, for four hundred people four miles from here who have got nothing else — and it is stopped until a bench somewhere swears in another carrier, and there is no instrument in this city that says how a bench swears in a carrier, because nobody has ever needed one.”

“You are telling a room full of people that you have a job.”

“I am telling a room full of people that a rule that nobody wrote for them is keeping a door shut and that the person who keeps it shut is a man who does not live in their district and is not paid by it and cannot be produced by it, and that the only thing standing between four hundred people and a stopped appeal is that I said yes in nine words in a room in Orison, and I have walked eleven miles since and I would do it again and I am not going to pretend to you that it is a noble thing, because if I do not go then the door stays shut and the fault is mine and not theirs, and that is a sentence I would like in the minute as well.”

The drover said, “You could have said the first half and kept the rest.”

“Yes,” he said. “I have thought about that on eleven miles of road and I have not slept on it and I am telling you that I could have, and that if I do that again I am a gate with a better head on it, and I would rather be a carrier who is found, because being found is the only protection I have got.”

Jonas Pyle put the pen down and said, “Then the bench will minute that the whole of it has been read out in this room and that a man in this room offered to carry it and was refused by the carrier in person, and that the bench does not hold a copy of the fourth term and has sent to a district four miles away for one, and will minute which district and why.”

And Marguerite Skeath said the thing that the room had not thought of, which was that if a term cannot be copied because it has a man's name in it, then the paper will keep losing the parts of itself that matter, and that a bench cannot be a hundred and forty years old and keep losing its own conditions one by one in a clerk's hand, and that they ought to write their own copy out, in the room, from the words, and swear it themselves.

It took until the light went. Six of them wrote and four read and a woman of fifty-one who kept the chapel book did the copying, because the chapel book has a hand in it that people in that district can read, and the ninth sheet was done at the seventh bell by candlelight and sworn by all four and by two drovers who had come in and stayed, and the man who had offered to carry signed it as a witness, which nobody had asked him to do and which he said he wanted because he wanted to be on a page.

And the copy is in a kitchen four miles up the lane, under a weight, in a cloth, with the string in the order it was sworn, and Nethergill cannot pay for printing and did not print it, and it is the only complete copy of the charter of the district of Halloway in this world that was made in a kitchen, and it binds nobody, and it is the only one of the four in this district.

Nobody in this city can come back for it.

And the woman who laid the two sheets on top of each other in a kitchen and found the half that was missing, so that there are four complete copies in the world instead of three and a half, is twenty-nine years old and cannot appeal, and was not in the room, and had walked down the lane twice to look at the bar for a man on a road.

# Chapter 53: The Form

They came down the bank at the fifth bell on the fourteenth day and the smell had got worse in two days, and Wenna Tace was at the standpipe, and there were four loads of sand and lime standing on the fen road above the gate that had come down the night before and gone back up again.

“They were mine,” she said, before anybody asked. “District work. The apron round the standpipe, the one that stops the pipe taking the water sideways when the river is up. Two years I've been telling them. Four loads. Sent up at dusk and turned back at the sixth bell.”

“By whom.”

“By the form,” said Wenna Tace.

---

The sanitation room was fuller than it had been on the thirteenth, and it was fuller by about nine people who had come to find out why the carts were going back up the hill, and Nell Ambrey let them all stay, which was a decision with a cost in it and she made it before she sat.

Corva Silt had the form on the table when Ilyan came in, face down, under her hand, and she had a fresh sheet of district paper under it and the seal was out, and she had been printing since the fourth bell.

“Read it out,” said Nell Ambrey.

“There is a form for a public instrument of a district,” said Corva Silt, “and I filled it in yesterday and I had it printed at the fourth bell this morning, and it is numbered, and the number is on it, and the number is what makes it the district's instrument and not a copy of somebody else's, and I want that said first because everybody in this room is going to say I copied a bridge.”

She turned it over and pushed it two inches into the middle of the table.

“Will somebody read it,” she said. “I am the clerk of it and a clerk reading her own instrument is not worth the room's time.”

Wenna Tace got up. She read the first clause twice, which nobody asked her to do, and Nell Ambrey said *once, Miss Tace*, and Wenna Tace said *the word in that one is the one they are all going to argue about and I am not going to be asked for it twice*, and then she read on, slowly, because it was longer than it looked, and she read the whole thing once, and she stopped at the third clause, which is the one Cael Rundle had already put his finger on, and looked at the clerk to be told whether there was more.

> **FORM H-9. LOW-WATER GATE, HALLOWAY. SANITATION INSTRUMENT, NUMBERED, PUBLISHED AT THE FOURTH BELL, THE FOURTEENTH DAY.**
> **ONE. THE GATE AT THE LOW-WATER CULVERT SHALL REMAIN CLOSED TO ANY PERSON WHO IS NOT A LAWFUL TRAVELLER OF THIS DISTRICT, A LAWFUL TRAVELLER BEING A PERSON ENTERED IN THE TRAVELLER BOOK KEPT AT THE GATE SIDE.**
> **TWO. NO LOAD SHALL PASS THE GATE IN EITHER DIRECTION WHILE THE TIDE IS IN, AND NONE IN THE DIRECTION OF THE DISTRICT AT ANY TIME, THE CULVERT ROAD BEING A PENAL ROAD AND NOT A HAUL ROAD.**
> **THREE. THE GATE SHALL BE WORKED BY THE GATE HAND AND BY NO OTHER PERSON. ANY PERSON WHO OPENS THE GATE OTHERWISE DOES SO AT THEIR OWN COST AND MAY BE CHARGED FOR THE WATER THAT COMES UP.**
> **FOUR. THE SANITATION COMPLAINT OF THIS BENCH, ENTERED ELEVEN WEEKS AGO, IS NOT DISCHARGED BY THIS INSTRUMENT AND IS PRESERVED IN FULL.**
> **FIVE. THIS INSTRUMENT MAY BE READ BY ANY PERSON AND COPIED BY ANY PERSON AT THE COST OF THE PAPER.**

“Number three,” said Cael Rundle.

“Number three,” said Corva Silt, “is mine. I wrote number three in the small hours and I wrote it because a notice that says who may open a gate and does not say it is the gate hand is a notice that says anybody may open a gate, and if anybody may open a gate then the water comes up. It is a good clause. It is the only clause in the instrument that is not off another sheet.”

The room sat with that, and Ilyan looked at the clause and could not read it, which is a thing he had been preparing to say for a month and had never had a better place to say it than a numbered instrument with a district seal on it in a room that had not asked him.

“I cannot tell you,” he said, standing up, “whether number three is a lawful thing for this bench to do to a gate, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. I cannot read a clause. I have said that in open court on a bridge and it is entered in the record there and it goes in here too, and I have two hundred guild words and none of them are these. If you want to know what number three means, the person who can tell you is at this table, and it is not me.”

“Then you will sit down and be quiet, then,” said Cael Rundle, without much edge, because he had said *then you are a man in a room who will not give his name* in a lane two days ago and had got a worse answer than he expected and was not sorry.

“No,” Ilyan said. “I will not. I will read you the numbers, which I can do, and I have had them since the thirteenth and nobody wanted them, and I want them wanted.”

---

He put Coll's slate on the table and turned it round so the room could read it upside down, which Wenna Tace did for him.

“Forty-one people at the standpipe at the sixth bell. Nine at the ninth, after the pump's second shift comes on at the eighth. Nineteen at the seventh. Eleven carts turned back since the sheet went up. Four loads of district sand and lime turned back last night. Nine carts came through this gate in a good week before the sheet, and the same nine in a bad week, and it has not been more than eleven in any week since midsummer, because there is no haul on this road and there never has been. That is the whole of the traffic and it has been counted for twenty years by the gate hand.”

“Then the carts do not matter,” said Nell Ambrey.

“The carts do not matter to the water and they matter to everything else in the room,” said Ilyan. “The water is the sixth-bell queue and the ninth-bell queue, and those two numbers are a shift and not your sheet, and your sheet has not touched them and is not going to, and if you shut that gate to every cart in this district the queue at the sixth bell tomorrow morning will be the same forty-one. What your sheet does is take eleven carts a month off a road that only ever had nine, and the eleven it takes are the ones that would have carried the low water out.”

“Then shut the carts and leave the people,” said Nell Ambrey.

“That is your decision, convener, and I am not entitled to it, and I have said so twice now and I will say it again if it helps.”

“It helps,” said Nell Ambrey, and did not make it.

Kett stood up, and he had it in his hand, and it was the one sheet in the city that every person in that room had a reason to want.

“Number two,” he said. “Read number two again.”

Wenna Tace read number two again: *the culvert road being a penal road and not a haul road.*

“Who wrote that,” said Kett.

Corva Silt said, “I did.”

“Where is that from.”

“It is from the district's own book of roads. The penal road book. It is the oldest paper in this building and it is a hundred and sixty years old and it is kept upstairs because somebody had the sense to keep it. The culvert road is in it as a penal road. It is not a haul road. It has never been a haul road.”

“It has a barrow go up it two hundred times a year,” said Wenna Tace.

“A barrow is not a haul,” said Corva Silt.

“A barrow is *four loads to a cart*,” said Wenna Tace. “I know because I have done the cart. Miss Silt, I have done the cart, that is how I know what a haul is, and I have never once been paid for it as one.”

That got into the room further than anything Ilyan had said all morning, and it got in because it was a number too, and it was a number about a person the room had not thought of as a person, and the room had been careful with numbers all its life.

“Enter it,” Nell Ambrey said, and Corva Silt entered it, and it is in the day book for the fourteenth, and it is the second entry in two days that began with a woman's own arithmetic.

---

The turn was not a speech and it was not a clause. It was the cart, coming back down the hill at the sixth bell in front of eighty people with everything still on it.

“Clause one is the bridge's sentence. Clause four is your own and it is good and it is honest and I want it kept. Clause three is yours and it is the best clause on the sheet. And the district's road book is a hundred and sixty years old and it says this road is a penal road, which means this gate was never a gate anybody hauled through, and there is no version of the ninth bridge's charter that makes you wrong about that. You are not copying us. You have used our paper to say something true.”

He put the sheet down.

“Which means,” he said, “that in four days you have built an instrument with your own seal on it that does what a fair rule does, in a room with no advocacy in it, on the strength of a document a man asked to be able to be argued with, and I have to say to this room that I do not know whether to be proud of the rule or sorry for the paper, and I have been trying to work it out since the twelfth day and I have not, and I am not going to stand here and tell you that the template is a monster. The template is a *frame*. Somebody put a good frame on a bad floor and nailed it up, and the good frame is what everybody is looking at, and it is going to be the frame every time.”

Nobody in the room had wanted to hear that, and two people wrote it down.

Then Ilyan was asked, and the asking was not by Nell Ambrey, and Corva Silt had put the names in the notice and so the room was entitled.

The first question came from the back of the room, on the strength of the Bench's order read aloud by Corva Silt at the eighth bell, from a man Ilyan had not seen before, about thirty-five, with a builder's apron on and lime in the seam of it.

“You are the one on the paper,” the man said. “Right. Here is the question and I have asked it four times and got four answers and none of them was a person. What did you do about this.”

“About which part.”

“About *this*. Our gate.” The man put his thumb on the sheet. “Not about us. About the paper. There was a paper and it was your paper and it was a bad one in our lane and somebody had to do something about it. What did you do.”

“I asked for the ninth bridge's settlement to be entered as a precedent available to other districts,” Ilyan said, “so that a district could read the terms of it and not guess at them. I did not send it to Halloway. I did not know Halloway existed. I had it done because I had learned in three weeks that a rule nobody can read cannot be argued with, and I asked for it in a corridor at two in the morning and I was not frightened of it, and a person I work with told me to be frightened and I did not do it, and four days later a boy on a bicycle brought me a copy of it off a gate.”

“So you made it printable,” the man said.

“I made it printable.”

“And you cannot read it.”

“I cannot read it,” Ilyan said. “Not the settlement. Not this form. Not clause three. I have about two hundred words and they are load words and they are bell words and they are a count book, and I have been in this city a month and I have been the instrument of a thing in four districts and I cannot tell you what it says. That is not modesty. That is the shape of what I am, and if somebody needs a document read they must ask a clerk, and there is one.”

“And the man who cannot read it is the reason we have to have one,” said Cael Rundle.

“Yes,” said Ilyan. “And if you want to make a rule about that, make it about me, and not about the paper.”

The builder's apron man thought about it for about as long as it takes a load to come off a span.

“Then you are no use in this room,” he said, “and I am sorry, because I have a bad thing and I want somebody to make it good, and there is no somebody. And I have been in this room three times and there has been a court man on the bridge four miles up the road and a recordwright from a town that has an archive in it, and there is one lawyer’s clerk in this district and she is thirty-one and she printed it.”

Then he sat down, and it was over, and nobody moved for a moment.

---

The consequence that could not be taken back happened at the sixth bell, in full view of about eighty people, and it happened because a form is a form.

Bryn Sallow had been at the gate since the fifth bell and the form was on the board beside her with its number on it and the seal on it, and she could read numbers and not clauses, and she was doing her thirty-year job, which is that when the tide comes in, the gate goes down.

The tide came in at the fifth and three quarters. The gate went down. It takes four minutes to bring the gate down with a pole and a hook, and she did it, and it took her the four minutes, and the water came up behind it and stopped where it always stops, and Weir Lane dried, and about two hundred people who live behind that gate have never known anything else.

And at the sixth bell a carter came up off the fen road with four loads of sand and lime on a district contract and a paper from the district's own yard, and he did not stop at the gate, because he has made the run forty times, and he came down the bank and put the wheels in the mud by the sill and started to unload.

And Bryn Sallow went to the chain and took the handbell off its hook — a handbell, a bell on a chain on a gate, attached to nothing whatever, attached to no deck and no joint and no load and no span — and she rang it, and the sound went across a lane and a bank and a fen road and about two hundred people heard it.

“Off the approach,” she said. “That is the tide and the gate is down.”

“Two minutes,” the carter said. “Two minutes and I have it off.”

“No,” said Bryn Sallow.

He looked at the sheet on the board, and then at her, and then at the sheet, and he said, not unkindly, in the voice of a man who is tired and has a bad day, “You have not read that.”

“No,” said Bryn Sallow.

“Then you cannot do that.”

Then he went back up the bank, with the load on, and the four loads of sand and lime went back up the fen road, and the apron round the standpipe did not get its sand, and the pipe will take the water sideways the next time the river is high, and there is a woman on Weir Lane who has been waiting eleven weeks for a doctor who is coming about the smell.

And a boy of about nine stood on the bank and watched the whole of it, because it was the sixth bell and there was nothing else to do.

Bryn Sallow did not know what she had done. That is the part that cannot be taken back, and Corva Silt wrote it into the day book in the district's own hand before the bench rose, at Ilyan's request and not the bench's, because Ilyan had understood in the lane what any record has to be for, and this one was not about a clause.

> **AT THE SIXTH BELL THE GATE HAND WORKED THE GATE IN THE MANNER OF INSTRUMENT H-9 IN THE PRESENCE OF EIGHTY PERSONS. THE GATE HAND CAN READ A NUMBER AND CANNOT READ A CLAUSE. THE GATE HAND HAS NOT READ THE INSTRUMENT.**
> **THE GATE HAND WAS NOT INSTRUCTED. NO PERSON INSTRUCTED THE GATE HAND.**

Corva Silt read it back before she entered it and then entered it, and then she said, without any particular tone at all, “I wrote that instrument. He worked it. Neither of us spoke to the other one about it, and that is how instruments do most of their work in this world, and I have known that for six years.”

Nell Ambrey rose, and the room did not rise, because the room was on Weir Lane and Weir Lane's water had just gone out and the smell had started again, and there were about two hundred people who needed to be at home before it got worse.

“Fourth bell tomorrow,” she said. “This instrument stands until somebody in this room says a different thing about it in a way I have to answer, and Mr Vester is here to answer things about a paper, and the district pays him nothing for it and he has not asked, and the record will show both of those facts.”

She looked at Ilyan.

“And I will say the one thing I have learned in three days and I have not said it and I am saying it now. If somebody had come down that bank on the second day with a form of ours and said *convener, this closes your gate and here is a public instrument with your seal on it, and it is right, and I am sorry* — I would have been glad of it. That is the part I cannot get past. Somebody might have come.”

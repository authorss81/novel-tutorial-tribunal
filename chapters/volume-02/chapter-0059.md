# Chapter 59: The First Appeal, and the Woman It Does Not Reach

The field closed on the twenty-second day and closed in nine minutes, and the closing was a formality and the formality was the point: Harrow discharged the field, preserved the coupling of the two records, entered the burden schedule and the two declarations and the keeper's letter, and entered the gate hand's four hours and the balance of the tide as unpaid, and then he said the sentence that closed it.

“Enter that this court has taken no finding on the gate, has no power over the gate, and has expressed no view on whether it should be open or shut, and that everything this court has entered about the gate has been entered about paper, and that two pieces of paper in this field said true things to each other and both of them are still true, and the field is discharged.”

Two days later, on the twenty-third, Wenna Tace asked whether there was a way to refuse the gate.

Nobody had asked her that. She had heard the term, because the term was read aloud twice in a room she was standing in, and the term is short and the whole of it is a right, and she is a woman who can count a load and read a notice and she put the two together on her own over two days and came down the bank at the seventh bell with a question and a piece of paper.

“There is a way to refuse a burden,” she said, “on the bridge. It was read out. The refusal goes in a column and it is against the burden and not against the person and it does not put you back on any list and it does not take anything off you that is already entered. I have heard it four times and I remember every word, and I would like to refuse the four hours and the balance of the tide, and I would like the refusal to go in the column.”

“That is the term,” said Halloway's own bench, through Corva Silt, who had brought four chairs and a day book, and who said it in the voice she had learned for reading instruments aloud. “The refusal is entered in a register kept in the ward and sworn by the landlord of the lower road.”

“Then I want it sworn by the landlord of the lower road,” said Wenna Tace.

“There is no landlord of the lower road in this district,” said Corva Silt.

“There is a landlord of Weir Lane,” said Wenna Tace. “His name is Bream and he takes a shilling a week off forty of us and he has never once written a name on anything in his life, and I am not asking Mr Bream to swear anything. I am asking whether the instrument that does it is in this district or four miles up the road.”

“It is four miles up the road,” said Corva Silt.

“Then I am four miles too far away,” said Wenna Tace, “and I have been for eleven years, and I did not know that until the fourteenth, and I would like somebody to write down that I did not know that until the fourteenth, because in about a year somebody is going to tell me the right existed all along, and I would like it on a page that it is the wrong distance away, and that nobody came down here and told me there was a column somewhere with my name going into it."

Corva Silt wrote it down, and it went into Halloway's day book, and it is the third entry since the twelfth that began with a woman's own arithmetic, and the day book is a district day book in a district that has never paid anybody a wage in a line with a name on it.

Harrow said, “Is there another route.”

“There is the appeal,” said Sera Quill.

She said it standing, and she said it to Wenna Tace and not to the court, and she had the sixth term of the charter in her own copy and she did not open it.

“Any person affected by a ruling on this span may challenge it before the review date, in a public sitting, on notice to every party named in the instrument. That is term six, and it is in the charter, and it is the court that wrote it, and the court said out loud on the day that it does not suspend access, housing, or work, and it is on the inside of the back cover of a book in my coat with the appeal date on it in my own hand.

“Wenna Tace. Are you affected by a ruling on this span.”

“No,” said Wenna Tace.

“No,” said Sera. “You are not, and I am going to say the rest of it in front of everybody because it is my instrument and I am not going to have it be a thing that gets found out in a year.

“That term says *on this span*. The span is the ninth bridge. You are at a culvert four miles down the road. The word on this span is the whole of the door, and it has been the whole of the door since a court amended a charter on a bridge in a week when the whole of the case was about whether a man could stop a cart.

“The same thing is true of the refusal, and it is worse in the refusal, because the refusal is the thing people use when a rule is too much, and it is entered in a column in a ward, sworn by a landlord, on a lower road, and it is the best instrument anybody in this city has built in nine years and it cannot go four miles. Hanne Kolm is sixty-one and she walked forty minutes with that box under her arm and she is four miles up the road and she is not coming down here, and I am not asking her to, because a sworn column is not a form and it is sworn by a person and a person cannot be posted.

“Both of the two things that were built in this city for the person who has been harmed are on one bridge in one ward, and neither of them is any use to you, and neither of them is any use to the four hundred and eleven loads, and the reason is not a conspiracy and it is not a clerk's malice. It is two hundred years of *this span*, and I have been the person who carries these terms to another district for six days and I have got them here in a coat and I put them in a room in Halloway in the first five minutes because nobody was going to ask for them, and a district that has not asked has no door in it, and I went in the front and there was a clerk who is thirty-one and kind, and behind her there is a wall, and the wall is nineteen years old and it has a petition on it and I have not read it and neither has she and neither has anybody in this case.”

“Then who do I write to,” said Wenna Tace.

It went round the room and nobody took it, and it is the correct answer to a question asked in a room, that everybody looks at the floor, and about nine people did, and the ninth person was Harrow, who did it on purpose.

“You write to me,” he said.

“You are four miles up the road,” said Wenna Tace.

“I am, and I will tell you what I will do with it, and it is not much and I would rather say it than have it found out.” He put the tray down on the step. “A court can hear a challenge that names a condition, a burden, and a remedy. It cannot hear a grievance about a wall. If you came to me with your two hundred loads and no condition, I would have to tell you that I have no finding to make and no instrument to enter, and I have said that to three people this month and I have said it kindly and it is the same sentence every time.

“But a refusal is a different thing. A refusal does not need a court. A refusal needs a place to be written down and a person who will swear to it, and there is no such place in this district, and the only such place I know of is a column on a lower road four miles up the fen road, sworn by a landlord with a sealed volume in her own house, and it is the only instrument in this city that records a burden and no address, and if you wrote your refusal on anything it would have to be that.”

“Then I will take it up the road,” said Wenna Tace, “and it is four miles and I have a barrow.”

“You are not taking a barrow twelve miles,” said Cael Rundle, from the wall, “and I have got a cart, and you will ride in it, and I am not doing it as a favour, because I moved the motion on the fifteenth to have the precedent withdrawn and it was refused, and I have been thinking about what I actually wanted on the fourteenth and I have not got it, and I am not going to sit here on a bank and be the man who let a woman carry her own refusal up four miles on a barrow.”

The appeal was filed on the twenty-third, at the ninth bell, and Ilyan filed it, and it is the first appeal in this case and the first one anybody has ever used anywhere that any of the people in this room can remember, and it took eleven minutes to file and four bells to serve.

That is the part that has to be in a record: it worked.

Roke wrote it. He read it back. Odile read the conditions of the charter aloud to make sure the ground was right and Harrow checked her against his own copy and neither of them differed by a word. Larch read the list of parties on notice back into the field, and the list is fourteen names and one seal, and it is the Ro household and Neris Ro first, because a clerk writes the party with the most to lose at the top.

The System put it in front of Ilyan at the moment Roke entered it, and it was a record of a challenge and not a rule about the world, and it was the fourth thing since the hearing that had gone into this city's record out of his own account and could not be taken back, and the other three were two copies of a private sequence from a game called *The Sovereign's Wake* and a precedent he had asked for in a corridor.

**CHALLENGE ENTERED BEFORE THE REVIEW DATE UNDER TERM SIX OF THE NINTH-BRIDGE CHARTER.**
**CLAIMANT: I. VESTER, ATTACHED RECORD-HOLDER, A PARTY BY THE RULING, LIMITED TO THIS MATTER.**
**PARTIES ON NOTICE: EVERY PARTY NAMED IN THE NINTH-BRIDGE INSTRUMENT. FOURTEEN NAMES AND ONE SEAL.**
**CONDITION DISPUTED: WHETHER A REVIEW HELD AT ONE SPAN MAY BE RECEIVED IN A BENCH THAT HAS NOT ADOPTED THE INSTRUMENT.**
**BURDEN: THE BURDEN OF THIS CHALLENGE IS THE COURT'S. IT IS NOT THE CLAIMANT'S AND HE HAS NOT ASSIGNED IT TO ANYBODY.**
**WHAT IS NOT ASKED: THAT ANY GATE BE OPENED OR SHUT. THAT ANY PERSON BE PAID. THAT ANY HOUSEHOLD LOSE AN ACCESS, A ROOM, OR A WORK RECORD.**
**THE APPEAL DOES NOT SUSPEND ACCESS, HOUSING, OR WORK. THAT IS IN THE CHARTER AND IT IS NOT A FAVOUR.**

“This is the fourth,” he said, to nobody, on the step.

“The fourth what,” said Sera.

“The fourth thing I cannot un-enter. Two sequences, the precedent, and this.” He looked at the list of fourteen names. “And I am not counting the field, and the field is the one that cost a man his afternoon. I want that said while it is in front of me, because in four years there is going to be a clerk who reads these four entries and thinks they are the same size, and they are not, and the one on the bottom is the only one I chose on purpose and the only one that made anybody's life different in a way I can point at.”

Harrow said, “Then say it in the record and I will enter it, and I will enter the reason, which is that a claimant who knows which of his own entries is the expensive one is a claimant whose entries can be relied on, and this court has relied on two of them and been wrong about neither.”

> **THE CLAIMANT STATES THAT HE HAS ENTERED FOUR THINGS INTO THIS CASE'S RECORD WHICH HE CANNOT WITHDRAW, THAT TWO OF THEM ARE HIS OWN PRIVATE SEQUENCE, THAT THE THIRD IS THE PRECEDENT, AND THAT THE FOURTH IS THIS CHALLENGE, AND THAT HE STATES WHICH OF THE FOUR COST SOMEBODY AN AFTERNOON SO THAT A CLERK IN FOUR YEARS DOES NOT ASSUME THEY ARE THE SAME SIZE.**

Neris Ro got the notice on the twenty-fourth. It is the second piece of paper in a month about her own household that she did not ask for, and she read it standing in a lane on the lower road with a day's washing in a basket, and she came down to the workroom at the eighth bell and put it on the bench.

“I have read it,” she said. “Every line of it is about my crossing and my room and my work, and I have got the sixth line right, and the sixth line is that the appeal does not suspend anything, and I want it read back in the room with my name in it, because in about two years somebody is going to try to make this thing suspend my crossing to get me to stop watching it, and I would like the paper to have my name on it about that before they try.”

“It does not suspend,” said Harrow.

“I know it does not suspend. I am not asking,” said Neris Ro. “I am asking for the arithmetic to be entered while the woman who can check it is standing up.”

Ilsa Bray taught Tamsin the letters of the guild's book over two evenings and refused to help her afterwards, and this is a story about what that refusal cost, and it is not a small thing, and it is the only thing in a month that has an ending anybody could call good.

Tamsin Ro came down the fen road on the twenty-fourth with a slate, which she put down on the bank when she got to the top of it and did not pick up again, and she is ten, and she was not on anybody's list and was not called and came, which is a different thing.

She stood at the top of the bank and looked at about two hundred people on a flat and a gate with a woman at it and a bell on a chain that was attached to nothing, and she said:

“Is that the sentence?”

“That is the sentence,” Ilyan said, “and I am not going to read it at you. You have read it. It is not yours. It was written out in a counter's hand on a bridge — she wrote it herself and she would not let anybody change a word of it — and it is filed in a guild's book in the guild's words, and a copy of it is filed beside the instrument in a town four miles away, and it does not govern and the instrument does, and I could not read either of them when I was in your room, and I have said that in open court and it is in the record.”

“I know I wrote it,” said Tamsin. “What I want to know is whether the machine is the same.”

“No,” Ilyan said, “and I am not going to be able to tell you why in a way you would like, and I am going to try anyway, because you have told me twice that you will stop everything yourself if you are put in a room as a reason, and I am telling you that this is me putting you in a room as an example, and it is not a reason for anything, and if you want to go down the bank now I will not say one more word about it.”

Tamsin Ro went down the bank and stood next to Bryn Sallow for about four minutes and asked her three questions about the chain, and came back up.

“The chain is on a nail,” Tamsin said, “and the nail is in the post, and the post is in the ground, and that is all it is fixed to, and it is the same as my rope except that my rope goes over a wheel and the wheel is on a pin and the pin is worn on the underside where you cannot see it.”

“It is exactly the same,” said Ilyan, “and yours has a term in a charter about it and mine has a girl in it, and I do not know what to do with that and I have been trying all afternoon, and the answer is that a machine is a machine and a witness is a witness and the difference between them is about four inches of iron and thirty years of somebody not being asked anything.”

Tamsin considered this and rejected part of it.

“No,” she said. “The difference is that somebody looked at the pin.”

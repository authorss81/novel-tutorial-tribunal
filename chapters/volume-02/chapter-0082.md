# Chapter 82: Half a Day

Tam Blore is twenty-two and he has been under the west end of a deck for eleven months and one of those months was a month when the deck was sitting on four props and everybody came and looked at it, and he has not been able to explain to anybody in the room he sleeps in why that was the best month of the four and why he would go back to it.

He is at the cut banks, which are nineteen miles from the ninth bridge, working a deck under a man of sixty called Harl Mecken, who has had nine apprentices and has buried two of them in trades they did not finish. Blore came down on the second of the second month with a box on a drover's cart and has been in a room over a smithy since, four beds and a window that does not shut, and at the end of a season the room is somebody else's and the bed is taken and the next place is eleven miles off and the trade does not pay for the move.

The second hand was gone on the twenty-seventh of last month. He was not told.

---

He was under the deck at the cut banks at the second bell on the third of the fourth month, scraping scale off a seat plate with a bar and a chisel, and Mecken was on top with a line, and the first cart of the day had not come yet, and then a man came down the ramp at the side, walking on the outside of the kerb the way a man walks who has been on a lot of bridges.

Blore did not know him. That is the honest first thing. He had been in the ninth bridge's crew list as a name under a hand for eleven months and he had spoken to four men and been paid by a clerk, and this was a man of about sixty with grey in the beard and a rope burn on the inside of one wrist, and he stood at the foot of the ramp and looked at the underside of the deck for a while without saying anything at all.

Blore went on scraping. Then he stopped, because a man who looks at a deck that long is not a man passing.

“You’re Blore,” the man said.

“Yes.”

“Sedge. Oren Sedge. I keep the ninth bridge.” He said it the way a man says a thing he has said a few thousand times and has never had to explain. “I want to talk to you on the bank, not under there, because under there I would be talking about the ninth bridge and you would be listening to me talk about the ninth bridge with a chisel in your hand.”

---

The bank at the cut banks is a strip of ground between the ramp and the water with a flat stone on it that four men have had their tea on since before Blore came. They sat on it. Sedge did not sit. He stood with his back to the cut and looked at Blore for about the length of a bell before he began, and Blore understood afterwards that the man was deciding what order to say it in.

“I have come to take something off you,” said Sedge. “On the twenty-seventh of last month, at the fourth morning bell, at the ninth bridge, in the open air, a review was held. It decided a sum. Four new timbers were bought for the fourth bridge, which is four miles up the fen road, at ninety-six pounds, out of the ninth bridge's district road fund. That fund is four hundred and ten pounds a year and out of it went two hundred and ten for two hands under a deck at every change, and fifteen to tell another district what it had adopted, and forty-four pounds and sixteen for the charter of a district four miles down the road, and ninety-six for the timbers. The under-deck watch went from two hands to one hand for this year and the man who lost the second hand is you.”

Blore said nothing. The stone was cold.

“You are the only person in that record who is named as having lost something in it,” said Sedge. “I want you to hear the whole of that and not half of it, so I am going to say the next part slowly. Your name is in the disposition of a public review. It is in a clerk's hand. It says your name and it says second hand and it says the under-deck watch reduced, this year. Nobody put your name there but one man and it was not me and it was not the guild and it was not a keeper.”

“Who.”

“A man called Vester. He is the first defendant in that case and he has got about two hundred guild words and a bad leg and no power over anything at all, and he can only speak in one matter and that is not this one. He asked to be produced at that review with his report, because a ruling says he must be, and when he was produced he read out a list of everybody that case had cost, because that is the only thing anybody made him do, and your name was on it and he put it in front of about ninety people and the clerk wrote it down.”

Blore said, “In front of ninety people.”

“In front of ninety people, in the open air, on a bridge, at the fourth morning bell.” Sedge shifted his weight. “I want to tell you why I have come nineteen miles to say this in my own mouth instead of putting it in a letter, and then I am going to tell you what it costs you, and the second is worse than the first and you are not going to believe me for a bit.”

“All right.”

---

“A letter from a trade comes at the change,” said Sedge. “You would have got it standing up with a barrow in your hands in the middle of a working tide and you would have read it in a moment and you would have taken it for a summons about something else, and there is a word on the guild's paper that a man in a hurry reads as *you are finished*. So there is not going to be a letter. There is me, on a cart as far as a drover happened to be going, and then the rest of it on my own feet, and I could not come before because I have a bridge and the count is taken at every change and the count is one man now and if I am not on the deck at the change there is no count at all, and I have a keeper's acknowledgment in my own name that ends with the keeper, and I am not going to be a man who leaves a span.”

“You could have sent somebody with it in a cart.”

“Yes,” said Sedge. “And that is what the guild did not do, and the reason the guild did not do it is that the guild has not got a cart and has not got money for one, and it lent four benches to a court it has no standing in, and its six crews have not voted for anything this year because I have not put anything to them, because I know what they would vote for. They would vote to keep two hands under a deck and not buy the timbers, and they would be wrong, and I could not make them wrong, because I do not have the number they would need. I have got a bridge that has not moved in fifty-four days and a number is not an argument to six men who want their rate in the winter.”

He sat down on the stone then, which he had not meant to.

“So I did it myself and I told the court in the open air that I had wanted to say out loud that I am the man who tells him and not the man who took it, and that I did not take it, and that I would have taken it just the same if I had not been in the room. That is in the record and I would like you to have it from my mouth as well as from theirs.”

---

Then he came to the number, and he got as far as the first two words of it, and Blore said it for him.

“Four hours a day,” said Blore. “At the ward rate, out of that fund, that is a hundred and five pounds a year off a hand under a deck, and I have done it four times since the twenty-seventh of last month in my head because a man under a deck can do a rate in his sleep and I have had nothing else to do with my head. Have I got it right.”

“You have got it right,” said Sedge. “And I am going to say the rest of the line, because there are four ways to say it and I have said all four to four different men this month and every one of them got the same thing. Four hours a day, at the ward rate, out of that fund, is a hundred and five pounds a year off your wage. The rate is not a secret and it is not anybody's invention, it is the ninth bridge's own rate for a hand under a deck at every change, and it is written in the ninth bridge's charter, and a charter is the only kind of page in this city anybody can be sent to and got an answer out of.”

“So that’s a hundred and five.”

“That is a hundred and five. And I will tell you the thing that is going to sit in your chest, which is that the district has forty-four pounds and four shillings left in the year after everything I have read out to you, and there is no more in it until the rate is set in the spring, and if your father had asked me in March I could have told him the same.”

Blore did the other half of the arithmetic, because that is what he is for. He reads a load off a mark on a plate and knows in one glance whether a cart will take it, and the sum was quick and it was worse than he had expected.

“A hundred and five a year and forty-four pounds and four in the fund,” he said. “So the fund could pay me back for about five months. If it was owed. Which it is not, because nobody has said it is owed to me, you said it is not yours and not the guild’s, so who owes it.”

“That is the first useful question you have asked me,” said Sedge, “and the answer is: the district owes it, and the district is a bench and a clerk and about four hundred people, and it has forty-four pounds and four shillings in a year that is already spent on a bridge four miles away and a charter for a district four miles the other way, and I have been in that district's room and its money is a slate and a piece of chalk, and none of those three things is a person, and neither of them has said anything to you yet.”

---

“Sedge,” said Blore. “There is a man in the city you can send for.”

Oren Sedge looked up.

“There is a man in the city you can send for,” Blore said again. “That is what a friend of mine at the weighhouse said before I came down. He said, if the thing is written down anywhere, there is a man in the city that is the door, and a district can be written to, and a clerk can be asked a question in writing, and if a thing is owed then a thing can be said to. He said it in a tap room and he has never read a charter in his life and he was not being clever, he was telling me what there is in the world.”

“There is,” said Sedge. “A man called Vester carries nine sheets of a charter to any district that asks for them, at his own cost, and there is an appeal in that charter and there is a woman in a room over a pump who can read it and cannot appeal from it, and a man at the ninth bridge who took a keeper's acknowledgment in his own name. All of that is true and none of it is a wage.”

“Why isn’t it a wage.”

“Because a wage is money for work already done, at a rate, on a day, to a person, in chalk, and the charter does that for about two hundred people in a district four miles away and it does not do it for you and it is not addressed to you, and he is not a bench and he cannot be sent for, and he has told me so himself in about six words and I have never had anybody explain that shape to me as plainly since I was twenty.” He said it evenly. “I have had a rule explained to me that I could not carry out. That is a thing that happens about once in nineteen years and I have never got over it. You can be owed and there is no door, and the door is the whole of what an owed thing is.”

---

Blore was quiet for a while, and the cart came, and he shunted it across on the deck and came back, and when he came back he sat down properly, with his elbows on his knees.

“There is one more thing,” said Sedge, “and I am telling you because in about four years somebody is going to ask you what happened at the ninth bridge in the year of that case and you are going to have to answer, and the answer you are standing here with is that a road fund did not carry two wages, which is a dull and unanswerable thing, and there is a better one available and it would be false.”

“What’s the false one?”

“That you were let go.” Sedge said it flat. “You were not let go. The crew list went to one name in the winter, because the guild ran out of money in the winter, and that is months before the review and it is not the review. What the review did was not touch a job. It took a second hand out of a fund and bought four timbers with it, four miles up a fen road, for a bridge that has wanted them for twenty-nine years. If you go down that road then in about four years there will be a story in a room in a city that says a trade dropped a man in the year of a case, and every young man in this trade in the county will hear that story before they are twenty-five, and it will be about a man of sixty called Mecken and it will be about nobody.”

“He didn't drop me,” said Blore.

“He did not drop you and he is not going to drop you,” said Sedge. “And the man who will have to say that out loud in about four years is you, standing up, and I would rather you had it now from a man who was in the room than from a clerk reading a disposition.”

---

He stood up, and looked at the deck, and then said the thing that was actually going to cost Blore the most, and he said it in a way that showed he had been carrying it down nineteen miles.

“Now the part I have not told you and came for,” he said. “You have been doing your best to work out how to get the hundred and five pounds back and you have got to the right place. Forty-four pounds and four in a fund, a bench that can be written to, a man who can be sent for, an account that can be carried. That is a recoverable-looking thing. I want to put the other shape in front of you and I have not got a way of doing it that will not sound like a threat, so I am going to give it to you as a fact and you can be as angry with me as you like.

“Blore. There is one hand under the deck at the ninth bridge this year, and that hand is a man who was there before you and is a better hand than you are. And in the cut banks the work has a place for a hand this week because Harl Mecken is sixty and has two of us. And this year there is a place for you here and nowhere else in this county where there is a hand out on a deck, because the span at the ninth bridge is not short of one hand and the deck at the fourth bridge has four square holes in it and the man who keeps it is sitting on it. That is not a punishment and it is not anybody's revenge and it is the true shape of a road fund that has spent its year. You are not owed a job. There is no job. There is a bench of four with a fund in it that has forty-four pounds and four shillings left, and a chamber in a city that a district can be written to, and a man who can be sent for and is not a bench, and not one of those three is a person, and in four years when you are twenty-six you will have two seasons of a span and one of them will have had four hours a day taken off it, and an apprenticeship in this trade wants two seasons and one is not two, and that is not anybody's cruelty. That is arithmetic that has been in the book since long before you were born.””

Blore did not say anything for a while. The water came up under the cut the way it does and went back and did it again.

“Two seasons,” he said.

“Two seasons of a span. One is not two. I have been trying since the twenty-eighth of March to find the sentence that makes that fair and there is not one and I have been a keeper for twenty-six years and I have watched men lose things for worse.”

“Then what do I do.”

“Do you want the honest answer or the one that gets you through the winter.”

“The honest one.”

“The honest one is that you go and stand in that room,” said Sedge, “because your name is in it and nobody has ever asked you a single thing and you have never been in a room in your life. And when you get there there is nothing in it for you. That is what I am telling you, and I have walked nineteen miles to tell you it, and the room is four miles off that road, and I have been in it. You will walk into a stone room and a man with a seal will say that the court has no power over a district's road fund, and it will be true, and he will be right, and you will go out again poorer in nothing and richer in one piece of paper that has your name on it. And then you will have two choices and they are both about a room and neither of them is about money.”

He waited for it, and Blore asked it.

“Which are they.”

“Come back down here and finish a season in a shed with a man of sixty, and in about two years go and put your name down where you put it on the board at a weight, where things are short, and be a man who has had four hours a day taken off and said so in a room and not hidden it.” Sedge picked up his cap. “Or go up the fen road four miles and ask them, in front of a bench, why the money for a bridge four miles off is not for the hand that was under it. And I will tell you what happens the second one, because I have been in that room. Nothing happens. It gets written down. And being written down is the only thing in this city that has ever been any use to anybody who is not already standing.”

---

Blore worked the rest of the day under the deck at the cut banks. He was at the second hand's place on the seat plate and the first hand's place at the top, and there is no second hand's place at the top at the cut banks because the deck there is short of nothing, so he was where he was always at, and the difference was that there was no second pair of eyes over his shoulder at the change and there was no second opinion about whether the chisel had gone into good iron or bad.

At the change he stopped and stood on the seat with his back to the cut and looked at the underside of a deck that had not moved, and he said out loud, to nobody, in the voice of a man reading a mark on a plate, “Nothing.”

Then he went down and told Harl Mecken, because he had to tell him and because he worked for him, and Mecken listened with his thumb and his forefinger on his own chin and then said, “Aye. Well. You've come down here for a season and there's a season here. I'll not have you saying I kept you.”

“I wasn’t going to say it.”

“Good,” said Mecken. “Because I'd only be half believing it.”

And at the ninth bell Blore walked the road back up to a room over a smithy with four beds and a window that does not shut, and he did not go to a stone room, and he did not put his name down anywhere, and he lay on his back in the dark and did the arithmetic once more, all of it, the four hours and the hundred and five and the forty-four pounds and four and the fund that is spent and the two seasons and the one, and then he got up and got out a pencil and a scrap of a shipping docket that was on the sill and he wrote on it, in the dark, in the middle of it, because there was nowhere else to write and it was the only flat thing in that room:

> **T. BLORE. FOUR HOURS A DAY. ONE OF TWO SEASONS.**

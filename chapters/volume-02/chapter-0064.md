# Chapter 64: The Charter That Is Worse to Write

A charter is not an instrument. An instrument is a page and a seal and a date and a person who is responsible for having written it, and it can be done in an afternoon by anybody who has done ten thousand of them, and Bel Ordway had proved that on a step in the open air with a weight receipt. A charter is longer, and it has to be *held*, and being held is a thing no document in this city has ever had to be.

It took nine sheets, and it took three days, and on the second day Sera Quill came into the room where it was being done with a piece of chalk and put a line on the floor, and the line was nine feet long, and she said: *that is the length of a thing nobody in Halloway can read.*

The room was the recordwright's room off the second gallery of the basalt court, which is a stone room with a table in it and a stove that does not draw and three chairs, and eleven benches' worth of people had been in and out of it over three days and had all, in the end, been asked to do one job each.

Ansa Rell had said at the first of the three days that she would not touch it, and she had not, and she had said why, and it was the sentence the whole of this matter has turned on, in a different key: *a magistrate who writes this becomes the author of it, and an author cannot be a person who hears an appeal against it, and this court is going to hear appeals against it, and if the court writes it the court is disqualified and I would rather be useful than tidy.*

Sel Harrow had written the procedure and refused to write any of the words, on the ground that a trialwright who drafts is a trialwright who cannot rule on his own draft, and had said the thing out loud which is the only interesting thing about Harrow and is the reason anybody in this case will take his refusals seriously: *I have been asked three times in eleven years to draft a covenant and I have done it twice and both times it was good and both times I was the man nobody could argue with afterwards, and I would rather be a man who can say no to a thing than a man who made it.*

Odile the court translator read every draft aloud, in full, twice, and never once said that a draft was clear, because it was never clear; she said that a draft was *readable*, and that the two were different, and that on the third day she read a term four times to a woman of twenty-nine with a barrow's handle in her hand and stopped between each reading to ask her whether she had got it, and got it four times, and was satisfied on the fourth and not on the first three.

Sera Quill drafted, in longhand, at a table, with the stove going, for three days.

---

The reading was done on the bank, from the fourth bell on the third of the month, with about a hundred and ninety people behind a rope, because a charter that gives residents an appeal is a document that has to be read by the people who will use the appeal, and the people who will use the appeal in that district are the people at a standpipe, and one of them reads for the rest.

Wenna Tace read it. Not because she offered. Because the bench of four asked her, and she said the thing she said about everything, which is that she had asked for the tally and would ask for this too.

And here is what a charter costs, and it is not the argument and it is not the money, it is the reading.

At the fourth bell, on the second page, she stopped.

“*The persons of the low water*,” she said. “Which persons.”

“That is the district's own phrase,” said Sera. “It is in the sanitation complaint.”

“It is in the complaint because a man wrote it eleven weeks ago and had never been down here in daylight,” said Wenna Tace. “It does not say a person. It says a water. So who is in it. Is the woman at the gate in it. Is the man who pumps in it. Is the child who sleeps in the six yards you are all calling the bit by the wall — in it, or out of it, and if he is out of it then what has he got, because he is on the flat every day and he is not in the traveller book either, and the count did not get him because he was asleep.”

“He is in it,” said Sera. “He is in the district.”

“Then it does not need the water,” said Wenna Tace. “Cut the water. It is nine words and they are the nine words that will keep somebody out in about four years, because a district will want to shrink it, and the way you shrink a thing is you find the edge of it and you move the edge in, and the edge of *the low water* is the water, and you can move that with a tide.”

And Sera took the pen out and cut nine words on a bank in the mud with a knife first, because the ink had not gone, and that was the first of about forty things that were going to happen over three days and it took ninety seconds.

At the sixth bell on the second page she stopped again.

“*The resident of this district.*”

“Define it.”

“Define what.”

“*A resident is a person who habitually dwells within the district*,” said Sera, reading her own words, which she had taken out of a common form of the kind that every bench in the city keeps in a drawer, and which had been written by somebody in a good hand in about the year anybody's grandmother was a girl, and which is in a drawer in the room they are in.

“Then a man who sleeps on the flat in the wet is not a resident,” said Wenna Tace, “because the flat is not a dwelling, and a woman who is on the flat for nine days out of every fourteen is not a resident, and a man in Weir Lane who is out on a cart for three weeks is not a resident. I have read that form before. I have read it in a drawer in this district for eleven years. I want it out of it, and I do not want it in a charter that a person is meant to be able to appeal on, because you cannot appeal on a thing that has thrown you out of its own front door before you got there.”

They cut it, and it took eleven minutes to find a replacement, and the replacement — *a person who is in the district, and a person who has been in the district, and a person who expects to be in the district* — took forty minutes and four people arguing and it is the ugliest clause in the whole of the ninth-bridge charter's successor and it is the one that four benches in nineteen years would not have been able to write, and it is the clause Wenna Tace says she can read.

At the eighth bell she stopped for the third time, and this one was not about a word.

“There is no *haul* in it,” she said.

“There is no haul in it because there is no haul in this district,” said Sera, “and I have looked for one for two days and I cannot put a thing in a charter that nobody can say what it is, and if I put it in then a clerk in a court in Orison in four years is going to have to decide what a haul is where there is no cart, and I do not know, and neither do you, and I have asked nine people in three days and not one of them has ever heard the word used for a barrow.”

“It is a barrow's load,” said Wenna Tace. “I have hauled one for eleven years and I have hauled one hundred and fifty of them in the last eleven months that are in no book of this district and cannot be entered by anybody in any room in this city, because there is no rate for them, and the woman who hauled them is standing on a bank reading your charter to about a hundred and ninety people and she has been paid forty-one times at a third of a haul rate, and the hundred and fifty are not in the charter, and they are not going to be in the charter, and I want that read out.”

Nobody read it out.

“It is in the record of the thirty-first,” said Harrow, from the top of the bank, “in a district's own hand, and it was entered in this city a week ago and it is not in the charter and it will not be, and I am entering now, at the magistrate's direction, that the person who is to be the read-aloud of this charter has stated that the largest single want of the persons she is reading it to is not provided for in it, and that the reason is that the word for it does not exist in this district, and that the charter is not made smaller by that and is made smaller by it.”

“Enter it,” said Ansa Rell, from the step, “and enter that I said it, and read the second one back to me.”

---

The paper was nine sheets and the paper cost, and the cost was the sentence that made the room go quiet on the second afternoon, because it was arithmetic and arithmetic is the one thing nobody in the room can argue with.

Nine sheets at the district's own rate of three pence is two shillings and threepence for one copy, and a charter is nine sheets and it is printed in nine, and sworn in one, and served in eleven, and the paper alone is thirty-one pounds. The rest is the serving of notices, the swearing of the page, and the keeping of it, and a holder who is to be paid for the keeping of it at four hours in a week at the district's rate, which is the wage of a person doing nothing visible, and which comes to about a pound eleven a quarter, and which nobody has ever paid anybody in that district for anything.

Halloway's own road fund is two hundred and forty pounds a year and it is paid out of the cartage on a road that is shut, and there is nothing behind it. The clean instrument, if it were made, would be paid out of that same fund and would leave a margin. The charter cannot be paid out of it at all, because the cartage is nil, and so it has to come out of the pump. And the ninth-bridge district's road fund is the only fund in this case that has anything in it, and it owes the fourth bridge four props, which cost ninety-six pounds, and which the ninth bridge's charter says must go back on the same cart before the review or the district pays.

Nobody said anything for about eleven seconds, and then Meridith Vale, who had come up the hill that morning to hand in a paper on the question and who had been sitting at the back of the room for a day and a half without speaking, said:

“I will say the arithmetic and I will say it now so that it is in the record in a clerk's mouth and not in a citizen's, because I am the one who pays for it and I would like the paying to be visible.

“The ninth bridge's district road fund is four hundred and ten pounds a year. The props are ninety-six. A full day's count, two hands under the deck, at the ward rate, which that fund pays, is one pound four a day and there are two of them, and that is going to be two hundred and ten pounds a year and I have entered it in my own hand and I have checked it twice. There is a hundred and four pounds left in that fund for the year and there is a bench clerk in it and a piece of paper and a rope. The paper for a charter is thirty-one pounds.

“So a charter can be got up the road for thirty-one pounds, at the cost of the props, out of a fund that has already promised the props to a bridge four miles away whose keeper is not a party, has said four words in writing, and has had his four words nailed to our frame. And I am not going to do it and I am not going to not do it. I am going to write it in my day book as two lines with a date against them and let the people who can decide decide, and the seventeenth of the month is the day the two texts are before the court and I am going to bring this book up the hill open.”

---

The term that named Ilyan was written on the second afternoon, and he was not in the room when it was written, and he was told about it on the third morning, by the woman who wrote it, standing in a doorway, without any preamble, which is how she does everything.

“The second half of the fourth term,” said Sera. “Read it.”

He read it. It took him four seconds and it took him eleven, because it is a legal sentence and it is about nine inches long.

> **THE FOURTH TERM. THE TERMS OF THIS CHARTER ARE CARRIED TO ANY DISTRICT THAT ASKS FOR THEM BY THE ATTACHED RECORD-HOLDER NAMED IN THE NINTH-BRIDGE INSTRUMENT, AT HIS OWN COST AND WITHOUT PAY, AND IF THE ATTACHED RECORD-HOLDER REFUSES, OR IS UNABLE, OR IS NOT WITHIN FOUR MILES OF THE DISTRICT THAT ASKS, THE TERMS ARE NOT CARRIED AND THE APPEAL GIVEN BY THIS CHARTER IS STAYED UNTIL ANOTHER CARRIER IS SWORN IN THAT DISTRICT, AND NOBODY ELSE MAY CARRY THEM.**

He read it a second time.

“You have put my name in a poor district's charter,” he said, “four miles from a bridge, with a duty on it, and a consequence.”

“Yes.”

“And the consequence is that if I say no, four hundred people lose a door.”

“Yes,” said Sera. “And I want you to hear the two halves of it in the right order, because I have been up all night and I am not going to be careful this morning.

“The first half is that I could have written it so that a carrier who refuses costs only himself. It would have taken four minutes. A clause saying a person may decline, and the district may find another, and nobody is stayed. That clause is better law. It is worse for the people, because a district with a clause like that is a district where the terms travel until the first tired man on a fen road says no, and after that the road is four miles of nothing for a year, and a year is eleven months of a queue at a pump.

“The second half is that I have written you into a document in a district where you have never lived, as a body, with a duty and a consequence, and the consequence is not on me. And it is in your own hand to refuse, and I have put a finding in that the refusal is against this district and not against you, and I have made it stay the appeal, and I did that on purpose, and I am telling you that I did it on purpose and I am going to say so in the record.

“You wrote to me on a step in a lane at the end of the first month that a rule that works where it was written and fails everywhere else is a rule that has been given a body by somebody who made it citable. Well. It has a body. I have made it your body. You will be in that district's record for as long as the charter stands, and there is no door in that district that you can go through without somebody being able to say *the man who carries our terms is in the room*, and in four years there will be a clerk in a bench in Orison who has never heard of you who will say your name in a sentence about a carrier, and there is no way for me to write that clause so that your name is not in it, and I have spent two days looking.”

“Ilyan, I am not your advocate and I am not your friend this morning and I am the recordwright of this charter and I answer for the record and not for the outcome, and the outcome of this clause is that a man who wanted one page in a corridor has made a small district able to call him. That is what I did. I would like to know whether you are going to argue with me about it now or in four years, and I would prefer now, because I have got nine sheets to finish and a stove that does not draw.”

He looked at it for a long time and did not argue with her, and she went back in, and that is the whole of what happened between them that day and it was the first time in five weeks that she had come to him and put something in his hands that she was not asking him to check, and he understood afterwards, sitting on a step in the rain with a leg that had gone again at the fifth bell, that she had told him the cost out loud before she wrote it, which is the opposite of what he has been doing to people for five weeks, and that this was the first time anybody had done it to him.

---

The fifth term was the one the whole of the third day went on, and it has no holder in it, and the court did not have a name to put in it, and the reason there was no name is that the terms go: to a person, in a place, with a rate, at a moment.

Sera wrote the term and the term says: *this charter is held by a person in the district who keeps this page, and swears an entry made in it, and does not hold it for a district.*

The System put the record of it in front of Ilyan while Roke was copying it fair, and it was a copy of a draft and not a rule about the world.

**TERMS OF A SHARED CHARTER, DRAFTED AT THE FIRST VERDICT ON THE THIRD DAY, IN A MATTER CONVENED ON THE TWENTY-NINTH.**
**TERMS DRAFTED: FIVE. THE FIRST FOUR ARE COPIES OF TERMS OF THE NINTH-BRIDGE INSTRUMENT AND ARE MARKED AS COPIES. THE FIFTH IS ORIGINAL TO THIS CHARTER.**
**THE FIFTH TERM REQUIRES A HOLDER: A PERSON IN THE DISTRICT WHO KEEPS THE PAGE AND SWEARS AN ENTRY MADE IN IT.**
**HOLDER ENTERED: NONE. NO PERSON HAS AGREED. NO PERSON HAS BEEN ASKED.**
**WHAT IS NOT ASKED: WHETHER ANY DISTRICT ADOPT THIS CHARTER. WHETHER ANY HOUSEHOLD IS UNHOUSED. WHETHER THE NINTH-BRIDGE SETTLEMENT BINDS ANYWHERE IT HAS NOT BEEN ADOPTED.**

Bream came up the fen road on the fourth of the month at the seventh bell to say one thing, and he said it standing in the doorway of the recordwright's room with his hat in his hand, and it took ninety seconds, and the room did not stop him, and nobody in it wrote anything down for the first two minutes of it.

“You have got a charter in there with five terms and four of them are a bridge's,” he said, “and the fifth one is a person. And I have read the copy because the reader of my lane is a woman of twenty-nine and she read it out at the pump and she sent word up to me by a boy of nine.

“I am not here to hold it. I told the magistrate on a bank and I will say it once more and then I will go down that road. I have been a name in this case twice and I am not going to be a body in it and that is the whole of my business and I said it in front of about a hundred and ninety people.

“I am here to ask you not to put Weir Lane in it.”

Sera said, “There is no lane in it. There is a district.”

“There is a lane in it,” said Bream, “and you know there is a lane in it, and so does everybody in this room. The charter gives the people of this district a right to appeal a burden put on them. The burden this district is about is the water. The water is on Weir Lane. The bench that would hear the appeal is the bench of four, and two of the four of them sleep on Weir Lane, and the one who set the rate is the one whose mother you sat next to on a bank. And the page has to be held by somebody in this district, because a page held four miles up the road is the mistake we have been living inside for a month.

“So every road in this charter runs to Weir Lane, and the only way to hold the page is to live on Weir Lane, and the only people on Weir Lane who are not ratepayers and not on the bench are the woman who reads and my wife, and my wife is fifty-one and cannot read, and it is not going to be my wife. So the page is going to be held by the reader. And every appeal this district ever brings is going to be a thing that happened on Weir Lane, because that is where the water is, and everybody in the district will know it, including the two people on the bench whose own taps it comes out of.

“I am not frightened of a page. I am frightened of a page with my name on the top of it, and a number of forty under it, and a clause that says *Weir Lane* on it, and that page living in my own lane, in a house I rent to a man who mends shoes. I have been a landlord for nineteen years and nobody has ever known a single one of my forty. That is the only thing I have got and it is not a small thing in this district, and I have never once written a name on anything, and I would like to go on being a man nobody can produce.”

Nobody spoke for a while.

“I can give you the district instead of the lane,” said Sera. “The district is in it and the lane is not and I will cut the lane out this afternoon and you will not find it in a page again.”

“Then cut it out,” said Bream.

“It has to be cut out, and I want you to know what it costs, because I am the person who is doing the cutting and I do not intend to do it behind your back.

“A charter that names a district can be appealed about by anybody in the district. A charter that names a lane can be appealed about by the people in the lane and by nobody else. The lane is where the people are. If I cut Weir Lane out of this charter, then a person in Weir Lane who is shut out of it is a person shut out of a district charter, which is a weaker thing and a slower thing and a thing that will take an appeal about the whole district to reach, and an appeal about the whole district in a district with a bench of four is about four years long, and the four people on that bench are two of them asleep on Weir Lane.

“Your protection costs this district its sharpest door. That is the trade and I have made it, and I would like it said in the record that I made it and that a man of fifty-four asked me for it and that I gave it to him, and that I am not a servant of yours and that you did not have the standing to make me and I did it because you were right.”

Bream put his hat on.

“Then it is a worse charter,” he said. “I know it is a worse charter. I asked for it anyway. Do not thank me and do not tell anybody at the pump.”

He went down the road. It is entered that he asked and it is entered that he was right and it is entered that it made the charter worse, and it is entered that he was not thanked, and the fourth of those is the only one of the four that anybody has ever bothered to check.

---

They finished at the third bell on the fifth of the month with nine sheets and four terms copied from a bridge and a fifth term with a hole in it, and Sera Quill read the whole of it out in a stone room with a stove that did not draw, and it took forty minutes, and at the end of it nobody said anything, and then Harrow said the thing that set up everything that came after.

“The first four terms are about a bridge,” he said. “They are the ninth bridge's terms and they are the best terms in this city and they have been carried four miles and printed on a gate and copied by a district that adopted them and by a district that did not, and the fifth term is about a person, and there is no person.

“I am not going to rule that this charter is invalid. It is not invalid, it is a charter with a hole in it, and a charter with a hole in it is enforceable in every part that is not the hole, and the hole is the part that keeps it in the district, and if the hole is left then in four months somebody will make a copy of this with a bench's name in the fifth term and it will be good law and it will be enforceable and the district it names will not know it exists.

“So I will say the true thing and it is the last thing I will say in this room for a while. A rule with a person in it can be broken by that person. A rule with a body in it can only be broken by a court, and a court in this city has eleven places in a morning and nineteen names on a board. You have all been in this room for three days waiting for somebody to find a way to write a charter that cannot be broken. I have been doing this for eleven years and there is no such thing, and the only question is what a person does when they find out.

“The terms are carried by a man four miles away whose name is in the fifth sheet and who is not here. The page is kept by nobody. And on the fourth of this month at the fourth bell, in a room with a door, both of those are going to be somebody's decision and neither of them is going to be made by a court.”

She looked at Ilyan, who was standing in the doorway with a leg that had not been his since the fifth bell, and who had read the fourth sheet that morning, and who did not go back to the table.

“You have one night,” said Sera. “You do not have to decide it tonight. I want that said too, because I know exactly what it looks like and it looks like a man being given a thing to say at a moment. You do not have to. Go and sleep and eat something and do not read a document, and do not stand in a smell, and be at the basalt at the fourth bell.”

# Chapter 79: Nine Yards With No Name In Them

A man with a cart has been lifting the chain at Lowgill bar since the middle of the month before last, and lifting the pennies out of the slot with his fingers and putting them into a tin under his seat, and there is nothing whatever anybody can do about it, and nothing in this case found it.

He is called Rennick Hale and he is forty-one and he is not a criminal and he says so, and he says a thing about it that is the whole of this chapter and which he said in a cold room off a lane to a magistrate's clerk and did not know he was saying anything.

“There is a bar,” he said. “It says so on a board. There is a chain on it and I lift the chain and I put the money in the slot because I am here and nobody is here, and that is right, and if I did not put it in the slot then it would not be a bar, it would be a chain, and then people would drive round it and the lane would get nothing and the candles would get nothing and the two bridges would get nothing. I have four children and I have been the front of that chain for eleven years and I am not going to say to you that leaving the money in the slot has been easy.

“About a pound a week in a good month, and there is a stone in the slot and you cannot get a penny into a slot with a stone in it unless somebody takes the stone out, and the person that takes the stone out is me, because I am the one that is here. And in the middle of the month before last I started taking the money out and not putting it in, and there is a tin under the seat of my cart, and it is not much in it, and I have not spent a penny of it.” He said that last part without any weight at all, which was the worst way he could have said it. “I have wanted a roof on the two rooms at Carrisand and there is a house coming free in eight weeks and I have eleven pounds of arrears in it that I do not have. I am not going to be tried for it and I am not going to be forgiven for it either. I am telling a clerk of a court because you asked me and because if I do not say it now I will say it in a worse place in a year.”

“How much is in the tin,” said Dessin Nahl.

“Four pounds and about six,” said Rennick Hale. “Ten weeks. Put that in your book with my name on it, because it is the only money in this lane that anybody can account for and I would like it accounted for under my own name while I am standing here saying it.”

Nobody wrote *ward* on a board in that room and nobody needed to. It is a guild's word and it is a hundred and ninety years old on a post at the end of a lane, and what the word describes is the arrangement Rennick Hale described, in a cold room off a lane, to a clerk from a court in another city, without knowing that he was describing anything.

---

The Nine Yards are about four miles beyond the bar, on the same lane, and they are not in Nethergill.

That is the whole of the trouble and it is a boundary that moved in a way nobody can now account for, and the movement is a hundred and forty-one years old and is written in a book in Fallowditch, and Fallowditch said on the eighteenth of last month, in its own hand, in public, that it holds nothing a person may swear to.

The Nine Yards pay Nethergill's rate. All of them. Sixty-one people, nine yards, nine gates, one well with a bucket on a chain, a chapel with a fortnight of nothing in it in the middle of the month, and a share of a schoolmaster who comes four times a month, and they have paid it for as long as there has been a rate, because a rate follows a road and a district's boundary followed a hedge, and the hedge went round the Nine Yards and the rate went straight up the lane.

So they pay for the bar, and the bar is not theirs, and they are not in the bench that runs the bar, and they have never had a vote in anything, and if they send a man to Nethergill's room about the bar, that man is a stranger in a room and is owed a hearing by nobody.

And then a matter came before the First Verdict, and it came because Rennick Hale, who has a cart, and is a person, and lives eleven miles from a city, was in a room in that city on the fourteenth of the month having come up to ask a bench about a boundary hedge, and heard a magistrate say out loud that eleven benches in this city hold nothing a person can swear to, and asked a question of his own.

“Magistrate,” he had said, at the third bell, on the fourteenth, with lime on a different man’s apron four feet away, “you have made eleven benches say out loud that they hold nothing. Can a bar be one of them? A bar is a thing a person can find and a person can swear to and a person can pay into, and there is no keeper of it, and if a bench in a lane can say *we hold nothing* then a bar at the end of a lane can be a bar with nothing in it, and I would like to know whether that is in your city, because a slot with a stone in it and a chain on the road is a slot with a stone in it and a chain on the road and a man’s cart standing next to it three days in seven.”

Nobody had asked him anything and he had been nine minutes in a court listening to a builder rub his own name off a board with his thumb, which he said afterwards he understood none of and which he said afterwards was the only reason he spoke.

Rell did not rule on it. She did the thing she has done four times in two months, which is to put it on the record in the shape it arrived in and refuse to improve it, and then she set it, and the setting of it is the part that cost.

> **THE MATTER: LOWGILL BAR, DROVERS' LANE, AGAINST NOBODY, BY A CARTER OF FORTY-ONE WHO SAYS HE IS NOT THE KEEPER OF IT AND IS THE MAN AT THE FRONT OF IT.**
>
> **THE RESPONDENTS: THE BENCH OF FOUR OF THE DISTRICT OF NETHERGILL, WHICH RUNS THE BAR. AND NOBODY ELSE, BECAUSE THERE IS NOBODY ELSE TO NAME.**
>
> **THE PLACE: NINE YARDS UP THE SAME LANE, PAYING NETHERGILL'S RATE, IN NO DISTRICT, NOT ON ANY LIST, AND NOT A PARTY TO ANYTHING BECAUSE A PLACE IS NOT A PERSON.**

---

The System put the notice in front of Ilyan on the seventeenth, in a room over a pump, four miles from a court, and it was a record of a notice and not a rule about the world.

**NOTICE OF THE FIRST VERDICT, THE SEVENTEENTH OF THE THIRD MONTH, ON A MATTER IN WHICH NO RESPONDENT IS A PERSON.**
**PARTIES NAMED: R. HALE, CARTER. THE BENCH OF FOUR OF THE DISTRICT OF NETHERGILL. THE KEEPER OF LOWGILL BAR. THERE IS NO KEEPER OF LOWGILL BAR AND NONE HAS EVER BEEN SWORN IN THIS CITY.**
**SERVED ON: THE SAID CARTER, IN PERSON. THE SAID BENCH, BY A CLERK, IN ITS OWN ROOM. THE KEEPER OF LOWGILL BAR, BY NAILING THE NOTICE TO THE POST AT THE BAR, IN THE OPEN AIR, AT THE THIRD BELL.**
**THE COURT'S OWN NOTE, WHICH IS NOT A RULING: THE MATTER AFFECTS PERSONS IN A PLACE THAT CANNOT BE NAMED IN IT, AND THE COURT'S STANDING ORDER REQUIRES NOTICE TO EVERY NAMED PARTY, AND EVERY PERSON IN THAT PLACE IS UNNAMED, AND THERE IS NO CLAUSE IN ANY INSTRUMENT IN THIS CASE FOR NOTICE TO A PLACE.**
**THE COURT ACCEPTS SERVICE ON THE POST. THE MATTER MAY BE HEARD.**

That last line is the whole of what happened in the third month of this case and it took four bells and a young woman of twenty-nine with a piece of chalk.

---

Merrin Coke is twenty-nine and keeps a tally of the Nine Yards in charcoal marks in a shutter, because there is no book in the world that belongs to them, and she has kept it since she was seventeen and it is the only record anybody has that the nine gates are nine gates and that the sixty-one are sixty-one.

She is not a hauler and she is not a clerk and she has no want in this case except about a pound a week.

She walked four miles to Nethergill on the fifteenth and a clerk told her that a district cannot act for people who are not in it, and then she walked seven miles down the fen road to a room over a pump on the sixteenth, at the second bell, and she put a shutter with charcoal marks on it on a table next to a barrow and asked a woman of twenty-nine whether she could swear to a page in it.

“Swear what,” said Wenna Tace.

“That nine gates is nine gates and there is sixty-one in them and a bar at the end of the lane and a pound a week out of the slot since the middle of the month before last, and that a man called Rennick Hale takes it in his own hand and puts the stone back, and I have known it since I was at my mother's and my mother knew it before me and there is nobody to say it to.”

“I can read that,” said Wenna Tace.

“I know you can read it.”

“And what do you want.”

“I want it in a book that somebody can come back with,” said Merrin Coke, “because a chalk mark on a shutter is not a record and I have got a barrow of my own coming at the second bell and I have to be on the flat by the sixth or the water takes me.”

And Wenna Tace said the thing that is now the finding of the eighteenth of the month and that has been entered in three hands in two districts and that nobody has read both of them:

“I will read it to anybody you like. I will not hold it. I cannot hold it. The fifth term says a holder is a person in the district, and I am a person in a district four miles from you, and a charter of a district is a district's, and there is no instrument in this city that carries a page from a room to a place, and I have asked a recordwright about it twice this month and got the same answer both times and the answer was no and the reason was that a page is a record about a person and a person is the only thing a page is for, and a place is not a person, and I have been the door in this district for thirteen days and I am telling you that I can be the door in one district at a time and not in a place, and I would like it minuted in both of those sentences and not joined up with a semicolon.”

---

He was in the room and he said the thing that is not a good idea, and he said it because it was the plainest thing available and he had been tired for nine days.

“I will hold it.”

Three people told him no and they told him in three different registers, and two of them told him for the same reason and one of them told him for a different one, and the one who told him for a different one was a recordwright of a public record and expressly not his advocate, and she told him at the fourth bell in a room over a pump with a barrow against the wall.

“You are a person in no district,” said Sera Quill, “which is the only reason a carrier's clause can name you at all. Hold a page and you are a person in a district, because a holder is a person in a district by the fifth term, and the fifth term is a copy of nothing and is mine and is the only one of the five that is original, and it does not travel either. So the moment you swear a document into a page in the Nine Yards you are a holder, and a holder may not be a party, may not appeal, may not appear in support of one, and may not be named in one. And here is the part I did not want to say in a room with a barrow in it.

“The second half of the fourth term says the terms are carried to any district that asks, and it names you, and nobody else may carry them. If you are a holder anywhere, then anywhere that holds a page is a place you have a duty in, and a holder is not available for anything, and the only thing this clause has ever had in it is one man. The court cannot appoint a second carrier. There is no instrument in this city that says how a second carrier is sworn in. The clause says *until another carrier is sworn in that district* and there is no procedure, and Harrow has said out loud in this case that a line he did not enter is a line he may not rule on, and the absence of that procedure is a line nobody entered.

“So if you swear a page in the Nine Yards, you are a holder, and a holder is not a carrier, and the carrier is a man in a room with nine sheets who is the reason the appeal in Halloway is not stayed. And the appeal in Halloway is not stayed for about four hundred people, and it will be stayed the day you open a page, and the day you open a page is a day in a lane fifteen miles from a bridge, and nobody in this city will be able to tell you that you did it, because the bench that would say so is the bench that runs a bar four miles the other way and does not know the Nine Yards exist.”

Nobody in the room said anything, and the barrow was outside and could be heard.

“You are not refusing me because you are frightened,” she said. “You would have done it if I had not been here. I want that entered, because the reason is going to be misread in about four years as a question of standing, and it is not a question of standing. It is that a man cannot be a door in a lane and the only door at the same time, and that is a true sentence and I wrote it and I did not see it when I wrote it, and it has been in nine sheets since the seventeenth of last month.”

Harrow said the thing that has been his procedure four times now and is not a rule, and which on this occasion he said on purpose.

“Enter that the court has no power over the charter of the district of Halloway and takes no view on any of its terms,” he said, “and enter that a trialwright has observed the same question twice in a month about the same instrument from two different directions, and that the second time is the one that matters, and that this is the third procedure I have invented in public and I intend to go on inventing them, and that none of them is a power and all of them are the smallest thing I can do with a record, and that I have been frightened of the duty to answer and this is worse.”

And Merrin Coke said the only thing in the room that anybody could act on, and she said it while she was putting her shutter inside her coat, and she was not being clever and she had walked eight miles in two days.

“Then the money is a cart's,” she said. “Is it. I have got a barrow and I have sixty-one people and I have had a pound a week out of that slot since the middle of the month before last and I have got it in a shutter in charcoal and there is no book in the world that says so. And you have all spent the morning on the door and the door is a woman and a house, and I have been in two rooms today and in both of them I was told that the trouble is that there is no paper, and I have been sitting on a barrow on a bank for two days waiting for a piece of paper.”

She stopped at the door.

“I am not being clever and I am not blaming anybody. I would like it entered that four pounds and six shillings has been going into a cart for ten weeks out of a slot that is for a lane, and that a man has said so out loud in a room in your city and asked for his own name against it, and that the sixty-one people in the Nine Yards have paid Nethergill's rate for a hundred and forty-one years for a bar they do not own, and that I have known it since I was at my mother's, and that I have spent a day and a half getting a piece of paper about it into two rooms and have left with a piece of chalk and a shutter, and I would like somebody to write all of that down and I do not care where.”

Corva Silt wrote it down, in the district's own hand, on the back of a slip, in a room over a pump, and read it back to her twice, and Merrin Coke made her read the part about the sixty-one twice. And then the clerk of the Orison chamber did a thing that has not been done in this case, which is that she sent four pounds and six shillings down eleven miles in a cart with a docket in her own hand saying *R. HALE, LOWGILL BAR, MONEY TAKEN FROM A SLOT, ENTERED AGAINST HIM BY HIS OWN ASKING AT A HEARING IN ORISON*, and the tin came into that room over a pump on the second of the fourth month and the woman counted it out on the table and put it in an envelope with the docket in it, and the envelope is in a room over a pump, and a man with a cart knows it is there.

And the money went back into the slot at the third bell on the second of the fourth month, in front of nine people, one penny at a time, by the man who had taken it out, and the stone went back in, and the chain went across the road. And that is the whole of what was done about four pounds and six shillings, and it took a cart and a docket and a hearing, and it happened in eleven days, and it should not have taken eleven days, and everybody in the three rooms it happened in knew that.

The finding of the eighteenth of the month is in two hands in two places seven miles apart and neither hand has read the other, and it is short: there is now, in writing, in this city, a document that explains why a place of sixty-one people cannot have a page, and the document is the charter of a district four miles from a bridge, and the reason is a person, and the person is a man who carries nine sheets up a lane for nothing.

The four pounds and six shillings was found and named and carried and counted and put back in a slot in eleven days. It was the only thing in this chapter that got fixed, and it got fixed because a man with a cart is a person, and a person can be named, and the person who found out about it was a clerk who went up a stair with a piece of paper. Nobody asked how much longer a tin would have stayed under a seat in a lane with no address in it, and the answer is that it is the answer to every question in this chapter and nobody put it anywhere.

# Chapter 90: What a Page Is For, Which Nobody Has Answered

They produced him on the twenty-sixth of the fourth month at about the second bell, in the room over the pump, in the district's own office, in the flat voice of a clerk producing a witness, and they did it in the ordinary way and he could not refuse it and would not have wanted to.

Corva Silt stood behind the table with her hands flat on it and read out the words she had to read out, and they were these, and she had written them herself the day before and had gone home and eaten and come back.

> **IN THE MATTER OF A FIGURE ENTERED IN THE BOOK OF THE BENCH OF FOUR ON THE TENTH OF THE THIRD MONTH, IN A BENCH'S OWN HAND, BY A PERSON WHO IS NOT A CLERK AND IS NOT A PARTY, AND WHICH IS NOW ON THE FACE OF A RATE THAT IS READ ALOUD AT A PUMP FOUR TIMES A YEAR, THE PERSON WHO WROTE THE FIGURE IS A WITNESS IN THIS MATTER, AND A WITNESS MAY BE PRODUCED, AND IS PRODUCED, IN THIS ROOM, ON THIS DAY.**
>
> **HE IS NOT ASKED ABOUT THE FIGURE. HE IS ASKED THREE QUESTIONS AND THE THREE QUESTIONS WERE PUT TO HIM IN ADVANCE BY THE CLERK AND HE HAS SEEN THEM, WHICH IS NOT WHAT A WITNESS USUALLY GETS, AND IT IS BECAUSE THE CLERK HAS READ FOUR SHEETS OF HIS OWN FIGURES OUT ALOUD IN THIS OFFICE IN A LOUD VOICE BY HERSELF AND DECIDED THAT SHE WAS NOT GOING TO DO IT AGAIN WITH HIM IN THE ROOM.**

The three questions were these. *Did you count it yourself. Do you know that it is wrong. Would you write it again.*

“Yes,” he said. “Yes. And I said that before I did it and it is in the book in my hand and it is in the clerk's hand and it is in the gate hand's hand, and the third one is yes, and I would like it entered that I said yes twice so that a clerk in four years does not think I hesitated.”

“Nothing is entered twice,” said Corva Silt. “That is the first thing you have said in this room that I have not put in the book, and I am not putting it in, and you may be annoyed with me about it in four years.”

---

Then she asked him, in her own voice and not the clerk's, the question she had not written down.

“Your name is in the matter again.”

“It is.”

“I have spent a morning with it in March,” said Corva Silt, “and I am not going to insult you by pretending I did not know it was going to happen again when I wrote the figure of thirty years, and I am not going to tell you that you could have refused, because you could not have refused a witness and a witness cannot refuse, and I do not want to hear a man say he would have refused it. What I want to know is what it is going to cost, and I would like an answer that is not about a rate.”

“It is about a rate,” he said. “It is an eighth of a penny a bucket. A bench of four cannot strike a number out of its own book, so a number I put in on the tenth of March is going to be read out at a pump four times a year, and it is wrong, and there is no figure in this city from thirty years ago that would make it right, and the woman it is owed to refused it and was not compelled and cannot be overrule. And I put it there. Nobody made me. You asked me for a number and I gave you one I had told you was wrong, and the reason I did that is the same reason you wanted it, and I am not going to pretend the reason was different, because it was not, and if I had been able to give you a shape I would have, and you could not have carried a shape, and I knew that when I gave you the number, and I gave it to you anyway and I have been sorry about it on about six separate days since the tenth of March.”

Corva Silt looked at him for a moment with the pen not moving.

“Thank you,” she said. “That is a thing I needed to hear and I am not going to make a minute of it. Sit down, because there is a second part and the second part is not a question, it is a notice.”

---

The notice was that he was going to stop counting.

“I have been counting the gate hand's tide since about the sixth of the second month,” he said, “from the fifth bell, on a slate with a line down the middle of it, in my figures, which are a load-carrier's and are wrong by about four minutes in any hour, and the two sets of figures were four minutes apart in a four-hour day and she wrote them both down herself and put them side by side and said a person is not a measuring instrument. I have done it about two days in five since the eleventh of the second month, and it is the only reason there is a number in that book at all.

“I am stopping. On the twenty-sixth, at the second bell, in this room, with you writing it down, so that there is a date on it that is not mine.”

“Why,” said Corva Silt, “and do not give me a noble answer, I have had four of those this month and two of them were mine.”

“Because I said the figure was wrong before I wrote it, and I wrote it anyway, and she refused it, and I have gone on counting for a month since, and counting is the part that makes the shape a number.” He put his hands on his knees. “The honest reason is that I keep it clear. It clears in my leg and it does not clear when I rest, and it clears inside a burden of my own, and the counting is a burden of my own, and for about three hours a day the weight of a woman of forty-four at a gate is my weight and I can put it down. And that has been the arrangement for about a month and it is not an arrangement anybody agreed to and she does not know it is there, and I have worked out this month that what I have actually been doing is holding a woman up for three hours a day so that a bench of four can read a number, and the number is wrong, and the woman said no.”

“You are going to be asked to say that in a room in four years,” said Corva Silt, “and if you say it in that room you will be a man who made a woman thirty years into a job for a man with a leg, and I want you to know that I will have a copy of this entry, and I will hand it to whoever brings it.”

“Enter the whole of it,” he said, “including that I do not know whether I am doing this for her or for the book, and that I have gone back and forth on it since the twelfth of this month, and that I have decided that the reason does not matter because the thing is the same either way.”

She entered it, and it took her a while, and this is the seventh thing in the record he cannot un-enter, and he named its size himself and would not let her write it fast.

> **THE SEVENTH THING THIS CLAIMANT HAS ENTERED INTO THIS CITY'S RECORD WHICH HE CANNOT WITHDRAW, NAMED WITH ITS SIZE.**
>
> **THE FIRST SIX ARE AS STATED BEFORE AND HE HAS NOT GONE BACK TO THEM.**
>
> **THE SEVENTH IS THIS: HE HAS BEEN PRODUCED A SECOND TIME AS A WITNESS, IN A DISTRICT HE DOES NOT LIVE IN, ABOUT A NUMBER HE PUT IN A BENCH'S BOOK WITHOUT THE CONSENT OF THE PERSON IT WAS OWED TO, WHO REFUSED IT AND WAS NOT COMPELLED AND CANNOT BE OVERRULED. A WITNESS CAN BE PRODUCED TWICE. A WITNESS CAN BE PRODUCED THREE TIMES. HE HAS NOW BEEN ASKED THREE QUESTIONS IN ADVANCE BY A CLERK WHO HAS READ FOUR SHEETS OF HIS FIGURES ALOUD IN HER OWN OFFICE BY HERSELF, AND HE WAS TOLD THE THREE QUESTIONS IN ADVANCE, AND THAT IS NOT WHAT A WITNESS USUALLY GETS, AND HE HAS ENTERED THAT HE KNOWS WHY SHE DID IT AND HAS NOT SAID IT OUT LOUD.**
>
> **HE IS ALSO ENTERING THIS, AGAINST HIMSELF, AND IT IS THE PART THAT COSTS: FROM THE TWENTY-SIXTH OF THE FOURTH MONTH HE IS NOT COUNTING THE TIDE. THE HOURS WERE WRONG. THE RATE IS WRONG AND CANNOT BE CORRECTED. THE FIGURE CANNOT BE STRUCK OUT OF A BOOK THAT IS NOT HIS, AND A BENCH OF FOUR HAS NO POWER OVER A NUMBER THAT A WITNESS ENTERED, AND THE NUMBER STANDS AND IS READ ALOUD FOUR TIMES A YEAR AND IS WRONG.**
>
> **AND THE ONLY MAN IN THIS CITY WHO COULD HAVE MADE IT MORE RIGHT BY COUNTING MORE CAREFULLY HAS STOPPED, AND NOBODY HAS ASKED HIM TO, AND THE GATE HAND HAS NOT BEEN TOLD AND WAS NOT ASKED AND IS NOT TO BE TOLD BY HIM, AND HE IS ENTERING THAT HE STOPPED FOR A REASON THAT IS ABOUT HIS OWN BODY AND NOT ABOUT HER, AND THAT HE BELIEVES THAT, AND THAT HE WOULD LIKE IT ENTERED THAT HE BELIEVES IT.**
>
> **THERE IS NO LINE ANYWHERE IN THIS CITY THAT PAYS A MAN FOR HAVING BEEN PRODUCED.**

---

She read it back and did not tidy it and did not put her own name against it, and then she said, in a different voice, the thing that made it a document and not an act.

“I am going to say one thing and I am not going to put it in the book, and you are going to let me say it, and then we are going to close this room.

“On the tenth of March you wrote a number into a bench's book after the woman had said no in front of me, and I let you, and I have thought about it every day since, and I have concluded that I would have let you again and that the reason I let you was not that the district needed a figure. It was that the district has had a debt in it for thirty years in a shape that nobody could carry and a shape cannot be carried out of a room, and a number can, and a number is a thing that can be *wrong in a way that can be argued about*, and that is the only door out of a shape anybody has ever found.

“And it is not a good door, and it opened onto a figure of a thousand two hundred and fifteen pounds that cannot be paid and a hundred and fifty hauls that have no figure at all, and it has cost that woman a claim she spent thirty years not having, and it has cost you a hand that will be a scar at the base of your thumb in four years and a counting you are stopping. I do not know that I was right. I know that I would do it again and that is the part I am going to have to carry, and I would rather you did not make a face.”

He did not make a face, and he said, “I have no standing in this district and I have never been any good at a face anyway.”

---

Bryn Sallow was at the gate side at the sixth bell on the twenty-sixth, at the turn, working an oak frame with two hundred feet of chain on it, and he came up the flat with the slate under his arm out of a habit he had had for a month and stopped about four feet short of the sill-stone with it still under his arm.

“I am not counting it,” he said. “I have stopped. It is entered in the room at the second bell today with a date on it and my name on it and there is no more slate.”

Bryn Sallow worked the chain for a while before she said anything.

“I have been standing here thirty years,” she said, “and you are the first person who has ever told me that the counting was his and not a measurement. And you have just told me you have stopped for a reason that is in a book in the district's own hand and I am not allowed to be a party to and cannot be overrule, and I have got a moment to say something and I am going to say it and then I am going to go back to the gate.

“You have made yourself a man who cannot be produced on a number. In four years, when somebody in a city asks how many hours a gate hand stands in a year, there will be a number, and it will be mine, and it will be wrong by about two hundred and two pounds and by a rate nobody can find, and you will not be there to say it is wrong. That is what you have bought and it is a real thing and I am not thanking you for it.

“And the other thing, which I am not going to let go, and which you did not ask me for. I would have gone on with it. I have got a number on a slate that only exists when a man from a bridge is standing on this bank, and I have known that since the twelfth of the month before last, and I did not tell you to stop and I was not going to and I have not stopped counting in my own head once in thirty years and I am not going to now. I have been counting my own tide in my own feet since I was fourteen. There is no slate. I have never needed one. So you can have your three hours a day back and I will still be right, and you should write that down somewhere as well, because in about four years a clerk is going to read a book in which a man says he stopped for a woman and there is going to be nothing in it at all about the woman, and the woman is the part that was never on your side of it.”

Then she worked the chain, and the water came up the flat, and about forty or fifty people went up the bank in a line, and the gate was half up and the sixth bell was the turn, and the span of a low-water culvert is a thing a person does not stand still in twice a day for thirty years.

---

The letter from Fallowditch came into the chamber in Orison on the twenty-seventh of the fourth month, on the fourth bell, with a docket on it that said *enclosed, a line, no question asked*, and it had a wedge-and-chain seal on it and the wedge-and-chain is the seal of a bench of three and there are not many of them in this city.

Roke entered it and put it in front of a trialwright of eleven years, and the clerk who had written the finding on the docket board in the fourteenth week read it out in the flat voice that a clerk reads a thing out in, and the words in it were these and nobody improved them.

> **ENTERED IN THE BOOK OF THE BENCH OF THREE OF FALLOWDITCH, THE THIRTEENTH OF THE FOURTH MONTH, BY ITS OWN CLERK IN ITS OWN HAND. THIS BENCH READ THE NINE SHEETS OF THE CHARTER OF THE DISTRICT OF HALLOWAY OUT ALOUD IN ITS OWN ROOM ON THE THIRTEENTH OF THE FOURTH MONTH AND MADE AN ENTRY OF THE READING.**
>
> **AT THE FOOT OF THE NINTH SHEET, UNDER THE FIFTH TERM, IN THE COPY THAT WAS CARRIED AND NOT IN THE SHEET ITSELF, THERE IS A LINE. IT WAS READ OUT IN THE ROOM BECAUSE THE NINTH SHEET WAS BEING READ OUT.**
>
> **CONTINUITY PRESERVES THE RECORD AGAINST THE HOLDER.**
>
> **THIS BENCH HAS NO POWER OVER IT. THIS BENCH HAS ADOPTED NOTHING. THE MAN WHO CARRIED IT WAS IN THIS ROOM ON THE THIRTEENTH AND ASKED THIS CLERK THREE QUESTIONS AND ANSWERED THEM, AND HE TOLD THIS BENCH WHAT HE WAS ENTITLED TO TELL IT AND DID NOT TELL IT THE REST. THIS BENCH DOES NOT KNOW WHOSE HAND THE LINE IS IN AND IS NOT ASKING. THE DATE THE ASK ARRIVED IN THE DISTRICT OF HALLOWAY IS THE ELEVENTH OF THE FOURTH MONTH AND THE DATE THIS SHEET WAS STAMPED IN A CHAMBER IN THIS CITY IS THE TWENTY-SEVENTH.**
>
> **NO REPLY IS ASKED FOR.**

It is the ninth time. Sel Harrow read it twice at the counter in a stone room off the second gallery with about nine people in the room and a tray with a sanded edge, and then he said the sentence he has said every time, in the same words, and the words did not vary in any part of them, and it was the sixth time he had said them.

“I will not rule on a line I did not enter.”

Nobody asked him who wrote it, or when, or whether it was in the copy at the counter at Halloway, and he did not offer. He entered it as an unentered line, with a date, and did not find it, and did not use it, and did not let it be used, and said that he would refuse on the seventh time and that on his eleventh year as a trialwright he had come to the conclusion that a line he did not enter is a line he may not rule on, and that this is not modesty and is not neutrality and is the only procedure he has.

Nobody named anything. It is in the record. And the person it is about is four miles down a fen road, and eight days ago about two hundred people went up a mud bank in the cold because a man who sells salt put his name under a rate, and she read it out to them and has not read the line that is under it, and she has said that she reads a charter in order and a charter ends at the fifth term, and she has asked him not to read it to her, and nobody in this case will.

---

On the twenty-ninth of the fourth month, at the fourth bell, four people were in a room over a pump, and what they made was the smallest instrument anybody has made in this case and the only one that a person can actually use, and it took forty minutes and it is not a rule and it binds nobody outside four miles of a shed.

Wenna Tace wrote it out in the middle of her own page, which is where she puts the things that matter, and Corva Silt copied it into the bench's book in the district's own hand, and Merrick Vaunt read it back to both of them standing up, and Garvin Slade was not there and was not asked and is a salt seller and a man who withdrew his consent from a rate.

> **ENTERED IN THE BOOK OF THE BENCH OF FOUR, THE TWENTY-NINTH OF THE FOURTH MONTH, AT THE FOURTH BELL. A BENCH OF FOUR IS A BODY AND THIS ROOM IS OVER A PUMP AND ABOUT TWO HUNDRED AND FORTY PEOPLE LIVE INSIDE IT AND ONE OF THEM IS THE ONLY PERSON WHO CAN READ THE BOOK.**
>
> **THE RULE OF THE NINTH OF THIS MONTH, MADE BY ANOTHER BENCH ELEVEN MILES OFF, SAYS IN ITS OWN WORDS THAT A DOCUMENT GOES BACK TO THE PERSON WHO BROUGHT IT AND THE ENTRY STAYS. THIS BENCH HAS COPIED NOTHING AND HAS READ NOTHING OF THAT BENCH'S BOOK AND HAS ARRIVED AT THE SAME PLACE BY A DIFFERENT ROAD IN A FORTY-MINUTE ARGUMENT ABOUT A WOMAN OF SIXTY-ONE WHO WASHES FOR FOUR OTHER PEOPLE.**
>
> **THE ENTRY IN A BOOK IS NO USE TO A PERSON WHO CANNOT READ IT. THIS DISTRICT HAS ABOUT TWO HUNDRED AND FORTY PEOPLE IN IT AND THIS BENCH HAS NEVER ASKED HOW MANY OF THEM CAN READ AND CANNOT AFFORD TO ASK AND IS ENTERING THAT IT HAS NOT ASKED.**
>
> **THEREFORE: THE BOOK IS OPEN AT THE PUMP ON TWO DAYS IN THE WEEK, AT THE FOURTH BELL, AND ANY PERSON OF THIS DISTRICT MAY ASK FOR IT TO BE READ ALOUD TO THEM, AND A PERSON SO ASKING IS NOT TO BE ASKED WHY.**
>
> **THE COST OF A READING IS ABOUT AN HOUR OF SOMEBODY'S TIME. THIS BENCH HAS NO MONEY. THE READING IS DONE BY A PERSON WHO IS NOT A HOLDER AND IS NOT A CLERK AND IS NINETEEN AND IS NOT PAID AND WAS NOT ASKED AND IS BEING ASKED NOW, IN THIS ROOM, BY THE PERSON WHO ASKED FOR THE RATE. THIS IS ENTERED SO THAT A CLERK IN FOUR YEARS DOES NOT READ A GENEROUS DISTRICT.**

Nobody improved it. It is the second time in a fortnight that a rule made in a room with a person in it has cost somebody an hour that nobody is paying for, and both times it was a body of three or four that had told the truth about itself in public and then found out what the truth costs when it is written in a hand.

And what it bought is this, and it is not much, and it is real.

A woman of sixty-one who has drawn at that standpipe for nineteen years with a yoke and two buckets and who has stopped, and who is the best washer in the upper street, and who does not know how to read the book, can now come to a pump at the fourth bell on two days in the week and ask a boy of nineteen to read out a page to her, and she is not to be asked why.

That is a rate that has not gone up. That is a page that a person can use. That is a ninth-bridge's district road fund with forty-four pounds and four shillings in it, of which about a tenth has gone on four months in a city, and a charter that costs forty-four pounds and sixteen a year and is twelve shillings over a line, and a bench of four that is a body, and a gate hand who is owed a thousand two hundred and fifteen pounds and about six thousand hauls that have no figure at all, and a washer of sixty-one who is one pound ten shillings and fourpence a year poorer for it, and a man of thirty-one whose left hand is a scar at the base of the thumb in four years and who cannot clear a leg that belongs to him any more, because he stopped counting, and the counting was the only thing that ever cleared it.

None of that is a right that survives a district being squeezed. A right that depends on a boy of nineteen being willing is not a right. It is nineteen, and it is unpaid, and it is in a room over a pump, and the woman who asked for it is the one who cannot appeal from one word of it, and the man who wrote it out is a clerk of thirty-one who has asked five times to be relieved of the print authority and has been refused five times and will not ask a sixth.

And the sentence that was said at about the sixth bell on the twenty-ninth, on a bank, by a woman of twenty-nine, to a bench of four and a clerk and a boy of nineteen, is a question and not a rule, and it is the first thing anybody has asked out loud in four months in this city.

“Nobody has ever told me what this page is for,” said Wenna Tace.

She was sitting on the barrel lid with the barrow against the wall and the water coming up the flat and about two hundred people going up the bank in a line, and she had read the entry out twice and had not said anything for a while.

“I keep it. I swear entries in it. I read it out at the pump four times a year and twice a week I have a boy of nineteen reading it to whoever asks, and I cannot appeal from one word of it, and I cannot be a party, and I cannot appear in support of one, and I cannot be named in one. And in four months not one person — not one — has come to that door and told me what a page is for. Not what it may be used for, I have worked out what it may be used for, I have worked that out twice a week in about four minutes. What it is *for*.

“And I have wanted to ask somebody for a month, and there is nobody here, and it is not a question for a bench of four and it is not a question for a man with a leg, and it is not a question for a recordwright who is a day's walk off and carrying nine sheets for a district that cannot pay for them. It is a question about pages. And there is one room in this city that anybody in this case knows of that has ever had a page taken out of it, and it is not a bench and it is not over a pump, and the page came out of it before any of us came over here. I have never been in it. I have heard two people say that the place is empty and the thread of the book is cut and there is a mark beside the empty place in a newer hand than the book, and I have believed them, and I do not know what a page is for and I am not going to find out by asking two men in a lane.”

Corva Silt did not say anything. Nobody said anything for long enough that the chain came up at the gate two hundred feet away and the water came over the sill-stone.

“Send her that,” said Wenna Tace. “Send the woman who reads for a living a letter with a question in it that she has not got to answer for anybody, and I will pay for the paper out of a pound and eleven a quarter, and I am not going to be in it above the question, because I am the holder and I am not allowed to be in a support of anything and I am not going to start in a letter.”

“I do not know what it costs,” said Corva Silt, “and I have been a clerk for six years and I know every figure in this district to the penny, and that is because every figure in this district is about this district. I shall have to go and find out what it costs to put a sheet of paper into a city, and when I have found out I shall send you the figure on the back of it, and it will be my day and not yours, and I shall enter that too.”

“Write it down,” said Wenna Tace. “And put the question at the top and my name under it and hers at the bottom and do not improve either of them.”

And in a room in Orison on the second of the fifth month a recordwright of a public record and expressly not the advocate of anybody took a folded sheet of cheap paper off a desk with a debt's old address on the back of it, and read a hauler's question, and did not answer it, and sat down at a table and put a licence in front of her with four terms on it, and read the fourth one twice.

> **SHE TAKES NO MATTER OF HER OWN WHILE SHE HOLDS THIS ONE.**

Nobody had ever asked her whether that term was about matters or about questions, and she sat with it for a while, and then she got up and put on a coat and did not go out, and did not go and look for the six-year-old page of her own family, and did not open the book, and wrote in the back of her own book, in her own hand, with a date on it, four lines that are in no record but hers:

> **A QUESTION IS NOT A MATTER. A MATTER IS A CLAIM, A SUBJECT, A BURDEN, A WITNESS, A REMEDY AND AN EXIT.**
>
> **I HAVE SPENT FOUR MONTHS CARRYING NINE SHEETS FOUR MILES FROM A BRIDGE AND I HAVE NOT LOOKED AT A PAGE OF MY OWN IN SIX YEARS AND TONIGHT A HAULER OF TWENTY-NINE HAS ASKED ME THE FIRST QUESTION NOBODY HAS ASKED ME SINCE I CAME HERE.**
>
> **THE ANSWER IS IN A ROOM FOUR MILES FROM THE PLACE I AM STANDING AND THE SLOT IS EMPTY AND THE THREAD IS CUT AND THE MARK IS IN A NEWER HAND.**
>
> **AND I HAVE NO STANDING IN THAT ROOM AND I AM NOT A PARTY TO IT AND THE PAGE I AM LOOKING FOR IS A PAGE OF MY OWN AND I HAVE NOT LOOKED FOR IT IN SIX YEARS, AND TONIGHT THE QUESTION IS ABOUT A PAGE OF SOMEBODY ELSE'S, AND THAT IS NOT THE SAME THING, AND I WANT IT WRITTEN DOWN THAT I KNOW THE DIFFERENCE.**

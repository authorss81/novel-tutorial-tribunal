# Chapter 84: The Only Room In This City With Anything In It

The folder came back.

Adela Cray brought it into the bench's own room over the shop that had been a chandler's, on the sixth of the fourth month, at the second bell, and she put it on the table in front of Perditta Nye, and it was in her apron, and it had been in her apron for four weeks, and the bench's book said in the bench's own hand that the bench held it.

“Then it does not,” said Nye.

“It does not,” said Adela Cray. “You wrote in your book on the eleventh of March that this bench held it. I have read that page. I cannot read the rest of your book, I have told you that, I can read about four words together and a number. But I had it read out to me by a man from the church who does the notices, in the second week of March, because I wanted to know what you had done with it, and you had written *the bench held it*, and I went home and put it in my apron and it has been in my apron ever since.”

“Your man read out one line. I know which line.”

“Then I would like the line taken out.”

“No,” said Perditta Nye. “I will not take it out, and I will tell you why in the ordinary way and then I will tell you the other thing, which is that the line is the only true sentence this bench has written in three years and I am not going to have it read in a room over a chandler's shop by a man from the church who cannot read either.”

---

Aldis Wray had come to her a second time on the second of this month, which was the day after the district read a rate of half a farthing a bucket at a pump four miles and one bridge away, and eleven miles from the fen road there is no pump and no rate and no reading, and he had brought nothing.

He had said, standing in a lane, that he had four rooms and one free, and that the free one was in three weeks, and that the four in the other one would have to be somebody's, and that he was not doing anything wrong and had said so the first time and would say it again. And he had said that she was the fourth week.

“Three weeks is not a fortnight’s notice,” said Nye. “That is a thing I want on the record before anything else because it is the sort of thing that gets in a book wrong and then a book is wrong for thirty years.”

“It was a fortnight and then three weeks because the second one was not mine to give on the day he had rooms cleaned,” said Adela Cray. “I am not trying to be fair to him. I am trying to be accurate, because I have found out this month that being accurate is the only thing I have.”

She is about forty. She is not a clerk and not a holder of anything and she has never been in a room with a door she could shut. She has an agreement four years old that was in her name from the day she signed it and has never once been produced anywhere, and she is right about a great deal and wrong about one thing, and the one thing she is wrong about is going to be the finding of the eighth of the fourth month.

---

Her want, when you asked her, and she answered it in one sentence: *I want a room where a piece of paper about me can go and stay.*

“You have a room,” said Nye. “Over a chandler’s. With a bench in it and a bench’s book in the bench’s room.”

“That is not the same thing and you know it is not the same thing, and I will tell you what the difference is so that you do not have to say it. In this room a piece of paper about me goes into your book and then goes out again in my apron, because a book is not a place you can keep a thing in. Your book is about *things that have happened*. A piece of paper about me is about *me*, and the two do not go in the same book, and everybody in this district knows they do not go in the same book, and I have not got anywhere else for it.”

“That is the best description of this that anybody has given me in three years,” said Perditta Nye, “including the man from the chamber, and I am going to write it down, and I want your leave, and I am going to write it in the bench’s book with your name on it, and I am going to tell you now that that is the price of the room and the price is your name.”

“All right,” said Adela Cray.

---

The other two came in over the next two days, and the second of them is the one that made the room stop working for a fortnight.

The first was a man of about sixty with a folded sheet who wanted the bench to hold it and did not want his name against it, and Nye took the name and he went away, and that is a whole thing that took nine minutes.

The second came on the eighth of the fourth month at the second bell and he was about thirty and he was a coal man and he had a page in his hand that came out of his mother's things, and it was a weighbridge sheet off a weighbridge four days up the fen road, dated a hundred and forty years ago, and he wanted the bench to hold it because his mother had been told all her life that she had been short at a weighbridge and he wanted it to be in a room somewhere with her name on it.

Nye read it out. It was in a hand nobody in the room could read and it was in no district's book and it had come out of a house, and the coal man wanted it in the bench's book with his mother's name on it, and the only reason he wanted it there was so that somebody could find it again in about four years when he was gone.

“It is a hundred and forty years old,” said Nye, “and I cannot read it and neither can you and neither can the man from the church, and if I put it in this bench’s book and write your mother’s name against it, then in about two years a bench in this city is going to write to this bench and say *do you hold a page, book, column, register, schedule or sworn instrument which a person affected may swear to*, and this bench is going to have to say yes, and it is going to have to produce a hundred-and-forty-year-old weighbridge sheet in a hand nobody can read against a dead woman’s name.”

“That is what I want,” said the coal man.

“That is what you want and I am telling you what it is,” said Nye, “and I am not refusing it and I am going to do it, and I am going to do it with a column in it that says *not legible to this bench*, and I am going to read that column back to you at the quarter, and if you do not like it you can take it back out of the room and I will enter that you did.”

He thought about it for a while.

“Put the column in,” he said.

---

The bench of three wrote its own rule on the ninth of the fourth month, in its own room, in public, on a day it appointed, and it is the first rule in this case that has been made by a body which is not a bench of a district with a rate in it, and it took four hours and the whole of the ordinary work of a bench of three for a fortnight.

> **FALLOWDITCH, THE NINTH OF THE FOURTH MONTH. ENTERED IN THE BOOK OF THE BENCH OF THREE, IN ITS OWN HAND, ON A DAY THE BENCH APPOINTED, IN ITS OWN ROOM, IN PUBLIC.**
>
> **ONE: ANY PERSON OF THIS DISTRICT MAY BRING A DOCUMENT TO THIS BENCH'S OWN ROOM, ON A DAY THIS BENCH APPOINTS, AND IT SHALL BE READ OUT IN THE ROOM, AND AN ENTRY SHALL BE MADE IN THIS BENCH'S BOOK, AND THE ENTRY SHALL HAVE A DATE AND THE NAME OF THE PERSON IT IS ABOUT.**
>
> **TWO: THE DOCUMENT ITSELF GOES BACK TO THE PERSON WHO BROUGHT IT. THIS BENCH KEEPS THE ENTRY AND NOT THE THING. THE THING BELONGS TO THE PERSON AND THE ENTRY BELONGS TO THE ROOM, AND A ROOM THAT KEEPS THE THING IS A ROOM THAT LOSES THE THING, AND WE HAVE HAD THAT ONCE ALREADY THIS YEAR.**
>
> **THREE: A PERSON MAY ASK THAT AN ENTRY BE MADE WITHOUT HIS NAME OR WITHOUT THE NAME OF THE PERSON IT IS ABOUT. IF HE DOES, THE ENTRY SHALL SAY UNNAMED, AND THE ENTRY MAY BE PRODUCED AND MAY NOT BE TIED TO ANY PERSON, AND THIS BENCH ENTERS THAT IN A DISTRICT OF THIS SIZE AN ENTRY THAT SAYS UNNAMED IS AN ENTRY EVERYBODY CAN IDENTIFY, AND THAT THIS IS THEATRE, AND THAT THIS BENCH IS DOING IT ANYWAY BECAUSE IT IS BETTER THAN NOTHING AND BECAUSE A MAN MAY TAKE WHAT HE CAN GET.**
>
> **FOUR: THIS BENCH HAS NO MONEY FOR THE DAY. THE DAY IS PAID OUT OF THE THREE OF US OUT OF OUR OWN PURSES, AT NO RATE, AND THAT IS ENTERED SO THAT A CLERK IN FOUR YEARS DOES NOT READ A GENEROUS BENCH. THERE IS A RATE IN THIS CITY WE COULD USE AND WE HAVE NOT GOT IT.**
>
> **FIVE: NOBODY OUTSIDE THIS DISTRICT MAY USE THIS BENCH'S ROOM BY THIS RULE. THIS BENCH IS ELEVEN MILES FROM THE FEN ROAD AND FOUR MILES FROM THE FURTHEST OF ITS OWN TWO PLACES, AND A ROOM WITH A RULE IN IT REACHES THE PEOPLE WHO CAN GET TO IT AND NOT ONE PERSON FURTHER. THAT IS ENTERED SO THAT NOBODY IN FOUR YEARS CALLS THIS A ROOM FOR A DISTRICT.**

The third member of the bench was out on a boundary for two of the four days and took the other two without comment.

Adela Cray read the rule out in the room on the ninth and got most of it, because Nye read it to her twice first, which is a thing Nye now does for everybody and has stopped apologising for.

Then she said the part that was wrong, and she said it as a woman who wants a room to keep a paper in and not as a lawyer, and she was wrong in exactly the way that a person is wrong when they have been refused for four weeks.

“I have been in this room twice in a fortnight and both times I have walked out with the thing in my apron,” she said, “and you have just made a rule saying I always will, in writing, with a date on it, and called it durability.”

“It is durability,” said Perditta Nye. “In about four years there will be a page in this book with a date on it and your name on it, and nobody can take it out, and the landlord cannot say in a lane that there is nothing behind you, because there will be something behind you, and it will be a line in a bench's book and a man who has not got a copy and cannot get one and has to come and ask me, and I will read it to him, and it will take an hour of a bench of three's time and it will be worth it.”

“It is worth it to you,” said Adela Cray. “It is not worth it to me. What I need is for a man in a lane to be able to put my four-year-old paper in front of somebody and have them go white. And a line in your book about it does not do that, and you know it does not, because you told me in the second week of March that the thing you could give me was a thing I could put in front of anybody and that the man I want to shake is not standing here, and you are the one who told me that.”

“Yes,” said Perditta Nye. “I am the one who told you that. And it has not changed and it is not going to change, and this rule does not make a fortnight's notice into a thing a bench hears, because a fortnight's notice is not a document, it is a date and a man with four rooms, and there is nothing in this city that hears a date and a man. What this rule does is that in four years there is an entry, and an entry is heavier than nothing, and I have watched nothing lose twice this year and I am prepared to be laughed at.”

“I am not laughing at you,” said Adela Cray. “I have four weeks.”

---

The rule was used on the same day, at the fourth bell, and the person who used it had read it standing up in about a minute and had understood every word of it, including the third of the five, which nobody had explained to him.

He was the man of about sixty with the folded sheet, and he came back into the room at the fourth bell and said, “I want mine unnamed.”

Nye did not argue. She wrote it in, in the book, in the same hand, with the date, and said the third term out loud while she wrote it, exactly as it stands.

“Unnamed,” she read, “and the entry may be produced and may not be tied to any person. That is the term you have just asked for and I am going to tell you what it is, because the bench wrote it and the bench is not going to pretend it is something else. In this district an entry that says unnamed is an entry every person over about thirty can identify, and you and I both know it, and so does the man it is about, and so does the man he is frightened of.”

“I know that,” he said.

“Then you want it because the name is the part that can be got at.”

“Yes.”

“Then here is what you are buying and I want it said out loud in the room because somebody is in the lane,” said Perditta Nye, “and it is this. In four years, when the man comes with a lawyer, there is a line in this book that says that a document exists and is dated, and there is no name on it, and he cannot get at it by asking who it was about, because it does not say. And he will not believe that. And you will know he does not believe it. And a man who does not believe a thing is harder to frighten than a man with nothing at all, and that is worth what it is worth and not a penny more.”

He stood in the room for a while with his hands behind him.

“My sister keeps the family things,” he said, “and she would have my name on it inside a month, and she would have had my name on it a hundred and ten years ago when it should have been settled, and I have spent nine years being the one it was not settled with. Unnamed is better than that.”

“It is,” said Nye, “and I am not going to improve on unnamed, and I have never in three years been gladder to be laughed at than I am at the word *theatre* in a rule I wrote on a bench of three's own motion nine days ago.”

She read the entry back to him twice. He could not read it, and he made her do it, and when she had finished he touched the book with one finger on the line, the way a man touches a gate he has just had closed that was standing open in the wind, and then he went out into the lane and down it and did not look back at the shop.

Nye entered, at the fifth bell, that the entry had been made and used on the day it was made, and that the bench was aware of what it was.

That was the ninth of the fourth month, and the whole of the durability of Fallowditch's room was one used entry with the word *unnamed* in it, and everybody who went past the shop that evening knew whose it was.

---

On the tenth of the fourth month Perditta Nye sat in her own room after the second bell with a sheet of paper in front of her and did not know what to write on it, and this is where the next three weeks of her life begin and she did not know it yet.

She had been a clerk for three years and before that she had been a woman who could not read a contract, and she had got the post because the man before her had gone to sea. She had built a room in four days and it works, and she was proud of it, and the pride lasted about nine days, and what came after the pride was this.

A room with a rule in it needs to know what it is a room *for*.

She had a hundred-and-forty-one-year-old survey book she could not let anybody swear to. She had a hundred-and-sixty-year-old book that two letters have now told this bench nobody may swear to. She had an entry with *unnamed* in it that everybody in the district can identify. She had four entries and a woman of about forty and three weeks of notice, and she had a column she had invented four days ago in a room over a shop, and she had not got anything in it that was a charter.

And what she wanted, sitting there at about the third bell on the tenth, was a copy of the charter of a district eleven miles down the fen road — nine sheets, sworn, sealed, five terms, with a person in the fifth of them and a rate on it and an appeal in it and a man named in the second half of the fourth — because it is the only document anybody in this case has produced that a poor person was *given*, and she wanted to know what one looked like when it was in the room, so that she could tell the coal man and Adela Cray and whoever came next what a page was supposed to be for.

Fallowditch is eleven miles from the fen road. A cart from Fallowditch to the fen road is a day's hire and the district has nine pounds in the year. She wrote the ask herself, in her own hand, on the back of a rule she had made four days ago, and she wrote it the way her bench writes, with the date and the four names and the seal, and then she sat looking at it for about a minute.

She was not going to send it. That was the thing she understood on the ninth and did not put in the rule: a bench of three that asks a district four miles off for the terms of a charter it has not adopted, in a letter, in a cart, at a cost, is a bench of three that has spent nine pounds and a day and got nine sheets it cannot read, bind nobody, and come back for — and then a second one of those, and then a third, and a bench of three's ordinary work is a boundary and a book nobody may swear to.

And then she thought about the third member, who had been out on a boundary for two of the four days and had come back and read the rule and said nothing, and who was fifty-eight and had a farm, and about the woman of about forty standing in the room saying *I have four weeks*.

She folded the sheet and put it in her coat and went out and did the boundary, and the ask went down the lane on the eleventh in a hand that is the bench's own.

And about eleven miles away, in a room over a pump, a woman of twenty-nine finished her second run of the day, and came up the bank, and sat down on the barrel lid, and said to the step, to nobody in particular, that a bench of three eleven miles down the fen road had been in her head all afternoon and she could not say why and it was not about the rate.

“It is about the page,” she said. “It has always been about the page.”

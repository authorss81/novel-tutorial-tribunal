# Chapter 68: Four Terms and a Fifth

The choice was set for the fourth bell of the seventeenth of the month and it was taken in the open air at the First Verdict with the wind off the water and the block wet on one side, and about two hundred people were on the flat four miles down the fen road and none of them were in the room, and the room knew that, and nobody had fixed it, and the reason nobody had fixed it is that fixing it would have taken a cart and a day and a half out of a district that had no money and a gate hand who could not leave the gate.

It is entered, at Ansa Rell's direction, and before any argument, and on the face of the record, and in almost these words: *that the person whose thirty years is the subject of the first four terms of one text and of the whole of the fourth term of the other has declined to come, and has said in writing that the reason is that the gate shall be worked by the gate hand and by no other person, and that if the gate hand is not at the gate the gate is shut, and that the gate being shut is a flat nobody has counted since the twentieth, and about two hundred people, and that she is not going to be the reason a court in Orison decides a thing about her in a room she is not in.*

She wrote it herself and it is four sentences and it has no signature because a gate hand does not sign, and Corva Silt read it out and entered it and did not add anything, and the room sat with it for about a minute, and Ilyan found that he had no way at all of doing anything with it, which is new.

---

Bel Ordway argued the clean instrument, and he argued it well, and he argued it as a man with a cart and a district's water in front of him and not as a man who has read a book.

“I have said the reasons once and I will say them short and then I will stop, because there is a man in this room who has been talking for a week and it is my turn and I have a weighbridge at the seventh bell.

“Eight clauses. A person on foot goes through and may not be refused. A cart goes out when the tide is out and comes in when the tide is in. A gate hand is paid four hours a day by name in chalk out of the district's own road funds. A quarter of a penny a bucket at the standpipe and not at a door. And nobody is unhoused, and nobody is put on a list, and it is one sheet, and I have read it, and I filled in ten thousand forms in nineteen years and I have never been more competent at anything.

“I am not going to pretend I do not want it for my cart. I have lost two seasons of hauling off that low water in a month and I have not said so out loud until the day before last and I am saying it now on a step in front of nine benches.

“And I am going to say the other thing, because I have been four days on it and it is the reason I am standing here and not sitting at the wall. This instrument does not give one single person in that district anything they can do anything with. It is payable to a bench of four. It is read by a bench of four. It is appealed to — nothing. It is a good rule and a good rule is a thing you live inside. Every person I have ever been inside a rule with has been inside it for a very long time and has never once been asked whether it was the right one, and I have not got a better answer than that and I have had forty years to look for one.”

And then Cael Rundle, who was not a lawyer and had been in this case since the fifteenth of the last month and had a bad chest in the wet, said the thing that made the argument real, in nine words.

“It is the water,” he said. “Mine. Weir Lane. A quarter of a penny a bucket, and my daughter is fourteen and she walks to that pump every morning and she is not in a book and she is going to pay a rate to a woman at a gate, and I voted for it and I would like it minuted that I voted for it and knew that when I voted.”

---

Wenna Tace argued the charter, and she came up the fen road on the seventeenth at the second bell with the barrow on a cart because the bench of four would not let her walk it, and she argued it standing on the step with her hands empty, which she had not done in any room at Orison in a month.

“I am not a lawyer and I am not a delegate and I am not a person anybody sent,” she said. “I am the reader of that district and I read the charter out on a bank in the mud to about a hundred and ninety people on the third of this month and I cut nine words out of the second page because the word *water* would have kept a boy of nine out of his own district, and I cut a second clause out of the third page because a form that says *a person who habitually dwells* throws a man out of its own front door, and if I have cut two clauses out of your charter then somebody in this room should know that I have been in it and I am not being paid for it.

“The clean instrument is good. I have said so and I will say so again. It is short and it is honest and I can read it. And I have been inside a rule for eleven years that I could not read, and the thing about a rule you cannot read is not that it is unfair. It is that there is nobody on the other side of it. When that rule was printed on a gate two miles from where I live, a man with a barrow could stand in front of it and put his hand on it and say *this is not fair*, and he would be wrong to, because the rule is not unfair, it is nothing to anybody. It does not have a person in it. There is nobody to be angry at.

“Clause four of the clean instrument puts a quarter of a penny on every bucket in Weir Lane for ever, and no person in that district can appeal it, because there is nowhere in the world to appeal a quarter of a penny to. And a quarter of a penny is not a large thing. My mother died in the year they last moved that rate and I know exactly what a quarter of a penny is in a house of five, and I am telling this court that in four years somebody on this bench is going to be asked to set that rate again, and it will be a quarter of a penny again, and there will be forty people in that lane, and not one of them will be able to do one single thing about it, and they will all be able to see it.”

She stopped there, and did not add anything, and the room understood that she had just made the argument for a thing that does not yet exist and had not been drafted and might not be drafted at all.

Harrow put the two costs on the step in the flat voice.

“The clean instrument is eight clauses, it can be made in an afternoon, it is payable on the fourteenth, and nobody is unhouseable on it. On the first quarter of it, the low-water gate stays shut to loads out of the district at all times, which is right, and the district’s carts come back, and about nine carts a week come back onto a road that has been shut for a month, and a cart is four barrows and a barrow is not a person.

“The charter is nine sheets and one of the five terms has no person in it, and the person is not found by writing harder, and if the fifth term is left open then the charter is a rule with a hole where the district is, and in four months a clerk in some other district will print it with a bench’s name in the hole and it will be good law. It also costs about forty-four pounds a year, and it cannot be paid out of the district's own road fund at all because the cartage on that fund's road has been nil since the fourteenth, so the money comes off the pump instead, and there is a bridge four miles away with four square holes in it that the district's money is contesting, and the ninth-bridge district's fund is the only fund in this case with anything in it. and there is a bridge four miles away with four square holes in it that those paper pounds are contesting.”

Then Ansa Rell said the thing that nobody had planned, which is that she was going to vote her own weight for the clean instrument, and she said why in front of everybody, and it was the right reason and it was the reason that had been in her bag since the eleventh day.

“I am for the clean one,” she said. “I have been for it for a month. One legible rule, payable, in a room with one clerk, that costs a poor district one sheet and a quarter of a penny and does not cost it the question. I am going to say the rest of it out loud so that nobody can tell me later I did not.

“It is the right answer. It is the correct answer. It is the answer I would give to a bench that asked me for one instrument and a date, and I have wanted it since the eighteenth of the last month and I have told nine benches I wanted it, and I am not going to pretend today that I want the other one because it is better for four hundred people in a lane, because it is not better for them by a single penny until somebody in that lane can use it, and at present nobody in that lane can use it, and I have been asking eleven benches for five weeks whether they hold anything and the answer is no in eleven hands.

“But I am going to say the other half, and this is the finding I have to make as a magistrate and I am making it now rather than at the end, because a magistrate who saves it for the end is a magistrate who has been arguing.

“If this court makes the clean instrument today, then in four months a bench of four in a poor district is paying a woman four hours a day out of a rate on a pump, and there is no person in that rule, and no door out of it, ever, for as long as the rule stands, and the rate goes up when the water goes up, because a rate on a pump goes up when a pump goes up, and there is nobody who can be asked to say no. I have read a form of it in eleven districts. It is the best form this city has and it is the exact shape of a thing that has no person in it.

“If this court makes the charter today, then in four months a poor district has nine sheets, a reader with a barrow, a hole in the fifth term, and half a farthing on a bucket at a pump for ever. And a thing in a poor district that a poor district has to pay for is a thing that a poor district will lose, and I know that and it is the reason I have wanted one rule for a month and not nine.

“I am going to put my weight behind the clean instrument and then I am going to hear the rest of this room, and if the rest of this room puts me out, I am going to write the minute of it myself and sign it and it will be in this case's record for as long as this case is, and nobody is going to be able to say that a magistrate was railroaded into a difficult thing by a man from a bridge.”

---

Sera Quill said the part that was against him, and she said it standing, and she had the fair copy of the fifth sheet in her own hand and did not put it down.

“The second half of the fourth term,” she said. “It is his name. It has been his name for nine days and I read it out on a bank in the mud to about a hundred and ninety people, and it is the reason I have not been able to write the fifth term, and I am going to say why in front of this court because it is a fact about my own work and the court is entitled to it.

“The fifth term wants a holder. A person in the district who keeps the page and swears an entry made in it. I have asked nine people in this city and I have not found one person in that district who can hold a page.

“The man of fifty-four said no on a bank in front of about a hundred and ninety people and said he would say no again. The clerk of the bench cannot, because a clerk is a body and the term wants a person. The gate hand cannot, because she cannot read a page and she is at a gate and she told a field that she has never counted her own hours and would not start for a page. And the reader of the district is the only person in that district who can read, and is a person, and lives in a room she rents, and hauls about two hundred loads a year, and has been paid by that district forty-one times in eleven years at about a third of a haul rate.

“I have not asked her. I want that on the record, because in four years a clerk is going to read this charter and read her name in the fifth term and think that she volunteered. I have not asked her, and I have been in her district for nine days, and I have read a charter out to about a hundred and ninety people on her bank, and I did not ask her, because I am the recordwright and I have a stove that does not draw and I have four days of paper and the fifth term has a hole in it and a hole in a term is something I know how to live with, and a person in a term is something I have to be responsible for.

“I have been told by a magistrate that I am not her servant and I am not, and I have been told by a man from a bridge that he is not a post and I am not his advocate and I am not, and I am the recordwright of this charter and I answer for the record and not for the outcome, and the outcome of a fifth term with a hole in it is that this charter is enforceable in four parts and holds a district in the fifth, and the outcome of a fifth term with a person in it is that a person in a poor district has just been made responsible for the law of a bridge four miles away.

“I am going to write the fifth term either way and I am not going to put a name in it that I have not asked, and if this court finishes today with a hole in it then I will enter my own name against the fact that the recordwright of a charter left the fifth term open, and it will be the second thing in a month I have entered against myself and it will be the one that costs me my licence and I would rather it did it in my own hand.”

The room did what rooms do, which is nothing, for about nine seconds.

Then Ilyan said the sentence that is the only thing he said all day that anybody has ever written down, and he said it in a guild's words, standing four feet inside the rail with a leg that had gone at the fourth bell and was not his, and it is in the record in nine words.

“I am for the charter, and the reason is that a gate is not the problem.”

That is the whole of it. He did not say that a rule with a person in it can be broken. He did not say that four hundred and eleven loads are on a flat. He did not say anything about the ninth bridge or about what the record cost. He said that a gate is not the problem, and it is the only sentence anybody said on that step that could have been said by a bridgekeeper.

Kett looked at him for a moment and then said, to the court, in the voice of an advocate who has just been given an argument he did not agree with:

“My client has made a submission of one sentence and it is correct and it is not an argument, and I am obliged to say that it does not carry his weight and mine, and that I am going to argue the clean instrument for the rest of the hour, and that the court is entitled to know that I have been instructed in nothing and have been out-argued this morning.”

“You have been out-argued by a man with nine sheets and a bad leg,” said Harrow, “and both of those facts are now in the record, and the second one is not, and I am not going to enter it, and Mr Roke is going to stop looking pleased.”

The positions were taken in the order they were taken and there were five of them and they are the whole of the room.

Bel Ordway: the clean instrument, and his own water, and a quarter of a penny.

Ottla Prenn: the clean instrument, and a city that has one rule and one place to ask about it, and a delegate who has been in three common-rule sittings in a month.

Abel Kearn, thirty-five, a builder out of the upper street: “I do not know, and I am not going to pretend, and I have a bad thing in that district and both of these fix it and neither of them fixes it, and I would like whichever one gets chosen to be chosen by people who have to live in it and not by me.”

Cael Rundle: the clean instrument, and his daughter's bucket, and the words *I voted for it and knew that when I voted.*

And the magistrate: the clean instrument, in her own hand, signed, for ever.

Which is three, one who does not know, and a magistrate's own weight, and which is not a decision, and the claimant's one sentence is not in that count because the claimant is not a party to either text and has never asked to be, and Ansa Rell said all of that, and said that the thing a court in Orison had just done was go into the well and refuse to vote and that she was not going to be thanked for it because the vote was in the room and the room had one member in it who could read the charter, which was not a legal test and she was going to use it anyway.

---

Then Nell Ambrey, the convenor of Halloway's bench, stood up, and she had come up the fen road at the second bell with Wenna Tace, and she is sixty and she entered a sanitation complaint eleven weeks ago into a bench of four and it has cost her the low water and it has not yet cost anybody anything at all, and she had been in the room for two hours without speaking.

“I want something entered and I want it entered against me,” she said.

“Go on.”

“In the twenty-seventh of this month the bench of four will have to answer the magistrate's question in writing, in public, and it will have to say it in front of whoever turns up. It will have to set a rate. It will have to serve notices, which cost money, and swear a page, which costs more, and pay somebody to keep it, which costs most, and it will have to hear whatever is brought. And I entered a sanitation complaint eleven weeks ago, and the complaint is the reason there is a court in this city at all, and I am the convenor of the bench that entered it, and under either of these texts my complaint is not heard this year.

“That is the cost of this afternoon and I have come four miles up a road to say it out loud, because if I say it here it is entered, and if I do not say it here then in three months it will be nobody’s fault and it will be on me, and I have been the person it happens to for eleven weeks and I would like it to belong to a decision.”

Nobody improved that either.

“The bench can hear the complaint first,” said Ansa Rell. “It is in your gift and you know that.”

“No, magistrate, it is not in my gift, and it is not because anybody has taken it,” said the convenor. “It is in my gift on a day when I have nothing else, and I am going to have something else.”

---

Harrow made the question at the seventh bell and the room went quiet in the way a room does when everybody has known the answer since the second day and nobody has said it.

“Term five,” he said. “A holder. A person in the district, in a place, with a rate, keeping a page, and not holding it for a district. I have asked nine people. A magistrate, a court, a clerk of four, a man of fifty-four on a lane, and a gate hand in writing, and four benches. And I have got five refusals in good order and one refusal in a bad order, and the four benches are the other four, and I am not going to bother this room with them, because a bench is a body and a body does not refuse a thing. It declines to hold it. That is the same answer with a different word on it and it is how this case started.

“Who in this room reads.”

The wind came off the water at the same angle it had come off it at the second sitting.

“Miss Tace,” said Harrow, “the court has not asked you a single question this afternoon. I have been aware of that for about two hours and I have been waiting to see whether anybody else would notice and nobody did, and it has taken a trialwright of eleven years and a magistrate and four benches and a man from a bridge to get to a question with a one-word answer.

“You read. You are a person. You live in a room you rent in a district. You hauled about two hundred loads last year and were paid forty-one times at a third of a haul rate, which is a thing that makes you the only person in that district who can name a rate and know whether it is the right one. Do you understand what term five is going to make you?”

“Yes,” said Wenna Tace.

“Say it.”

“It is going to make me the person a charge goes to,” she said. “It is going to make me the place a person brings a thing that has no other place to go. And when somebody in that district wants to know what the rule costs, they are going to be told to ask the reader, and I am going to be the reader, and I am going to be the person who says *that is what it says* and then I am going to have to live on the lane with them while they find out what it actually costs, and there are going to be people who will come to me at the fifth bell with a thing they are frightened of, and I will not have a bench behind me, I will have a slate and a room and a barrow, and the whole of my authority will be that I can read and everybody knows I can read.

“And I will be the person the four of them come to when they cannot set a rate, and when a rate goes on the pump, and when it goes up.”

“Term five also says something else,” said Sera Quill, and she read it and it is her own sentence from the ninth day and she did not soften it. “A holder is not a party. A holder may not bring an appeal under this charter, or appear in support of one, or be named in one. That is in there because I put it in there on the fourth day, and I will tell you why, and the reason is that a person who can be appealed against and a person who carries the appeals cannot be the same person, or every page in this charter is a closed door with a woman in it.

“You have been in this case since the fourteenth of the last month,” said Sera. “You asked a room who you write to, and nobody answered you, and then a trialwright answered you, and the answer was *me*, and he was four miles away. That is what this term fixes and it fixes it by taking the one thing out of that lane that you have wanted since the twenty-third.

“You will hold the page. You will not be able to appeal.”

Nobody said anything.

“Then I will not do it,” said Wenna Tace.

“Say the other thing,” said Harrow.

“Then I will do it and I will not be able to appeal,” said Wenna Tace, “and put those two things in the same line, because they are both true and I have been writing a flat voice at a queue for eleven years and I am the only person in this room who knows how to do that.”

And she said one more thing before she agreed, and it was not for the court, and the court entered it anyway because the court enters everything, and it is in the record because it is the sentence the whole of this case has been walking toward for six weeks without knowing it.

“I want it entered that I did not volunteer, and that a trialwright asked me a question in a room instead of a magistrate, and that I am doing it because the alternative is that the fifth term is empty and the alternative to that is a bench of four setting a rate on Weir Lane every quarter with nobody in the district able to say one word about it, and I would rather be the person they have to say the word to.”

“That is entered,” said Roke.

“Enter it in her words and do not tidy it and do not put *witness* against her name,” said Ilyan, and Harrow said, “that is the first thing the attached record-holder has said all day that is not an argument, and I am entering that too.”

---

The charter was made at the ninth bell on the seventeenth of the month, in the open air, in the wind, by a bench of four and a recordwright and a magistrate, and the fifth term has a person in it, and the recordwright put the name in herself and read it back and did not look at anybody.

The carrier's clause is in the second half of the fourth term and it names a man who is standing four feet inside a rail with a leg that is not his, and it says he carries the terms to any district that asks, at his own cost and without pay, and that if he refuses the appeal is stayed, and Ilyan said yes to it in nine words, in the record, with his own name in it, and did not make a speech, and the speech he had made was one sentence about a gate and it had cost him the only advocate he had ever had.

Nobody thanked him for the carrier's clause and the room was right not to, and afterwards Kett found him on the step and said, without any warmth at all, “I argued against you for three hours and you have given that district a person to send for. I would like to know what you think you have done and I would like you to be accurate about it.”

“I have made myself findable,” said Ilyan.

“Yes,” said Maren Kett. “That is the honest word for it and it took you a month to get there and it did not require a single good argument, and I am a lawyer of nineteen years and I am telling you that the two are not the same thing and that this city is going to spend the next ten years finding out which one of them it wants.”

---

At the last bell the two texts were on the step and one of them was the law of a poor district from the ninth bell that day and the other of them was eight clauses and a signature, and neither of them was anywhere near safe, and the difference between them was a person with a barrow who could not appeal and a rate on a pump that could not be appealed, and both of those had been true by the ninth bell of the seventeenth of the month and had not been true at the fourth bell, and nobody who was in that room could have told you at the fourth bell which way it would go.

Four miles down the fen road, at a gate, a woman of forty-four stood with one foot on a sill-stone and one on a bank and worked a gate in the water for about two hundred people, and she had not been in the room, and the clerk's lane entry stood, and the flat was where it had been, and on a slate with a line down the middle of it there were six days of a gate hand's tide in a load-carrier's figures with a name at the top of it, and a man who had carried nine sheets four miles up a road and down it again was sitting on a barrel lid at the gate side with his back against a wall and a leg that was not his and a page in his own case's record that could not be taken back, which was the fifth.

# Chapter 51: The Gate at the Low Water

The smell came up the road in three waves, and the first one told you it was a low-water district, the second one told you the low water was the fourth day out of a seven-day tide, and the third one told you that about nine hundred people had decided between them not to pay somebody to take it away.

Ilyan had walked the four miles from the weighhouse with the print rolled inside his coat and one of Coll's spare slates under his arm, because he had learned in a month that walking into a room with nothing in your hands is walking in to ask. The fen road went down past the salt wharf and then stopped being a road and became a bank, and the bank was mud to the axle in two places, and above the mud the ground was laid in stone that had been laid about a century ago by somebody who had had a plan.

The standpipe was at the low point of the whole district, which was a decision, and the decision had been made by somebody who did not have to drink the water.

At the sixth bell there were forty-one people at the queue. Ilyan counted them twice, because counting is the only thing he could still do at a glance, and because a number he could not get would be a number he could not use. Forty-one at the sixth bell. At the ninth bell, later, when the pump's second shift had been on for an hour, there would be nine. He wrote both figures on the slate and put a rule between them and did not yet know what the rule was for.

The print was nailed on a board by the gate, and it was not a notice in a frame. It was a single sheet, district paper, from a district form, and it had been put up with four nails and no glass, and rain had been at it for nine days and it had gone soft at the corners and the fourth paragraph was the only part still crisp.

Kett had been in the room since the fourth bell. Ilyan could see that because there were nine sets of boots in the doorway of the sanitation room and one of them was a district officer's and one of them was Maren Kett's, and because a man standing in a doorway with a paper in his hand for four hours changes how he stands.

Bryn Sallow was the gate hand. Ilyan learned that in the first ten minutes, from the queue, from three separate people who said the words *Bryn* and *the gate* in the same breath, and then he watched her work out what the gate was.

The gate was two leaves of oak on a frame, and under it a culvert, and above it a road, and the water came up under the road and the gate was how you stopped it coming up onto Weir Lane. When the tide was out the gate stood open and the low water ran through the arch in a hand's depth of black water with a grey skin on it. When the tide came in, the gate came down, and the lane went dry, and the water went wherever the water went, which was not somewhere anybody in Orison had a name for.

Bryn Sallow was forty-four and had been at that gate for thirty years and stood with one foot on the sill-stone and one on the bank, which was not a posture, it was a way of being a person who was already standing there. She had a handbell on a chain and a pole with a hook on the end and a key to a hut that was full of nothing but a broom and a second chair.

She rang the handbell when the cart came. Not the gate, the handbell. A handbell is attached to a hand.

---

The room was the sanitation room, and it was two rooms: a bench room with a long table and forty-one chairs, and a smaller one behind it with a stove and the district's roll-case, and the argument was in the bench room because that is where a room puts the thing it intends to decide.

Ilyan was not in it.

He stood in the lane for the whole of the first hour and a half and he was not in it because Kett had told him in Orison, in a corridor, that his name was on the paper and every person in that room would read the name and stop listening, and because when Ilyan had said *then I will not say my name*, Kett had said, in the flat voice of a man who had been right about a great many things, *then you are a man in a room who will not give his name, which is a worse man and it is the same man.*

So he stood in the lane. And standing in the lane turned out to be the most useful thing he could have done, because a man standing in a lane beside a gate in a district that cannot read is, within about nine minutes, a feature of the landscape, and the queue told him things.

The first thing the queue told him was that the sheet on that board was not new news. It had been printed nine days ago and nailed up on the day it was printed, and the copy of it had come up the fen road on the fifth day, which is how a thing two days old gets to be a week and a half old in a poor district, and the district had held two hearings on it, and the thing that had changed in the last four days was not the sheet. The thing that had changed was that a cart had been turned back.

“A cart came down at the second bell two nights ago,” said a man with a bucket and no cart, “and she would not open it, and it went back up with everything on it.”

“Whose cart,” Ilyan said.

“Yours is not, friend.” The man looked at his boots, which were cleaner than everybody else's. “Nobody's cart from here. That's what I mean. It was a carter off the fen road with stone on it for somebody's yard, and the gate was shut because the sheet says the gate is shut to anybody that is not a lawful traveller of the district, and a carter off the fen road is not a lawful traveller of the district, and stone is a load, and she did not open it.”

“And who is the lawful traveller of the district.”

“Whoever is in the book,” said the man, and nodded at the gate-side board, where under the district's own seal there was a bound book on a shelf under a sloping roof, and the book was four inches thick and had a date on the spine in a hand that had not been taught to write.

The book was a lawful-traveler book. It was the ninth bridge's instrument with the *span* taken out.

That was the first time in thirty-one days that Ilyan had heard a thing he already knew come back at him in a different voice, and the difference between this and the print was that the print was a document and this was a book with names in it, and a book is used.

---

Wenna Tace was the reason he found out the rest of it.

She came up the lane at the seventh bell with a barrow, and the barrow was a two-wheeled thing with iron tyres and a body made of a churn with the lid off, and it was full to the top of a grey and black and sweet thing that the smell had been about, and she was twenty-nine years old and she hauled it two hundred times a year out of the low water and up to the fen road.

She read.

That was the whole of the reason. Nobody else in that lane could read and she could, and so the practice in Halloway was that if a paper went up, Wenna Tace read it out, standing, at the queue, in a flat voice, once, and then everybody argued about it. She had been doing it for eleven years. She did it in the same voice she used to count a load, because a load and a notice are both a thing somebody has to get through.

She got to the fourth paragraph on the third reading of the queue, and she stopped, and she looked at the sheet, and she looked at it again, and she said:

“*The gate at the low-water culvert will remain closed to any person who is not a lawful traveller of the district.*”

She said it twice, which she had never done in eleven years.

“Cael,” she called, without turning round, “come and hear that word.”

And a man of about sixty came up out of the queue with his coat buttoned wrong, and Wenna read him the sentence a third time, and Cael Rundle said:

“That is the bridge word.”

“It is the bridge word,” Wenna said. “It is the same word off the same paper, and on the bridge it means a person in a book, and here it means a person in that book, and look at the size of that book.”

Ilyan looked at the size of that book.

Four inches thick, and the entries ran in four hands, and the last two hands were a hundred years dead, and the book was a lawful-traveler register with a district's authority on the cover and the ninth-bridge access condition copied into the head of it, word for word, including the words *of this span*, with *span* struck out and *district* written in over it, in a different ink, by somebody who had done a careful job.

Then Cael Rundle asked him whether he was the man on the paper.

Ilyan said that he was.

“And you are the one whose name is under it.”

“I am.”

“Then I will ask you it here and not in there,” said Cael Rundle, “because I am fifty-eight and I have paid this district a levy for thirty-one years and I have a right to be tired. Is that sheet yours.”

“It is an extract of a settlement in a charter of the ninth bridge, and the settlement was made by a court, and my name is on it as one of the grounds, and I asked for it to be put there so that it could be argued with. I did not send it to Halloway. Nobody on this bridge sent this to Halloway.”

“Then I will tell you what I think it means,” said Cael Rundle, “and you can tell me I am wrong. It means that in four districts, right now, this morning, there is a piece of paper that says a person who is not in a book is not a trespasser, and the district that wrote the sheet has taken that paper and written the opposite on it and nailed it to a gate, and there is not one person in that room who has read the sheet that knows the difference.”

“They know the difference,” Ilyan said. “Some of them do. Kett does. It is the phrase in the paper that has done this, and the phrase is two hundred years old and it is the ninth bridge's own word and it is in the access condition, and the access condition is what I could not get changed, and I have been in this city a month and I have already spent the only thing I had that anybody could have spent, and I am not going to stand in a lane and pretend it was somebody else's afternoon.”

“You are not standing in a lane,” said Cael Rundle, “anyway. You are standing at a gate.”

---

He was in the room at the eighth bell, and only because the bench's own rule required it, which is that a person whose name is on a produced document may be asked questions about the document.

The bench was four people and a clerk. The convenor was a woman of about sixty called Nell Ambrey and she had put the sanitation complaint in front of her own bench eleven weeks ago, because the low water had gone out for nine days in the tide month and the smell had got into the upper street and eleven houses had a doctor between them. She was not wrong about the smell. She was going to be proved right about the smell and about the sheet, and it was going to cost her the low water, and she sat at the head of the table with her hands flat on it, and she had the look of a person who has called a thing a problem and has got what she asked for.

The clerk was Corva Silt, thirty-one, Halloway's bench clerk, holder of the district's print authority, and she was the one who had printed the sheet, and she had done it lawfully, and she did not hide that.

“I will say it before anybody else does,” she said, when Ilyan had sat down. “I printed it. The bench's print authority runs to any public instrument of a district or of the Bench, and there is no fee and no permission and there is a form for it, and on the second day after the bridge hearing I filled the form in with a copy of a public instrument that had been made public on purpose for anybody to copy, and I put a copy on that board with four nails and no glass, and I sent a copy up the fen road to the ninth bridge on the fifth day, because there is a man four miles up that road whose name is at the bottom of it and it seemed to me that he ought to have a copy of what is being said in his own name. I did not ask the ninth bridge. I was not required to ask the ninth bridge. I did not know there was a ninth bridge, Mr Vester, I knew there was a word in a paper about a proper crossing.”

“You are right about the authority,” Ilyan said.

“I am aware,” said Corva Silt.

“Then tell them about the fifth paragraph.”

“The fifth paragraph is the one about the gate hand being paid,” Corva said. “Yes. I will tell them about the fifth paragraph.”

And the room went into the thing that rooms do, which is that a fact gets picked up and turned over until it can be put down in the shape somebody already wanted.

Kett took the whole of the second hour, and he was magnificent, and Ilyan watched it happen from a bench with no vote in it. Kett produced the ninth bridge's settlement as an extract, and produced the read-back in the guild's words, and produced the four-column sheet and the four hands in it, and said the two sentences he had said in Orison in a corridor a month ago, and said them better, and said them to a room that had a queue standing in the doorway because the water was let at the ninth bell.

And then Bryn Sallow, at the back, in the voice of a woman who has been at a gate for thirty years and has never once been asked to say anything at a bench, said the thing that turned the room.

“If it is the bridge's word,” she said, “then it is two hundred years old, and it means a person in a book, and a book is not a person.”

Nell Ambrey said, “Then the sheet is wrong.”

“No,” said Bryn Sallow. “The sheet is not wrong. The sheet is right. I am the one that is wrong, because I am the gate, and I have been the gate since I was fourteen, and I do not know a word on that paper, and the paper says I am not to open the gate, and it is four inches thick and it is a hundred years out of date, and nobody has told me that in thirty years, and if the paper is right then I have been shutting that gate against lawful travellers since I was fourteen years old, and if the paper is wrong then somebody has to tell me every morning which one it is, and they have not told me either, and I would like it written down that nobody told me.”

Kett had his hand on the table. He did not use it. He looked at it.

Nobody wrote it down for about nine seconds, and then a woman at the back with a slate under her arm asked whether it was entered, and Nell Ambrey said it was entered, and Corva Silt wrote it in the district's day book in the district's own hand with the hour against it.

*THE GATE HAND STATES THAT NO PERSON HAS TOLD HER WHICH OF TWO THINGS IS TRUE, FOR THIRTY YEARS, AND ASKS THAT IT BE WRITTEN DOWN.*

It is in a district day book in Halloway with the hour against it in the clerk's own hand. It is the first thing in this city that has been entered on the word of the person who does the work.

---

Wenna Tace asked the question, and she asked it at the ninth bell, and it was the only question anybody asked in the room that Ilyan could not answer in one sentence and had not tried to.

She stood up at the back with the barrow still outside the door and the smell still on her hands.

“I am not on your book,” she said, to Ilyan, “and I haul two hundred loads a year out of this district and up that bank, and I go through that gate thirty times a day with a load on, and there is no name of mine in that book at all, and the paper on the gate says the gate is shut to anybody who is not a lawful traveller of the district, and I want to know one thing. Am I lawful.”

“No,” Ilyan said. “You are not on the register. The charter says a person who is not entered in the lawful-traveler register is not a trespasser by that fact alone, and a keeper admits a person on foot or with a handcart or a wheelbarrow who offers no load and does not enter the load path, and you are on a load path with a load, so the sentence that helps you is the first one and not the second.”

“So the bridge’s own paper has a sentence in it that says I am not a trespasser,” she said.

“Yes.”

“And it has another sentence in it that says a person with a load is not admitted.”

“Yes.”

“Then the bridge is not a rule, it is two sentences pointing opposite ways, and everybody who has ever been in that room has been whichever one they wanted,” said Wenna Tace. “And the paper on that gate has one of them and not the other. And I want to know who took the other one out.”

Nobody answered her, and Ilyan understood in the middle of the lane, with a barrow's worth of the low water drying on his own boots, exactly what had happened to the ninth bridge's settlement, and that it was not a theft, and that nobody had to be cruel, and that a bench clerk with a lawful print authority and a sanitation complaint and a good hand had taken the half of a document that was easy to apply and had left the half that was not, and that no person in that room had ever needed the half she was asking about.

Kett's written instruction was produced and read, at his request, at the last bell, by Corva Silt, in full, with the protest in it: *ONE LEGIBLE RULE, FOR THE MAINTENANCE AND CROSSING OF SPANS AND GATES, WITHOUT A ROOF.* It took under a minute. He listened to it being read and did not object to the reading, and when it was over he said, “Enter that the second half of that instruction was not written until a sheet was already nailed to a gate,” and Corva Silt entered it, and then she closed the day book and said that the bench would sit again on the fourteenth and that anybody might attend.

Nobody in the room said the word *exception* once.

---

They walked back up the bank in the last of the light, four of them, and the tide had begun to come in, and by the time they were on the fen road the gate would be down and Weir Lane would be dry and the smell would begin again at the far end of it, where it always began.

Kett said, on the road, in the voice of a man reading out the parts of an argument that hurt:

“They will not strike the sheet.”

“No.”

“They will not strike the fourth paragraph.”

“No,” Ilyan said. “And they are right not to. The sheet is a lawful copy of a public instrument and it says what the instrument says, and the instrument does say that a person who is not on a register is not a trespasser by that fact alone, and the district has the right to read that as a reason to make a list, and the list they made is four inches thick and a hundred years out of date and nobody has told the woman at the gate that it exists.”

“Then what is to be done.”

“Somebody has to go and stand in their room.”

“Somebody has to go and stand in their room,” Kett agreed, “and it is going to be you, and I have known that since a corridor on the ninth bridge, and I am going to say the thing nobody is going to want said at the weighhouse next week. I asked for one legible rule. I was right that one legible rule was wanted. I was right that this city cannot afford nine of them, and I was right that a man with a lamp and a rule and no children is cheaper than a bench. And I did not ask for it to be *this* rule. I did not know there was going to be a rule to copy. That is my afternoon's work and it is nobody else's and I have brought it up here myself.”

The count had been taken at the seventh bell by two hands and one of them had been a district hand, and the unloaded period was at the twelfth day of twenty, and the bell on the ninth bridge had been rung at every change, and its pin had been looked at on the seventh morning by two people, one of whom had pulled the rope, and would be looked at again on the fourteenth.

---

Harrow read it out on the record, and Roke wrote it, and it is the reason Ilyan is standing in a district four miles down the fen road on the fourteenth day, which is where this ends and where the rest of it starts.

“*The attached record-holder, whose name is on the produced instrument, and who has no constituency, and who may be heard in the ninth-bridge matter only, is attached to a sitting of the Halloway bench on the fourteenth, as a witness to his own attached observation, and to nothing else.*”

“That is a punishment,” Ilyan said.

“It is a fact,” said Harrow, “and I am not going to call it a punishment, because a punishment has an end that somebody sets, and what is going to happen in that room has no end that anybody has set, and you are going to walk into a room where your name is the first thing anybody says and the second thing anybody wants to throw at the other side, and you are going to be the attached record-holder, and the attached record-holder may not be cited as a permit, a schedule, a levy, an acknowledgment, or a bearer of labor, and there is nobody standing behind you in that room with a bench.”

“I know,” Ilyan said. “I asked for it to be citable.”

“You asked for it to be citable in a corridor at two in the morning, and you were not frightened, and Sera Quill told you to be frightened, and four days later a boy on a bicycle brought you a sheet off a gate, and it is not the archive and there is nobody behind it and nobody ordered it.” Harrow put the tray down. “I am sorry about the corridor. I am not sorry about the rule. The rule is the only reason a court in this city will ever hear a complaint from a person the district can walk past, and if the price of that is that a man with a foreign name is a man you may point at in a poor district, then the price is what it is, and I would rather it were paid in one room at a time than in a city at once.”

Sera was at the foot of the steps, and she had the read-back under her arm and a slate of her own, and she said it before Ilyan could arrange his face.

“I will come with you,” she said. “And coming is not the same as being on your side. I am a licensed recordwright of a public record and I carry the terms of the ninth-bridge matter to any district that asks, and Halloway has not asked, and I am going anyway, and I am going to read the guild's words into a room that has never heard them, and where you are on the ground I will be correcting you in front of a copy. You will want me there. I am not coming because you want me there.”

“Then I will want you there and be wrong about the reason,” Ilyan said, and she wrote the time on her slate rather than looking at him, and then she said, “And you will be wrong about it, and I will let you stand there being wrong, and that is the whole of the service I have been retained for.”

And the tide came up under the road behind them, and the low water began, and the smell did not.

# Chapter 61: The Word in the Guild's Book

The basalt block was wet on the west face and dry on the east and the wind came off the water at an angle that made the wet side colder, and there were nineteen names on the docket board at the seventh bell on the twenty-ninth and eleven places in a morning, and a question put in writing by a clerk with a seal had taken one of the eleven and given the other two away.

Dessin Nahl wrote the two down on the back of the board's tally in her own hand, because a clerk does that without being asked, and she read it out to nobody because the two names belonged to people standing about nine feet away on a step and would have heard themselves.

“A water matter off the cut banks,” she said, “and a name matter that has been on this board since the eighteenth. The name matter has been on it eleven days. It is going to be on it eleven days more.”

Nobody argued with her about it, because she was right, and because the only thing in Orison that cannot be bought with money is a place on a board, and everybody who came up the hill that morning knew it, and two people had just found out what it cost.

The court had no furniture either. That is the second thing about a basalt court and the first one people forget: there is no throne and no dais and no roof and no bench, because a court that sat indoors would be a room and a room can be shut, and the block has been standing in the open air for four hundred years so that anybody may stand in front of it and be answered by somebody wearing a different colour from their own. So the places are places on the step, and the step is stone, and eleven people standing on a step for three hours is a thing that is done to knees.

Oren Sedge's guild lent four benches, which is more than the guild has done in a month, and the guild is out of money, and the benches came down off the fen road on a handcart with a rope round them, and Ilyan carried two of them up the last thirty yards because nobody else was going to and because a man with no place on a docket is a man who carries.

He set them down and put his weight wrong and had to sit on one of them for about nine seconds before he got up off it, and Tarin Keel, who had come up the road with him and had said nothing for the whole climb, looked at him and then looked at the ground and did not say anything at all, which was the correct amount.

---

Ansa Rell came out of the well of the court at the seventh bell and put a hand on the block and did not climb it, and the first thing she said was the reason.

“This is not a hearing anybody asked for on this ground,” she said. “It is a hearing on a question put in writing by the clerk of a district four miles from here, nineteen years in his post, who has answered a question of mine in the only way available to him, which is honestly. Mr Vale is not in the room. He is on a bridge with a seat that has five days left on it and a count that runs at every change with two hands under the deck, and he has sent a paper and not himself, and I have read the paper twice and the paper is better than his being here would have been, and I want that said by somebody before this morning is over.”

Nobody had to ask who had sent the paper. The question itself was on a slate on the step, in a district hand, nineteen years old and still legible.

> **WHETHER A DISTRICT MAY PAY A PERSON WHO HAS NO ADDRESS.**

“Rell,” said Bel Ordway, from the back, with a salt cart on the weighbridge and four minutes of an hour already gone, “before you go any further, that is a question about a wage, and you have said it twice, and I have been in this city for an hour and I do not have it yet.”

“It is a question about a category,” said Ansa Rell, “and I know that, and I put it where I could see it. If I had put it in the language of a wage somebody would have answered it in the language of a wage and we would have paid somebody and been finished. Enter what I have said, Mr Roke, and enter that I said it before anything else, because in about four years a clerk will read the record and think the court had not noticed.”

The System put the sitting in front of Ilyan while Roke was still writing it, and it was a record of a sitting and not a rule about the world.

**SITTING CONVENED AT THE FIRST VERDICT ON THE TWENTY-NINTH DAY, ON A QUESTION PUT IN WRITING BY THE CLERK OF THE NINTH-BRIDGE'S DISTRICT.**
**WHAT IS ASKED: WHETHER A DISTRICT MAY PAY A PERSON WHO HAS NO ADDRESS.**
**WHAT IS NOT ASKED: WHETHER ANY GATE SHOULD BE OPEN OR SHUT. WHETHER ANY RATE SHOULD BE SET. WHETHER THE NINTH-BRIDGE SETTLEMENT BINDS ANYWHERE IT HAS NOT BEEN ADOPTED.**
**THE COURT'S OWN NOTE IS ON THE FACE OF THE DOCKET AND IS NOT A RULING. THE SITTING IS TURNED TO A CATEGORY AND NOT TO A DOCUMENT.**

That last line was hers, not the docket's, and the System had rendered it as though it were the same kind of thing as the rest, and Ilyan noticed that and did not know what to do with the noticing.

---

The room found the category in the first nine minutes, which was faster than anybody expected, and it found it the way rooms find things, which is by somebody saying the word and then being unable to stop.

“The ward is where the poor people are,” said Ottla Prenn, who had brought draft eleven and had said this once already in this court and had asked for the distinction between a delegate's opinion and evidence to be entered, “and the ward has no name, and a rate cannot be paid to a place, and that is the whole of the wall.”

“Mr Prenn,” said Ansa Rell. “Who is in the ward.”

“That is what I have just said.”

“No, it is not,” said Ansa Rell. “You have said where they are poor. I have asked who they are. Those are different sentences and only one of them can go in a record, and it is the one that goes in a record that I am about to ask a witness to answer, and I have four witnesses and two of them have not come up the road and one of them is a builder with an apron on and lime in the seam of it, and I would rather have the room in trouble for ten minutes than be handed a number.”

Abel Kearn stood up, which nobody in Orison had seen him do before, and he said:

“I live in it.”

He said it in a builder's voice, flat, and it went round the step and stopped, and he went on.

“Convener, I have a trade. I am thirty-five and I have lime in my apron every day of the year and I have put up nine walls in a poor district four miles from here, and I have never once been hungry, and I have never once had a bucket come out of that pump, because I have a well. So I am not your poor people and I am not your carters and hauliers and gate hands either, because I do not have a cart and I have never once hauled anything that anybody was paying me for, and I have been on that bank about forty times in a month helping people up it with a rope.”

He put his hand flat on the stone.

“You have got two words for the same four hundred people and I am in neither of them, and I am telling you that as a fact about me and not as an argument, and if I am going to be in a category then somebody had better come and find out what the category is, because I have lived in it my whole life and I could not tell you.”

Ansa Rell said, “Enter that,” and Roke wrote it, and she did not say anything else about it, and Ilyan found that he had stopped being able to hear about nine of the ten things in the room, because a builder had just described a category in a sentence and a category is exactly the thing nobody in this city can make.

Then Cael Rundle got up, and he got up slowly, and he has a bad chest in the wet and the wind was coming off the water at an angle.

“Your ward,” he said, addressing Prenn and not the magistrate, which Ilyan noted and Rell noted and Roke entered, “is where the carters and the hauliers and the gate hands live, and I have said so in a written paper and I will say it standing up. And I am a cart owner and my cart goes up that hill, and I am not poor, and I have not been poor in nineteen years, and I pay a man to drive it. So you will forgive me if I point out that the carters are not the poor people, they are the people the poor people are paying.”

“You are both right,” said Ansa Rell, “and you have both said it twice, and neither of you is going to be able to say it in one sentence, and that is not a failure of the room. That is the thing. Mr Quill. Will you put the two words on the slate where they can be looked at, and will you read them out, and will you tell me whether they can be one word.”

“No,” said Sera Quill. “They cannot, and I can tell you why in a sentence and I am not going to like the sentence. They cannot be one word because one of them is about money and one of them is about work, and a person can be in this district with money and be out of it with work inside one morning, and I have watched it happen at a standpipe on a bank and it is not an argument, it is a queue.”

“It is a queue,” said Wenna Tace, from the step, and everybody turned, because she had come up the fen road with the count and had been standing at the back of it for about an hour and had said nothing, and nobody had asked her to say nothing and nobody had known she was there.

Halloway's reader had a slate under her arm and a barrow's handle in her hand and forty years of being the only person on a street who could read, and she said:

“Forty-one at the standpipe at the sixth bell on the twenty-eighth, and nine at the ninth, and nineteen by the seventh. It is not a queue of poor people and it is not a queue of workers. It is a queue at a pump, and everybody in it is somebody's neighbour, and the man who is carrying the water is not in the queue and is not one of them either, and he is paid by nobody, and his name is on my barrow.”

---

The court got to the word at the ninth bell, and it got to it the way it got to everything, which is by somebody else doing the work.

Ilyan had been asked four times by that point and had said four things and three of them had been the wrong shape. A category is made of words he does not have. He has about two hundred guild terms and they are the terms of a bridge trade — a deck, a load path, a seat, a joint, a bell, a rope, a pin, a wedge, a cart, a barrow, a rate, an hour, a count — and a category is made out of *district*, *resident*, *ratepayer*, *household*, *person affected*, *body corporate*, and he had none of the seven, and he had been in a city of instruments for five weeks and it was worse here than it had ever been anywhere, because in Halloway the words had been about a gate and in this room they were about a person.

He said so. He said it out loud, standing four feet inside the rail, in front of eleven benches' worth of people and a magistrate, and it cost him the only thing he had to spend.

“I cannot do this in the law's words,” he said. “I want it entered that I said that. I have about two hundred words and they are a bridge's words and this is a hearing about a kind of person, and I do not know what your words mean when they are put next to each other, and I have known that for five weeks and I have been in three rooms today where the words were the whole of the subject, and I am telling the room instead of standing there finding out.”

“It is entered,” said Roke.

“Thank you. It is not an excuse and I am not going to give it as one. I am going to do the only thing I can do, which is look in a book.”

“You may look in any book in this court,” said Ansa Rell, “and Mr Quill will find you one in a moment, and I would like to say something first, because I have watched nine benches do this to four men in a month and I am not going to do it to a fifth. A guild's book is not evidence. A guild's book is a trade's own working vocabulary and it will say what a word means to the men who use it and not one word about what it means to a bench. If you find something in it that helps, it is a witness, and it has to be brought here and be asked about, and somebody who is not you has to be right about it before it is worth anything.”

“I understand,” said Ilyan. “I have been the man in the room who is right about a number and wrong about the man for a month. I would rather be brought here than be believed.”

Sera found it in about four minutes, and it took four minutes because a guild keeps its terms the way a ship keeps its charts, in a case, on a shelf, under weights, and the ninth bridge's case had come down the fen road on a handcart with the benches because the benches had to sit somewhere and the case was under the benches.

It is a book about nine feet long and two inches thick and it opens anywhere, and every page has four columns, and the columns are a word, and what the word is for, and where it came from, and when a bridgekeeper last complained about it. It is the ninth bridge's book and it is Oren Sedge's book and it is in a guild's words, and it is the only book in this city that anybody has ever written down what the words *mean*.

And the word was in the third column from the end, under a heading Ilyan could read without difficulty because the heading was a bridge heading: *DUTIES CARRIED, NOT DISCHARGED.* And in the third column, with a star against it, and the star meant somebody had complained about it:

> **WARD. NOT A PLACE. THE WORK A DISTRICT OWES AND HAS NOT GOT ROUND TO. A GUILD'S WORD, OLD, FROM BEFORE THE BOOK IS ANYBODY'S GRANDMOTHER'S. IT MEANS A THING YOU DO, NOT A THING YOU LIVE IN.**

He read it out in the guild's way, because the guild's way was the only way he had, and it went into a basalt court like a stone into water.

Nobody spoke for about four seconds, which in this court was a long time.

Then Ansa Rell said, “Mr Quill. Is that the court’s word or is that the guild’s word.”

“It is the guild's,” said Sera. “It is in the trade's own vocabulary with a complaint against it and a date, and it is a witness and not a finding, and the court is right that it has to be asked about.”

“Then it has to be asked about,” said Ansa Rell, “and it has to be asked about by somebody who is not him, because he found it and I would rather it came back from a bench.”

They asked three benches. Odile the court translator found it inside a minute, in a common-record of the city that is a hundred and ninety years old and is called the standing of the wards, and it uses the word in the second sense, in a clause about unperformed duties, and it lists eleven wards and five duties each and then it says which of the fifty-five have been done and by whom.

Eleven of fifty-five.

The traveller book at Halloway, which is four inches thick and a hundred years out of date and has a front leaf in a different hand from the rest, says the same thing in the same way, in a district's own book, about one ward, with five duties on it, and it names the year and it names the last man who did any of them, and he has been dead thirty-one years.

And Hanne Kolm's sworn volume, which four miles up the fen road has a column in it, and which came up the hill the day before on a strap with a woman of sixty-one's hand on the strap, and which the court had asked to see and had not expected to get: it does not use the word. It is the only document in this case that does not use the word, and the reason is that she is a landlord of a lower road and the duties of a lower road are the ones she has actually done, and they are not called a ward anywhere on that road by anybody who lives on it.

---

The room came apart at the ninth bell, in the way rooms do, into four conversations at once, and Ilyan sat down on one of the guild's benches because his left leg had stopped taking instructions somewhere around the eighth bell and he was not going to find out in front of a magistrate when.

Here is what it came apart into, and all four of it mattered, and he could not follow three of the four in the words they were said in.

Harrow said the finding was about the ninth bridge and not about the wards, and that the court had no power over the wards and had said so on the face of a field once already, and that the court had no power over them now either and was going to say so again on the face of this record, and that it was going to be the second time and would be entered as the second time.

Bel Ordway said that if a ward were a duty and not a place then the city had been paying for a duty for a hundred and fifty years, and asked out loud, in front of everybody, how much, and nobody could answer him, and he said *then somebody who can, please*.

Corva Silt, who had come up the fen road on the third day after the ruling to answer a question she had not been asked and had been in the room for two hours without speaking, said one sentence from the back.

“This district has a duty called the low-water road,” she said, “and nobody has ever done it, and the person who does it is a woman at a gate.”

And nobody picked that up, because Rell had already begun to say the thing she had come to say, which was that the court could not build a remedy out of a definition at ten bells at night with no money and would not try.

---

The finding, when she read it out, was not a finding of law and Rell said so four times in ninety seconds.

It was: that the word *ward* is used in this city's own records in two senses, and that the sense in which a rate may be paid to a ward is the sense in which a ward is a piece of work and not a place; that in the standing of the wards, one hundred and ninety years old, eleven of fifty-five such duties have been performed and forty-four have not; that the district of Halloway has five such duties and has performed none of them, and that the one everybody has been arguing about is performed daily, by a person, at a gate, on the low water, for nothing; and that *a duty with nobody in it is not a duty nobody does*, and that this court has no power to make a seat, a name, or a body out of a piece of work, and is not going to try to do it on a step in the open air in one afternoon.

And then she read the last part, and she read it slowly, and she looked at Ilyan while she read it.

“Which is the reason this room is in trouble,” she said, “and it is not the reason the court gave for the trouble, and I want both of those entered. A rate may be paid to a ward only if the ward is a body, and the ward is not a body because the ward is not a person and never was, and a thing that is a duty cannot be paid anything, it can only be given work. The word that has let four hundred people be paid at a gate for thirty years is a word for a job. And a job is done by whoever is standing nearest. That is the whole of the mechanism and it is not a conspiracy and there is no man in it. When a rule says *the ward pays*, the court has to ask the ward to send a name, and there is no name, and the name is not found, and in the gap the work is done, because the work does not wait. It has been done at that gate every tide for thirty years out of a rate that nobody wrote down.”

She put the slate down.

“That is a worse answer than a wall,” she said. “A wall you can go round in a fortnight.”

---

Ansa Rell recessed at the tenth bell, and the court stood in the open air with nobody in it and the gutter channel in the step worn by four hundred years of people walking out of it not knowing what they had been in, and the bench of four stayed on the step because the benches were guild property and the guild was out of money and the guild's rule was that a thing lent is a thing left.

She said what she would do in the morning and she said it to the step and not to Ilyan, and Ilyan was grateful for that in a way he had not expected to be.

“There will be two texts on this table tomorrow at the fourth bell,” she said. “One of them I can write. It is a good text and it is a fair text and it can be written in an afternoon by any competent person in this city, including me, and it will close a gate, and it will be payable, and it will not unhouse anybody. I have had it in my bag since the eleventh day and I have not put it on a table because I have been waiting for somebody to make me.”

“And the other one,” said Bel Ordway.

“The other one does not exist,” said Ansa Rell. “Nobody has written it, in this city or in any district I have ever been in, and the reason is the second note on the docket and you have all read it: it is a piece of paper a person can swear to in a room with no address in it. The first text is that paper. The second text is not a paper. It is a set of people who will put their names to something, and the reason it does not exist is not that it is difficult. It is that every person who would have to put a name to it can be told, correctly, what it would cost them, and in four benches in nineteen years all of them have been able to work that out.”

She looked at the board with the two names taken off it.

“I am going to say the other thing now and then I am going to go home. Both of these texts cost money. I want that said tonight, in daylight, by me, before anybody in this room starts telling themselves that one of them is free, because the honest position is that neither of them is free and the difference between them is not money. One of them can be read by a person before they have to live inside it. The other one can be refused by a person who can read it. Neither of those is a small thing and only one of them is new.”

Nobody said anything, and she went down the step, and the room began the four conversations again, and by the time Ilyan got his weight onto his right leg and stood up off a guild's bench, the light on the wet west face of the block had gone from grey to a colour he had no word for.

Then Odile, the court translator, said his name, and he turned round, and she was holding the ninth bridge's book in both hands and had it open at the third column from the end.

“Mr Vester,” she said. “I want to say something about how you found that, and then I am going to let it go and I am never going to raise it again.”

“All right.”

“You did not look it up because you knew the word,” she said. “You looked it up because you did not know the word, and then you read down a page you did not understand until you got to a heading you did. A page of a man's trade vocabulary is mostly nothing to a man from another trade. It is not a book anybody would open. It was open on a bench, upside down, under four benches, in the rain, for about four hours, with the pages bent, because it was a weight.”

“I did not know that was where it was going to be,” Ilyan said.

“No,” said Odile. “Nobody does. That is why it worked and it is not a method and there is nothing to teach anybody.”

She put the book down on the step and squared it, so that the pages it had been bent in were closed.

“And I want to say the other thing, and I have thought about it for an hour and I am going to say it once. Your licence holder told this court twenty minutes ago that she cannot tell you what a category is, and she is right and she is the only one here who is. You told this court that you could not do the work and named the reason, and the room wrote it down. In my trade, in eleven years, that has happened four times, and three of those times it was a man who wanted somebody else to do it, and one of those times it was a man who had run out of words. I do not know which you are and I have decided it is not my trade, and I would like to be recorded that I said so, because in about four years somebody will ask me.”

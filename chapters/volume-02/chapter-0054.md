# Chapter 54: The Name on the Bottom of the Paper

At the fifth bell the sixth-bell queue was already there, because the fifth-bell queue is the sixth-bell queue forty minutes early and everybody knows it except the people counting it, and Ilyan counted it twice, at the fifth and again at the sixth, and got forty-four and forty-four, and the cart count since the sheet went up was twelve, and the twelve were district work and stone for yards and not one of them was rubbish.

The pump's second shift came on at the eighth bell and a boy of fifteen worked the handle and the water came up at the rate it comes up, which is a hand's depth in a quarter of an hour into a stone sink with a lip on it, and the smell of the low water does not come off the water. It comes off the four hundred loads that are still on the flat below the gate because the carts that would have carried them out have been going back up a hill since the second day.

Wenna Tace came down the bank with the barrow at the sixth and went along the flat to the low place and filled it, and the way she filled it was the way you do a thing you have done nine hundred times, and she did not hurry, and the barrow's iron tyres made a noise on the stone that carried about four hundred yards because the flat is stone and the bank is mud.

Bryn Sallow was at the gate.

There is not much to say about a woman standing at a gate for four hours, so Ilyan watched her do it, and after a while he asked her a question, and the question turned out to be the only useful thing he did all day.

“You ring that at the tide.”

“Every tide,” said Bryn Sallow. “And every time the gate goes down and every time it comes up. That is a handbell. It is a handbell on a chain.”

“Right.”

“It is not like your bridge,” she said. “I heard your man say it on the twelfth, the one about a bell and a wheel and a pin and a post. I have been ringing a thing for thirty years and this thing is a bell a man bought in a market, and it is attached to me, and when I stop ringing it there is no bell, and nobody on this bank is watching a wheel.” She rang it, once, at nothing in particular, and let it die. “That is the whole of the difference between my machine and his and I would not be telling you at a bench.”

“All right,” Ilyan said, and wrote it down, and it is the shortest entry in his hand from this whole business.

---

Cael Rundle moved it at the seventh bell, and he moved it well, and he moved it before anybody had the morning warm.

He did not move to strike the sheet on the gate. Everybody in the room had been expecting that and he was not a man who wasted a morning on the thing everybody expected.

He moved like this: that the ninth-bridge settlement, being a public instrument entered as a precedent witness, had now been shown to produce an instrument in this district which shut a gate to a district's own contractor and to a woman with a barrow who had done the work of a cart for eleven years; that the district had not asked for it; that the person whose name was on the precedent was in the room; and that he asked the bench to enter that he was not asking to have the sheet destroyed, because destroying the sheet would put the water on Weir Lane, and he was asking to have the ninth-bridge settlement withdrawn from use in this district, meaning the sheet, meaning H-9, meaning anything in Halloway that rested on a sentence of it.

He said, and he said it without any heat at all:

“I do not want the water. I want the paper gone. I am fifty-eight and I have paid this district a levy for thirty-one years and I have got a bad chest in the wet and I would like to walk to the pump without reading a notice about a word, and if a paper from four miles up the road can do this to a lane in three days then it can do it to this bench, and the fix is not to argue about one gate. The fix is to take the paper out.”

“You have not read it,” said Kett.

“I have read the fourth paragraph of it four times because it is nailed to a board in the rain and I have to stand in front of it every day of my life,” said Cael Rundle, “and I have had somebody read me the rest of it, twice, and I understand it, and I have understood it since the twelfth day, and I am not the problem here. Mr Quill is the problem here. The recordwright understands it.”

Sera did not answer, which Ilyan had learned over a month was how she answered.

Corva Silt entered the motion. It is in the day book for the fifteenth: *C. RUNDLE, RATEPAYER, WEIR LANE, MOVES THAT THE NINTH-BRIDGE SETTLEMENT BE WITHDRAWN FROM USE IN THIS DISTRICT, ON THE GROUND OF ITS PRODUCTION OF INSTRUMENT H-9, AND STATES THAT HE IS NOT ASKING FOR THE DESTRUCTION OF H-9 AND THAT HIS HOUSE AND HIS CHEST ARE ON WEIR LANE.*

And then the bench turned, because it had to, because a motion has to go somewhere, and it went to the man whose name was at the bottom of the paper.

---

The question was fair. Ilyan wants that on the record and he wants it in these words, because he has been in this city a month and he has learned that the difference between a person who is being made to answer and a person who is being punished is about four seconds of silence before the question is asked.

“Mr Vester,” said Nell Ambrey. “You are the attached record-holder and the name on the precedent, and Mr Rundle has asked a question and it is a fair one, and I will not have it softened, and I will not have it sharpened. Will you answer it before this bench or would you rather be answered to later in the day.”

“Before,” Ilyan said. “Now.”

“Then here it is in my words and in his and I would like Corva to write his and I will read mine.” She looked down at the slate she had brought. “Your own settlement says, in words I have had read to me twice because I do not read law and I am not ashamed of that, that a person who is not entered in the lawful-traveler register is not a trespasser because of it alone. My gate is shut to every person who is not in a book. Is my gate unlawful by your paper.”

“Yes,” said Cael Rundle, before Ilyan had got anything out. “That is the question. I have got a lane of forty houses and a boy of nine who watched a load of district sand go back up a hill because a woman he has known his whole life cannot read a clause, and I am asking a man whether a rule he made citable has made that lawful.”

And then he was finished, and the room waited, and Ilyan had about nine seconds in which he understood the whole of it, which is that the question was fair, that it was the fairest question anybody had asked him since he crossed, and that he did not have the answer, and that the answer was in a charter under an amended span propped over a channel four miles up the road and that the person who could read it was standing in the room and had said on the first day that she was not his witness.

“I cannot answer it,” he said.

“Do not do that,” said Cael Rundle. “Do not do the thing where you will not read it and it becomes a virtue. I have watched you do that twice.”

“I have not done that,” Ilyan said. “I am going to tell you what I can and what I cannot, in the order it happened, and you will be able to tell which is which and you may check me on all of it, and I would rather be checked than believed.

“What I can tell you is that I have read your gate, your culvert road, your traveller book, your pay book, and the sheet on the board, and I have read the first paragraph of H-9 and I have had the third clause read to me twice, and I have read a great many numbers, and I will answer anything in this room that is a number or a distance or a load. What I cannot tell you is whether a district's gate is within or outside the ninth-bridge access condition, and I am telling you that in front of sixty people in a room where I have no standing and no constituency and no advocate, because a court in this city entered that I cannot read instruments, and it is on the record, and the last thing I want in this city is a room where a man is careful and quiet.

“And I am not going to be careful and quiet, so let me be exact about why I cannot answer. It is not that I am frightened. It is that the sentence you have quoted is about a span. It has a deck in it and a load path in it and a keeper in it and a bell in it. Your gate has none of those things. So the sentence may reach your gate and it may not, and the difference is in the machinery of a legal instrument and not in the plain sense of the words, and I do not have the machinery, and nobody in this room has it, and there are about nine words of that machinery in the whole of the district and they are in a town four miles up the road.”

“Then go and get them,” said Cael Rundle.

“Yes,” Ilyan said. “And here is the cost of that and I am going to put it on the table before anybody finds it out and puts it there for me, because that is what I have learned to do and I have learned it from a woman who makes me correct her in front of a copy.

“If somebody goes up that road and comes back with the machinery, that is four hours there and four hours back, or one of the two hours we spend waiting for an answer we cannot get in this room, and the day does not stop for it. And nobody in this room can read it. And it is four miles up the road where there is somebody who can. And that is an hour, and the hour is not mine to spend and it is not yours to keep, because while four of us sit here waiting for a sentence, the sixth-bell queue is standing on the bank and the four hundred loads are on the flat, and the gate is worked by one woman who cannot sit down, because if she sits down she is not at the gate, and there is no line in this district on which her hour can be paid. The line that pays an hour like that is a term of the paper you have asked me to withdraw, and you are right, and that is the part that is hardest to write down.”

He stopped, and about eleven people looked at the floor, which is the sign of a room agreeing with something and not wanting to be counted.

“You have named a cost,” said Nell Ambrey. “Corva, what does the cost come to in hours.”

Bryn Sallow answered it herself, from the gate end of the room, without being asked and without moving.

“An hour,” she said. “If the question goes up the road it is an hour here, from now, before anybody can say one word about the gate, and I stand it. I have stood it before. It is not the bench’s fault and it is not the carter’s fault and I would like it written down that the hour is a door I have to hold, because the tide is in and the gate is down and the pipe is running and if the gate is not on its hook at the turn then the low water comes up into the lane and does not go back till the third bell.”

“Write it,” said Nell Ambrey.

Corva Silt wrote it, and read it back, and then stopped with her pen on the page.

“Convener,” she said, “there is no line to put that hour on.”

“There is no line,” said Nell Ambrey.

“Then it is not bought,” said Corva Silt, “and I have entered a day in this district's book with a gate hand in it and no rate against it, and the rate against it is in a charter four miles away that the gate hand cannot read and that we are being asked to withdraw.”

She entered it, in the district's own hand, with the hour, and it went into the book in the form that Ilyan had first seen in a different room on a different bridge, which is the form of an honest office writing down the true reason in its own name with the time on it: **THE OFFICE HAS NO LINE.**

Nobody spoke for about a minute.

Then Cael Rundle said, and he said it to Ilyan and not to the bench, and he did not soften it:

“You are not the price of this. I want that said in this room because I have read what your advocate said on a bridge about that and I do not trust it, and I will not be one of the people who puts a foreign man on a document and then tells the lane it was his fault. The paper would have come. You did not send it. Nobody has to be cruel for a paper to arrive; you said it yourself on the second day. I am not your punishment and you are not my excuse. What you are is the reason I have got to be careful now, because if a bridge in Orison can do this by accident then a man in a lane can be shut by one, and I have got a boy of nine who watched it happen and I would like him to grow up in a lane where the answer to a paper is a person who comes down the hill. That is all you are to me. I am not going to call you a cause, because I do not believe in causes at a bench, and I have got a bad chest.”

“All right,” Ilyan said.

“And this,” Cael Rundle said, and pulled the sheet out of his coat, “is your precedent, which I have had read to me twice, and I am going to say one sentence with it and then I am finished.

“Your paper says a person not on a register is not a trespasser because of it alone. My daughter is fourteen and she is not on that book and she was not born in this district and she has never been in this lane and she walks to the pump every morning and has done since she was six. And on the fourteenth day, because of your paper, a carter told her, in front of a child, that his load was not going up the hill for a girl who is not in a book. And she came home and asked me whether she was a trespasser. And I said no. And I am fifty-eight, I said no, and I could not make her believe me, and that is the whole of what I came down here for and I have been three hours being clever about it.”

The room was extremely quiet.

Ilyan said, “I am sorry,” and then stopped, and Corva Silt's pen stopped, and he said, “No. I am not going to take that back, and I am not going to leave it, either. I am sorry is a thing a man says when a thing is done to him. I am going to say the other thing and then I am going to stop talking.

“The settlement says a person not on a register is not a trespasser by that fact alone, and it also says a keeper admits a person on foot, or with a handcart, or with a wheelbarrow, who offers no load and does not enter the load path. Those are two sentences and they point in different directions, and I have said that in this room before and a woman with a barrow said it better than I did. And I have been in this city a month and I have not once been in a room where a person asked me to explain those two sentences to a fourteen-year-old, and it turns out that is what a precedent is for. I have been talking about districts. There was a girl.”

Nobody responded to that, and it was correct that nobody responded to it, and Ilyan understood that a room does not have to answer you in order to have heard you and that being heard in a language nobody else is speaking is not the same as being forgiven.

---

The answer came at the ninth bell and it cost an hour, because the bridge was propped and unloaded and the count was running with two hands under the deck and a bell rung at every change, and what came down the fen road at the fourth bell was a sheet in Ilsa Bray's hand, brought by the boy of fifteen who worked the pump handle, because the boy would not leave the pump and said so.

It was four lines, and it had the guild's letters on it, and it had been read off the frame of a bridge in a keeper's words, and Sera read it out standing up:

> **THE ACCESS CONDITION OF THIS SPAN IS ABOUT THIS SPAN. A SPAN HAS A DECK AND A LOAD PATH AND A KEEPER. A GATE ON A CULVERT HAS NONE OF THOSE THINGS, AND THE SENTENCE IS ABOUT A PERSON AND NOT ABOUT A LIST, AND A DISTRICT MAY MAKE A LIST OF ITS OWN.**
> **I HAVE READ THAT FOUR TIMES AND I HAVE WRITTEN IT ON THIS.**
> **THE NINTH BRIDGE'S OWN COUNT, WRITTEN BY ITS OWN COUNTER: A PERSON WHO IS NOT ON A REGISTER IS NOT A TRESPASSER BECAUSE OF IT. THAT IS THE SENTENCE. THE OTHER SENTENCE IS ABOUT LOADS.**
> **I AM NOT A LAWYER AND I COUNT LOADS.**

Four lines, off a bridge, in a guild hand, and it is the answer, and the answer is that the district was right all along, and that a hundred and sixty years of a penal road book was right, and that nobody in this room needed a court for any of it.

Cael Rundle read it twice.

“She has written on a bridge that my gate is lawful,” he said.

“She has written that a gate is not a span,” said Sera. “Which is a different sentence and it is the one that is on the paper. And she has written it four times because she has been asked to write things down by hand since she was fourteen and she does not trust a thing written once. And Mr Vester did not write any of it and could not read it when it was written and I have never seen him read it, and I am not going to let anybody in this room believe otherwise.”

“Then withdraw the motion,” said Nell Ambrey.

“Convener,” said Cael Rundle. He was standing. He had not sat down for three hours. “No. I am not withdrawing it, and I am not withdrawing it because of that sheet, and I want it in the book in these words, because if I withdraw it now I lose the only thing I came down here for.

“The ninth bridge's paper is not lawful here. I am not asking any more. I am asking for one thing and it is not about the ninth bridge at all and it has taken me three days to be able to say it without shouting. I am asking for a line in this district's book on which an hour of work can be paid to a person by name. Not a ward. Not a rate out of a charter. A line. There are nine hundred people in this district who have done work that this district has never paid anybody for, and the ninth bridge found that out in a month and fixed it with a sentence you can say out loud on an approach, and you have the same problem and no sentence, and the paper on the gate is not going to write it for you, and neither am I, and neither is he.”

“That is a bench matter,” said Nell Ambrey.

“It is a bench matter,” Cael Rundle said, “and I am a ratepayer of this bench and I have put it in front of you and I will take it away if you enter that it is a bench matter and name a date. If you do not name a date I will take it up every day until you do.”

Corva Silt wrote it in the book. Nell Ambrey named the third day of next week. And then the bench did the thing that Ilyan had been waiting an hour to see whether it would do, and it did it in about nine seconds, and it was Corva Silt's doing and not the convenor's, and it was the first time in this whole business that a form went backwards because a clerk had thought about it.

She struck out clause two of H-9.

“I am striking clause two of my own instrument,” she said, “because clause two says no load passes the gate, and that is the same sentence as a bridge's, and it is why the district's own sand went up the hill, and if I printed that again I would be the clerk who put a bridge in a district's road, and I am not going to be that.”

“The bench will not have you do that alone,” said Nell Ambrey.

“The bench can have it minuted on this afternoon's entry, and the bench can do it properly on the third day of next week, and the gate will be shut to loads for one more day and a half and then it will not, and I would rather be shouted at on the third of next week than have it be mine,” said Corva Silt. “Enter the reason. *Because the clerk of it has read the terms of an instrument she printed and does not have the terms of the road she lives on.*”

“It is in,” said Corva Silt, and entered it.

And the room emptied into a lane at the last bell, because the pump's second shift comes on at the eighth and the queue at the ninth was nine, and the first shift comes on again at the fifth and the queue at the sixth will be forty-four again, and there is no conversation in this world that outlasts a shift on a hand pump.

At the gate, on the way out, Bryn Sallow rang the handbell once, at nothing, to clear the lane of two hundred people who had to get home before the water came back up, and the sound went out flat across the flat and off the fen road, and Ilyan stood and listened to it, and it was the same note as the note on the ninth bridge, and neither of them was attached to anything.

---

They walked up the bank in the dark and Kett said, to the road, “That is the request going up, and you know it is.”

“Yes,” Ilyan said.

“It is not about your precedent. It is about whether a rule a court made for one span can be picked up by a district that never adopted it, and it is going to be asked at the weighhouse inside a week, and the reason it is going to be asked is that four districts have a man with a precedent and one of them has a rule made of a copy, and the difference between those two things is a question that somebody with a seal is going to have to answer, and today the answer was that a gate is not a span and a sentence about a person is not a sentence about a list.” He was quiet for a moment. “I have spent nineteen years wanting one legible rule and I have just watched a ratepayer ask for a line in a book, and it was the better argument, and he is not a lawyer, and he is the only person in three days who has said anything that is not about a bridge.”

Sera said, “You are going to be produced at the weighhouse.”

“I know.”

“You are going to be produced and you are going to be asked what a precedent is, by somebody with a seal who wants a good answer, and the good answer is a word and the true answer is a hallway.” She had the four lines in her hand and she did not fold them. “I am going to carry the terms to the weighhouse because it is the third of the four things my license says I do, and I am going to say in a room full of clerks that on the fifteenth day a district's own bench declined to withdraw a ratepayer's motion, and that a clerk struck out her own clause for a reason she put in the book in her own hand, and that in four days a bench in a poor district has done more honest administration than I have seen in this city in a month, and I am going to say that to a magistrate and it will not help the district and it will help the record.”

“It will be the first time anybody has said anything good about Halloway in the history of this case,” Kett said.

“It is also the first time anybody has said anything good about it in the history of that district,” said Sera, “which is the same sentence with a different word in it, and one of them is true and the other one is a thing we are about to be saying to a magistrate, and I would like somebody to notice which.”

# Chapter 62: Payable, and Smaller Than the Truth

Bel Ordway came up the hill at the fourth bell on the thirtieth with a salt cart on the weighbridge and a folded sheet under his arm, and he had not been to a court in nineteen years and it showed in the way he held the sheet, which is the way a man holds a thing he wrote himself and is not sure of.

He put it on the stone of the step face up, squared it with two fingers, and stepped back from it, and everybody in the room understood that they were being shown something by a man who wanted them to look at it before he said anything about it.

> **FORM L-3. LOW-WATER GATE, HALLOWAY. SANITATION INSTRUMENT, NUMBERED, PUBLISHED BY THE BENCH OF HALLOWAY AT THE FOURTH BELL, THE THIRTIETH DAY.**
>
> **ONE. THE GATE AT THE LOW-WATER CULVERT IS CLOSED TO LOADS IN THE DIRECTION OF THIS DISTRICT AT ALL TIMES, AND CLOSED TO LOADS FROM THE LOW WATER WHILE THE TIDE IS IN. IT IS OPEN TO LOADS OUT OF THE DISTRICT WHILE THE TIDE IS OUT.**
>
> **TWO. THE GATE IS WORKED BY THE GATE HAND AND BY NO OTHER PERSON.**
>
> **THREE. FOUR HANDS AT THE GATE, FOUR HOURS A DAY FROM THE FIFTH BELL, BY NAME, ON A DAY-BOOK LINE, IN CHALK, AT THIS DISTRICT'S OWN RATE, PAID OUT OF THIS DISTRICT'S ROAD FUNDS.**
>
> **FOUR. A WATER RATE IS SET BY THE BENCH OF THIS DISTRICT, TO BE CHARGED AND COLLECTED AT THE STANDPIPE AND NOT AT ANY DOOR, AND THE YIELD OF IT SHALL BE ENTERED IN THE DAY BOOK AGAINST THE FOUR HANDS AND NOT AGAINST ANY OTHER LINE.**
>
> **FIVE. THE FOURTH PARAGRAPH OF ANY PRINTED INSTRUMENT IN WHICH THE WORD *LAWFUL TRAVELLER* MEANS A PERSON ENTERED IN A BOOK DOES NOT APPLY TO THIS GATE, AND THE WORD *TRAVELLER* IS NOT USED IN THIS INSTRUMENT AT ALL.**
>
> **SIX. THE GATE SHALL BE OPENED AT ALL HOURS FOR A PERSON ON FOOT, OR WITH A HANDCART, OR WITH A WHEELBARROW, WHO OFFERS NO LOAD AND DOES NOT ENTER THE LOAD PATH. THIS IS NOT A FAVOUR AND MAY NOT BE REFUSED.**
>
> **SEVEN. THE SANITATION COMPLAINT ENTERED BY THE CONVENOR OF THIS BENCH ELEVEN WEEKS AGO IS NOT DISCHARGED AND IS PRESERVED, AND THIS INSTRUMENT IS NOT AN ANSWER TO IT.**
>
> **EIGHT. THIS INSTRUMENT EXPIRES AT THE THIRD SITTING OF NEXT QUARTER UNLESS RENEWED BY THE BENCH, AND EXPIRY IS NOT REFUSAL AND ENDS NO WAGE ALREADY ENTERED.**

Eight clauses on two sheets, folded once, in a weighhouse hand, and the paper was not the district's letter paper, and Ilyan found that out later and it was the smallest true thing in the morning.

Ansa Rell read it twice and did not comment, and handed it to Odile, and Odile read it out in the way a person reads a thing out when there are people present who have never read an instrument in their lives, which is slowly, and twice for the numbers, and once for the words that do the damage.

Nobody said anything for a while, and then Abel Kearn said, “That is a good instrument.”

“Yes,” said Ansa Rell. “It is. Enter that the court has been asked to choose between two texts and one of them is already on the step and one of them does not exist, and enter that the first of those is a good instrument, because in my experience the room always wants the bad one to be the alternative, and it never is.”

---

The advocate for it was Bel Ordway, and he had not asked to be, and he told the room so, which took about a minute of the morning and got him the rest of it.

“I will tell you why I wrote it and I will tell you first, before the argument, because I have got a cart on a scale and I am not going to pretend to be disinterested in a room where a cart is worth talking about. I wrote it yesterday on the back of a weight receipt at the weighhouse, and it took me an hour and a half, and I have filled in ten thousand forms in nineteen years and I am a competent person at this and I have never once been more competent.

“My district has a gate four miles from here on a road that goes down to a low water. That road is shut. Eleven carts have turned back off it in a month and four loads of that district's own sand went up a hill in front of eighty people and did not come back down. I have lost two seasons of hauling out of that low water in a month and I have not told anybody the price of it, and the price of it is mine, and I have been sitting on this step for two days telling myself I minded about the gate.

“I minded about the gate on the fourteenth day when my cart turned round. I have not minded about it once since. I minded about it because it was expensive.

“And what I have found in three days is that nobody four miles down that road is going to be able to read what happened to them, ever, by anybody, and that if you are one of the four hundred people standing on a flat waiting for the water to go out, you are going to be governed from this day forward by a sheet somebody copied out of a bridge charter, and you are not going to be able to find out what it says, and you are not going to be able to say no to it, and the only person in this city who is going to be able to say no to anything is a man who cannot read it.

“So I wrote a rule that you can read. One sheet, two sheets, eight clauses, and every one of them in words a person uses. I can read it and I have never had a day of schooling past the ninth year and I have read it. It closes the gate to loads in your direction at all times, which is right, and it opens the gate to loads out of the district when the tide is out, which is what a gate on a low water is for and which nothing in this city has said in writing until this morning. It says a person on foot goes through, and says it is not a favour, and says it may not be refused, and my clerk has entered a note beside it that says the first draft said *may* and that a man of forty-five can be refused at six in the morning by a tired person and a shut gate, and that *may not* is the whole of clause six and the rest of clause six is decoration.”

He put his hand on the stone.

“Everybody in this room is going to tell me it is small. It is small. It is the smallest thing anybody has put on a step in this case and I have put my name at the bottom of it and I would like it read out.”

Odile read the last line, which was underneath clause eight and separated from it by about an inch of paper, and which was not a clause and was not numbered.

> **BEL ORDWAY, CONVENER, WEIGHBRIDGE BENCH. NOT A SOLICITOR AND NOT A DRAFTER AND NOT ASKED BY THE COURT. I WROTE THIS MYSELF AND IF IT IS WRONG IT IS MINE.**

“He put his name under it,” said Harrow, from the back, “and that is a thing this court has watched nine men decline to do.”

“It is a thing I have watched four hundred people decline to do for four days,” said Ordway. “It is cheaper than a cart.”

---

Cael Rundle seconded it, and his reason was the water, and he said so in the plainest sentence anybody said all day, and then refused to soften it.

“I am a ratepayer of Weir Lane,” he said. “Forty of us pay a shilling a week off the top of a hovel, and I pay it, and the man who takes it has never in his life written a name on anything, and I moved in this court on the fifteenth day that the ninth-bridge paper be taken out of use in a district four miles away, and I was refused, and I did not withdraw the motion, and I asked for a line in this district's book on which an hour of work could be paid to a person by name, and I was given a date, and I have been waiting for the date.

“This is the date. Clause three is the line. It is a line, it is in chalk, it is by name, and the money for it comes out of the water, and I have known that since the sixteenth day when a woman with a barrow said it out loud in front of sixty people, and I said then that I did not believe her, and I was wrong about that and I have come four miles up a road to say so.

“I am not going to pretend that I mind. Weir Lane's water is going to cost something. It is the first time in nineteen years that anybody in this district has paid a tax to anybody. It is a quarter of a penny a bucket at the standpipe and the yield is estimated at fourteen pounds a quarter and four hands at four hours is about thirteen, and the bench has set it at fourteen and a half, and I know what that is, I have done the arithmetic on the walk up, it is a rate set to pay a wage and not one penny more, and it was set that way by a woman who cannot read a clause, on the fourteenth, in front of eighty people, after a carter took four loads of the district's own sand and lime back up the hill and did not come down again.

“My daughter is fourteen. She is not in the traveller book. She walks to that pump every morning and has done since she was nine, and if this instrument is made, she will be one of the people who pays a rate to a woman at a gate, and the woman at the gate will be paid by name in chalk, and my daughter will be one of the people whose money it is. I have thought about that for a day and a half and I have not slept and I am not going to pretend to you that I do not think about it. I am going to vote for it, because I asked for a date and the date is here and my daughter is not a trespasser by the fact of a book, and if this is the only line that is ever going to exist then I would rather it was the real one and cost something than a speech about fairness that costs nothing and is on a step in Orison.”

He sat down, and Wenna Tace, four miles away on a bank, would have said something about that speech if she had been in the room, and Corva Silt was in the room and did not say it.

---

Then the court did the thing it does, which is ask for the burden, and it asked the man who has nothing but numbers, and he gave it to them in the wrong words and the court made him say it again, and the second time is the one in the record.

“Mr Vester,” said Ansa Rell. “What does that instrument cost.”

“I do not know what it costs,” said Ilyan. “I can tell you four numbers and I cannot tell you what they mean, and I would rather say the first thing than the second.

“Here are the numbers. The gate is shut to loads out of the district on the flat, and the flat had four hundred and eleven loads on it on the twentieth, and that is my count and it may be wrong by forty, and I have said that in four rooms now. Of those, a woman with a barrow has hauled forty-one since the sheet went up, at about a third of a haul rate, because a barrow is four loads to a cart and there is no cart. The carts have taken eleven and gone back up the hill. The yield of the rate is fourteen and a half pounds a quarter, and the wage is about thirteen. That is a margin of about a pound ten, and one pound ten is what is left over after a gate hand has been paid for four hours a day in a district where nothing has ever been paid to anybody.

“And I cannot tell you what any of that is, because four hundred and eleven loads and a third of a rate and a pound ten are numbers, and the sentence I do not have is the one that says who is short, and I have been in this city five weeks and I have got very good at the numbers and I have never once been able to say the sentence.”

“Who is short,” said Ansa Rell.

“I do not know,” said Ilyan. “I want to say the haulier, because she told me in a field and she is the only person in this case who has said the sentence about the water out loud, and I have been using her. And I have been using her because she is the one who told me, and that is exactly the mistake I made on a bank in front of sixty people and got written down, and I am not going to make it again in a room with a magistrate in it. It is not her. If you ask the gate hand she will tell you what a week of a rate on Weir Lane is in a household of five, and it is not the man who wrote the instrument and it is not me and it is not the man with a cart. It is the thirty-one people in a lane, and the woman whose fourteen-year-old walks to a pump, and I do not know how to say that in a way that is worth putting in a record because it is the same sentence as the one about the flat.”

“Then the court will ask,” said Ansa Rell, “and the court will pay for the asking, and the court is going to say plainly that the cost of asking a question is four miles of road and a day of somebody's work, and the court has asked it, and the cost of the asking is the court's and not any party in this room.”

---

The answer came back at the ninth bell on a cart, and it took a day and a half to be written, and the reason it took a day and a half is that the gate hand could not leave the gate and so the district sent its clerk to the bank and wrote down what was said and read it back and then the gate hand made her correct two words of it and had it carried back and the clerk entered the corrections herself.

Corva Silt read it in the open air with the light going off the block, and it is the whole of what the morning cost and it cost three lines of it.

> **FROM THE LOW-WATER GATE, THE THIRTIETH DAY, AT THE SIXTH BELL, IN THE WORDS OF THE GATE HAND, WHICH ARE HER OWN AND HAVE NOT BEEN IMPROVED BY ANYBODY: THE RATE IS THE RIGHT SIZE. I SHOULD HAVE HAD IT THIRTY YEARS AGO. I AM NOT GOING TO ARGUE AGAINST IT IN FRONT OF A COURT. AND I AM NOT GOING TO PRETEND I AM GRATEFUL EITHER, AND YOU CAN PUT THOSE TWO THINGS IN THE SAME LINE BECAUSE THEY ARE BOTH TRUE AND IT IS THE ONLY LINE I HAVE GOT.**

Nobody said anything about it, which was correct, and Harrow entered it without a heading, and then — after a pause long enough that two people looked at each other — entered, in his own hand and under it, *the court notes that it has now asked a woman at a gate a question about money for the first time in nineteen years and that the answer contained a relief and a grievance in one sentence and that this court has no procedure for separating them and does not propose to invent one at a bench on a step.*

Then the court put the choice, which is what the sitting is for, and it did it badly and on purpose.

The magistrate said that she had the clean instrument, and that she had had it in her bag since the eleventh day, and that she was now going to stop being the person who brought it, because she was going to do the thing that frightened her more, which was to ask the room to want something else, and there was nothing else on the table, and everybody in the room could see that, and she was not going to pretend otherwise and use it as an argument.

“I am going to put the question,” she said, “and I am going to put it badly, and I am going to put it now at the ninth bell of a second sitting of a question about a category, because if I put it well you will answer it for the wrong reason. There is a good instrument on this step and it is signed at the bottom. The other thing is not on the step. The other thing is a set of people who would have to put their names to a paper that does not exist, and every one of them can be told exactly what it would cost them, and in four benches in nineteen years that is what has happened eleven times and the eleven were all valid and none of them was affordable.

“So the question is not which instrument. The question is whether this court is prepared to say out loud that the second thing does not exist, in a room with eleven benches in it, in a city where a sheet has been nailed to a gate four miles away and is going to stay there, and the answer to that question decides a great deal and none of it is about a gate.”

She put her hand flat on the stone, and Odile wrote it, and Ilyan understood that she had just told the room that the clean instrument was a confession and not a victory, and that she had done it out loud and on her own motion, and that she was still the magistrate and still going to win the argument or lose it on the merits and that this had cost her the whole of the advantage she came up the hill with.

“This court is not going to hold the question open for a fortnight to make a better speech about it,” Ansa Rell said. “It will go into the record as two texts and no answer, and the answer will be given in a room, in front of the people it is about, on a day the parties will be given notice of, and Mr Harrow will fix the day and it will be the day the pending challenge is heard, because that is the one date in this city that is already fixed and it is the only date I am not going to move for anybody.”

---

The step emptied at the last bell and the guild's four benches stood in the open air with nobody in front of them, and Tarin Keel counted them and put them on the handcart himself, which is a thing a neutral boundary does when the thing in the field has been cartography, and Oren Sedge came down the fen road in the dark for them and did not get off the road to help and Ilyan understood that was also a thing a man does.

Ilyan's leg went on the last hundred yards of the climb and there was nothing to hold and nothing to sit on, and he put his back against a wall on the hill and let it be what it was for about two minutes, and the low water came up in his nose in three layers, the day over the night, the flat over the water, and under both of them the chain, moving about four hundred yards away at the wrong end of an afternoon that had finished an hour ago.

A cart came up past him going down to the fen road, and the driver slowed without being asked, and it was Ansel Nyle, of the low fen, with one bridge and thirty-two years of nothing filed, coming down to look at a gate he had read about and had not seen.

He pulled up. He looked at Ilyan against the wall and at the leg.

“Four months,” Nyle said, “is what I would guess, and I am going to guess in front of you rather than behind you, and you can tell me to stop.”

“Four months is not wrong,” Ilyan said. “It is eleven days.”

“Eleven days,” said Nyle, and got out and helped him up the last of the wall with one hand and no ceremony at all, and then went and looked at the sheet of paper that Corva Silt had nailed to a gate board nineteen days ago and did not touch it and read it standing up the way a man reads a thing he has no standing to touch.

“Your eight clauses are smaller than ours,” he said, looking up at the sheet and not touching it.

“It is smaller than yours,” Ilyan said.

“I know it is,” said Nyle. “I want to say a thing and then I am going to go down this road, because I have a peat cart coming in the morning and the ward line in my district is nine minutes and the toll is short. If the small thing is made, then in four months a clerk in Orison will say the ninth bridge is the ninth bridge and there is a rule about gates and it closes on a day the clerk picks and the man in the low fen will be the man who cannot stop a cart because the power to clear the deck lives in a page four miles away. I copied that page in an afternoon in the fifth day, and I have used it twice, and I was paid twice, and both of them are written in my own book with the hours beside them.

“But I am not going to say to this court that I would rather be a man with no power and a copy of nothing,” said Nyle, “because I said that in a letter four days ago and I have had two days with it and I have had two days with the answer too and the answer is that I adopted that page in an afternoon in the fifth day and I have been a man with a power for five weeks, and nobody has asked me once what it feels like to ring a bell and stop a cart that is not mine.”

He got back up on the cart.

“Your rate is the right size,” he said. “Mine is. Tell her I said so and tell her I said it from a man with a cart and not from a man with a gate, and it does not carry.”

Then the road was empty and the water went out below the low water and somewhere on it a woman with a barrow was in the dark with four hundred loads and a pair of feet, and about a mile up the fen road a clerk of thirty-one was writing in a day book that a man had put his name at the bottom of a rule and that a woman had put two true things in the same line, and that both of those had happened in the same afternoon in the same city and that nobody in Orison had voted on it yet.

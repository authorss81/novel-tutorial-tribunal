# Chapter 83: A Right With No Door In It

The boy who had asked her what a haul was came back on the fourth of the fourth month, at about the second bell, and he had asked for her because he had been told at the weighhouse that the girl on the flat could read.

He was nineteen. His name was Merrick Vaunt and he was a lighterman's mate on the low water, which meant he got up at four in the dark and pushed a pole under a flat boat for a man who owned it, and he had been taught letters at nine at a school that comes four times a month from the upper street and had had, in his own words, one of those four days in his head ever since.

“Corva Silt sent me,” he said, on the step of the room over the pump, with his hands wet. “She said to ask you if you'd read the quarter's rate out loud to me so I can learn it, and I said I'd not ask a person for a free thing, and she said it isn't free, it's a day of the boat's time and the boat'll wait.”

“What's your name.”

“Merrick Vaunt. And the reason I'm asking is not because I want to read it,” he said. “It's because I've been told the answer to a question twice now and it's been two different answers and I'm the one people ask.”

---

The first answer was hers, on the first of the month, on a mud bank, at the sixth bell, to about two hundred people. *A haul was a barrow's load.* The second answer was a carter's, in the tap room at the weighhouse, on the same evening, and it was *a cart's load, and if you want it the other way you're talking about a barrow and a barrow's not a haul.*

“So it's a barrow's load,” said Merrick Vaunt, “and the entry says a barrow's load is four loads to a cart, and the rate says a haul in this district is two thirds short of a haul, and I've got no way to tell anybody which one of those is right because I'm the one asking and everybody keeps saying *well, you know, you've not been in the room*.”

Wenna Tace put the barrow against the wall the way she did and looked at him.

“Sit on the step,” she said. “I have got to do two runs before the water and I will read it to you while I am doing them and you can ask me things and I will tell you when I know and I will tell you when I don't.”

---

She did read it to him, twice, on the flat and then up the bank, in the voice she uses at the sixth bell on the first of a quarter, which is not a voice for a person and is a voice for about two hundred, and it is slower than anybody expects and there is no place in it where you can put your hand.

> **A HAUL IS A BARROW'S LOAD, AND A BARROW'S LOAD IS FOUR LOADS TO A CART, AND THE DISTRICT'S OWN RATE IS A THIRD OF ITS OWN HAUL RATE, AND SO A HAUL IN THIS DISTRICT IS PAID AT THREE TIMES THE DISTRICT'S OWN RATE, TO THE PERSON AT THE MOMENT OF THE WORK, BY NAME, IN CHALK. THE HUNDRED AND FIFTY HAULS OF THE LAST ELEVEN MONTHS WHICH ARE IN NO BOOK OF THIS DISTRICT ARE ENTERED AS OWED BY THIS DISTRICT AND ARE NOT PAID, THE DISTRICT HAVING NO MONEY.**
>
> **THE RATE WAS SET BY A BENCH OF FOUR. IT WAS ASKED FOR BY THE HOLDER OF THE FIFTH TERM, WHO CANNOT APPEAL FROM IT, AND IT WILL BE READ ALOUD AT THE SIXTH BELL ON EVERY FIRST DAY OF A QUARTER BY THE SAME PERSON, AND NOBODY MAY APPEAL IT, AND IT IS THE LARGEST SINGLE WANT OF THE PEOPLE IT IS READ TO.**

"That's me," she said. “That's my name in it, sort of. That's the second barrow I want and the hundred and fifty that I did. Read it back to me.”

“I can't read that,” said Merrick Vaunt.

“No,” she said. “Say the second one. You can get most of the second one.”

He got most of the second one.

---

They were still on the bank at the fifth bell when Corva Silt came down with a slate and put it on the barrel lid, and the slate had a sum on it in the district's own small hand, and she had done four versions of it over two days and it would not close either way, and she was thirty-one and had asked five times in six weeks to be relieved of the print authority and had been refused five times and had said she would not ask a sixth.

“Two things,” she said. “One. I have been over that entry four times in two days and it does not come out, and it does not come out because it is not a sum, and I have stopped trying and I am telling you instead of you finding it out at the quarter in front of two hundred people.”

“Which part.”

“All of it, and here is the part that does it. The district's own rate is a third of a haul rate, and a haul is paid at three times the district's own rate, and this district's haul is two thirds short of a haul, and all three of those are the same entry and the first two of them are each other exactly, and the third one is not them, and I have checked it nine ways and there is no way to make a number out of a haul in this district that is both of those things at once.” She put the chalk down. “And I will tell you what it is not. It is not a mistake. I set it and I would set it again. It is a rate that was set in a room by four people who did not know what the district's own rate is a rate for, and I have been the clerk of this bench for six years and nobody in this district has ever told me.”

---

That was the sentence, and it was said by accident at about a quarter past the fifth bell on the fourth of the fourth month, and three people heard it and one of them wrote it down and one of them had to be asked what he meant by it.

“What do you mean,” said Ilyan Vester. He was sitting on the third step of the room over the pump with a slate of his own that he did not need, because it was Bryn Sallow's figures on the back of it and he had not been able to read them since the fourth of the month before last and had not asked her to read them again.

“The rate,” said Corva Silt. “The district's own rate, the one we have had since my grandmother's time, the one a load is paid at. It is a rate for a *load*. A handcart's load, out of the bank, at the gate side. It is not a rate for a barrow and it is certainly not a rate for a cart, and this bench has never once written down what it is a rate for, and it has never once been asked, and I have read the standing of the wards and it says a duty and not a thing you pay, and it does not say what a load is.”

“Then a haul is four of them.”

“A barrow's load is four loads to a cart,” said Corva Silt. “Which is a fact about barrows and carts and not about money, and somebody wrote it on that page on the seventeenth of the second month because it was the way of saying the barrow was small. And then the four of us set a rate for a haul on the twenty-eighth of last month, and we set it at the district's own rate, which is a third of a haul, and a hauler in this district is paid a third of a haul, and every hauler in this county is paid a haul. And the two numbers are in the same entry and they do not make one another and there is nothing in this city that says which of them a barrow-load is worth.”

“The woman was the gate hand,” said Wenna Tace. “Bryn Sallow. She was outside.”

“I know she was outside. I told her at the step, at the tenth bell, at the gate side, with the slate in my hand, and she said it in about eleven words, and then she said *I have heard that figure and I want a number with it* and I went and got a number from a man who was in the room over the pump and I wish to the work I had not.”

---

Bryn Sallow was told, and came, and stood at the gate side with one foot on the sill-stone and one on the bank and did not go near the room over the pump, which she has never done in her life, and which is not a complaint.

Then Wenna Tace asked the question, and she asked it standing up and in her own words and without a slate, and it is the only time in this case that somebody has asked a bench of four a question that the bench of four had to look up.

“I want to appeal my own rate.”

There were four people in that room and about nine outside on the flat.

“Which one,” said Cael Rundle.

“The haul,” she said. “Not the water. The water is right and Cael said so before anybody and I am not going back on it. The haul. I asked for it on the seventh of March because I have wanted it for eleven months and because there is no clause about a barrow in that charter and there is no clause about a barrow in it because I cut it out of nine sheets in a room in this city, and I would like it at a full haul rate and I know the district has no money and I know that, and I have read the water rate out loud to two hundred people and I am going to keep doing it.”

“That's not an appeal,” said Nell Ambrey. “That's wanting something.”

“It is both and I know it is both,” said Wenna Tace, “and I am asking whether the second half of it can be argued, and if it cannot then I want it written down that I asked.”

The System put the question and the answer in front of Ilyan while Corva Silt was still getting a fresh slate, and it was a record of a room and not a rule about the world.

**IN THE ROOM OVER THE PUMP, THE FOURTH OF THE FOURTH MONTH, AT THE FIFTH BELL, BEFORE A BENCH OF FOUR AND THE HOLDER OF THE FIFTH TERM.**
**THE QUESTION, ASKED BY THE HOLDER OF THE FIFTH TERM OF THE CHARTER OF THIS DISTRICT: WHETHER THE RATE FOR A HAUL, SET BY THIS BENCH ON THE TWENTY-EIGHTH OF THE THIRD MONTH, MAY BE APPEALED BY ANY PERSON.**
**THE ANSWER: NO. IT WAS SET BY A BENCH OF FOUR IN ITS OWN ROOM. THE ONLY PERSON WHO ASKED FOR IT IS THE PERSON WHO ASKED, AND THE FIFTH TERM SAYS A HOLDER MAY NOT BE A PARTY, MAY NOT APPEAL, MAY NOT APPEAR IN SUPPORT OF AN APPEAL, AND MAY NOT BE NAMED IN ONE. NO OTHER PERSON OF THIS DISTRICT ASKED FOR IT AND HAS ANY STANDING IN IT. NO PERSON OUTSIDE THIS DISTRICT HAS ANY STANDING IN IT. THIS COURT HAS NO POWER OVER THE CHARTER OF THIS DISTRICT AND TAKES NO VIEW AND HAS SAID SO TWICE.**
**THE RATE IS READ ALOUD AT THE SIXTH BELL ON THE FIRST DAY OF A QUARTER AT THE STANDPIPE BY THE PERSON WHO ASKED FOR IT, TO WHATEVER PEOPLE COME.**
**WHAT IS NOT DECIDED HERE: WHETHER THE RATE IS FAIR. WHETHER THE HUNDRED AND FIFTY HAULS ARE OWED. WHETHER THE ENTRY'S TWO SENTENCES CAN BOTH BE TRUE. WHETHER A RATE CAN BE LIVED INSIDE.**

---

The answer took about four minutes and Nell Ambrey gave all of it herself and did not give it kindly, and when she had finished Wenna Tace asked one more question and it was the one the machinery had no procedure for at all.

“Then what is the pump for.”

“The pump is a public place,” said Nell Ambrey, “and it is a place a rate is read out loud in, four times a year, by the person who asked for it, to whoever comes. And you can stand in it. You can stand in it and say a thing about that rate out loud, in the open air, in front of two hundred people, and I cannot stop you and no bench in this city can stop you and there is no rule against it anywhere, and not one person is under any obligation to answer you when you do.”

“So it is a hearing.”

“No,” said the convenor, who is about sixty and has been losing a sanitation complaint and a sheet for eleven weeks. “It is a pump with about two hundred people standing near it. If I were to call it a hearing I would be inventing a thing in my own district in a month when we have four letters out of the chamber and no money, and then in four years somebody in a city would put *Halloway* against *a hearing* in a column and it would be me that said it first.”

---

And that is the shape of the whole of the fourth of the fourth month, and it did not get any better by the sixth.

An appeal can be sworn. That is what makes an appeal a thing a person can carry: it has a room, a counter, a clerk, an entry, a date, a name, and a person who will serve it, and it goes where it is going whether anybody is pleased or not, and it costs a district forty-four pounds and sixteen a year and about five people a day come and stand in a room over a pump about it.

A rate is not that. A rate cannot be carried. A rate is a thing a person has to be inside, and being inside it is the whole of what it does, and the only part of it that comes out into the open is four times a year in the voice of the person who asked for it, standing on a bank, with a barrow against a wall, reading to whoever came.

And the machinery in this city has a procedure for the first thing and not one word for the second.

There is no office for a rate. There is nobody in this city a person may write to about a rate. A rate is set by a bench in its own room, and it binds the people inside the district whether they are before the bench or not, and the only person it names is the one who has to read it out, and she is not permitted to be anything else.

---

The cost of the asking fell exactly where it always falls, on the person who asked, and it fell on the fifth of the month, and it was this.

A bench of four that has answered a question in public has priced a room in its own district, and everybody in this case knows that now, because one of them paid for it with a fortnight's notice eleven miles away in the middle of March. Halloway's bench had not been asked anything in public in nineteen years. It answered one question on the fourth of the fourth month, in its own room, with about nine people on the flat outside, and the answer it gave was that its holder cannot appeal a rate the holder asked for.

Corva Silt wrote it out and then she read it back twice and then she asked, in the ordinary way, whether it was going in the district's book.

“It is going in the book,” said Nell Ambrey.

“It will be read out,” said Corva Silt.

“It will be read out,” said the convenor, “because it is in the book and she reads the book.”

“And that is the second time in three weeks that a person in this district is going to stand on that bank and read a thing about herself out loud to two hundred people that she did not choose to have written.”

“It is,” said Nell Ambrey, “and I am not going to pretend it is a small thing, and I will tell you the reason I am putting it in anyway, and it is not that it is right, it is that the answer is true and it is on a page somewhere else already in a clerk's own hand in a chamber in this city, and this district set a rate two thirds short on the twenty-eighth of March and told two hundred people about it on the first of April, and if a person in this city can point at the chamber's answer and say *and does your rate say who can ask about it* then somebody in this district is going to ask that question anyway, and it is much better that the answer is in our own hand.”

Then she looked at the slate, and at Oakes, and at the two hundred pounds of nothing that the district has.

“Do you understand what you have just cost me,” she said to the room in general, but she was looking at Wenna Tace. “You have put it in a book that a clerk can come back with, in a district of about two hundred people, and it is going to be read out at the pump. And there is no money in that room and there is not going to be any more money in it before the year turns, and the rate you asked for is two thirds short and cannot be appealed, and the answer is now in the book. You did not get the money and you got the sentence.”

“Yes,” said Wenna Tace. “And I would like it in the book that I asked, and I would like the reading to say that I asked.”

“It will say that you asked,” said Corva Silt.

---

What the fifth of the month added was a boy of nineteen who could not read the entry and got most of the second half of it.

She had him back at the second bell on the fifth because Corva Silt sent him again and because she had worked out, in about a day, that there is a thing she had not had for eleven years and did not know she did not have until it was pointed out by a nineteen-year-old with a pole under a boat.

There is a rule in this city that a person may not be a witness to themselves, and it is the same rule that stops her appealing her own rate. So a page she keeps has one pair of eyes on it and a clerk in a room down the lane who is not the holder and did not print it and has asked five times to be let off the printing.

“A page with one pair of eyes on it is a page that cannot be checked,” said Wenna Tace, on the step, at the second bell on the fifth of the fourth month, “and I have known that since the eighteenth of the second month and I have not done anything about it, and you did it in one sentence standing in the mud with your hands wet.”

Merrick Vaunt looked at the slate.

“I said *everybody keeps saying you've not been in the room*,” he said. “I did not think about it. I have never been in a room.”

“No,” she said. “You have not. That is why it worked.”

---

She made him do it twice, and she made him do it out loud, and she was very strict about it and she did not raise her voice once.

Then she wrote the entry herself, in her own hand, in the middle of the page where she puts the things that matter, and she read it back before she inked it, and when Merrick Vaunt read it back to her afterwards at the second bell on the sixth of the fourth month he got all of it, including the numbers, including the two hundred pounds of nothing, including the line about the two sentences that will not both be true, and including her own name four times.

> **THE FOURTH OF THE FOURTH MONTH, AT THE FIFTH BELL, IN A ROOM OVER A PUMP. I ASKED THE BENCH OF FOUR WHETHER THE RATE FOR A HAUL COULD BE APPEALED BY ANY PERSON AND THE ANSWER WAS NO, AND THE ANSWER IS IN THIS DISTRICT'S BOOK AND WILL BE READ OUT AT THE PUMP, AND I WANTED IT TO BE.**
>
> **AND THIS IS WHAT I HAVE FOUND OUT AND NOBODY ASKED ME: AN APPEAL IS A THING A PERSON CARRIES. A RATE IS A THING A PERSON IS INSIDE OF. I HAVE TO STAND ON THIS BANK FOUR TIMES A YEAR AND READ IT OUT AND I AM ALLOWED TO SAY WHATEVER I LIKE AND NOBODY HAS TO ANSWER ME, AND THAT IS NOT AN APPEAL AND NOBODY IN THIS CITY HAS A NAME FOR IT.**
>
> **AND THE ENTRY SAYS A HAUL IS PAID AT THREE TIMES THE DISTRICT'S OWN RATE, AND THE DISTRICT'S OWN RATE IS A THIRD OF A HAUL, AND A HAUL HERE IS TWO THIRDS SHORT OF A HAUL, AND ALL THREE OF THOSE ARE THE SAME ENTRY AND TWO OF THEM ARE EACH OTHER EXACTLY AND THE THIRD IS NOT EITHER. THE CLERK HAS GONE OVER IT FOUR TIMES. NOBODY IN THIS DISTRICT HAS EVER SAID WHAT THE DISTRICT'S OWN RATE IS A RATE FOR, AND UNTIL SOMEBODY SAYS IT, A HAUL IN THIS DISTRICT CANNOT BE PRICED AT ALL AND THE HUNDRED AND FIFTY HAULS I AM OWED WILL KEEP HAVING NO FIGURE ON IT FOR AS LONG AS I AM HERE.**
>
> **I DID NOT GET THE MONEY. I GOT THE SENTENCE. I AM THE PERSON WHO ASKED FOR THE RATE AND I CANNOT APPEAL IT AND I AM THE PERSON WHO READS IT OUT AND I AM THE ONLY PERSON IN ABOUT TWO HUNDRED PEOPLE WHO CAN READ IT. THE BENCH SAYS THERE IS A BENCH OF FOUR AND A CLERK AND A PUMP AND A ROAD WITH NO CARTAGE ON IT. IT DOES NOT SAY THERE IS A SECOND READER.**
>
> **AND THERE IS ONE NOW AND HE IS NINETEEN AND HIS NAME IS ON THIS PAGE AND HE IS NOT A HOLDER AND HE IS NOT A CLERK AND HE IS NOT PAID FOR IT AND NOBODY HAS ASKED HIM, AND I AM ASKING HIM, AND IT IS NOT IN THE CHARTER.**

“It is in the page,” she said, when he had finished. “That is all the instrument there is. It is a page.”

“A page in a room over a pump,” said Merrick Vaunt, “that a man can be sent for.”

“That is exactly what it is,” said Wenna Tace, “and do not say that in a loud voice in the lane, because if you say it in a loud voice in the lane then in about four years there will be a bench in another district that has heard that a page in a room over a pump is a thing you can be sent for, and there are nine of them in this city and every one of them has said in its own hand that it holds nothing.”

He put the slate down against the wall and picked up the barrow.

“Corva Silt said to tell you the boat waits,” he said, “and she said to tell you that if you are going to do it twice a week then she cannot pay you for it and neither can the bench, and that you should decide that now and not at the quarter.”

“Then tell her I have decided,” said Wenna Tace, getting the near end up. “Twice a week, unpaid, and I will read it out on the bank where people can hear me, because the second time I do it I want somebody to know that I have read it before.”

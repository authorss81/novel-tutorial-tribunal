# Chapter 55: One Legible Rule

The First Verdict is a block of black basalt about the size of a house and it is four hundred years old, and there is no throne in it and no dais and no roof, and the reason Orison is a city instead of a village is that for four hundred years a person could stand in the open air in front of a piece of stone and be answered by somebody wearing a different colour from their own.

What it has around it is the rest of the city, and the city was the thing nobody in the case had looked at.

Nine bridges run off the water in a fan, and between them the districts are not neighbourhoods in the sense that a hill is a neighbourhood; they are claims. Every one of them wants a hearing, and the basalt block holds eleven in a morning and has held eleven in a morning since before the Codification was a document, and the docket of the day is a board outside the rail, and a clerk writes names on it, and the writing of a name on that board is the only scarce thing in Orison that cannot be bought with money, and everybody who came that morning knew it.

There were nineteen names on the board at the second bell and the basalt had eleven places to hear and it heard nine, and every one of the nine was a bridge matter, and the other two places went to a water and to a name, and the name is still on the board and will be heard when the next board comes up.

Ilyan had seen none of it, because a man whose name is on a document does not get put on a docket. He was in the second gallery, which is a step up and behind a rail, and Sera was on the floor, and Odile was in the well of the court with the ninth-bridge lines in her hand, because the nine benches wanted the conditions read by somebody who could be stopped, and Sera Quill stood beside her with the read-back in the guild's words in the other hand, and the two of them read in turn all morning without once disagreeing about a word, which is a thing that happens about four times a year in this city and did not happen at all in the first month of the case.

He lost most of it anyway. He followed the short matters and lost the long one at the point where a district argued about which of two rivers had a bed, and he was honest about it at the interval, in a corridor, to a chamber clerk who had brought him a cup of water and had not been asked to.

“My name is Dessin Nahl,” she said. “I am the clerk of this chamber and I have written nine of the eleven on that board and I did not know whose name was in the second gallery until the fifth bell, and I want to say that you are the first person to tell me that in a month, and I am going to remember it, and I am also going to tell you that the fourth district to come in with a paper this week did not bring a bridge.”

---

The common-rule sitting was called for the seventh bell by a magistrate nobody in the case had met, and she was forty-four and had been a magistrate for eleven years and had written the procedure for how a chamber may sit on a matter affecting more than one district, and she introduced herself in one sentence and then made the room work.

“Magistrate Ansa Rell,” she said. “I am not here to hear the ninth bridge. I am here because eleven benches in this city have now asked this chamber for the same instrument and I would rather have one instrument that eleven benches can look at than nine instruments that eleven benches have to read past. Nobody in this room gets to object to the ambition. You get to object to the text, and the text is coming at the second bell, and I want the burdens stated before the opinions, and if a burden is going to be paid by a person in this room I want it named before that person leaves.”

Somebody at the back said, “That is Mr Vester’s procedure.”

“It is Mr Harrow’s,” said Ansa Rell, “and Harrow said so out loud on a span and said he had invented it, and I have borrowed it and I have said where I got it, and that is the only courtesy I intend to observe. Mr Vester. You are in the gallery. You are not on the docket and I am not going to put you on it. If you want to say something you will say it from the floor and the floor is not where you are.”

“Then I will say it from here,” Ilyan said.

“No,” said Ansa Rell. “From the floor or not at all. It is not discourtesy. If I let a man speak from a gallery then in six weeks a man with a bench will ask me to hear him from a gallery, and there is no floor left and the ground goes. Come down when you have something that is not about your own record.”

He did not come down for a while.

The text was Ottla Prenn's draft eleven, and she had brought it herself, and it was four pages and it was good, and it had a keeper's clearance in it and a pay line in it and an exit in it, and it was the clause the four districts at the fen crossing had failed on in the middle of the case, and it had failed then because two districts could not produce their own maintenance terms.

Ansel Nyle stood up, and Nyle was the low fen, and Nyle had one bridge, and Nyle said:

“We produce our maintenance terms.”

The whole room turned round, because for a month the low fen had been the reason the conference could not sit.

“We adopted the ninth bridge’s settlement entire, in the district, on the fifth day, and we did not adopt it as a precedent. We adopted it as our own maintenance term. There is one bridge in the low fen and I keep it. I am fifty-one and I have been in the post since I was nineteen and I have filed nothing for thirty-two years and I filed our whole maintenance term in an afternoon by copying a public instrument out of a book, which is what a clerk does, and I am not a clerk. I have called the deck clear twice since the sixth day. Both times it was a peat cart that could not turn. Both times it was off the deck in nine minutes. Both times I was paid out of our road funds to the person on a line in chalk, because the term told me what I was allowed to do and somebody in the low fen had to tell me where the money was, and that somebody is me, because there is nobody else.”

“Did your load-fee pay it,” said Bel Ordway.

“No,” said Nyle. “And that is the finding I came to make. The toll is short on a thin week and the ward line is not, because the ward line goes out of road funds and the toll does not, and a man who has to stop a load for nine minutes gets his nine minutes from the same place whatever the week looks like. The ninth bridge settlement told us that and we had no way of knowing it.”

Nobody in that court had heard a rule work. They had heard a rule be argued about for a month and they had heard it be refused, and this was the first time anybody had stood in front of nine bridges' worth of benches and said that a thing had been *carried* — and Ilyan, in the second gallery, found that he could not stand up, and did not try, and that this was not modesty.

“Cut banks adopted it on the ninth,” said Ordwin Sarn, from the back, and stood up to say it himself, which nobody expected from a man of that age. “Same instrument. We have a pay line that pays. We have never had one. Our clerk is a woman of thirty-four and she has been at it nine days and she cried on the seventh, about the water, and she says that is a normal thing and it happens in every district and it is the only part of this that nobody warned her about.”

“Convener,” said Ansa Rell, “you have four minutes of the hour and you have a salt cart on the weighbridge. Say what your cart needs.”

“Four minutes,” said Bel Ordway, “and the cart is a salt cart with a load on it, and I have been sitting in this court for two hours. I will say the whole of it in one sentence and then I will go and I would like it entered that I said it in one sentence. One legible rule for the maintenance and crossing of every span and gate in the four districts and the fen, at one rate, on one form, heard by one bench, and I will give up my two hours for it, and I would like the court to understand that the reason I want it is not tidiness. It is that I am tired of my own clerk.”

“Tired of your own clerk is a reason a man gives at the fourth hour of a day like this,” said Ansa Rell. “Put the tidiness in. What do you actually need.”

“I need to know in advance what a span may carry and who pays for saying so,” said Bel Ordway. “That is all. I have a cart on a bridge I did not build with a load I did not weigh, and there are two benches in this city that would each tell me a different thing about it, and both of them are right, and I have to go and find out which one has the seal this month.”

Nobody took it further and the sitting went on and it was, for about an hour, the best hour of Ilyan's month, and then it stopped.

---

It stopped on term four, and it stopped on a fact, and the fact was that a rate may be paid only to a body with a name, and every chamber in the city knows it, and the ninth bridge's settlement is the first instrument in ninety years that pays a wage to a person without going through a body, and the reason it can do that is that the ninth bridge's term says the ward rate is a number and not a recipient.

“Read it,” said Ansa Rell, and Odile read it, and the room heard *an empty line is a record and a full line is a wage* and about six people in eleven benches said nothing, which is how you tell.

“Then the common rule is one clause long,” said Ottla Prenn, “and it is this clause, and it is the only clause in four districts that cannot be drafted, because the ward is not a body, and the ward has no name, and the ward is where the poor people are, and the ward is the thing you would most want to pay.”

“Speak only to what you know,” said Ansa Rell, gently, in the voice of a woman who has done this eleven years. “Who is telling you the ward is where the poor people are.”

“I am,” said Ottla Prenn. “And I am also telling you the ward is where the carters and the hauliers and the gate hands and the people who do not have a paper live, and I am saying it as a delegate and not as a clerk, and I would like that distinction in the record because in about four years somebody is going to move that it was not evidence.”

“It is not evidence,” Ansa Rell said. “It is a delegate's opinion and it is well founded and I am going to use it. Who is the clerk who cannot find a line?”

“Mr Vale is the clerk of the ninth bridge's district and he is not here and he has not been in this court since the ruling and he is paying a wage every week out of a road fund on a line in chalk on a frame, and he is nineteen years in the post and he is the man who entered in his own hand that his own office cannot pay a ward rate.”

“Enter that,” said Ansa Rell. “Is Mr Vale in the city.”

“He is on the ninth bridge and the span is propped and the seat is curing and the count is running every change with two hands under the deck and the props come out in two days.”

“Then his answer will be taken in writing and put in on the eleventh day, and I am going to do something this morning that I have never done in a chamber and I am doing it because the room has just told me it cannot finish. Listen to what the room has actually said.

“Eleven benches. Nine districts' clerks. Four drafts. And in three hours the argument has not been about a rule. It has been about a category. Mr Prenn says the ward is where the poor people are and the ward has no name. Mr Rundle of a low-water district in this city says, and he wrote it and I have read it and it is in my file, that the ward is where the carters and the hauliers and the gate hands live, and I have no idea which of those two men is right about the same four hundred people and I am not going to pretend the record decides it, because the record has never once decided it and every one of the eleven benches in this room would like it to have.

“Both of those men are right about somebody. That is not a drafting problem and it is not a missing document and it is not anybody in this room being unreasonable. It is a city that has two words for the same poor and will not pay either of them.

“So: the draft is not adopted and the draft is not withdrawn. Draft eleven goes to the eleven benches with a question on the face of it, and the question is the ward, and I am not asking a single bench in this room to answer it, because nine of you would answer it wrong for the same reason and two of you would answer it right for reasons that cannot be printed in a clause.

“The sitting stands to the eleventh day. The ward question is read into the record without an answer. And a magistrate's own note is entered, and it says the thing that is missing is not a rule and not a remedy: it is a piece of paper that a person can swear to in a room with no address in it. I have asked four magistrates whether they have one. None of us does. That is the finding of this morning and I am not going to pretend it is a smaller thing than a common rule, because I asked for a common rule in the morning and by the afternoon I have learned that I asked for a fair one and the fair one is nine times the work.”

She stopped, and then she said one more thing, and it was the reason the eleven benches had come, and she said it to the whole floor.

“Before anybody goes: I am going to say what I came for, because this court has a habit of leaving things in corridors. I came for one rule, and one rule is cheaper than nine and it is the correct answer, and I am not going to be coy about it in front of nine benches that have asked me. Every span in this city should have a keeper who can call the deck clear. Every keeper should be paid for the hour at the moment of the work, by name, in chalk. Every person should be able to read a rule before they have to live inside it. Every district should be able to hand a load on to the next district without a fortnight of argument about whose form it is on. That is a good rule. It is the best thing anybody has put in front of me in eleven years, and most of it is a bridgekeeper's work and the rest of it is a court work, and I want it city-wide by the end of the month.”

Nobody spoke, and the reason nobody spoke is that a magistrate in the well of a basalt court had just said the true thing, out loud, in daylight, and had also just said that she wanted it by the end of the month.

---

He came down off the second gallery at the ninth bell, while the benches were writing their burdens out, and he did it because Ansa Rell had told him to and because he had decided in a corridor that being in the room was worth more than being right about it from a rail.

Ansa Rell let him get four feet inside the rail, which is the same four feet that Tovin Dain got on a span, and which is the standing a person gets who is neither a party nor a delegate.

“Two things,” she said. “One is a burden and it is yours, so I am stating it before you state it. Your name is on the ninth bridge's settlement. It is in four districts and in nine chambers and in one poor district on a gate board on a numbered form. If I write a common rule in the next two weeks, your name is on that too, because your observation is one of the grounds of the instrument it comes out of, and a rule that stops anybody will cite you, and a rule that costs anybody will cite you, and you will be in eleven rooms in a month being a reason. I cannot prevent it. I have thought about it for two days. I am telling you rather than letting it arrive.”

“Yes,” Ilyan said.

“And the second thing is a question and I am going to ask it now while there are nine benches in the room, and I do not want a careful answer.

“The settlement says a person not on a register is not a trespasser because of it alone. The settlement says a keeper admits a person on foot, or with a handcart, or with a wheelbarrow, who offers no load and does not enter the load path. The settlement says the ward rate is a number and not a recipient and is paid to the person at the moment of the work. Those three sentences are why the ninth bridge is better than every other bridge in this city, and two of them cannot be written city-wide, because one of them needs a list and the other one needs a ward and the ward has no name. So: take the list out of the sentence and the sentence is a person. Take the ward out of the sentence and the sentence is a person. I am asking you which of those two removals is the one that will be used against the people the rule is for, and I am asking you in a room where nine benches will hear the answer and use it, and I am asking a man who cannot read the instrument.”

The floor went quiet, and Ilyan stood four feet inside a basalt court in a city where nine bridges met and found the thing he had, which was a number, and it was this: over the eleven benches represented, there were two hundred and eleven named bodies and one unnamed, and the unnamed one was the largest, and the number of people living in it was not in any of the eleven benches' papers because it had no name to put them in.

“I cannot tell you which,” he said. “I do not know the machinery well enough to know which sentence they will pick, and I know enough to know it will not be a sentence anybody picks on purpose.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the only one I have got and it took me nine seconds to find it instead of three, and nine seconds is the most I have ever managed,” Ilyan said. “And I will tell you the true thing, which is that both of them are the same removal, and you know it, and you asked me so that somebody would say it in a room.”

Ansa Rell looked at him for a moment longer than was necessary.

“Then somebody has said it in a room,” she said, “and the sitting is adjourned, and Mr Vale’s answer is due in three days, and Mr Quill will carry the terms of the ninth-bridge matter to any district that asks, which is one of the three things her license says she does, and I want it said that I intend to use her for that and that she is not mine.”

And the nine benches went out into a city of nine bridges and docketed themselves for the eleventh day, and the basalt court stood in the open air with nobody in it, and the gutter channel in the step had been worn by four hundred years of people walking out of it not knowing what they had been in.

---

Bel Ordway caught Ilyan at the rail with the salt cart two hours gone, and walked with him toward the bridge, because the cart was going that way and the man had no interest in stopping.

Nyle was still standing at the bottom of the step, in the wrong place, doing arithmetic on his fingers.

“One bridge in the low fen,” he said, “and the ninth bridge’s term on it, and my district’s road funds, and Mr Sarn in the cut banks doing the same with one of his, and neither of us has ever once asked for it, and I would like the record to show that a man who is given a power will not necessarily use it, and that the reason he does not use it is that the power is bounded by the loads standing and cannot be sold on.”

“Write to your own count book,” Ilyan said. “Get it on paper, with the two nine minutes in it and the hours beside them. A keeper who has not had to use the power twice is going to be asked one day why he has the thing at all, and if you have the two nine minutes written down, nobody can ask you that, because the answer is in your own hand in your own book and it is a fact and not an argument.”

“I will write it in,” said Nyle. “And I will tell you what Mr Sarn will write, because Sarn is the cut banks the same as I am the low fen and we came a long way together in the middle of your hearing and we have not had a conversation since. He will write: *then somebody should be measuring.* And he will be right, and it is the single most useful sentence anybody has said about a keeper in thirty-two years, and he has said it to me and not to you, and I am passing it on because you are the only man in the city who might do something with it.”

There is no better answer anywhere in this city than that, and Nyle had it in nine words, and Ilyan wrote it on Coll's slate and underlined it twice, and then took it out again and rubbed it out, because the only part of it that was his was the part about somebody being asked why, and that was not true either, and it was only true of him.

Sarn was at the weighbridge taking a cart's weight in the last of the light and he had about a minute and used it badly and well.

“Cut banks adopted it on the ninth,” he said, “and the ninth is a day you will want to remember, because on the ninth our northern neighbour sent a man down our road with a paper of theirs and asked for our form, and we sent him ours, and I gave it to him myself, and he is not a clerk, he is a road man, and I have thought about it for nine days.”

“Which neighbour,” said Ilyan.

“That is not a question for a man with a salt cart,” said Sarn, and put his weight on the scales, and then — because he had nine days of it in him and about a minute of light left — he said: “Their span has been on one keeper's family for two generations and the keeper is a woman of seventy-one and she was the daughter of the last one. They have never had a district. They have had a toll and a keeper and a book of names and that is the whole of the administration and it has been that way since before I was in the trade. And the man who came down our road had a *list of hands*.”

“A list of hands.”

“Copied out, he said. Eleven names, all dead, he said, and he said it in the tone of a man apologising for a wet coat, because he did not think it was odd. And he said they had entered the ninth bridge’s settlement term for term on the fourth, with the list of hands against it, and that it took a quarter of a day to do.”

“Whose names,” said Ilyan.

“Dead ones,” said Sarn. “I asked him twice. He said, *there is nobody to enter, it is a form, you write the last ones*.”

The cart went over the scales with a sound like a door closing in another room, and Ordwin Sarn took his hand off the beam and looked at it, and Ilyan looked at the water and the nine bridges and the basalt court standing empty in the open air, and thought: a rule with a witness list and nobody in it, in a place with no district, that nobody can challenge because a challenge has to be given to every party named, and there is nobody to give it to.

He said it out loud, because he had learned in a month that saying it out loud is the only way it stops being his.

“I am not going to chase that tonight,” he said, to Sarn, who had a cart to unload. “There is a preservation office in this city that has been keeping records on purpose for four years and I have not gone near it and I am not going to go near it tonight either, and if I chased every odd thing that came up the road in a month I would be the man who made records stop coming, to everybody, and that is a price I have been told about once and I have not forgotten it.

“Write it on the scale-house wall in cutbanks in the same hand you weigh in. Not for me. For whoever asks in a year.”

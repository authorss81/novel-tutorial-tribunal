# Chapter 97: The Aggregate Bill

Meridith Vale came down the fen road on the twenty-second of the fifth month at the second bell with a slate and a book and his own cart money, and he has been the clerk of the ninth bridge's district for nineteen years and has never once entered a false reason, and he paid for the road out of his own pocket and entered that, and nobody asked him to.

He came because a bench of four in a district four miles from his own had written to him in the district's own hand asking him a question, and because the answer to it could not be given on paper, and because he is a clerk and when a thing cannot be given on paper he comes.

The question was on a sheet of the district's paper with four names and a mark on the bottom of it, and it had been read in the room over the pump before he came into it, out loud, twice, by Corva Silt, and the room had about eleven people in it and the tide was out and the flat was walkable.

> **THE SECOND OF THE FIFTH MONTH. TO THE CLERK OF THE ROAD FUND OF THE DISTRICT OF THE NINTH BRIDGE, WHICH IS THE FUND OUR CHARTER IS CHARGED TO, AND WHICH IS NOT OURS, AND WHICH WE HAVE ASKED ANYWAY.**
>
> **ONE: WHAT DOES OUR CHARTER COST YOU IN YOUR YEAR, AS THE YEAR IS SET OUT AT THE PUBLIC REVIEW OF THE TWENTY-SEVENTH OF THE THIRD MONTH.**
>
> **TWO: WHAT WILL IT COST YOU WHEN THE YEAR IS SET AGAIN, IN THE SPRING, AND BY WHOSE HAND, AND ON WHAT DAY, AND TO WHOM MAY A PERSON OF THIS DISTRICT COMPLAIN.**
>
> **THREE: WE HAVE READ THE FIGURE OUT TWICE AND WE ARE NOT ALL OF US ABLE TO ADD, AND WE WOULD LIKE THE FIGURE AGAIN WITH SOMETHING BESIDE IT.**
>
> **NO REPLY IS ASKED FOR. A REPLY HAS COME ANYWAY AND WE ARE ENTERING THAT IT CAME ANYWAY, IN OUR OWN HAND, ON THE BACK OF THIS SHEET, WHICH WE ARE NOT ENTITLED TO DO AND ARE DOING.**

---

He gave it standing up, the way he gives everything, in about eleven minutes, and he read off the slate and then off the book, and nobody stopped him and nobody asked him anything while he was doing it, which is unusual and is because a clerk reading a fund out is doing the most ordinary thing anybody does in this city.

The charter of the district of Halloway costs forty-four pounds and sixteen a year. It is charged to the road fund of the district of the ninth bridge, which is four hundred and ten pounds a year. The year as set out at the public review on the twenty-seventh of the third month is: four new timbers for the fourth bridge, ninety-six pounds; the telling of Coldwater, fifteen; two hands under a deck at every change, two hundred and ten; this charter, forty-four pounds and sixteen. What is left in the year is forty-four pounds and four shillings. The charter costs twelve shillings more than what is left, and we will pay it twelve shillings over the line, and I have said that in a letter and I am saying it again with a date on it in a room.

And the hundred and five pounds that came back when the under-deck watch went to one hand is in a different line and a clerk cannot move a line into another line without a bench's minute.

“That is the first part,” he said, “and you have all of it, and it is a bill and it was never a notice, and I told the woman who holds your page that in a letter in the second month and I am telling you in a room.”

He put the slate down on the barrel lid, on the chalk, which is the lid the rate is on, and about nine people looked at the back of his coat while he did it.

“Now the second part, and I have come four miles for it and not for the first part, because the first part you have got and the second part is the one you cannot get off paper.”

---

“A fund is not a bag of money,” said Meridith Vale. “A fund is a list of lines, and every year the clerk of the fund writes the list out again for the ratepayers of his district, and the ratepayers read it, and some of them object, and an objection is heard, and if the objection is sound the line comes out or comes down. That is the whole of the power anybody has over a fund in this county and it is not much and it is real.

“And the clerk does not write the lines in the order anybody wants them in. The clerk writes them in the order of what they buy. That is the rule and the rule is about a hundred and forty years old and there is no minute about it and there is a copy of it in the room behind me and nobody has read it since the last clerk retired, and I have read it, and I read it in about nine minutes when I took this post, and I have never mentioned it to anybody because it is not interesting until you want something.”

He turned round and looked at the slate.

“Two hands under a deck at every change buys a body on a span, at the ward rate, by name, in chalk, on the day. A man can see that. I have stood on a bank and watched a hundred and five pounds' worth of it in a year, which is four hours a day for one man, and I know whose, and his name is in a disposition and he was told about it in a room over a smithy nineteen miles off by a keeper who walked to say it, and it is the only line in this fund that anybody can point at and see a person in.

“Your charter buys a room over a pump. Nine sheets, a pay line, a page, an appeal, a rate of half a farthing a bucket, a fifth term with a person in it, and about five people a day who come into a room and stand in it and argue about a rule. And every one of those is worth more to me than the four hours a day. I am not going to insult you by pretending otherwise, because I have read your page and your book and I sat in this room in March when a man of thirty-one put a wrong number in it and said out loud that it was wrong.

“And in the spring, when the year is set again, I have to write the lines out in the order of what they buy, and I have to rank them, and there is nobody in this county who can overrule the ranking, because the ranking is not a decision, it is an order, and it is mine to make and mine to defend and mine to read out at a rate meeting in front of about forty people in this district who pay this rate.

“And I am going to tell you now, in this room, on the twenty-second of the fifth month, with a date on it, exactly what I am going to do, and I have not done it yet, and I want it entered that I had not done it when I said it, and that nobody asked me to say it and that it cost me four miles and a day's wages out of my own.”

He put two fingers on the slate.

“I am going to write the charter below the hand under the deck. Not because I think it is worth less. Because it is, in the order I am required to write in, a thing that buys a room, and I cannot put a room above a body, and if I put a room above a body then a clerk in my chair in about four years is going to ask why, and the answer will be in a letter from four miles down a fen road, and the letter will be in this city, and a person in a room in this city will read the letter and think that a bench of four in a poor district had a say in how a bridge's money is ranked, and that is the exact thing that has happened eleven times in four months in nine districts and I am not going to be the twelfth.

“So the charter goes below a hand under a deck, and the twelve shillings it is over the line by goes over the line again, and the year after that I will be writing the list out for the third time, and one of these years there will be a line in it that this case has never thought of, and the line will be above yours, and the day it goes above yours is the day your appeal stops costing forty-four pounds and sixteen and starts costing whatever the line below it costs, and if the line below it is nothing then your appeal costs nothing and is not enforceable, because nobody in this city can be sent for.”

Nobody in the room said anything for long enough that a chain came up at the gate two hundred feet away.

“I am the man to be angry at,” said Meridith Vale. “I have thought about it for four days and I have not found anybody else. I have thought about it because in March a magistrate said in the open air that a body that pays a person to prepare the paper has bought the paper, and she is right, and this is the same thing with the sign on the other side, and it is my hand on the list and nobody else's. I have a rate meeting in this district in about three weeks and forty people pay this rate and none of them live in your district and all of them have been paying it for their own water for about a hundred and forty years, and when I read the list out in the order I have to read it out in, somebody is going to stand up and ask me why the poor district's law is at the bottom, and I am going to have to give them the reason I have just given you, and the reason is that a room is not a body.

“I would like it entered that I have not moved the hundred and five and could not have moved it without a minute of a bench of my own district, and that a minute of a bench of my own district is the only door into that line that exists in this case, and that no bench of four in a district four miles off can ask for one, and that if somebody asks me for one I will refuse, and that I am refusing it in advance, in this room, with a date on it, and that I have told them before they asked because I have been in this case long enough to know what happens to a clerk who waits to be asked.”

Corva Silt wrote it out in the district's own hand. It took her a while. She read it back twice. She read the second half of it a third time.

“It is the first letter anybody has written down in this district's book that says a thing is going to happen to us in the spring,” she said.

“It is the only thing anybody has written down in any book in this case that says what is going to happen in the spring,” said Meridith Vale, “and I have put it in yours and not in mine, because yours is the one that hurts, and a clerk who puts the price of a thing in the book of the person who pays it is a clerk who has noticed.”

---

The System put the bill in front of Ilyan Vester at the third bell on the twenty-second in the room over the pump, and it was a record of a room and not a rule about the world, and it is the only copy of it he will ever have, because he cannot read a slate and cannot read a clerk's hand and did not read either figure correctly on the day and was told so.

**IN THE ROOM OVER THE PUMP, THE TWENTY-SECOND OF THE FIFTH MONTH, AT THE THIRD BELL. A CLERK OF A FUND FOUR MILES AWAY READING OUT A YEAR IN A ROOM HE HAS WALKED TO.**
**WHAT THE CHARTER COSTS: FORTY-FOUR POUNDS AND SIXTEEN A YEAR, OUT OF A FUND OF FOUR HUNDRED AND TEN, WHICH IS A LITTLE UNDER A NINTH.**
**WHAT THE YEAR LEFT AFTER EVERYTHING ELSE: FORTY-FOUR POUNDS AND FOUR SHILLINGS. THE CHARTER COSTS TWELVE SHILLINGS MORE THAN WHAT IS LEFT AND THE DISTRICT WILL PAY IT OVER THE LINE.**
**WHAT THE HUNDRED AND FIVE POUNDS IS: A HAND UNDER A DECK, WHICH IS IN A DIFFERENT LINE AND CANNOT BE MOVED INTO THE CHARTER'S LINE WITHOUT A MINUTE OF A BENCH THAT IS FOUR MILES AWAY, AND NOBODY FOUR MILES AWAY CAN ASK FOR THAT MINUTE.**
**WHAT THE CHARTER BUYS, IN THE WORDS OF THE CLERK WHO READS THE FUND: A ROOM OVER A PUMP. NINE SHEETS. A PAY LINE. A PAGE. AN APPEAL. A RATE OF A HALF A FARTHING A BUCKET. A FIFTH TERM WITH A PERSON IN IT. ABOUT FIVE PEOPLE A DAY WHO COME INTO A ROOM AND ARGUE ABOUT A RULE.**
**WHAT THE CLERK OF THE FUND SAYS HE WILL DO IN THE SPRING: WRITE THE CHARTER BELOW THE HAND UNDER THE DECK, BECAUSE A FUND IS A LIST OF LINES IN THE ORDER OF WHAT THEY BUY, AND A CLERK CANNOT PUT A ROOM ABOVE A BODY.**
**THE COST OF THAT, AS THE CLERK OF THIS ROOM HAS IT ENTERED AND AS THE CLERK OF THE FUND WILL NOT: A POOR DISTRICT'S LAW IS PAID FOR BY A BODY UNDER A DECK, AND THE PRICE OF THE LAW IS THE PRICE OF THE HAND, AND THE HAND IS A MAN OF TWENTY-TWO NINETEEN MILES OFF WHO HAS LOST FOUR HOURS A DAY AND HAS BEEN TOLD, AND NOBODY IN THIS DISTRICT HAS EVER MET HIM AND HE IS NOT NAMED IN THE CHARTER AND HE IS NOT A PARTY.**
**WHAT IS NOT DECIDED IN THIS ROOM: WHETHER THE SPRING RANKING IS FAIR. WHETHER A ROOM IS WORTH LESS THAN A BODY. WHETHER A BENCH OF FOUR MAY ASK FOR A MINUTE OF A BENCH FOUR MILES OFF. WHETHER THE TWELVE SHILLINGS WILL BE OVER THE LINE TWICE OR THREE TIMES.**

---

And then the second half of the afternoon, which was not about the money and was about the unit, and it is the reason Meridith Vale walked four miles instead of sending a letter, because he did not in fact have a reason to walk four miles about the unit and he came anyway.

Nell Ambrey put it on the table. It is about fourpence a year to write down and it took eleven days to find out whether it was fourpence or nothing, and on the fifteenth of the fourth month a clerk of this bench wrote down that this district's own rate is a rate for a load, and a load is what one man takes off the bank in one go on a cart, and there are no carts on the fen road, and therefore a load is a figure in a book and not a thing.

“A bench of four may agree a rate for a load in its own book, in its own hand,” said the convenor, “and may not appeal it, and nobody may appeal it, and it costs about fourpence a year to write down. Nobody has done it in eleven weeks. I am going to put it that we do it today, and I am going to put it that we do it now, while a clerk from another district is standing in this room with a slate, because in about three weeks he is going to read a list out in front of forty people in his own district and I would like there to be one line in this book that is a fact about this district and not a bill.”

Oakes had it ready, because he is thirty-four and has held a seal for two years and has been unable to make a number add up since the fifteenth of the fourth month.

And then the cost, and it was not money, and everyone in that room had known it was coming for eleven weeks and nobody had wanted to be the one to say it out loud, and the person who said it out loud was the hauler.

“No,” said Wenna Tace.

“We have not asked you.”

“You have been asking me since the seventh of March,” she said, “and I have said no four times, and this is the fifth, and I am going to say it differently, because this is the fifth and I have had a letter from a recordwright and four days in a building and a letter with no reason in it to two people I am fond of, and I am in a mood.”

She put the barrow against the wall the way she does.

“To say what a load is worth, somebody has to say how many loads come off that bank in a day, in a week, in a year, in a tide, in a wet month, and there is no day in this district. That is not a thing I am refusing to say. It is a thing about the pit. The pit has them when the pit has them. I do not choose and I cannot choose and if I said a figure it would be a figure about the pit and not about me, and the day somebody wanted to be hard with me they would take my figure and say I said it about a day that never came, and they would be right, and I would have no way of showing that the day never came except by keeping it in my own head like everything else.”

“Then what is in the book,” said Corva Silt.

“What is in the book is that a load is a barrow in this district, and that the four in the guild's sentence is a fact about a cart and a cart does not come, and that this bench has said what a load is and has not said what a load is worth, and that the two debts in this book — ” She stopped and started again, and everybody let her. “The two debts in this book are mine and hers, and they are a hundred and fifty hauls and six thousand hauls, and they are both barrows, and a barrow is a load, and neither of them is money and neither of them can be money, and a bench that has agreed what a load is has not bought anybody anything, and the only thing it has done is make it plain in its own hand that two women in this district are owed a thing that has a name and no price.”

Nell Ambrey wrote it. It took four minutes. It is in the book of the bench of four of the district of Halloway and it is the shortest instrument this case has produced and it is the only one that says plainly that it has not done the thing it was made to do.

“Read it to me,” said Wenna Tace.

“It is in the book.”

“Read it to me anyway, because in about four years there will be a woman of sixty-one in this room who cannot read it, and she is going to be me, and she is going to ask somebody, and they are going to tell her that a rate for a load was agreed on the twenty-second of the fifth month by a bench of four, and she is going to say, and she will be right, *and what was it worth*, and there is nobody in this city who can tell her.”

Corva Silt read it out.

And then Meridith Vale, who had a slate and had not expected any of this and had come four miles for a different thing, did the thing that made the twenty-second of the fifth month a day people in two districts will argue about for a month, and he did it in one sentence, standing at a barrel lid with his finger on a chalk rate.

“Do you want to know what a load is worth in a room with a bench in it,” he said, “or in a room with forty ratepayers in it, three weeks from now, in my district, where none of them live in yours and all of them pay for the water their own children drink.”

“Which one is the one where they can be angry at somebody,” said Wenna Tace.

“Neither,” said Meridith Vale. “In the first room you can be angry at four people. In the second room you can be angry at me, and I am the one who writes the list out, and I have told you what I am going to do with it, and I have done it in your book and not in mine, which is the only favour I have.”

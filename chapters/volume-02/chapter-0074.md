# Chapter 74: A Burden in the Body, Taken

The stone came off the chapel flat on the second of the month, in eleven barrow-loads, and there were two of them working it who were not haulers, and neither of them had been asked.

It came off because the chapel had had a fortnight of nothing and the schoolmaster had not come and the fund that pays for the candles and the two bridges and the schoolmaster's four days a month is a penny a load at a bar nobody stands at, and in the middle of the month a waggon of hedge had come off the Orison road, had gone round the chain, had put six feet of a load on the chapel flat in the dark, and had gone on up the lane, and the bar is not a gate and the waggon was not refused because a waggon of hedge is not a waggon of draught. That is the clerk's own ruling and he was right and it is the whole of the trouble at Lowgill.

The stone was about the size of a chest. It had to go to the tip at the far end of the lane, which is four hundred yards, and there is no cart on that lane and no money to hire one, and the two persons who were on the chapel flat at the light were the woman who keeps the chapel book and the stranger.

She was fifty-one and had copied nine sheets by candlelight the evening before and had said, when she was asked, that she had done it because a bench of four could not read a ninth sheet out loud to itself.

“Give me the near end,” he said.

“You have not been asked for.”

“No,” he said. “That is why I am asking and not waiting.”

It took them the better part of a day for the eleven, and he took the near end and she took the far end because she had a grip of it and he did not, and the flat is soft on that side of the lane and the barrow came up twice and neither of them said anything about it. Four hundred yards, eleven loads, and a barrow is four loads to a cart, so the whole of that day's work was about two and three-quarter cart-loads, and a cart-load at the lane's own rate is about elevenpence, which makes the job two shillings and sixpence. There was elevenpence in the fund and it had been promised to the candles since the middle of the month.

He did that sum in his head on the second trip and did not say it out loud, and it is in the record afterwards, in a different hand, because Wenna Tace wrote it down on the fourth of the month when he came back down the fen road and told her about it, and she made him say it twice.

The leg was his own all morning. He put the token away on the sixth load and the low water came back in four layers in about a second and a half and the leg came back with it, and it thinned again in the ninth load on the flat with a barrow in his hands, and it did not go as a subtraction. It thinned, and the weight in it changed, and it became a tired leg, and then it stopped being his, and he had to put the barrow down on a stone on a chapel flat in front of about nine people who had come out of nine yards to find out what was happening, which is a thing he has never wanted and cannot stop, and which is the shape of the whole of this case: a man in a lane who is carrying something that is not his, and cannot put it down, and is watched.

Nobody helped him. The ninth bridge's tenth term says a district which pays a favour has learned to rule by habit, and he is on that flat because of a sentence he said in a room, and the term applies to him because there is no money to hire him and nobody to hire him to and he refused to be hired and said why out loud.

Marguerite Skeath stood on the other side of the barrow and did not ask and did not go for anybody, and the not-going-for-anybody took a long time and was the whole of what she did.

Then she said, “That is the cold coming up,” which is what people say about a leg in that weather, and he said yes.

He got the rest of it done in about two hours and it was slower and his left hand had gone by the eighth load and by the eleventh the skin across the base of his thumb had opened in four places and by the ninth bell on the third of the month it had not closed, and it was not closed on the seventh, and it was not closed on the eleventh, and on the fourteenth he had a scar at the base of his thumb that he will have for the rest of his life and that is the first thing about this that is going to be visible to somebody else in four years.

There is no line anywhere in this city that pays a man for having a leg. There is now no line anywhere in this city that pays a man for having a hand, and the charter of the district of Halloway has a pay line in it that pays a person at the moment of the work by name in chalk for work at the gate, and it does not reach eleven miles, and it would not pay him for this in any case, because the day was not hired and there is nobody to hire it and he would have refused to be hired and would have said the reason out loud, which is that a man who is paid to carry a stone for a chapel has made the chapel a district that pays a favour, and the whole of what that word *favour* is doing in the ninth bridge's tenth term is stop exactly that.

She gave him bread and a piece of cheese at the chapel door at the ninth bell and did not ask him anything and he ate it standing up, and then he sat on the flat with his hand open in his lap and looked at the tip heap for a while.

---

The token was warm at the fourth bell and he had not put his hand in his pocket.

It has never once been warm in a room and it was warm on a chapel flat in the open air, and he had learned since the sixth of the month that it is warm on a bank and it is warm on a flat and it has never been warm indoors, and that is a rule he has not tested and is not going to.

He put his thumb on it and the low water went out of his nose.

Not far away. Stopped, the way a note stops. The four layers went with it and they went all at once, in about two seconds, in the order they came, the day over the night, the flat over the water, the chain, and the door — and the leg went with them, and the hand stayed, which is the one thing about this that is a fact and not a story: the pain in his hand did not go, and he understood while it was happening that whatever the token takes, it takes from the other end of the ledger, and what it leaves is what he has actually done.

For fifty minutes he was a man of thirty-one sitting on a chapel flat in a lane in the weather, in a world with no layer on top of it, with a barrow-load of stone in the grass and eleven people in nine yards about forty yards away deciding whether to come over.

And the picture was there. It was there the way it had been there three times, and he had four minutes of it before it did the thing it had never done, which was that it stopped being a picture.

It had a floor.

That is the whole of what he got in the fifty minutes and he has gone over it every day since. On the sixth of the month it was a gate on a frame, from above and behind, at two hundred feet, in a light with no source, wrong hinge, wrong leaf, wrong proportion, the same every time from the same height. That is a *rendering*: a picture made for somebody, from a place somebody was standing.

He had spent ten days understanding that a rendering has a vantage and a vantage is a place and the place is not in this city. And on the second of the month, in fifty minutes, on a chapel flat, he got the next part of it, and it took him about two seconds to understand and then forty minutes to sit with, and the whole of the next part is five words: the vantage is a room.

Not a gate seen from a height. A room with the gate in it. There was a floor, and the floor was boards, and there was a table, and there was a lamp on the table with the wick down, and there was a window on the far wall with weather on the outside of it that was not this weather, and there was a chair, and there were people.

There were four people at that table and he could not see three of their faces, and the one he could see had her back to him and her right foot up on something and her hands on the table, and she was about forty-four, and there was no way in the world for her to be a figure he knew and Ilyan looked at her for about nine seconds and knew the shape of it and knew that he had never seen it and that he had been inside a rendering of a gate for a month and the gate was the subject and not the place, and the place is a room with four people in it, and one of them is a gate hand.

The vantage did not move. That is the part that came back and that he has written down twice. It was at the same height and the same distance on the sixth of the month before last, on a sill-stone at the foot of a gate, and it was at the same height and the same distance on a chapel flat, and the table in it was the size of a table you could put your hand on, which is not a thing that is true at two hundred feet and which he is not going to explain, because he has one fact and the fact does not close and he has learned in a month not to push on a thing that is not ready.

He put the token away on the sixth load and the low water came back in four layers in about a second and a half and the leg came back with it, and he finished the day in it, and the hand was the hand.

---

The cost came on the third of the month and it was not the hand.

It was the words.

He came up the fen road on the third at the fourth bell with his hand dressed in a strip off a shirt and a term's worth of nothing in his head, and Corva Silt had four sheets on the table that wanted checking against the fair copy because a clerk in a weighhouse office had found a discrepancy of one numeral in a figure that was not a figure, and she put them down in front of him and turned them round and said, "Read me the fourth line."

He read her the fourth line.

It is not a lie and it is not a failure of attention and it is the most frightening thing that has happened to him in this city. He looked at a line of about eleven words in a clerk's hand, on a sheet, four inches from his face, in good light, and he could see every letter of it, and he could see the figures, and he could see the seal, and there was no sense in it. Not foreign sense. Not the way a clerk's sentence had felt in the first week when he did not have the words for it. Nothing. The page was a picture of a page and he was on the other side of it looking at marks.

He said, “I cannot read that.”

Corva Silt did not move for about three seconds.

“Vester,” she said, “you read a nine-sheet charter to a bench of four last night.”

“No, I did not. A woman of twenty-nine read it to a bench of four last night and I stood next to her and I can tell you the order of the nine sheets and I cannot tell you what is in them.”

“How long.”

“I do not know. It began before the second bell and it is still here and the token is in my pocket and it is the fourth time it has ever done anything and the other three times it took something I can put a name to.” He put both hands flat on the table, and the left one hurt, and that was the small mercy in the middle of it. “Mrs Quill told me in the sixteenth that a one-use null passage was pulled up by a legacy emergency clause and that the clause is a way of getting a person into a field. I have not thought about that in nine days because there has been a great deal else. I have been treating the token as a thing that takes a smell, because the first time it took a smell, and it is not a thing that takes a smell. It is a door, and it is the only one I have got, and it is not getting cheaper.”

Corva Silt sat down. She did not write anything. That is the part he will not forget: a clerk of thirty-one with four sheets on her table and a pen in her hand, who has spent a month asking to be relieved of the print authority, sat down and put the pen down and did not enter it.

“If I do not enter it,” she said, “then in four years it is not on a page, and if it is not on a page then it is in two heads, and one of them is yours.”

“Yes.”

“I am not going to be that second head for free,” she said, “and I am not going to refuse to be it either, and I am not going to pretend that I have a trade's word for what I have just been told. I have a bench's words. I have got four of them that are about records and not about people, and I am going to use them, and they are these. I have entered a man as a reader. I have entered a man as a carrier. I have entered a man as the person who said a gate is not the problem. I have never entered a man as a thing that a device in his pocket does to his reading, and I am not going to start, and if the day comes when the day comes, that is what is in my head, and you will have to decide then whether to be in the room when I say it.”

He said, “That is fair,” and it was the only thing he had for about a minute.

“You cannot read.”

“I could not read from the second of the month until the sixth. On the fourth I could read the figures. On the sixth I could read the words standing up in daylight in a room, and on the seventh I could read them in the rain, and I do not know when it is coming back because I do not know how to ask the question. That is the other thing, because I have been in a city of instruments nine weeks being the man who is right about a number and wrong about the man, and this is the second thing I have been wrong about and it is me.”

Corva Silt said, “I have been wrong about two things this month and one of them was the first of the month and the other one is the man in front of me,” and she put the four sheets back in a pile and squared them, and said, “Sit down on the step and read me what you can when it comes back, and in the meantime I am going to read these out loud to myself in this room in a loud voice, because I have four sheets and a bench that has never read a line of a figure aloud, and that is a thing I can do in the next two days and I am going to do it.”

---

On the fifth of the month, in a room over a pump, Wenna Tace read out the whole of the charter to about two hundred people at the sixth bell, because the whole of it had never once been read out loud by anybody in that district, and she read all nine sheets and not only the barrel lid, and nobody had asked her to and she said so before she started and she asked for that to be entered.

What came of it is in the record, and it is the last thing the third month did. A man at the back of about two hundred people asked her a question about the fifth term, and she answered it, and he asked her a second question about the second half of the fourth term, and she answered that too, and he asked her a third one, which was whether the terms travelled.

“They do,” she said. “They travel to any district that asks. And I can tell you who carries them and I can tell you what it costs him and I can tell you that it is not me and it is not the bench, because it is a man and he is not one of us.”

“Can you come?”

And she looked at two hundred people and a barrow and a page with two entries in it and a line under the fifth term that she had known was there since the day the copy came and had not read, and she said:

“No. The fifth term says I am not a party and I cannot appeal and I cannot appear in support of one and I cannot be named in one. That is what holding this is. You cannot have the woman who reads it and the woman who takes it to the court both out of the same room, or every page in it is a closed door with me in it. So no. I cannot come.”

And the reading finished, and the quarter's rate was read, and about two hundred people went home up a mud bank, and the woman who cannot appeal swept up after them, and the barrow's handle was against the wall by the ninth bell.

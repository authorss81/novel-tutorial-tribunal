# Chapter 60: Four Hours, and a Name on a Chalk Line

The four paid hours began on the twenty-first at the fifth bell, when the flat is empty and the water is coming, and by the twenty-fourth they had run three days, and the first four hours this district had ever paid a wage for cost a quarter of what the work costs and the quarter is four hours out of a tide, and both of those facts were in chalk on a board at the gate side under the sheet with the seal on it, because Corva Silt would not post a wage anywhere that a person going for water would have to turn round to read it.

The board was a lid off a barrel and it had two lines on it and a third one under them in a different hand.

> FOUR HOURS A DAY FROM THE FIFTH BELL. BRYN SALLOW. THE DISTRICT'S OWN RATE. THE DISTRICT'S ROAD FUNDS. BY NAME. IN CHALK. EIGHT DAYS FROM THE TWENTY-FIRST.
> THE BALANCE OF THE TIDE IS NOT PAID AND IS ENTERED AS UNPAID AND IS NOT ENTERED AS THE WAGE OF A PERSON, BECAUSE NOBODY HAS ESTABLISHED A RATE FOR IT AND THE GATE HAND HAS REFUSED TO COUNT IT.
> THE THIRD LINE WAS SWORN IN THIS ROOM TWO BELLS AGO BY A LADY OF SIXTY-ONE WHO KEEPS A SEALED VOLUME UNDER A TABLE FOUR MILES UP THIS FEN ROAD, AND IT IS A SCHEDULE TO THAT VOLUME AND NOT A NEW INSTRUMENT AND NOT A NEW COLUMN, AND WHAT IS NEW ABOUT IT IS THAT NOBODY WILL TAKE IT.

The third line was there because Hanne Kolm had walked the four miles on the twenty-fourth, in daylight, two days after a ratepayer on Weir Lane had offered a cart to take a woman the other way up the road, and the cart was not wanted and the landlord came down instead, with the box under her arm, and would not let anybody carry it, and had said on the threshold of Corva Silt's office that she had sworn a volume about burdens and not about tenancies and that a volume is a thing you can lose and a column is a thing you have to look at, and that if the instrument did not come to the refusal then the refusal would come to the instrument, and that this was not a thing she had done before and she was sixty-one and had been asked.

She swore the schedule in the sanitation room at the fifth bell on the twenty-fourth, with the tide turning, and it took nine minutes, and it is the only sworn instrument in this city that has been sworn outside the district it was sworn in, and Harrow entered all four of the things and did them slowly.

> SCHEDULE TO A SWORN VOLUME, SWORN AT HALLOWAY THIS DAY BY H. KOLM, LANDLORD OF THE LOWER ROAD, OF A REFUSAL MADE AT THE LOW-WATER CULVERT ON THE TWENTY-THIRD.
> BURDEN REFUSED: THE GATE. FOUR HOURS A DAY, AND THE BALANCE OF THE TIDE, ENTERED UNPAID.
> PERSON WHO CARRIED IT INSTEAD: BRYN SALLOW, GATE HAND, THIRTY YEARS, AND NAMED HERE AS HER NAME AND NOT AS AN OFFICE.
> DATE, AND WHAT IT DID: THE GATE IS SHUT TO LOADS, AND THE FOUR HUNDRED AND ELEVEN LOADS ARE STILL ON THE FLAT.
> NO CONSENT. NO ADDRESS. THE ENTRY IS AGAINST THE BURDEN AND NOT AGAINST THE PERSON.
> THE PERSON WHO SWORE IT IS THE LANDLORD OF A ROAD FOUR MILES FROM THE PLACE IT WAS DONE, AND HAS SAID SO, AND THE COURT NEITHER ACCEPTS NOR REFUSES IT, AND IT IS HER DOING AND NOT THE COURT'S.

“That is not lawful,” said Corva Silt, three days after the swearing, in a lane, to nobody in particular, and then corrected herself in the same breath without waiting to be asked, which is a thing she learned in a room in about nine minutes and has not stopped doing. “It is not *provided* for. It is a sworn instrument sworn outside the ward by a person with no office in this district, about a burden she has never held and cannot pay, and if a clerk in this city wants to call it invalid then the invalidity is a fact about the instrument and not about the refusal, and the refusal stands either way, because the charter says a refusal is exercised and it does not say the refusal is conditional on anybody finding a column for it. So it is a bad instrument and the right is still good and both of those are true and I would like somebody in a court to explain them to me in a sentence longer than four words.”

Nobody did, and she went and turned the pump on for the day.

---

Wenna Tace got her eleven months on the nineteenth day, and she did not say thank you, and nobody asked her to.

What she said, to the bench, was: “Then it is paid, and I am going to take it, and I am going to make the run twice as often because I have money, and if the carts are not back on that road by the month’s end I shall be making the run twice as often and the carts will not be, and the smell will be the same, and the difference will be that I have been paid for it.” Corva Silt entered it, in the district's own hand, because it is the first time in this whole business that anybody has entered a want that is bigger than the thing it was got from.

Cael Rundle’s motion came back on the nineteenth as well, on a sheet from the weighing house, and Ordwin Sarn of the cut banks had written it in the middle of a letter about peat and had not added anything to it.

> **THE POND HAS A GATE AND NO BODY AND THE GATE HANDS ARE NINE. IN SIX YEARS I HAVE PAID EVERY ONE OF THEM ON MY OWN BOOK AND I HAVE WRITTEN THE HOURS IN MY OWN HAND AND THE HOURS HAVE NEVER BEEN CHECKED BY ANYBODY ELSE AND A MAN WHO IS DEAD CANNOT HAVE HIS HOURS DISPUTED. THAT IS NOT A COMPLAINT. THAT IS THE ANSWER TO YOUR MOTION AND IT IS FROM A SCALE-HOUSE.**

“It is from a scale-house,” Kett said, reading it on the bank, “and it is the second true document this month that has arrived from a building whose only function is weighing things, and both of them have been better than anything the four chambers have written, and I have been in this business nineteen years and I have stopped being able to explain to people what a scale-house is for.”

“It weighs things,” said Wenna Tace.

“It weighs things,” Kett agreed, “and that is exactly what I have stopped being able to explain.”

---

Ilyan's leg held for six days and then it did not.

That is the true shape of it and it is not a cure and it is not a rule. On six of the seven days from the twenty-first to the twenty-seventh the leg was his own in the morning and was gone by the fifth bell if he stood on a bank, and on the last of them he could not put his weight on it at all for about four seconds and had to be taken off his feet by two people, one of whom was Tarin Keel and one of whom was a boy of nine. It was his own again on the twenty-eighth, in a house, in a bed, with the door shut, and it was gone again on the twenty-ninth by the fourth bell, and nobody in this city knows which of those is the real one.

Bram Orren had come down the fen road on the twenty-sixth, on his own business, which is that a bell-runner's business is that a road is a road and he runs the line, and he had been up past the cut banks and had come back with a thing in his line book that he wanted to ask about and had asked about and got a straight answer to and had written down.

He said it on the bank, at the sixth bell, sitting on a barrow with his boots not touching the ground, and he said it the way he says everything, which is as a fact about a road.

“There is a toll at Carrisand, on the road past the cut banks,” he said, “on the far side of the water where the two districts meet. It has been taken for eight years. It is an eighth of a load, same as the ninth bridge's, and it has never once been short, and the book is in a hut and a woman takes it, and she is seventy-one and her name is Wen Lask and her father took it before her and his father took it before him, and they have never had a district. They have got a keeper and a book and a gate and that is the whole of it and the road is good and the carts do not break anything and the loads go through.

“The book has a list of hands against the ninth bridge's terms. I saw it because I asked to see it, because I ask to see everything, and Mr Sarn's man said no and then said yes because I am seventeen and he wanted somebody to say that it is normal.

“Eleven names. All dead. The last one died in the year my father stopped speaking to me and that is the year I ran the line for the first time. And they are not names of witnesses, they are names of the men who accepted the toll when the toll started, because that is what the form says, and the form is the ninth bridge’s settlement, copied out term for term, and the form has a line on it for a person to be named against each term, and the names went in dead, and the last one of them was her father, who was sixty when the toll started, and he did it for six years, and then his daughter did it, and she is seventy-one now.

“And here is the thing I do not understand and I have been walking two days to find somebody to ask. I asked Wen Lask how a person challenges a toll. And she said they cannot. And she did not say it crossly. She said it the way you would say the road goes up there. She said: *you would have to give notice to every party named on the form, and they are all dead, and the clerk's office that took the form closed the week the form went in, and the desk is a desk.*”

The bank was quiet in the way a bank is quiet.

“So I have written it down,” Bram said, “and I have not put it in anybody's file, and I am telling you because you are the only man I have met in two years who has ever written down a thing that was going to be used against somebody and then got a worse week out of it. If you chase it you will find a place where a rule is being kept with nobody in it and everything in it is true, and the road is good, and the toll pays a woman, and if you go up there with your record and your name on it, then in about a year that road will be a bad road for the only reason that anybody in that family has ever heard of, which is that a man from the city came and asked.”

He got off the barrow.

“That is not me saying do not do it. I do not know. I am seventeen and I run a line. I am saying that I have written it down and the writing is in a book and a book in my hand can be lost, and if you want it to not be lost then somebody has to be told, and she has said out loud in a room that she has sworn one already and that nobody will take it, and that is the whole of the road I can see from here.”

He nodded at the gate, where Bryn Sallow was standing with one foot on the sill-stone and one on the bank, in the position she had been in for thirty years and had never once been asked to leave, and who would not know he had done it.

---

Halloway's bench sat on the twenty-eighth and the only thing it did that anybody outside that building will ever hear about was to enter the district maintenance clerk's refusal, in the clerk's hand, with the hour on it, and the other three lines in that day book are the district's own business with a haul, a motion, and an hour of a gate hand, and none of the four is a wage. The board at the gate with a name in chalk on it has been up since the twenty-first and the bench did not touch it. Ilyan was not in the room for any of it and was four miles up the fen road on a span that had been open to foot and handcart and wheelbarrow for nine days with a west seat that had five days of Puck's fortnight still to sit in and nobody on it but a keeper and a counter and a bell rung at every change, and neither of those places knew anything about the other one, which is the way a city of nine bridges works and is the reason the word *exception* travels faster than a charter.

He was on the deck at the fourth bell on the twenty-ninth when a boy of nine came up the lower stair and asked for him by name, which is a thing that does not happen to a man without a constituency, and the boy said his name was Miko Ferren and that his mother was at the standpipe and that Wenna Tace had said that the man with the sentence on the slate was on the bridge and had better come before the sixth bell.

The sentence is the one about a trespasser. It is nine words and Ilyan had it in a guild's hand in a count book and a copy of it was in a court file in a town four miles away, and he read it out on the approach at the fifth bell to a boy of nine who had watched a load of district sand go back up a hill on the fourteenth and had asked his mother twice whether that made him a trespasser.

He read it badly. He read it the way a man reads a thing that he has heard four times and has not had to learn, which is quickly, and without the two sentences before it and the two sentences after it, and the boy's face did not do anything at all for about four seconds.

Then Miko Ferren said: “What is a register?”

And Ilyan found, with a clarity he would have called humiliating in anybody else, that he did not know. He knew what the ninth bridge's register was — eight feet of board, eleven hundred and six names, four hands, four decades, and a line scratched under the last one by an unknown hand nine years ago — and he knew that a strip of board a foot and a half long was nailed beside it with three lines on it and twenty-three names and a child's careful hand under them, and he could not put either of those into a sentence a boy of nine would understand, because it would require the word *sworn* and then the word *column* and then the word *burden*, and he had about eleven seconds.

“It is a book of names,” Wenna Tace said, from about nine feet away, in the flat voice, “and it is for people who cross, and your name is not in it, and I am not going to read him anything else, and tell him the next part.”

“The next part is that it does not matter,” Ilyan said, “and that he is not a trespasser, and that being missing from a book is not a crime, and that a person who is not in a book can still walk to a pump and does every morning, and that this is a sentence in a charter and not in a gate, and that a man on a bridge in a city four miles away lost a room over it once and a girl of fourteen was called a trespasser by a carter on this account, and the sentence is the reason I was not allowed to change the word, and the word is the word that is on the sheet nailed to that gate.”

Miko Ferren looked at the slate. “Is that all of it?”

“That is all of it,” Ilyan said.

“Can I be in it?”

He did not understand the question for a second, and then he did, and it went through him in about the same place the layer under the smell went through him that morning, which was a place he had not had before the nineteenth of the unloaded period.

“No,” Ilyan said. “You cannot be in it. Nobody can add you to it and nobody can be added, and that is the point of it, and the point of it is on a board a foot and a half long with three lines on it and a girl’s handwriting under them, and it says *these are names of persons who crossed, they are not a register and they replace nothing*.”

“Then I don't want to be in it,” said Miko Ferren, with enormous finality, and went and looked at the bell on the post to see whether it was the same as the one on the gate, which it was not, and which he was right to check.

And the leg went while he was doing it.

It went the way it had gone on the nineteenth, in about four seconds, from the hip, and Ilyan sat down on the sill of the keeper's hut on a propped bridge four miles from a culvert with a boy of nine's hand on his shoulder and a bell on a post, and the low water came up in his nose in two layers, the day over the night, the flat over the water, and under both of them a thing that was not a smell and had never been a smell, and it was a chain on a gate moving about four hundred yards away at the wrong end of the afternoon, in his right hand.

He had twenty minutes of it and then it went back to being a leg.

That is the honest account of what the reading costs, and it is not a fairy tale and it is not a curse, and it has been eleven days since the nineteenth and it has not resolved itself, and nobody in this city has a name for it. Four people have heard him say it out loud — a recordwright, a trialwright who entered it in a field without asking for an explanation, a record hand who wrote it down at the direction of the bench and was told nothing further, and a magistrate who walked into a bank and did not pretend she had not read the sentence on it. A fifth has it and never heard him say a word of it, and that is a route-arbiter, and he said the true sentence about it first, in front of sixty people, and was right, and then carried him off a step without making a thing of it. There is a sixth person who is the whole of it and has not been told and would not thank him, and whose afternoon is in his leg, and it is not going to be given back to her by any means available to him, ever, in his life.

---

The day-book line was closed out with the reverse of the board in it, a day after the wage stopped, and Larch came up with the roll because Larch comes up with the roll, and the four hours had been paid for eight days and the eighth day was the twenty-eighth, and a working tide at that gate is ten hours and a half by the gate hand's own count, so the four hours a day went on the board six and a half a day short of the work, and fifty-two hours of it went on the board as unpaid across the eight days, which is just under five working tides, and none of it was paid.

Nobody argued with him about it. Halloway's district maintenance clerk — a man of thirty-four called Oakes, who has held the seal for two years and had never once had to answer for a wage — had refused it in writing on the third of next week, in one sentence, on the grounds that the instrument H-9 did not require a watch and that a four-hour wage without an instrument behind it was a favour, and that a district which begins paying favours is a district which has learned to rule by habit, and that the office had no power to enter it and no line to pay it from, and that he was sorry.

That was the whole of the refusal and it was correct and it was Dain's argument from a month ago with his own name on it, and it had been right then too, and it killed a maintenance acknowledgment for a household that had never signed one.

Corva Silt had entered it anyway, on the board, in chalk, with the end date, eight days, and then on the twenty-eighth, the day the four hours ran out, she took the board down and turned it over and wrote on the back of it, in the district's own hand, what the district's clerk had written and the hour.

Then she carried it to the gate and put it on the gate-side board under the sheet with the seal on it, where about two hundred people a day go past it to the pump.

> THE FOUR HOURS ENDED WITH THE EIGHTH DAY AND THE DISTRICT WILL NOT CONTINUE THEM, AND THE CLERK'S REASON IS ON THE REVERSE AND IT IS CORRECT, AND A DISTRICT THAT PAYS A FAVOUR IS A DISTRICT THAT HAS LEARNED TO RULE BY HABIT, AND THIS DISTRICT HAS FOURTEEN FAVOURS ON ITS BOOKS AND NO WAGE LINE AT ALL.

Which is the ninth bridge's tenth term, and it is on the ninth bridge's day book, and it is in the Halloway bench's day book, and it is the reason a clerk in a district four miles off could not require a signature off a household with a bell and a child in it, and it is the sentence this whole business turns on, and nobody in nine districts has written a word of it and every person in it has paid a part of it.

Ansa Rell's clerk came up the fen road at the last bell with a docket, and Dessin Nahl read it on the step and then read it out loud, because that is what a clerk does with a docket in a lane.

Then he read the two notes underneath it, which are the part of a docket nobody ever reads out loud.

> **THE COURT'S OWN NOTE, FIRST, AND IT IS NOT A RULING AND IT IS NOT A DECISION: THE COURT CAN WRITE A CLEAN INSTRUMENT IN ONE AFTERNOON AND IT WILL CLOSE THE GATE AND IT WILL BE PAYABLE AND NOBODY WILL BE UNHOUSED. THE COURT CANNOT WRITE THE OTHER THING. FOUR BENCHES HAVE TRIED IT IN NINETEEN YEARS AND WHAT THE FOUR OF THEM HAVE PRODUCED IS ELEVEN INSTRUMENTS THAT WERE VALID AND NONE THAT WAS AFFORDABLE, AND THE COURT IS NOT BLIND AND THE COURT IS NOT OUT OF ITS DEPTH AND THE COURT IS AFRAID.**
>
> **THE COURT'S OWN NOTE, SECOND: THE MISSING THING IS NOT A RULE AND IT IS NOT A REMEDY. IT IS A PIECE OF PAPER THAT A PERSON CAN SWEAR TO IN A ROOM WITH NO ADDRESS IN IT, AND NO BENCH IN THIS CITY HOLDS ONE THAT TRAVELS, AND THE COURT HAS ASKED ELEVEN BENCHES WHETHER THEY HAVE ANYTHING THAT CAN BE SWORN FOUR MILES FROM THE ROAD IT WAS SWORN ON, AND ELEVEN BENCHES HAVE ANSWERED THAT THEY HAVE NOT.**

Sera read it twice and gave it to Ilyan and did not say anything about it for about a minute.

“They have asked,” she said.

“They have asked.”

“Eleven benches. And one sworn schedule, made on the twenty-fourth by a sixty-one-year-old landlord four miles outside her own district, standing in a room in a smell, about a burden she has never held and cannot pay, on a page that is a piece of paper sworn in a room with no address in it, and she swore it because there was nowhere else to put it, and the court has entered that it neither accepts nor refuses it.”

“Yes.”

“That is not a third thing,” Sera said, “it is a fourth thing, and I am not going to say the rest of it in a lane at the last bell of a day. This is my third term, and I have been carrying the terms of this matter to a district that has not asked me for sixteen days, and I have just found out what I have been carrying.”

She put her slate down on the sill of the hut.

“The hearing is on the twenty-ninth and I am not going to be able to write the thing that gets decided there, because I do not have it, and I have spent six years looking for it in a family chest and a guild book and a landlord's book and an archive, and it is a quarter of a sentence on the facing leaf of a page that is not on its shelf and has not been on its shelf since before I crossed, and the thread of the binding was cut and the slot is empty and the inventory mark beside it is in a newer hand, and the page says that a refusal is entered, and the entry is against the burden and not against the person, and the entry is open to the ward.

“Open to the ward,” she said. “Not sworn in the ward. *Open* to it. And I have been reading that line as a ward thing for a fortnight and it is not a ward thing, and I have been wrong in a lane, and I would rather be wrong in a lane than be wrong in a record.

“The entry is open to the ward. That does not say it is sworn in the ward, and it does not say the ward's clerk holds it, and it does not say the ward, and I have been reading a place into a sentence for six days because a place is what I had. A woman of sixty-one has answered that question without being asked, standing in a smell, four miles outside her own road, and eleven benches have written down that they have nothing, and neither of those things is a rule, and one of them is a person.”

“Twenty-ninth,” Ilyan said.

“Twenty-ninth,” said Sera, “and you will be in it with a name on a document and a leg that is not yours and a precedent that four districts are printing, and I will be in it with a quarter of a sentence and a sworn schedule, and there is no man in this city who can write the messy one, and I have spent a month finding that out in a room and a smell and a field, and I am not going to be sad about it in a lane.”

Below them the count came back at mark on a span with nothing on it, and on the last light load across the deck it had walked out one and a half and come back all the way, and the pale permanent set sat a finger's width from the iron ring where it had been sitting for six years and would sit for another five days before anybody could say a word about it, and the bell was rung for the change by a keeper who has looked at the pin on a named day by two hands, one of whom has pulled the rope.

Four miles down the fen road, the low water went out, and a woman let a handbell that was attached to nothing, and about two hundred people got off the flat and went up a mud bank in the dark, and on a barrel lid nailed to a board at a gate there was a name in chalk and a figure beside it, and the figure was four hours, and the four hours ended on the twenty-eighth, and the reason for the ending was on the back of the board in a clerk's hand, and the clerk had put it where people could read it going for water, which is the only kind of public that exists in a poor district, and which is why everything that has ever worked in this city has worked at a pump.

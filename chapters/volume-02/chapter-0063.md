# Chapter 63: A Count in a City With No Addresses

A magistrate can sit on a step in the open air in Orison and she can sit on a mud bank four miles down a fen road, and Ansa Rell chose the bank on the thirty-first at the fifth bell, and gave her reason before anybody asked, and the reason was Sel Harrow's and she said where she had got it.

“A category is settled by paper,” she said, “and this court has now spent two days settling a category with paper. We are going to go and find out how many people there are.”

Dessin Nahl came with the docket wrapped in oilcloth against the wet, and Odile came with her because a court that goes somewhere has to have somebody who can read in it, and Roke came with the record, and Harrow came because Harrow comes, and Tarin Keel came and put a rope and four stakes up at the top of the bank before anybody had decided where the court would stand, which is the ninth time he has done that in five weeks and the first time anybody has thanked him for it.

They stood on the flat side, which is the side the rule puts people on, and the field was on the mud above the standpipe with a hundred and forty feet of rope and the queue going past the end of it, and the count began at the sixth bell because that is when the water comes and that is when the district is in one place.

Wenna Tace had the tally. She had asked for it, which nobody had expected, and she had asked for it in front of eleven benches' worth of a magistrate's court, and what she said when they asked her why was: “Because you are going to write down how many, and if I am not the one writing the numbers then somebody is going to write down a number I could have stopped.”

---

Here is the count, and it is the count and not an estimate, and it is four numbers taken by a woman with a barrow's handle and a slate over two days, and every one of them is a different kind of number and none of them is a person.

> **AT THE STANDPIPE, THE TWENTY-EIGHTH DAY. THE SIXTH BELL: FORTY-ONE. THE NINTH BELL, AFTER THE SECOND SHIFT COMES ON AT THE EIGHTH: NINE. THE SEVENTH BELL, BEFORE THE SECOND SHIFT: NINETEEN.**
>
> **THE TRAVELLER BOOK AT THE GATE SIDE, FOUR INCHES THICK, A HUNDRED YEARS OUT OF DATE: NINE NAMES ENTERED IN THE HUNDRED YEARS IT HAS BEEN KEPT. THREE OF THEM ARE CROSSED.**
>
> **IN THE FIELD, BEHIND THE ROPE, AT THE SIXTH BELL: TWO HUNDRED AND FORTY. OUTSIDE THE ROPE, PASSING THE GATE ON FOOT OR CARRYING SOMETHING: NOT COUNTED, BECAUSE A PERSON GOING PAST IS NOT A PERSON IN A PLACE.**
>
> **THE FLAT: FOUR HUNDRED AND ELEVEN LOADS, ON THE TWENTIETH, COUNTED FROM THE FLAT BY I. VESTER ALONE, AND STATED BY HIM TO BE WRONG BY ABOUT FORTY.**

The second finding was Tarin's, and he made it before the count was half done, and he made it to nobody in particular, which is how a neutral boundary says a thing that applies to a whole field.

“Forty-one at the sixth bell and nine at the ninth is a queue and not a district,” he said. “Two hundred and forty behind a rope is a crowd and not a ward. I have counted fields with a hundred and forty feet of rope in them since I was twenty-two and not one of them has ever held a ward, and I am not saying that to be clever. I am saying it because you have come four miles to count people and there is nobody in this field who has said where they sleep, and in this city I do not think anybody knows, and I would like somebody to tell me I am wrong.”

“Can you give me a number for the number that slept,” said Rell. “Somebody give me a number for the number that slept last night and I will put it in the record and I will say where it came from.”

Nobody gave her a number, and about nine people tried to and stopped, and one of them was Rundle and what he said was: “I have got a figure and I am not going to give it, because if I give it to this court I have given the court the tenancy roll of Weir Lane, and I have been thinking about that for a day and a half and I have decided that a ratepayer of Weir Lane is not the person who hands a court the roll of Weir Lane, and I would like that in the record as a refusal that is not about the law.”

“Enter it,” said Rell, “as a refusal by a ratepayer, and enter that the reason given is that he is a ratepayer of that lane and not a man without a district, and enter that this is the third refusal entered in this matter by a person with something to lose, and that the court is beginning to understand that the refusals and the accounts come from the same people and that this is not a coincidence and is probably the finding.”

---

The count failed at the second change of the tide, and it failed the way a count fails, which is honestly, in daylight, and in front of the people it was counting.

They had forty-one and nine and nineteen and two hundred and forty, and Roke had it in a fair copy, and Rell stood up to read the returns into the record, and got as far as the heading.

“The persons of the low water, of the bank, and of the district of Halloway,” she said, “this day counted, being —”

And she stopped, and looked at the slate, and everybody in the rope understood at the same moment what the sentence needed and that the sentence did not have it.

“Where are they sleeping,” she said. “I cannot enter that. I have four numbers and I have no column to put a person in. I have four numbers and a place, and the place is the low water, and the low water is a hundred and sixty years of road book, it is a tide, it is flat mud with a hundred yards of it in the wet, and in nineteen years when somebody reads this return they are going to read *the persons of the low water* and they are going to think it is a place with a population and it is not a place with a population, it is a bank with a pump on it and a lane behind it, and I have just written the exact mistake this court came down here to find.”

It is the only time in five weeks that Ilyan has seen a magistrate with nothing to say, and it lasted about four seconds, and then she said:

“Then we will do it the way it is done, and I want it watched.”

They counted by place. Not by address, because there is no address, and there is no address because the ward is not an address and the ward has never been an address and the ward hall has a petition on its wall from nineteen years ago that nobody has read. They counted by the places a person in that district actually is, and the list took ten minutes and the ten minutes were the argument: the bank above the standpipe; the flat; Weir Lane behind the pump, with the room over the pump inside it; the upper street; and the two yards at the end of it that have no name at all and that a woman of twenty-nine called *the bit by the wall* and *by the pump*, and both of those went into the return exactly as she said them, with her name against them, because a court that puts a made-up name in a record is worse than a court that admits there is none.

And the return is four columns and a place in every line, and the places are five and not eleven, and the count of the places is the finding, and it took the magistrate until the second bell to say it out loud, and she said it in a voice that Ilyan has not heard her use before and has thought about since.

“That is the ward,” she said. “Not the people. The five places. And the ward has been a word for five places in a poor district for a hundred and sixty years, and the city has been paying a rate on it out of the water at those five places, and the rate has bought a woman with a chalk line and four hours and a district that has learnt to rule by habit, and the ward as a thing to be done is being done by a woman who is not in it and has not been in it for thirty years, and the ward as a place cannot be written down because there is no such place, and I have spent two days in this city finding that out and it has taken four sentences and a pump.”

Nobody wrote that down for about nine seconds and then Corva Silt wrote all of it down in the district's own hand.

---

Bram Orren came down the fen road on the second of the month and was in the rope for two hours and was the most wanted man in the field, and he had not come to be wanted.

He was seventeen and he ran a line and the line goes over the ninth bridge and down the fen road, and a bell-runner who runs a line comes down a road every third day whether or not there is a court on it, and he said so before anybody spoke to him, and he said it to the magistrate, which was either enormous cheek or the correct procedure and nobody in the field could decide which.

Dessin Nahl asked for the book. She asked for it in the way a clerk asks for a thing, and she asked for it because a clerk in a field with a person in it who is the only record of what crosses wants the record, and Ilyan saw her do it and wanted to stop her and did not, because she was doing her job and he had no standing in this field and had been told so on a step.

“Roke says you have a line book,” she said. “The court would like to see it.”

“No,” said Bram.

“What did you say?”

“I said no,” said Bram, “and I would like to say why in front of all of you, and then I will hold the rope for the count if somebody wants a rope held.”

He put the book down on the mud, flat side up, and did not open it, which was the most eloquent thing he did all week.

“A line book is not a register. That is the whole of it and I am seventeen and I have run the line since I was fourteen and I have learned that in the way you learn things when you are on your own, which is by having it go wrong. A line book says what crossed. It does not say who. On the sixth I wrote the number of carts by where they came from and I wrote a hundred and sixty-one barrows and I did not write one name, and it is not because I do not know names. It is because a name on a line goes past and comes back and does not stay where I put it.

“If this court puts my book in its record then every person in it is a number that crossed a place. And a number that crossed a place is a cart. And there is a thing in this city that turns a person into a cart the moment she is written down as one, and it happened to a woman eleven years before any of you were in this case, and she got eleven months of arrears out of it by accident, and she is the best thing that has happened in this case and she nearly did not get it.”

He picked the book up.

“I will tell you the two things in it that are useful and I will tell them in front of witnesses so that they are somebody else's numbers and not mine. One: I can tell you how many people crossed the weighhouse on any day you ask me since I was fourteen, to the best of a man walking a road with a cart, and I have never been out by more than nine in a fortnight. Two: I can tell you that in the two weeks after the sheet went up on that gate, the number of people who went down that road and did not come back up it was two hundred and nine, and I could not tell you one of them, and neither can anybody, because the road is a road and people go down a road and come up a road and nobody asks them.

“That is the whole of what I have. It is not a constituency and I have been told twice now and both times by people I would have helped. Give it to somebody else.”

Harrow said, “Enter that the witness declines, and enter that the witness is right, and enter that this court has wanted the book and was refused and that the reason given was a better reason than the court had for wanting it.”

“Nine names is nothing,” Bram said. “The book would be worse. You would have nine hundred and the nine hundred would be a number, and the number would be in a form, and somebody would print the form.”

---

The count of the lane behind the pump was where the day turned, and the day turned on a man of fifty-four who took a shilling a week off forty roofs and had never written a name on anything in his life.

He did not want to be counted. He came down to the bank because a bench of four sent a clerk to say there was a court on it, and he stood outside the rope for about ten minutes with his hat in his hand, and then came inside, and did not say anything, and let the count go round the lane, and it went round the lane in about forty minutes because it is one lane and forty doors and the count was done at the doors because you cannot count a lane from a pump.

The count found thirty-one households in Weir Lane and nine in the two unnamed yards, and forty roofs, and a district clerk who did not know there were forty roofs in it and had had a rate proposed on a lane and had had to find out how many people were on the lane in order to tell them what they would be paying, which is the first time in nineteen years that a bench in that district had asked a question about the size of the poor instead of the size of their rate roll.

And the clerk asked, at the pump, in front of eleven benches and a magistrate and forty people, in the ordinary way a clerk asks a thing:

“Mr Bream. How many names have you on that roll.”

Bream said, “Forty.”

“How many of them are on the traveller book at the gate.”

“None of them,” said Bream. “I did not know there was a book until you came down here. There is a book at a gate on a low water and I have been taking a shilling a week off forty people for nineteen years and I have never once had cause to look at it.”

“Enter that,” said Rell, and Roke wrote it, and Rell said, “and enter the next thing, which is that this is the only list of forty persons in this district in the world, and that it is a rent roll, and that it is a list of numbers and not of names, and that it is unsworn, and that the man who holds it has never sworn anything and never written a name on anything, and that the only reason the court knows it exists is that the court needed a denominator.”

And then she asked him the question a court asks once, and Bream answered it in four sentences, and it is the four sentences that made the rest of this case possible, and it is the reason there is going to be a fifth term in a charter at all.

“No,” he said. “I will not swear it. Not because it is not true — it is true, forty is forty, and every one of them pays me on the same day of the week and I have never once been cheated, and that is more than the traveller book can say about its own six. I will not swear it because a sworn list is a list somebody can use, and the day I put my hand to a page that says there are forty of us in Weir Lane is the day that page is a thing this bench can come back with, and the day after that is the day somebody comes for the lane. I have been a name in this case twice — a landlord of a road four miles off, and a man who takes a shilling a week — and I have been a body zero times, and I have got to fifty-four the way you get to fifty-four by never being anything, and I am not going to stop being nothing in a fortnight because a magistrate is on my bank.

“You can count my lane,” he said. “You can put forty on a page and call it forty and say where it is. You cannot have the names and you cannot have my oath, and if you come back for them I shall be here, and I shall still say no, and I shall not be angry about it, because I have had this conversation with myself for two days and it was not difficult.”

---

The cost of the count landed at the seventh bell and it landed on a person who had not asked to be in a room and could not get out of it.

The return had four columns and a place, and Corva Silt had added a fifth column because a district clerk counts households, and Rundle had looked at it and had not said anything for about a minute, and then had said:

“Take that column out.”

“It is the household column,” said Corva Silt. “Children are counted for the water. That is not a law anybody made, it is a thing that has been done since before me, and if I do not count them then in a quarter of a year the water is carried for forty people and there are fifty-two, and somebody has to carry the extra twelve buckets, and it will not be a man.”

“Then count them in a mark,” said Rundle, “and not against my name, and put the mark in the margin and not in a column with a name in it.”

“There is no other way to write it.”

“There is another way to write it,” said Rundle, “which is that you write the number and not the name, and the number is what a carter calls a house, and I have had a carter say a word to my daughter on this account and I am not having it written on a page in a court in Orison where a man in a book in four years reads it back and says there is a child of fourteen in that lane with nobody's business knowing it.”

Corva Silt looked at the column for a while and then she took the pen and put a line through the whole of it, in the middle, in the district's own hand, and entered in the margin under the heading that the household count is kept as a number and not against a name, and that the parent may see it and may ask for it to be taken out, and that a person of fourteen in that lane has been counted as a number and is not named in this return, and is not named in any page of this court.

And that cost the lane the ability to be counted accurately in future, which is a real thing and was entered by the clerk herself as a fact against the district and not against him, and which is why the estimate of the lane in this record is thirty-one households and is a number one man read off his own book and is wrong by at least one on his own side, and everybody in the rope knew it and nobody said so.

Wenna Tace objected to the same column for the same reason from a different direction and said so out loud, and then said the sentence that made the rest of the day worth doing.

“You have just built it again,” she said, to the magistrate, not unkindly, because she was talking to a woman who had listened to her for two days. “You came down here to find out who the ward is and you have found out where five places are and you have written them in a column, and the five places are a category, and the category is the thing you came to get rid of. You cannot count people in this district without deciding what a person in this district is, and you have decided it four times this morning and it is a place every time, and a place is what a gate is closed to.”

“I know,” said Ansa Rell.

“I know you know,” said Wenna Tace. “I am saying it because in about four years somebody is going to read that return and think a court went down to a poor district and counted the poor, and the reason it will not be any use is that it counted five places. That is not a complaint about the court. That is the court doing it in front of me so that nobody can do it in a room in Orison in the dark.”

---

They came off the bank at the ninth bell with the tide coming and the count written down in a fair copy that Roke had read back three times and Corva Silt had corrected twice, and Ansa Rell read the return into the record in the open air on a step of a bank, standing, with her hood off, in a smell, and entered that the count was taken by the reader of the district at the magistrate's request and not by a clerk, and entered that the magistrate had asked in advance for a number for the number of people who slept and had been refused a number by a ratepayer of the lane, and entered that the witness who was the only record of what crossed the road had been asked for his record and had refused it and had been right, and that the count stands.

And then she said the thing that sent the room home and sent the whole case to a date.

“This court came four miles to find out what a ward is, and it has found that a ward is five places and a duty nobody does, and that there is no number for the people asleep, and that the only list of forty of them is a rent roll held by a man who will not swear it and has told this court why, and that the only record of who crosses is a boy's line book which he has refused to give this court and would have been wrong to.

“I am not going to sit here and make a category out of that. I am going to take it back to Orison and put it in front of two texts, and the clean one is on a step and the other one is a set of people who would have to put their names to a paper that does not exist, and the count tells this court that the names are forty doors on a lane and a rate roll and a woman with a barrow and a man with a gate, and not one single body among them, and that a charter with a hole in it is still a charter, and a charter with a body in it is a person who can be asked to hold it and can say no.

“The choice is on a date. It is on the day the pending challenge is heard, because that is the only date in this city that is not mine to move, and I am not going to pretend that the two texts are equally good and I have already said in the open air that they are not.”

They came up the fen road in the dark, four of them, and the guild's four benches were still on the bank because nobody had come for them and the bank was going under at the top of the tide, and Ilyan carried two of them back up to the road on his own with a leg that had gone at the second change and had come back at the eighth, and Tarin Keel took the other two without being asked and without saying anything, and at the top of the road he said one sentence, and it was not about the benches.

“Two hundred and forty in a rope,” he said, “and about nineteen asleep in Weir Lane behind a shut door with a fever on, and I did not ask, and neither did she, and there is no line anywhere in this city on which the number of people who are not at the pump goes. That is not a finding. It is what I do not know.”

The road was empty, and the low water was under four hundred yards of water and a hundred and sixty years of road book, and the fifth place on the return, which is two unnamed yards at the end of a lane, was on the other side of it and had been entered by a woman of twenty-nine who would be back on it at the fifth bell in the morning with a barrow and forty more loads than yesterday, because the carts still were not going down, and the rate had not been made, and the count had not changed anything at all except that in a fair copy in a magistrate's hand, in a record in a city four miles away, a lane called Weir and a yard called the bit by the wall had been written down for the first time in a hundred and sixty years, with numbers against them, by a court that had come to find out what a poor person was and had found out where they were, which is not the same thing, and is not nothing, and is going to have to be paid for.

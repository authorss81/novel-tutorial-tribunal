# Chapter 52: The First Honest Use

The water was let at the second bell and Wenna Tace made the room empty.

That was the first thing that happened in the sanitation room on the thirteenth day, and it happened before anybody had said a word, and it happened because about half the people in it lived on Weir Lane and the water comes out of the low water once a day in front of everybody and you do not miss it if you are going to miss anything.

So the bench sat for twenty-two minutes with an empty room, and Corva Silt entered the fact in the day book with the hour, because a clerk enters the fact of an empty room when a bench has been waiting for the room.

Nell Ambrey did not use the time. She sat with her hands flat and let the room go and then let it come back, and when it came back it came back wet and it came back late, and that is a thing a convenor of a poor bench has to be able to do.

Sera Quill got there at the fifth bell, on foot, having walked the four miles in the dark with the read-back inside her coat and a lantern she did not light, because the fen road has stakes on it that a lantern makes a cart see.

She put the read-back on the table face down, and then she did not turn it over, and she said:

“Before anything else. I am not a witness for Mr Vester. He is attached to his own observation and he has no standing in this room and he cannot speak for anybody in it, including himself. I am a licensed recordwright of a public record. I did not bring you a settlement to follow. I brought you nine sentences in a keeper's words, because a licensed recordwright of the ninth-bridge matter carries the terms of that matter to any district that asks, and you have not asked, and I am going to read them anyway, and you may stop me at any point, and if you do I will go and stand in the lane.”

“Read them,” said Cael Rundle. “And read them slow, and read the numbers, because the numbers are the part that is not an argument.”

---

The read-back is nine sentences and it took eleven minutes and everybody in that room heard all of it, twice, because she read it once and Cael Rundle asked for it again and she read it again without being asked twice.

> A keeper may not stop a load.
> **What a keeper may do is call the deck clear, and the deck being clear is the whole of it.**

and

> A pause lasts as long as the loads standing on the deck, and when the deck is clear the pause is over. If the deck is not clear inside that time, the bell is a warning and the span is released, and no keeper has held anything.

and

> **The ward rate is paid at the moment of the work to the person performing it, by name, on a day-book line, in chalk. An empty line is a record. A full line is a wage.**

and the twenty-third sentence, which a keeper's counter wrote herself:

> **A person who is not on a register is not a trespasser because of it. That is the whole sentence and it is not the other sentence, and a district that wants the other sentence has to write the other sentence, in its own hand, and put its own name on it.**

The room did not obey it.

The room did the thing that rooms do with a text they have been handed, which is that it turned it over and looked for the part that would get somebody something, and it found that part in under two minutes, and it found it because a woman with a barrow was already standing up.

Wenna Tace said: “That last one. The not-a-trespasser one. That is me.”

“That is you,” Sera said. “It is you, and it is four hundred people at the fen wharf, and it is anybody who has ever come to this district with a cart and been told the road is not for them. It is not a promise that you may pass. It is a promise that you may not be called a trespasser *because* you are missing from a book. A district may still make a list, and this district has made one, and the list is a hundred years out of date, and the sentence does not touch the list. It only takes the word off you.”

“Then take the word off me in writing,” said Wenna Tace, “because I have been hauling out of this low water for eleven years and the district has paid me forty-one times and I have hauled two hundred and six loads a year and last year two hundred and nine, and the pay book says *slops, W. TACE* and then a number that is about a third of a load, and the number has been the same number since the year my mother died, and I have never had anybody say a word to me about it except a recordwright from a town with an archive in it, who walked down here on the first day and told me a sentence and then told me it was not hers.”

“It is not mine,” Sera said. “It is the ninth bridge's, and it is in a charter, and Mr Vester had nothing to do with it and has told me so in front of people, which is a thing a man does not usually do.”

“Then the bridge's sentence is worth eleven months of that number to me,” Wenna Tace said, “and I would like it paid today, and I would like it paid on a line with the loads on it, and I would like to be in the room when it is.”

The bench could not pay it.

It is a hundred and forty years since the ninth bridge's ward rate was in this district's hands, and Halloway's district rate is a district rate, and the district pays a district, and the district's answer, when Corva Silt put it in front of the bench in four sentences, was that the question was not who owed the money but *which rate applies to a road that is not a span*.

It is a good question. It is a better question than anything Orison's court had asked in a month. It went around the room twice.

The answer came from the gate.

Bryn Sallow said it while standing, and did not sit, and did not explain it twice, and the words she used were the words on a board on a bridge four miles away: “A rate is a number and not a body. You have been saying *a road that is not a span* like it is a different thing. It is a different number. You pay your number to the person, same as she does. That is all the ninth bridge's sentence says about paying. It does not say you may not pay your own.”

“Why can we not just pay our own rate?” said Corva Silt, who had been the clerk for six years and had a fair amount of paper on her and no money in any of it.

“Because the district's rate has got a rate in it for a *haul* and a rate for a *lift*,” said Bryn Sallow, “and neither of them is a rate for a woman pushing a barrow up a bank two hundred times, and the number on the pay book is neither of those, and that is why it has not moved in eleven years. Nobody set it wrong. Nobody set it.”

They read the pay book out in the room. It took a while because Corva Silt had to fetch it, and while she was gone the room talked, and what it talked about was not the ninth bridge. This is worth saying, because it is the first honest use of a precedent that anybody in this city has recorded and it lasted about forty minutes: a room full of people who had come to be told what a bridge had decided used forty minutes to work out, from a pay book and a bench rule, how to get eleven months of arrears out of a sentence in a charter four miles up the road.

And then they got it.

The answer was a bench minute, not a charter term, and it was the plainest thing in the room: the haul of waste from a low water is work done for the district's health; the district's own rate for a haul is a rate for a haul; the fact that a person doing a haul is not entered in a book the district made in some other century is not a reason to pay her at a third of the haul; the eleven months are arrears of the district's own rate and are due, and the ninth-bridge sentence has nothing to do with them, and a court four miles up the road is not necessary, and the district is not admitting anything.

Corva Silt read it back before she entered it, which is the one thing she does that Neral at the lock-and-ledger would approve of and half the clerks in Orison do not bother with.

> THE DISTRICT OWES W. TACE THE BALANCE OF ITS OWN HAUL RATE ON FORTY-ONE HAULS OUT OF THE LOW WATER, TAKEN OVER ELEVEN MONTHS, AT THE DISTRICT'S OWN RATE AND NOT AT THE RATE OF ANY OTHER BODY. THIS IS ARREARS OF A WAGE AND IS NOT AN ADOPTION OF ANY INSTRUMENT OF THE NINTH BRIDGE.
> THE ONE HUNDRED AND FIFTY HAULS AND MORE THAT SHE HAULED IN THE SAME ELEVEN MONTHS, WHICH ARE IN NO BOOK OF THIS DISTRICT, ARE NOT ENTERED HERE, AND SHE HAS ASKED FOR THEM, AND THEY CANNOT BE ENTERED BY ANYBODY IN THIS ROOM BECAUSE NOBODY HERE CAN SAY WHAT A HAUL IS WHERE THERE IS NO CART.
> **PAYMENT WITHIN THE SIXTH DAY. THE HOUR OF WORK IS NOT TO BE DEDUCTED.**

The last line was Bryn Sallow's and Corva Silt asked whether she had authority to add it and Bryn Sallow said “I have been the gate for thirty years and I know what it is to have your hour taken off you at the counter,” and the line went in, and nobody took it out.

Wenna Tace did not thank the court, the bridge, or the room. She said, “Eleven months,” and went out to her barrow. It was the most restrained thing that happened on the thirteenth day.

---

They tried the sentence on Bryn Sallow at the seventh bell and it did not work, and the way it did not work is the whole of what the four miles between the two places is about.

Bryn Sallow stood up and said: “I am going to use that sentence now. That is me. Not on a register. Thirty years and I am not on a register, because there is no register, because the gate is a gate and it is not a span and there is nobody in this district who has ever written a list of gate hands. I am not a trespasser by that fact alone. So I am asking, in front of all of you, for the gate to be open to the people of this district on foot, and I want it written down that I asked.”

“Ask it and it is written,” said Corva Silt.

“I have asked it.”

“Then it is written,” she said, and wrote it, and the room waited, and nothing happened, and about eleven people understood at the same moment what had just occurred, and Wenna Tace understood it first because she reads.

“It has not done anything,” said Wenna Tace.

“It has been written down.”

“It has been written down in a book about a bridge,” said Wenna Tace. “That sentence is in a *bridge*. It is about a span. It is about a deck and a load path and a keeper and a bell. It is not about a gate and it is not about a lane and it is not about water, and the paper on that gate is the bridge's paper with *span* struck out and *district* written over it, and the bridge's paper is the only thing anybody has, and the one sentence in it that says she is not a trespasser does not reach her because it is about the *wrong span*.”

The room made the noise a room makes when a thing it has been pleased with turns out to be a different thing.

“Then get it changed,” said Cael Rundle.

Nobody answered that for some time.

Kett answered it, and he answered it well, and what he said was that it cannot be changed at the ninth bridge because the ninth bridge is closed to loads and propped over a channel and has an amended charter with a review set for the fourth morning bell of the third month, and that a clause about a district's lawful-traveler definition is not that bridge's to write, and that the district's *own* register is a district instrument and the district may amend it tomorrow morning if it wishes, and that the bridge's sentence would then be the bridge's again and would read the same way it reads now, and would still be a sentence about a span.

“So the answer is no,” said Nell Ambrey.

“The answer is that the one usable sentence in this city is about a bridge and it will not reach a gate until somebody writes a different one for a gate,” said Kett. “And I have been in this business nineteen years and I want to say — and I will say it in daylight — that the reason we do not have one is that a rule for a gate has never had anybody in it who needed one, because everybody who needed one was the gate hand, and gate hands do not sit in benches. They stand at the gate. That is what the word is.”

Nobody had ever said that in a room in this city, and Ilyan wrote it down, and had to stop writing and write it again from memory in his own bad hand because his hand had gone across the page.

---

Ilyan's whole contribution to the thirteenth day was a number and a refusal, and both of them were in his own language and neither of them was in anybody else's.

He had asked the standing question — how many people at the standpipe, at which bell, on which tide — and had got four numbers, and had them written on Coll's slate, and they were these:

> **QUEUE AT THE SIXTH BELL: FORTY-ONE. AT THE NINTH, AFTER THE PUMP'S SECOND SHIFT COMES ON AT THE EIGHTH: NINE. BY THE SEVENTH, THE LINE IS NINETEEN.**
> **CARTS TURNED BACK SINCE THE SHEET WENT UP: ELEVEN. LOADS LOST: ELEVEN.**
> **THE SAME NUMBERS ON THE HALLOWAY SIDE OF THE GATE, IN A GOOD WEEK: NINE AND FOUR.**

Cael Rundle looked at that for a long time and then said, “Nine carts, and you have eleven turned back this month.”

“Yes,” said Ilyan. “And that is not the interesting number. The interesting number is forty-one people at the sixth bell and nine at the ninth, and that is because the pump's second shift comes on at the eighth bell, so the sixth-bell queue is a queue of people who cannot afford to be somewhere else at the eighth bell, and the ninth-bell queue is a queue of people who can. Your water is not rationed by a sheet on a gate. It is rationed by a shift, and the sheet has not touched it, and the sheet has taken eleven carts, and the eleven carts are the ones that would have carried the smell out.”

“Then take the carts off the sheet and leave the people on it,” said Nell Ambrey.

“That is a decision,” Ilyan said, “and it is your decision and not mine, and I am not entitled to it, and if I said it out loud in a room four districts would say that a foreign record-holder told a poor district what its notice may say. Write it down as the convor's own.”

Nell Ambrey looked at him for a moment. “I have not read a sentence of the ninth bridge's charter,” she said.

“You do not need it,” Ilyan said. “You need your own. It is the same difficulty in a different room and the same difficulty is why I am not able to help you with it.”

It was the first time in a month that he had told the truth in a form that was useful to somebody else rather than useful to him, and it cost him nothing at all, and he wrote it down afterwards, in the lane, in the dark, with the tide coming in, and the entry read: *I read four numbers and I could not read one sentence, and the four numbers were worth more than the sentence, and I have no instrument for giving numbers to a bench and I do not know how to make one.*

---

They came off the fen road at the ninth bell and the lamp in the sanitation room had gone out, and Corva Silt was still at the table with the day book open, alone, doing the thing a clerk does at the end of a day, which is to enter the day in a hand that the next clerk can read.

“Miss Silt,” Sera said. “You were going to tell us about the fifth paragraph and you never got to it.”

“The fifth paragraph is the gate hand's pay,” Corva Silt said. “This district has no gate hand's pay line. The gate hand is paid out of the gate, and the gate pays out of the culvert fund, and the culvert fund is what the district takes the levy into, and there is no line in it for a woman, because for thirty years the woman has been the gate and the gate has been the woman and nobody has needed to write it down anywhere but the head of the gate hand. When the sheet went up, somebody in this office wanted a paragraph to point at, because a notice that says nothing about who opens the gate is a notice that says the gate opens itself. So I found a public instrument that says how a gate should be paid for, and I put it in, and I did not read it.”

“You did not read it,” Sera said.

“I read the fourth paragraph four times because it was the sentence I was putting my district's name into,” said Corva Silt. “I read the fifth once, at night, on a lamp, and it said a ward rate paid to a person at the moment of the work in chalk on a day-book line, and I thought: we have no ward. I did not think: we have a woman. I did not write her name on anything. I have been a clerk for six years and I have never had to write a name on a notice in my life, because everything this district asks for is asked for by people who already have the paper, and I did not know what to do with a paper that wanted a name.”

Nobody said anything to her for a moment, and the wind came under the door of the sanitation room and moved the pages of the day book, and Corva Silt put her hand flat on them.

“But I am going to have to write a name on something,” she said, “because the bench cannot sit on the sheet as it is, because a sheet that only shuts a gate does not answer a sanitation complaint, and so either we take the sheet down and the water comes up Weir Lane, or we replace it with a notice of our own that answers our own complaint, and there is a form for that, and I filled one in at the third bell today, and I am going to print it on the fourteenth, and it will be numbered, because everything this district prints is numbered, and once it is numbered it can be numbered again.”

She shut the book.

“That is not a copy,” she said. “A copy is somebody else's mistake. That is an instrument. And when it is printed, Mr Vester, it will not have your sentence in it that she cannot use, and it will have my district's name at the top, and it will be *right*, and the thing it will do will be the same.”

“Then somebody has to stand in their room on the fourteenth,” said Kett.

“You are all coming on the fourteenth,” said Corva Silt. “I put your names in the notice. It is a public sitting and I can do that, and I did, and I would like it read back before I go home.”

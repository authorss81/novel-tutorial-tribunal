# Chapter 71: The First Ask

The paper came in on a cart that was not coming for anything, which is how half of what has happened in this case has arrived, and it was lying on the counter at the ninth bridge at the second bell on the thirtieth of the month with a stone on it to keep it from going.

It is not a big thing. It is a sheet of a district's paper, folded twice, and the fold has been done and undone and done again by somebody who could not decide, and the outside of it has a wet ring on it where a cup stood, and the wet ring is over three of the four names at the foot, which means it was folded after the names were written and carried in somebody's coat and not in a bag.

> **TO THE KEEPER OF THE NINTH BRIDGE AND TO WHOM IT MAY CONCERN. THE BENCH OF FOUR OF THE DISTRICT OF NETHERGILL ASKS FOR THE TERMS OF THE CHARTER OF THE DISTRICT OF HALLOWAY TO BE CARRIED TO US UNDER THE SECOND HALF OF THE FOURTH TERM OF THAT CHARTER, AND WE ASK FOR THE PAPER ITSELF AND NOT A COPY, AND WE CANNOT PAY FOR THE PAPER.**
>
> **WE HAVE HAD NO MATTER BEFORE THE FIRST VERDICT IN THE LIFE OF ANYBODY IN THIS ROOM. WE HAVE A SEAL AND A CLERK AND A ROAD FUND THAT HAS BEEN NIL FOR THREE YEARS BECAUSE THERE ARE NO CARTS ON OUR ROAD, AND WE HAVE A LANE, AND WE ARE NOT ASKING YOU TO GIVE US A GATE.**
>
> **JONAS PYLE, CONVENOR. HOLLIS DUNE, DROVER'S CLERK. MARGUERITE SKEATH, WHO KEEPS THE CHAPEL BOOK. AND THE FOURTH, WHO SIGNS BELOW ME AND IS NOT ABLE TO WRITE AND HAS HAD HER MARK MADE BY THE FIRST THREE.**
>
> **IF A MAN HAS TO COME FOR THIS THEN LET HIM COME. WE WILL HAVE THE ROOM WARMED.**

Oren Sedge read it standing up, twice, and then put the stone back on it, which is the most respectful thing he does, and said the thing that made it real.

“Nethergill,” he said. “Eleven miles. Off the drovers’ road, and the last two of the eleven are cart road in June and rut in everything else.”

“Is it a bench of four?”

“It is a bench of four and a seal I have never seen and four names, three of which are in a hand and one of which is a mark, and the mark is a good mark, because somebody taught her to make it properly and not just a blot.” He turned the paper round with one finger so that the wet ring was on the far side. “And there is nobody in this city who can tell you that this paper came from that bench, including me, and I have been on this counter nineteen years.”

---

The first thing to go wrong was not the walk. The first thing to go wrong was that nobody could check it.

That is not a complaint about the world. That is a description of the shape of the thing. A carrier who is not a party and is not a bench and is not a body may be handed a piece of paper by anybody, and the only question anybody ever asks of him is whether he will go, and the reason nobody asks whether the paper is genuine is that the consequence of going when you should not is a thing that happens to somebody else in a district four miles away.

He read the ask to Corva Silt at the fourth bell on the thirtieth, down the fen road, in a room over a pump, to a woman who has served her own district's appeal against the office of gate hand and printed an instrument in a fortnight that shut a lane in a district of her own.

“That is a bench of four,” she said, reading it standing, with her hand flat on the table. “That is four names and a mark made by three people who were being careful, because the mark is not a blot and it is not a scrawl. It is a good mark. My mother could make that and my mother is dead.”

“Then it is from Nethergill.”

“It is *in the shape* of being from Nethergill,” said Corva Silt, “and I want you to hear the difference, because in two months I have printed four instruments in this district and every one of them was in the shape of something somebody wanted, and two of them were the shape of something somebody wanted who did not want the thing they were going to get.”

“You have told me five times that the paper on the gate is not fair.”

“I have told you that it is *lawful* and not *fair*, which are different, and I have not said the first one nearly often enough.” She turned the paper over. “And here is the part I cannot do. I cannot certify that four miles down that road there is a bench of four. Not in the sense that I doubt it. In the sense that I have never been to Nethergill in six years and there is no page anywhere in this city that says what the bench of a district is, and if I certify one I am inventing a page, and a clerk who invents a page is the mechanism and the mechanism does not know it is the mechanism.”

“What I need is not a certificate,” he said. “I need a person to read it and tell me whether it is an asking.”

Corva Silt put the pen down and said the thing he had come four miles to be told, and she said it without any weight at all, which was how she said the heavy ones.

“There is exactly one person in this district who is allowed to read anything and it is not me,” she said. “It is her, and she is a mile up the lane, and she has a barrow, and if you want her to read this as a reader you will have to go and ask her to read it as a reader, and you will have to be careful how you ask, because she is the holder and a holder is not a party and if anybody ever needed to have her sworn about anything she could not be. She told that court she did not volunteer. I would like you to go up that lane on foot and not take the barrow.”

---

Wenna Tace read the ask twice standing in her own doorway with the light behind her, at the sixth bell, and then she read it a third time out loud, slowly, which is the thing she does to everything, and then she said:

“A bench of four has asked for the terms. That is a real ask. I can tell you that much because a bench asks with a seal and I have seen a bench's seal and that is not it — that seal is a wedge and a chain and Nethergill's is a bar and a hook, and I know that because the drovers used to come to that bar and leave the hook number on a nail and I have been in that lane since I was four years old.”

“That is you telling me it is genuine.”

“That is me telling you it is a bench of four and the mark is a good mark and the paper is the paper a bench of four writes on, and I have never once been to their room and I do not know whether they have a room.” She folded it back the way it had been folded, which took her three attempts. “And I want to tell you the thing that is actually wrong with it, and it is not fraud and it is not a bad seal, and if you go up that road you are going to find it out about four miles from here and it is going to be worse than a bad seal.”

“Go on.”

“There are two copies of the terms in this district,” she said. “I keep one. The bench keeps one. They are the same nine sheets and they were sworn the same afternoon and one of them was sworn first and I do not know which, because I was not in the room when either was sworn and the whole of the point of me is that I was not in the room when either was sworn. And the difference between them, when there is one, is a thing you find out by having the two in your hands at the same time.”

“So you cannot tell me whether this is the paper that was asked for.”

“I cannot tell you whether the *bench* sent the right one, no,” she said, “because I cannot see the bench’s copy from here and I am not going to send a boy up that road to look at it, because a boy who goes up that road to look at a page comes back with a page and then he is a person who has carried a page out of a bench, and a page is not a thing that is supposed to come out of a bench.”

And then she said the other thing, and she said it looking at him, and she had thought about it in the four minutes it took her to make up her mind.

“I will tell you what I can do and what I cannot do and I am not going to pretend it is the same size. I can read you the second half of the fourth term. I cannot give you the terms. A holder does not hold the charter. A holder holds a page with entries in it and a copy of the terms to swear an entry against, and a copy is nine sheets, and it is in a room, and it is not mine, and I have been asked twice this month to hand it over and I said no both times and I said no because I do not know what handing it over means.”

“Read me the clause,” he said.

She read it to him in the doorway in the flat voice, and it is short.

> **THE TERMS OF THIS CHARTER ARE CARRIED, AT THE CARRIER’S OWN COST AND WITHOUT PAY, TO ANY DISTRICT THAT ASKS FOR THEM. THE CARRIER IS I. VESTER AND NOBODY ELSE, AND NOBODY ELSE MAY CARRY THEM. IF THE CARRIER REFUSES, OR IS UNABLE, OR IS NOT WITHIN FOUR MILES, THE TERMS ARE NOT CARRIED AND THE APPEAL IS STAYED UNTIL ANOTHER CARRIER IS SWORN IN THAT DISTRICT.**

The System put the clause in front of him while she was still reading, and it put up the two halves of the fourth term as the record holds them, and not as she said them.

**SECOND HALF OF THE FOURTH TERM, AS IT STANDS IN THE CHARTER OF THE DISTRICT OF HALLOWAY, MADE THE SEVENTEENTH.**
**THE TERMS OF THIS CHARTER ARE CARRIED TO ANY DISTRICT THAT ASKS, AT THE CARRIER'S OWN COST AND WITHOUT PAY.**
**THE CARRIER IS NAMED AND IT IS ONE PERSON AND NOBODY ELSE MAY CARRY THEM.**
**IF HE REFUSES, OR IS UNABLE, OR IS NOT WITHIN FOUR MILES, THE TERMS ARE NOT CARRIED AND THE APPEAL IS STAYED UNTIL ANOTHER CARRIER IS SWORN IN THAT DISTRICT.**
**WHAT THE RECORD DOES NOT SHOW: ANY DISTRICT THAT HAS ASKED AND BEEN SERVED WITH THE TERMS. ANY NOTICE SERVED ON A CARRIER. ANY OCCASION ON WHICH THE CLAUSE HAS BEEN USED.**

“That is nine words I have never been asked about,” he said, “and it has been in a page in my hand for thirteen days.”

“It has been in a page in my hand for thirteen days,” she said, “under the fifth term, and under the fifth term there is a line in it, and I have not read the line.”

He waited for her to go on and she did not go on, and then he said, “You have not read it.”

“No. And I want that written down in whatever you write things down in, because you are going to think I am being careful and I am not, I am being a person reading a charter. I read a charter in order. That is what a charter is for. You begin at the first term and you go to the end of the fifth and then you have read the thing, because the fifth term is the end of the business, and there is nothing after the fifth term. There is nothing after the fifth term in a charter. That is what a term is. A term is the end of the business and then it is over.

“And there is a line under the fifth term in the copy in this room, and it has been there since the day the copy came, and I have read the fifth term to about four hundred people and I have not read one word after it, because there is nothing after it. I have put my finger on the end of the fifth term and said *that is the end of it* to a room full of people who believed me, and I was right, and there is a line under it, and I have known about that line for thirteen days and I have not read it, and I am not going to read it to you tonight.

“Do not tell me what it says. Do not read it to me this evening. I have got two runs to do and it is nearly dark and I have not had anything to eat since the second bell, and if I start thinking about a line under the fifth term tonight then I will not have done the runs, and the runs are the thing I have.”

“I am not going to read it to you tonight,” he said.

“Good,” said Wenna Tace. “And when somebody reads it to me, and somebody will — a trialwright comes down that road for it and he did once already on the twenty-third of the month, and I know he did and I have not asked him what he came for and he did not tell me and neither of us mentioned it — I am going to want it in a page, with a date, and I am going to want to know what it costs me, and I have four terms about me in nine sheets and not one of them is about a line under the fifth one.

“Now go up that lane. You are going to be four hours on that road and you have got nine sheets and a folded paper and a leg, and if you sit down anywhere on the way up I will hear about it from a drover by the fourth bell tomorrow.”

---

He went up the road at the seventh bell with nine sheets in a leather strap and a folded paper in his inside coat, and the four-mile line is a line on a map and not a line on a road, and Nethergill is eleven miles from this bridge.

That is the whole of the mechanism and it took him a mile to understand it and he understood it in the wrong order at first, and then again in the right order four miles later.

At four miles he is still bound. Past four miles he is not. A man four miles off has a duty and a consequence and a stayed appeal. A man five miles off has a favour. Nobody can call a man five miles off. Nobody can make him. Nobody can enter what he does or does not do after four miles, because the clause stops obliging him at four miles and does not start permitting him, and a thing that is not permitted and not forbidden is the exact shape of the thing this whole case has been about for a month.

He walked eleven miles. The first four were a duty and the last seven were not, and nobody in this city can put a word on the difference, and he went on past the point where he was free, and that is the only thing anybody can say about it.

And the leg was his own the whole way, and that is the other thing that has to be said about that day, and it is the discovery from the sixth of the month and it works: it clears when he is inside a burden of his own and it does not clear when he rests, and the walking was a burden of his own, and the walking was the best he felt for eleven days.

It stopped about forty yards past the bar at the end of the lane, on the far side, where the road drops into a hollow with a hard surface for about nine yards, and it stopped in the middle of nine yards, and it came back worse, and he had to stand on the verge for a minute and a half with his hand on a gate post that is not a gate and belongs to nobody, and the low water came back into his nose in four layers, the day over the night, the flat over the water, the chain under those, and the door under that, and none of the four of them is his and the last one is the only one he has not been able to put down.

Nobody was on that road at that hour. The drovers were three days gone and would be four days back.

Then a boy of about thirteen came up the lane on an errand, looked at him, and said, “Are you the one that walks down with paper?”

“I am.”

“Will you read it to us tonight?” said the boy. “Mrs Vole says you will. She has been to the bar twice to look for you and she has milked and she is not pleased about either.”

He said yes, and the boy went, and the hollow was empty again, and at the top of the lane there was a lit window and a woman standing in it with a book under her arm, waiting, who had not gone to bed.

---

She had the table cleared when he came in, and he took the nine sheets out of the strap and put them on it in the order the string has them in, which is the order they were sworn in, and she had a candle in a bottle because there was no oil in the house, and she was thirty-eight and she was a cowman's wife and she could read a notice and a psalm and a tally and nothing else in the world, and she had a boy's slate on the table with four lines on it that she had copied off the *ask* because she was afraid of the fold.

“Sit down,” she said. “You have walked eleven miles and there is no chair in this room that is not a chair for the table and you may have that one.”

He sat down in the chair for the table.

Then she said the thing that the chapter of that day is actually about, and she said it before he had said one word.

“I have been waiting for you since the second bell and I want you to know what I am and what I am not, and I am not going to be a person you explain something to. I am thirty-eight and I have had three children and the two that are here are asleep upstairs and the third is at his grandmother’s and I keep the bar’s tally in this book and I have kept it nine years, and I can read the tally and I can read a notice and I cannot read a contract, and I have known that since I was eleven, because I had a mother who could and she taught me the psalms and the psalms were enough and then I was married.

“And four times a year somebody has to come to this house and be told what a bench has decided, and there is nobody else, and that is what I am. I am a door. I have never once been asked to be anything else and I am not going to be one tonight.”

“I am not here to explain anything,” he said.

“Then what are you here for,” said Hester Vole, “because you have walked eleven miles and there is nobody in this district who has ever seen a man walk eleven miles for paper, and I would like to know before I read it out, so that I can be frightened in the right place.”

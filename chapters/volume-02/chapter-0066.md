# Chapter 66: The Price of the Telling

Two clerks of the Orison chamber came up the hill on the twelfth of the month with instruments in their hands, and one of them was Ottla Prenn, who had brought draft eleven to a common-rule sitting three weeks earlier and had been told by a magistrate in front of nine benches that the room could not finish, and the other one was a woman of fifty-one named Hulda Vane who had come four miles from a district called Coldwater and who had not been to a court in nine years and had brought two instruments and would only let anybody see one of them at a time.

Dessin Nahl came up the road with them and read the docket out loud in the lane outside Coldwater's bench house, because that is what a clerk does with a docket in a lane, and then she read the two names that were going to be off the board that morning, which were a water matter off the cut banks and a name matter that had now been on the Orison board twenty-two days, and neither of those people said anything, because a clerk who reads a board aloud in a lane saves two arguments a month and it is not her job to be kind about it.

Ilyan was on the deck of the ninth bridge at the sixth bell with Coll and a slate, counting a light load out and back on a span that had been carrying loads for ten days without a word out of anybody, and the west seat sat where it had sat for six years a finger's width from the iron ring, and a keeper rang the bell at every change with two hands under the deck, and the count came back at mark.

The message came up the fen road at the ninth bell by a cart that had four things in it and no driver, because a cart in this city can be sent with a message in it and be collected by whoever comes, and the message was a docket, and Dessin Nahl had sent it because a man whose name is on a document does not get told things and she had worked that out in a gallery five weeks ago.

It said, in nine lines, that eleven benches had been asked a question and had answered it in writing, and that Coldwater had answered it in a way that required somebody to stand in a room, and that the room was on the fen road side of the city and not in Orison, and that a cart had gone out from Coldwater at the second bell with a bench of five in it and two of its members were women and one of them was sixty-eight and had left a fire going.

---

Coldwater is on the weighhouse side and it is a working district, and its road is stone to the water, and its three spans are small and its bench is of five and it has always been the district in this city that does what it is told in the first fortnight after it is told.

They adopted the ninth-bridge settlement on the eighth day after the hearing. Hulda Vane put it in Coldwater's own book on the eighth day at the fourth bell, and she did it lawfully, and she did it the way Corva Silt had done it two days before her, and the reason she did it was a letter.

> **TO THE CLERK OF COLDWATER. THE NINTH BRIDGE'S SETTLEMENT IS THE ONE THEY ARE ALL USING. EVERY DISTRICT ON THIS SIDE HAS IT IN. I HAVE PUT IT IN MINE. IF YOU HAVE NOT GOT IT, ASK. — A CLERK AT THE WEIGHHOUSE, NO NAME ON THIS SHEET, WHICH IS HOW THEY DO IT UP THERE.**

“There is no name on the sheet,” said Hulda Vane, when they asked her about it, at the door of the bench house in Coldwater, in the rain, with her own bench of five behind her in a room. “There is a seal on it and there is a district on it and there are three lines. So I asked. I had never seen the instrument. I had read about it in four letters and I am a clerk of twenty-six years and I did not read it, and I copied it out of the district's book in an afternoon on the third of this month because a district's print authority runs to any public instrument and there is no fee and there is no permission, and I did that lawfully and I would do it again tomorrow and I am telling you what I did and not what I should have done, because the two of those are different questions and I have answered the first one in a book.”

“Why did you do it,” said Ilyan.

“Because Coldwater has never had a pay line,” said Hulda Vane. “Twenty-six years and I have never once been able to pay a keeper for an hour, because a keeper here is a man who owns a boat and mends a gate and is owed by the district, and the district owes him by not knowing what he is owed. And a letter came with a district’s seal on it saying this was the one they were all using. So I put it in the book on the afternoon of the third and I did not read it, and I have been running it for nine days.”

She held the second instrument out and did not let go of it.

“This is Coldwater's own. It is numbered, and it is sealed, and it is mine, and it is the only one of the two that is anybody's business, and I would like it read aloud, and I would like it read aloud by somebody who is not from Coldwater, because I wrote it at the fourth bell on the sixth of this month and I have had six days with it and I have not read it since.”

Odile read it.

> **FORM C-4. SLIPWAY LANE, COLDWATER. NUMBERED, PUBLISHED BY THE BENCH OF COLDWATER AT THE FOURTH BELL, THE SIXTH OF THIS MONTH.**
>
> **ONE. SLIPWAY LANE IS CLOSED TO CARTS AND TO ANY VEHICLE BEARING A LOAD, FROM THE STONE DOWN TO THE LOW WATER, ON THE GROUND THAT THE LANE IS USED BY THE PERSONS OF THE DISTRICT AND THAT THE SPAN IS NOT SAFE.**
>
> **TWO. NO PERSON WHO IS NOT ENTERED IN THE CARTER'S BOOK AT THE WEIGHHOUSE SHALL PASS THE TURNBULL WITH A LOAD, AND THE SAID WORD *TRAVELLER* IN THE SPAN'S INSTRUMENT IS NOT USED IN THIS INSTRUMENT AND IS NOT TO BE READ INTO IT.**
>
> **THREE. NOTHING IN THIS INSTRUMENT ENDS A RIGHT OF WAY WHICH EXISTED BEFORE IT. ANY PERSON DISPUTING THIS INSTRUMENT SHALL DO SO AT THE NEXT SITTING OF THE BENCH IN WRITING.**

The second clause is good. The second clause is better than good; it is the best thing in Coldwater's book, and it is there because Hulda Vane read the ninth-bridge instrument properly, all of it, in one night, after she had copied it, which she had not told anybody and which she admitted to in the rain in about four words.

“I did not read it before I copied it,” she said. “I read it after. And when I read it after I found a sentence in it that says a person not on a register is not a trespasser by that fact alone, and I went back and put the second clause in my own instrument so that nobody could do to my slipway what was done to a gate four miles the other side of this city, and I put it in myself, and I have been waiting six days to find out whether anybody in this city reads the second clause of an instrument, and the answer is that nobody had, and the answer came down a road with a docket on it.”

Nobody said anything for a moment, and then a woman of thirty-eight came up the slipway with a coal tally in her hand and asked, quite reasonably, why there were five men standing in the rain on a lane that is closed to carts.

---

Her name was Perdy Sill and she had a coal yard at the bottom of Slipway Lane and thirty-eight years old and a husband on the cut banks road three nights a week, and she had not asked for anything, and her want was a cart.

“I have a yard,” she said. “It is the only coal within a mile and it is the coal for the upper street, for the smiths, and for four houses where there is nobody in the traveller book and four houses where there is. And since the eleventh day there has been no cart down my slipway at all, because the lane is shut to any vehicle bearing a load, and the reason on the face of the paper is that the lane is used by the persons of the district and the span is not safe.

“And there is a carrier who comes. He comes with a sledge and he charges four times what he charged on the ninth day and he will tell you why, and his why is good, and I want you to hear it because everybody in this street is going to tell you he is a thief and he is not.

“He says the road is shut to a cart and open to a man with a sledge, and a sledge is not a cart in anybody's book, and a man on a sledge is not bearing a load in a vehicle, he is bearing it on his back, and so he is inside the instrument and I am outside it, and he has gone from four shillings to sixteen and he is entirely correct and he told me so on the ninth day before the weather turned.

“And I have thought about that for six days, and I have got it here and I want to say it and then I want to go and do my work. The instrument is not shutting a lane. The instrument is not shutting a coal yard. The instrument has made a man with a sledge sixteen shillings richer than he was and it has made me four times poorer than I was, and the person it is about is not me and it was not written about me and there is no word in it that I could take hold of and shake. If I put the second clause in front of a magistrate he will tell me it says a person not entered in the carter's book is not a trespasser by that fact alone, and it does, and I am not a trespasser, and I have not been a trespasser for six days, and I have lost four times my money and I cannot get a single thing about it in front of a court because the thing that happened to me is that a man is clever.”

Then she said the thing that put the whole of the last month of Ilyan's life into one sentence, and she said it to the step and not to him.

“I have read the ninth bridge's paper,” she said. “Four of our men had it off a cart on the third, the same day my clerk put it in her own book. I have read it twice. I do not know what half the words in it are for. And I have been reading it for nine days with a slipway shut and a man on a sledge charging me sixteen shillings a load he used to charge four, and I would like somebody in Orison to tell me what a wheelbarrow has to do with any of it.”

Nobody answered her, and she took her tally and went down the slipway on foot, because the lane is closed to carts and she was not going to insult anybody by carrying anything, and forty people watched her go.

---

The telling took the rest of that day and most of the next, and the reason it took that long is that everybody in Coldwater had believed there was nothing to tell.

A district that has adopted an instrument by copying it has not read it. A bench of five that has adopted it by copying it has not read it. A clerk of twenty-six years who has copied it and then read it, once, alone, at night, is the only person in that district who knows what is in it, and what is in it is that four of its ten terms are a keeper's work, two of them are a pay line, two of them are about a person who is not on a list, one of them is a bell with a pin that has to be looked at, and one of them is an appeal.

And the appeal is term six, and it is the only one of the ten that needs a person, and it is the only one of the ten that nobody in Coldwater has ever noticed, because it is a paragraph about a challenge and a bench reads a paragraph about a challenge the way a man reads a thing about a person who is dead.

It took Hulda Vane most of a day to get a bench of five to minute, line by line, what Coldwater had adopted and what it had not. The result, which is a page, and which is a better document than either of the two instruments:

> **COLDWATER, THE TWELFTH OF THE MONTH. ENTERED BY THE BENCH OF FIVE, EACH MEMBER NAMED.**
> **WE HAVE ADOPTED TERMS ONE, TWO, THREE, FOUR, FIVE, SEVEN, EIGHT, NINE AND TEN, AS OUR OWN MAINTENANCE TERM, BY COPYING THEM OUT OF A PUBLIC BOOK ON THE EIGHTH DAY WITHOUT HAVING READ THEM.**
> **WE HAVE NOT ADOPTED TERM SIX, WHICH WE DID NOT NOTICE, AND WHICH IS AN APPEAL.**
> **WE HAVE NOT ADOPTED TERM THREE IN ITS EFFECT, BECAUSE WE HAVE NO PERSON TO PAY AND NO ROAD FUNDS, AND WE HAVE SAID SO.**
> **THE NINTH BRIDGE'S SETTLEMENT IS NOT BINDING ON THIS DISTRICT AND WAS NEVER BINDING ON THIS DISTRICT. WE ADOPTED IT. WE ARE NOT BOUND BY IT.**

“That last line is the whole of the ninth bridge’s tenth term put in our own mouth,” said Ansel Nyle, who had come with them and who had not said anything for two days, on a step in the rain in a district that was not his, “and they worked it out themselves, in a room, in a day, and nobody told them.”

“No,” said Ilyan. “Somebody told them. A woman of thirty-eight walked up a slipway in the rain and asked what a wheelbarrow had to do with any of it, and that is what the telling was actually for, and we have spent a day and a half producing a page that says a thing which could have been said in nine minutes by a person who has a lane and four houses in it with nobody in the traveller book.”

Nobody argued with him about it and several people thought him modest and one person thought him a coward for saying it, and that one was right, and it was Cael Rundle, who drove the cart both ways and who said so on the way back:

“You have been in this city a month,” Rundle said, “and you have got very good at the reasons, and I am going to tell you the thing about reasons, because I am fifty-eight and my chest is bad in the wet and I have got nothing to do for two days but drive.

“They asked you to come because there is a document that a machine wrote and a machine cannot be argued with, and you came and you are right and it took a day and a half. And the reason a day and a half was worth it is not the page. It is that a bench of five had to sit down together, in a room, and minute, in five different hands, that they had not read a thing they had adopted. And now one of them is sixty-eight and she has told her granddaughter about it, I would put money on that, and in four years there is a person in that district who reads a paper before she signs it, and she will be able to tell you exactly which day she learned to do it and it will be a wet afternoon with a clerk in it and she will say the name of the clerk.

“That is not in the page. That is the price of the page, and the page is the cheap part.”

---

The price of the telling proper was paid at the weighhouse on the second evening, in a book, in a clerk's hand, in front of a magistrate, and Meridith Vale did it without being asked and without any difficulty at all, which was the frightening part.

“I put four pounds on this cart,” he said, “for the driver, and eleven pounds for the two clerks' wages while they were away from their benches, and the cart is the fourth bridge's cart, which is the district's, and the wages are out of the ninth bridge's district road fund, and I have entered it, and I am going to read you the line.

> **THE TELLING OF AN INSTRUMENT TO A DISTRICT THAT ADOPTED IT BY COPYING IS PAID BY THE DISTRICT THAT PRINTED IT. FOUR POUNDS AND ELEVEN POUNDS, OUT OF THE NINTH-BRIDGE'S DISTRICT ROAD FUNDS, ON THE TWELFTH OF THE MONTH. A DISTRICT THAT HAS COPIED A THING IT HAS NOT READ OWES THE TELLING TO THE DISTRICT THAT PUBLISHED IT AND NOT TO THE PERSON WHO WAS HARMED.**

“That is a rule,” said Ansa Rell.

“It is a clerk's entry and it is not a rule and it is not a ruling and this court has not made it and this court will not adopt it in a lane at the weighhouse at the ninth bell on a wet night,” said Vale. “But it is on a page in a book with a date against it, and in nineteen years a clerk in a bench will find it, and if anybody has to pay for telling this again they will know they do not have to do it themselves, and the whole of your chamber's trouble in this city is that every district is doing everything itself and there is nobody in between.

“And then I am going to say the other thing, because I have put my own money in and I am entitled to put the price of it in as well.

“The road fund is four hundred and ten pounds a year. The props are ninety-six. Two hands under a deck every change at the ward rate is two hundred and ten. There is a hundred and four pounds left and a clerk in it and a rope and a piece of paper. I have just spent fifteen pounds of the hundred and four on a cart and two clerks' wages, and the fourth bridge's four props are still sitting on that cart's boards at the yard where they have sat since the twentieth day of the unloaded period, and they are going to sit there until somebody pays ninety-six pounds for four new ones or somebody carries the old ones back at my district's cost, and there is a review in six weeks at which that account is going to be looked at by a bench that has never heard of the fourth bridge and has my own account in front of it.

“So: the props, or the telling. I have not chosen. I have written two lines in a book with a date against them and a column for the other one, and I have brought the book up the hill open, and if a magistrate wants to make that a rule she can do it in a room, and if a bench wants to make it an instrument they can do it in an afternoon, and until one of those two things happens it is my own clerk's entry on a wet night and it is worth exactly what a clerk's entry is worth, which is a great deal, in about four years.”

---

They came back up the fen road at the second bell on the fourteenth with a page and a rate and a carrier's four-shilling slip pinned to it, and they came past the fourth bridge, because it is on the road, and the fourth bridge is a span with four holes in it where four props should be, and the holes are square and clean and forty-four days old.

Ilyan had stood on that span twice in six weeks. Wist Anhalt was forty-nine and had been keeper for twenty-nine years and his four props had come off it on the day of the hearing and gone down the fen road on a cart and propped the ninth bridge's west seat for thirty-four days, and the ninth bridge's charter says they go back on the same cart before the review or the district pays, and they had not gone back.

Anhalt was on the deck. He had a lantern and a rule and he was checking a joint by a number in a book, alone, at the second bell, on a span with four square holes in it, and he did not stop working when four people came up the approach with a cart.

He looked at the cart, and at the two clerks in it, and at the number of them, and at the man with the bad chest driving.

“That is a great many people going nowhere,” he said.

“We have been at Coldwater telling a district what it adopted,” said Ilyan, and then stopped, because there were about nine things he could have said next and every one of them would have been about himself.

Anhalt went back to his joint for about a minute and a half, and then he straightened up and looked at the holes in his own deck.

“They are the district’s,” he said. “That is not a complaint. I put the four words on a paper a month ago and the court nailed them to a frame four miles away and I have never met the man who wrote them and I do not want to, because he did not write them for me, he wrote them about me, and that is a different thing and I have had a month to think about it and that is where I have got to.

“Thirty years and four props is what a span of that length wants in the fourth week of a wet month. Not four. I have never once had four. You cannot prop a deck with four. You can hold one place with four and hope the rest behaves, and it has behaved for twenty-nine years and I have never once told anybody that, because there was nobody to tell and a keeper who says his span is short is a keeper who is asking for money.

“And now there are four square holes in my deck and the four props are on a cart four miles away holding somebody else's seat together, and the district that took them owes me four, and a bridge four miles down this road is sitting on them tonight, and if the weather goes in the night the thing I am responsible for is the thing I have been responsible for since I was twenty, and it is a different bridge from the one you are all talking about.”

Nobody said anything, and Cael Rundle, who had been in this case for five weeks and who had asked for a date and got a date, said:

“I will take it back on the cart on the seventeenth. It is four props and a cart and two men and it is four miles. I will take it back at my district's cost and I will put it on the ninth bridge's day book, and I will tell Mr Vale that the district has paid, and I will tell him it is paid and not given.”

“You will not do that,” said Anhalt. “You will do it because you have a bad chest and you have a daughter and you think a man in a cart is a man who can be trusted with a thing. It is not a trust. It is a debt and you are not clearing it, you are moving it one span down the road, and I am not going to let a ratepayer of a lane four miles off spend his own money on my deck to feel like a man who pays his debts.

“I am not going to have it back on that cart unless the district pays for it, and if the district does not pay for it then the props stay where they are and I write it in my own book on the seventeenth with the date and the weather, and I do not send it to anybody, and I do not nail it to a frame, and I do not say four words about it in a room in Orison. I have done that once and it has been a month and I would like to stop.”

He got the lantern back off the deck rail and looked at the four holes one more time.

“You can tell your clerk that the account is open and it is on my side of the road, and he knows whose account it is, because he is the one who wrote it.”

---

The docket came up the hill on the fourteenth at the last bell and the appeal was set for the fourth bell of the sixteenth, in the open air, at the First Verdict, on notice to every party named in the ninth-bridge instrument, which is fourteen names and one seal, and the serving of it took four bells and two carts.

And at the top of the fen road, on a bridge that had been carrying loads for twelve days, the count came back at mark on a light load, and the pale permanent set had not moved, and a bell was rung for the change by a keeper who has looked at the pin on a named day by two people, one of whom has pulled the rope, and about a mile down the road a bench of five had minuted in five hands what it had adopted and had not noticed, and a page was nailed to a slab on a closed slipway that said a person not entered in the carter's book is not a trespasser by that fact alone, and a man of seventeen wrote a new line in a line book, and the fourth bridge's four props sat on a cart's boards in a yard with square holes opposite them and the weather was coming.

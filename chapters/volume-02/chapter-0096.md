# Chapter 96: A Question the Appeal Cannot Carry

Garvin Slade came up the bank on the seventeenth of the fifth month at the second bell with about four feet of a chair leg in his hand, which is not what he usually brings, and he brought it because he had been standing at his counter the day before with it in his hand and had not noticed for about half an hour, and he said so before anybody asked him anything, which is the only thing about him that has changed since the sixteenth of the fourth month and it has changed a great deal.

“I want to appeal the rule of the seventeenth of last month,” he said.

Nell Ambrey put her pen down. She is about sixty and she is the convenor and she entered the sanitation complaint eleven weeks before any of this began and it is still not heard this year.

“Which rule,” she said.

“The one that makes me be read out at a pump. Not the water. The water is right and Cael has said so four times. The reading.” He put the chair leg down against the wall, carefully, the way a man puts a thing down when he has decided where it goes. “About two hundred and forty people go up that bank in the cold because of a man who sells salt put his name under a rate, and the cost is the people who go up it, and it is paid by the people who go up it, and I put my name there. So I know what I did. I am not asking you to take my name out. I am asking you to take the rule out.”

“Why,” said Corva Silt, who has a slate and has had it in her hand since the second bell.

“Because it costs,” said Slade, “and because a woman buys a quarter of a pound of the coarse off me every week and she has a job at the second bell and she has had it nine years, and she came to my counter in the second week of last month and said she would rather not stand in a crowd, and I said come up, and she said that is the part I do not like, because I will not be the only one, and she was right, and she took her salt and went. And on the nineteenth of last month two hundred and forty people went up that bank and about a fifth of them did it because I am in a rule I did not make. And she was one of the fifth, and she had a job at the second bell and she went anyway, and I do not know whether she went because of the rule or because of my name, and neither does she, and that is the whole of what I have come about.”

Nobody in the room improved it.

“Then I am afraid I have to tell you four things,” said Nell Ambrey, “and I have to tell them quickly, and the third one is the one that is going to be the end of it.”

---

The first two took ninety seconds and they are both of them correct.

“A person may not appeal a thing that person made,” said the convenor. “That is not this district's rule. It is older than this district and it is the rule the rule was written out of, and it is a good rule, and the reason it is a good rule is that if the person who made a rule may appeal it then the making of the rule is a privilege, because anybody who does not like what they have made can go and take it back. You made the rule of the seventeenth in about twenty minutes in this room with three of us in it. You are not appealing it.”

“Then I am appealing Corva Silt.”

“You are not appealing the clerk of this bench and you did not know you wanted to until about four seconds ago.” Nell Ambrey did not raise her voice and did not look pleased. “I wrote the third line of that rule. Oakes is right about the third line and I would put it up again. I am not appealing it either.”

The second one took longer and Corva Silt had to explain it, and she explained it in the flat voice of a clerk reading a thing out, which is the only voice she has for this.

“And the second thing is that there is nobody else who can appeal it. A person of this district who is affected by that reading can complain to this bench, and this bench will hear it, and this bench will do nothing, and it will do nothing in public and in its own book with a date on it. That is a complaint. It is not an appeal. An appeal in this district is a thing that runs under the charter to a public sitting on notice to every named party, and the charter says an appeal is available to any person affected by a ruling on this span — and there is no ruling on this span about a reading at a pump, because a reading at a pump is a thing the bench does in its own room at a bench's own appointment, and the bench has not been asked to make a rule about it by anybody who is affected, it made it about itself, and a bench may make a rule about itself and may not appeal it.”

“Then what is left,” said Garvin Slade.

“The holder,” said Corva Silt.

And that is the third thing, and it is the thing that has been in this case since the eighteenth of the second month, and the holder was in the room four feet away with a barrow against the wall, because it is the second bell and she is always there at the second bell.

Wenna Tace put the barrow down.

“No,” she said.

“You have not heard it.”

“I have heard it four times since March and I have said no three of them and I am not going to be careful with you about the fourth. A holder may not be a party, may not appeal, may not appear in support of one, and may not be named in one. I keep the page. I read the rate out four times a year. I read a man's name out under the rate four times a year, and on the first of the seventh I am going to do it again, and there is nothing you can do to me about that and nothing I can do about it either. If you want to appeal that rule you would have to name me in a support of it, and there is a word on the fifth term that stops you, and I did not write the fifth term and I would not have written it and I am not going to pretend to be aggrieved about it in a room with a man who sells salt in my lane.”

---

The fourth thing was the only one that was not in that room, and it is why a clerk of the Orison chamber put a notice on a sheet of paper with a name on the bottom of it, and why the name on the bottom of it is one of twenty-two on a board that has had twenty-two for nineteen years, and why a man of thirty-one has been in this case since the seventeenth of the second month and cannot be in it in any other capacity at all.

The only person who can be named in a thing in that district is a man with limited standing confined to the ninth-bridge matter, whose name is in the second half of the fourth term of a charter four miles down a fen road, as a carrier with a duty and a consequence.

He is not a bench. He is not a party. He cannot be cited as a permit, a schedule, a levy, an acknowledgment, or a bearer of labor. He may not send somebody with his name on it. He may file and be heard in the matter with a named advocate, and there is a named advocate, and Maren Kett of the Orison chamber has been nineteen years in it and argued against his own client for three hours and said on the record that he knew the outcome on the day he took the brief.

The notice went down the fen road on a cart on the eighteenth, and it cost the district four shillings and sixpence of cart out of two hundred and forty pounds a year on paper and nothing in fact, and it cost Corva Silt a day, and it cost the boy of nineteen an evening, and it cost Garvin Slade the knowledge that his name is now in a second piece of paper in a city he has never been to, with a date on it, and he did not know that and she did not tell him and entered why.

And it cost Ilyan Vester a thing nobody in this case has ever cost him, and it is the opposite of everything the last nine weeks have been.

Carrying a notice is a duty of his own. The fourth term says the terms are carried at his own cost and without pay; a notice is not the terms and a man who is named in a thing may carry that thing, and it is not the terms but it is the same shape of work. The four-hour limit in his leg has a four-hour limit in it and not a rule, and what makes it run out is whether he is carrying something of his own.

He carried the notice down the fen road and back on the eighteenth and the nineteenth, and for the first time in two months and nine days the leg was his own from the first bell to about the fourth, and he has not been able to say out loud what that did to him, and he has not told anybody, and on the second evening he sat down on the step of a room over a pump for about a minute and a half and then got up.

And then it went, and it went worse than it has gone since he stopped counting, and he worked out, sitting there, that the four hours had not been a gift. They had been a trade. He has been holding a gate hand's weight out of his leg for four hours by carrying a piece of paper up a road, and the piece of paper was the weight, and the woman was not, and the woman has been in his leg since the twenty-sixth of the fourth month and she is still in it, and a piece of paper with a seal on it is a very good lever and it is not her.

He entered nothing about that. He entered, at the eighth bell on the nineteenth, in the room over a pump, with Corva Silt writing it down in the district's own hand and reading it back, four lines, and the first of them is that a man carried a notice for this district at his own cost and was paid nothing and the price of the cart was entered and the price of his day was not, because there is no line in this district or in this city on which a day is payable.

---

And what came back on the twentieth was not a place, and the reason it was not a place is the whole of this chapter, and it took a counsel of nineteen years to say it and about two minutes, standing at a counter in a room off the second gallery, in the open, in front of nobody at all, which is the only kind of open that there is in this city.

“There are twenty-two names on a board,” said Maren Kett, “and there have been twenty-two names on it for nineteen years except forty-two days in the spring, and there are eleven places in a morning, and there have been eleven places every month since before I came. A name does not get a place. A matter that is ready gets a place. That is the whole of the scarcity and it is not money and it is not a bench and it is not a judge's mood.

“Your matter is not ready. That is not a criticism of anybody in this room. Your matter is a rule made by a bench of four in its own room on a day it appointed, in its own hand, on a subject the bench chose, affecting about two hundred and forty people, and read aloud at a pump four times a year. It is not ready because it is not a document. A document has a party, a date, a seal and a thing that is claimed. A rate at a pump has a lid and a chalk line and a woman's voice. To carry it anywhere you would have to make it into a document, and the only way anybody in this city has ever made a thing into a document is by writing it out by hand, and the person who writes it out is a person who can read, and there is no line anywhere in this city on which a person is paid for reading, and in this case the price of reading has been paid by a refuse hauler, a gate hand, a cowman's wife, a landlord's mouth, a clerk, a boy of nineteen who is not paid and was not asked, and a washer of sixty-one who has walked four miles every morning for a month and told one man and not her district.”

He put the sheet down.

“And the second half of it, which is worse, and which I have had this afternoon from a magistrate in one sentence. This court has no power over any rate in any district in this city. It said so on the face of a field about a gate in the second month. It has now said it about money, in the same terms, and it is correct, and it is not a loss, and I am going to say the other half of it because I am the advocate of the limitation and I have been the advocate of the limitation since the seventeenth of the second month and I have never once pretended it is the other side of the argument.”

He looked at the board at the end of the room, which is not the docket board, and is a board about a matter, and has one name on it.

“Garvin Slade did the first available thing a poor person in this case could point at and use, and it worked, in the sense that it produced a name under a rate in a bench's own book, twice, on purpose, that will be read out four times a year for as long as the woman with the barrow is the holder. It worked and it cannot be used for anything. That is not a defect in his method. That is a defect in the machinery, and the machinery is made of paper, and the paper has a hole in it the shape of a person who cannot be named, and I have spent nineteen years in a chamber that tells this city it cannot make a place, and I have been right about that every day, and I have never once been able to say out loud what the hole is, and the hole is a bench cannot hear its own rule complained of by a person it has not injured in a way it can name.”

He picked the sheet up again.

“So here is the question, and I am entering it in my own book on my own motion, and I am not a witness to it and it does not travel, and I have been told this twice this year by the same person and I have not improved it.”

> **ENTERED BY M. KETT, COUNSEL OF THIS COURT, EIGHTEENTH OF THE FIFTH MONTH, ON MY OWN MOTION, AND NOBODY ASKED ME.**
>
> **A BENCH OF FOUR HAS A RULE. THE RULE COSTS ABOUT TWO HUNDRED AND FORTY PEOPLE A BANK IN THE COLD, FOUR TIMES A YEAR, AND THE COST IS PAID BY THE PEOPLE WHO GO UP IT. THE BENCH WILL HEAR A COMPLAINT ABOUT IT FROM ANY PERSON OF THE DISTRICT AND WILL ENTER IT AND WILL DO NOTHING. NOBODY MAY APPEAL IT. THE ONLY PERSON WHO COULD BE NAMED IN A SUPPORT OF IT IS A HOLDER WHO MAY NOT BE NAMED, OR A MAN WITH LIMITED STANDING CONFINED TO A DIFFERENT MATTER FOUR MILES AWAY WHO IS NOT A BENCH AND CANNOT MAKE ANYBODY ANSWER.**
>
> **THE QUESTION I AM ASKING AND CANNOT ANSWER: IS A RULE THAT COSTS PEOPLE SOMETHING THEY CANNOT AVOID, AND THAT THE BODY WHICH MADE IT CANNOT BE ASKED ABOUT BY ANY PERSON IT COSTS, A THING THAT ANYBODY IN THIS CITY CAN CARRY ANYWHERE AT ALL.**
>
> **AND I AM ADDING THE HALF OF IT BECAUSE NOBODY WILL ADD IT FOR ME. A THING THAT IS READY GETS A PLACE. A RATE IS NOT A DOCUMENT. AND THE DAY A DISTRICT AGREES WHAT A THING IS WORTH, IT HAS NOT FIXED THE RATE. IT HAS ONLY FIXED THE UNIT, AND THE UNIT IS THE SMALL PART, AND THE SMALL PART IS THE PART THAT A CLERK CAN CARRY.**

---

The answer, when it came, was that there was no place, and that there had never have been one, and that the board had twenty-two names on it and would have twenty-two names on it on the twenty-first and on the twenty-second and on the last day of the month, and that the matter is not ready and will not be ready, and that a thing that is not ready does not get a place whether it is right or whether it is wrong, and that the only way to make it ready is to make it a document, and that a document is made by a person who can read, and that there is a woman four miles down a fen road who is the only person in about two hundred and forty people who can read and who may not be named in a support of it and who reads a man's name out four times a year for nothing.

Garvin Slade was in the room over a pump on the twenty-first at the second bell to be told this by four people and a clerk who had walked four miles and back, and he stood by the barrow for a while.

“Then it is not a thing I can use,” he said.

“No,” said Wenna Tace. “It is a thing I can say, four times a year, with your name under it, to about two hundred people, and it is not a thing anybody can do anything about, and those are different, and the second one is better than the first one and I am not going to tell you it is not.”

“That is the first sensible thing anybody has said to me since the sixteenth of last month,” said Garvin Slade, “and I am going to go back down that lane and open that booth at the fifth bell and I am not going to be any different, and in about a year one of the two hundred and forty is going to come into my counter and ask me about a rule I made and cost them a bank in the cold, and I am going to hand them the book and let them read it themselves, and I have thought that through.”

He picked the chair leg up off the wall and looked at it, as though it had been in his hand for two days without being noticed, and it had.

“Mr Vester,” he said, at the door, to the man standing four feet from the barrow with nothing in his hands. “I have got your name on a thing and I have never met you and I did not want to and I have been told I cannot do anything with it.”

“No,” said Ilyan Vester. “You cannot. I am sorry.”

“Do not be sorry,” said Garvin Slade. “Be the man who turns up. That is all any of us wants and there is not one procedure in this city for it.”

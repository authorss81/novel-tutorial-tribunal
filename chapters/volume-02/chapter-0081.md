# Chapter 81: The Deck That Is Open in the Weather

The fourth bridge has four square holes in it and has had them since the twentieth of the first month, and it has been open to the weather since the twenty-seventh of last month, and on the second of the fourth month, at about the fourth morning bell, a loaded peat cart stood in the middle of the span with its near wheel hard against the second hole and the far side empty, waiting for the keeper to walk out and look at it.

Wist Anhalt is forty-nine. He has kept this bridge for twenty-nine years. He was sitting on the parapet with his back to the wind and a piece of chalk in his hand, and when the carter came up he did not get up, he said *wait*, and the carter waited, and then he got up and walked the span at the pace he has walked it since before the carter's father had a cart.

He went over the second hole. He went over the first. He came back and looked at the fourth and put the chalk in his pocket instead of using it, and then he went along the parapet and looked at the ends of the four new timbers that were lying in the mud on the north bank because they were cut on the road above the fen on the thirtieth of the third month and there is no water in the river to float a timber up to a deck and the man who would do it has not come.

The four new timbers were bought at ninety-six pounds out of the ninth bridge's district road fund. There is no more money for that fund this year. There are not new timbers under this deck and there is not a date when there will be, and the old four are in a yard four miles down the fen road, stacked, and they did not fail, and nobody has said they would.

“How is it,” said the carter, which is what you say to a keeper.

“Open,” said Anhalt. “It is open in the weather and it is open to loads and I have not stopped a load and I am not going to.”

“I know you are not. I asked how it is.”

“It is four holes and no more timbers until there is money for timbers,” said Anhalt, “and it has been open to the weather for six days, and I am the man who is on it, and nobody asked me what I think about that, and I have got about nine weeks of weather in me before somebody from that bridge up the road remembers this place exists.”

The carter took his cart across at a walk.

---

There is no person at this bridge except Anhalt, and Anhalt is not a party to anything, and on the twenty-seventh of last month a clerk of six years in the post stood in the open air at a bridge four miles down the road and entered that he had not asked him. That entry is the only thing in the whole of this case that anybody has done for the keeper of the fourth bridge, and it was done in a book that is not in this district and about a man who is not in it.

The same morning a letter came down the fen road with a cart, and it was addressed to the bench of four of the district of Halloway, and it was in the hand of Meridith Vale, clerk of the district of the ninth bridge, and it had come eleven miles and it was not a notice and it was not a demand and it was not served on anybody.

It was a page and a sum.

> **TO THE BENCH OF FOUR OF THE DISTRICT OF HALLOWAY, IN THE ROOM OVER THE PUMP, AND TO THE HOLDER OF THE FIFTH TERM, AND TO WHOM IT CONCERNS, WHICH IN THIS DISTRICT IS NOBODY.**
>
> **THE CHARTER OF YOUR DISTRICT COSTS FORTY-FOUR POUNDS AND SIXTEEN A YEAR. IT IS CHARGED TO THE ROAD FUND OF THE DISTRICT OF THE NINTH BRIDGE, WHICH IS FOUR HUNDRED AND TEN POUNDS A YEAR, AND YOUR CHARTER TAKES A LITTLE UNDER A NINTH OF IT. I HAVE PUT IT IN THAT WAY ON PURPOSE BECAUSE A BENCH OF FOUR THAT IS PAYING A RATE OUT OF ITS OWN BOOK OUGHT TO KNOW WHOSE BOOK IT IS IN.**
>
> **A PUBLIC MAINTENANCE REVIEW WAS HELD AT OUR SPAN ON THE TWENTY-SEVENTH OF LAST MONTH. THE YEAR WAS SET OUT AT THAT REVIEW AS FOLLOWS: FOUR NEW TIMBERS FOR THE FOURTH BRIDGE, NINETY-SIX POUNDS. THE TELLING OF COLDWATER, FIFTEEN. TWO HANDS UNDER A DECK AT EVERY CHANGE, TWO HUNDRED AND TEN. YOUR CHARTER, FORTY-FOUR POUNDS AND SIXTEEN. WHAT IS LEFT IN THE YEAR IS FORTY-FOUR POUNDS AND FOUR SHILLINGS, AND YOUR CHARTER COSTS TWELVE SHILLINGS MORE THAN WHAT IS LEFT.**
>
> **ONE HAND UNDER A DECK IS A HUNDRED AND FIVE POUNDS, AND THE WATCH WENT TO ONE HAND ON THE DAY OF THAT REVIEW, AND THAT HUNDRED AND FIVE IS THE ONE THING IN OUR YEAR THAT CAME BACK, AND IT IS IN A DIFFERENT LINE FROM YOURS, AND A CLERK CANNOT MOVE A LINE INTO ANOTHER LINE WITHOUT A MINUTE, AND THE MINUTE IS A BENCH'S AND NOT MINE. SO YOUR CHARTER COSTS TWELVE SHILLINGS MORE THAN THE LINE IT IS CHARGED TO, AND WE HAVE A HUNDRED AND FIVE POUNDS WE CANNOT PUT AGAINST IT.**
>
> **WE WILL PAY IT TWELVE SHILLINGS OVER THE LINE, WHICH IS WHAT A CLERK DOES WHEN A LINE IS TWELVE SHILLINGS OVER. I AM TELLING YOU BECAUSE A THING PAID SILENTLY BY ANOTHER DISTRICT IS A THING YOU CANNOT APPEAL, AND YOUR DISTRICT HAS AN APPEAL NOW, AND IT COSTS FORTY-FOUR POUNDS AND SIXTEEN, AND THE DISTRICT PAYING IT IS FOUR MILES AWAY AND IS NOT A PARTY TO ANYTHING OF YOURS.**
>
> **NO REPLY IS ASKED FOR. I AM A CLERK AND THIS IS MY FIGURE AND IT IS NOT THE COURT'S.**

It lay on the table over the pump from the first bell to the third, and about eleven people read it, and none of them could have said what it meant, and at the third bell Wenna Tace picked it up and took it down to the bench of four.

---

Nell Ambrey is the convenor and she is about sixty and she entered the sanitation complaint eleven weeks before any of this began and it is not heard this year because a bench's time went to an appeal. Oakes is thirty-four and is the district's maintenance clerk and has held a seal for two years and refused a four-hour wage in writing for a sentence that was right. Cael Rundle is fifty-eight and pays half a farthing a bucket on his own lane and said *this is the right rate* before anybody said anything else.

The fourth of them is Garvin Slade, who is thirty-eight, who has sold salt out of a booth at the top of Weir Lane since he was thirteen, which is twenty-five years, and who lives in a house he owns on Weir Lane, and who had never in his life been in a room like this one before the twenty-eighth of last month, when a bench of four set a rate for a haul and he was one of the four, and who is the man the gate hand has bought salt off since she was nineteen.

“The letter is right,” said Oakes. He had it in front of him and he had a slate and a chalk, because a rate is a figure before it is anything else. “Forty-four pounds and sixteen. I have had it in our book since the seventeenth and it came out of that fund and not out of ours, and I have never once asked whose it was, because our book has never had a column for that and because there was no reason to ask. A line is a line.”

“There is now a reason to ask,” said Cael Rundle, “because the man who sent it is telling us he is paying it and has told us he is not asking for anything and that is the most generous letter I have had off that road in eleven years, and it makes me feel exactly as bad as I expected to feel, and I would like somebody to say why that is a reason to do something.”

“Because you are now a district with a rate that another district’s road fund is one year away from being unable to pay,” said Oakes.

He wrote it on the slate, because he writes things down before he says whether they are true, and it is the habit of a man with a seal and not a lawyer.

> **OUR APPEAL IS PAID FOR BY A BRIDGE FOUR MILES AWAY. THE BRIDGE'S YEAR ENDS IN FOUR MONTHS. AFTER THAT NOBODY IN THAT DISTRICT IS OBLIGED TO PAY ANYTHING.**

Nobody argued with it for about eleven seconds, which is a long time in that room.

“Then say the other one,” said Garvin Slade. He had not said anything for nine minutes. “Say the one under it. If that is true then our water pays our notices, and our notices are what says we may appeal, and the appeal is about a bench, and the bench is us. And we set it. We set it at the end of March and we are the people who did it and every bucket out there is carrying our own lawyer.”

“It is not a lawyer,” said Oakes. “It is half a farthing.”

“Half a farthing a bucket, Garvin, and not one farthing, and I have said four times in this room that it is the right rate and I am not going to unsay it, because the fund has nothing in it and a rate that cannot be paid is not a rate. I have a girl of fourteen who walks to that pump every morning and she has never once said a word about it to me and I have looked for it.”

---

What the bench of four then had to do was take up a figure it had entered and could not discharge, and there is no procedure anywhere in this city for that, and there is no court that will do it either, because a court has no power over the debt of a district and has said so on the face of a field about a gate.

The figure was in the book in three hands with a line through it that said it was wrong.

A hundred and thirteen thousand four hundred hours at threepence the hour is one thousand four hundred and seventeen pounds and ten shillings. The person it is owed to is Bryn Sallow, gate hand at the low-water culvert, forty-four years old, thirty years in the post, who refused it in a room and was not compelled and cannot be compelled and cannot be overrule, and who corrected one third of it herself and unasked, and who is one of the four who has to be asked about it every quarter by a woman with a barrow.

The district's own road fund is two hundred and forty pounds a year. It is fed entirely by cartage on a shut road and the cartage has been nil since the fourteenth of the month before last, so the fund is two hundred and forty pounds on paper and nothing at all in fact.

And a third of that nothing, at the wrong rate, is still a thousand four hundred and seventeen pounds ten.

“It cannot be paid,” said Oakes.

“No.”

“It cannot be refused. She has refused it and cannot be overrule.”

“No.”

“It cannot be reduced, because there is no rate in this city from thirty years ago and the man who wrote the figure said so in the book in his own hand.”

“No,” said Oakes. “I read that line. He wrote that the correction for the rate cannot be found in any district in this city and is not entered as a number. It is in the book in the second hand and I have read it about nine times this week because I keep looking for the number underneath it.”

“Then what,” said Cael Rundle, “do we do with it.”

And that took about half an hour, and it was made in a room with a person in it and not by a court, and Nell Ambrey made it, and she made it in one sentence and then did not improve on it for a fortnight.

---

“We keep it,” said Nell Ambrey.

“Keep what,” said Oakes. “We have got it. It is in the book. It is in the book in three hands and two of them are the man who wrote it wrong and the woman who refused it and there is a line saying it is wrong and there is a number under the line that nobody can strike. What is there to keep.”

“The other thing,” she said. “We have not written down that a rate out of this district’s own water is being carried in another district’s fund. That letter is in your hand, Oakes, and I want it in our book with the figure against it and I want the figure of the charter’s cost entered in our book as a thing *we* owe, whatever anybody else’s fund says, so that when a clerk in this room in four years comes to this page he is looking at a number and not at a blank. And I want the four new props entered, and the day, and the fact that we were not asked and did not send anybody.”

Nell Ambrey is sixty and has a sanitation complaint in her gift only on a day when she has nothing else, and she has had nothing else since long before any of this began, and she said the rest of it in the voice of a woman who has been losing both a smell and a sheet for eleven weeks.

“Because a debt that can be neither paid nor refused is a debt that does not go away, and there is one kind of debt in this city that nobody can take off you whatever happens, and it is the one in a book in your own room in your own hand. And in four years the two hundred people who went up that bank on the first of the month are going to be asked by a woman with a barrow what a thousand four hundred and seventeen pounds is, and the answer is going to be that this district cannot pay it and never could and refused the figure and got it put in anyway. That answer is true and it is worthless. It is the only true sentence anybody in this district has said about money since the fourteenth of the month before last.”

“Then what is worth having.”

“The book,” she said. “A book a bench can come back with. That is the only durable thing in a poor district and it is not durable because it is well made, it is durable because it is heavy and it is signed and there are two more of us than there were. I have been on this bench nineteen years and the only reason I know that the culvert road is a penal road and not a haul road is that there is a hundred-and-sixty-year-old book in the room over the pump and somebody in a hundred and sixty years put it there. Everything else this district has ever had, I have carried in my own head, and my head is sixty.”

She put both hands flat on the table.

“So we enter what our law costs. Not what it costs us, because it costs us nothing yet, it costs a bench four miles away and I am not going to be sly about that in front of a man from that road. What it costs the people who pay it. Twenty-two names on a board in a city, four hundred and ten pounds of a road fund, and a district of about two hundred and forty people four miles off a hand with a barrow.”

“Twelve shillings over the line,” said Garvin Slade.

“No,” said Nell Ambrey. “Forty-four pounds and sixteen a year against forty-four pounds and four shillings in the year, and by this time next year that bridge's fund has a line for something this case has never thought of, and the charter stops, and it does not stop with a letter. It stops with the quarter's rate coming back to this pump and the notice not being served because there is no clerk's hour to serve it in and a clerk's hour is the largest thing in the whole of it, and the rate does not know that. The rate is a half a farthing a bucket and it does not know any of this.”

---

They entered it at the fifth bell, and it took Corva Silt the best part of an hour because she writes the district's own hand and the district's own hand is a small one and she does not use a clerk's short cut.

> **ENTERED IN THE BOOK OF THE BENCH OF FOUR OF THE DISTRICT OF HALLOWAY, THE SECOND OF THE FOURTH MONTH, ON A LETTER OF THE SECOND OF THE FOURTH MONTH FROM THE CLERK OF THE DISTRICT OF THE NINTH BRIDGE, AND NOT SERVED ON ANYBODY AND NOT ENTERED BY ANY COURT.**
>
> **ONE: THE CHARTER OF THIS DISTRICT COSTS FORTY-FOUR POUNDS AND SIXTEEN A YEAR AND IT IS CHARGED TO THE ROAD FUND OF THE DISTRICT OF THE NINTH BRIDGE AND NOT TO THIS DISTRICT'S FUND. THE NINTH BRIDGE'S FUND IS FOUR HUNDRED AND TEN POUNDS A YEAR. THAT YEAR WAS SET OUT AT A PUBLIC REVIEW ON THE TWENTY-SEVENTH OF LAST MONTH AS FOUR NEW TIMBERS AT NINETY-SIX POUNDS, THE TELLING OF COLDWATER AT FIFTEEN, TWO HANDS UNDER A DECK AT TWO HUNDRED AND TEN, AND THIS CHARTER AT FORTY-FOUR POUNDS AND SIXTEEN, LEAVING FORTY-FOUR POUNDS AND FOUR SHILLINGS. THIS CHARTER COSTS TWELVE SHILLINGS MORE THAN WHAT IS LEFT. A HAND UNDER A DECK IS A HUNDRED AND FIVE POUNDS AND THE WATCH WENT TO ONE HAND ON THE DAY OF THAT REVIEW, AND THAT MONEY IS IN A LINE THAT CANNOT BE MOVED INTO OURS WITHOUT A BENCH'S MINUTE. THEY WILL PAY IT AND HAVE ASKED FOR NO REPLY.**
>
> **TWO: THE FIGURE ENTERED IN THIS BOOK ON THE TENTH OF THE THIRD MONTH, OF ONE THOUSAND FOUR HUNDRED AND SEVENTEEN POUNDS AND TEN SHILLINGS, CANNOT BE PAID BY THIS DISTRICT AND CANNOT BE REFUSED BY THE PERSON IT IS OWED TO, AND STANDS. THIS DISTRICT HAS NO MONEY. THE ROAD FUND OF THIS DISTRICT IS TWO HUNDRED AND FORTY POUNDS A YEAR AND IS FED BY CARTAGE ON A SHUT ROAD AND THE CARTAGE HAS BEEN NIL SINCE THE FOURTEENTH OF THE MONTH BEFORE LAST.**
>
> **THREE: A DEBT THAT CANNOT BE PAID AND CANNOT BE REFUSED IS ENTERED HERE AS A DEBT THIS DISTRICT OWES, AND THE FIGURE IS WRONG BY AT LEAST TWO HUNDRED AND TWO POUNDS AND TEN SHILLINGS AND BY AN AMOUNT NOBODY IN THIS ROOM CAN NAME, AND THE PERSON IT IS OWED TO HAS REFUSED IT, AND IT IS READ ALOUD AT THE PUMP EVERY QUARTER BY A PERSON WHO CANNOT APPEAL FROM IT.**
>
> **FOUR: NOTHING IN THIS ENTRY IS A RULING, AND NO COURT HAS POWER OVER ANY OF IT, AND THE FIGURE BINDS NOBODY AND THE RATE BINDS NOBODY, AND THE ONLY THING THIS ENTRY DOES IS STOP A CLERK IN THIS ROOM IN FOUR YEARS FROM FINDING A BLANK.**

The fourth of the bench read it over twice and then said, “You have written down that we are poor.”

“I have written down what we are poor *by*,” said Oakes, “which is different, and you are the only man in this room who cannot tell the difference, and I have wanted to say that to you since the twenty-eighth of March.”

---

He walked the four miles back in the dark, because the letter had to go back up the fen road with the cart and there was no cart, and there is no obligation anywhere in this case for a man with limited standing to walk anywhere at all.

He had walked four miles in the last three weeks that nothing in this case had asked him to walk, and there was nothing in his leg for the first two of them and then there was, and then there was a gate hand's weight in it from the hip down at somebody else's pace, slower than his, and he let it run because carrying it is the only thing that makes it go and because he is a man who has worked out that a body is a thing you may spend.

At about the sixth bell he passed the fourth bridge on the way home, because the road goes past it, and there was a light on the parapet, and the keeper was still on it, four square holes and a man and a piece of chalk in his pocket, and no timbers under the deck and none coming.

Anhalt walked out to the middle and looked at him and did not ask who he was, and Ilyan did not tell him, and they stood about eleven feet apart on a bridge with four holes in it and neither of them said one word about a single thing.

“I am sorry about the props,” said Ilyan.

“They were not yours,” said Anhalt. “They were the ninth bridge's. I said four words about it and the words are in a book I have never seen.” He looked down at the second hole. “You want to say something else and you have been standing there a while trying to think of it.”

“I have,” said Ilyan. “It is not mine to say. Nobody asked you and I have noticed that and I am sorry about that as well.”

“You have noticed it from four miles away,” said Anhalt. “I have been standing on it.” He put the chalk in his pocket, which he had already done twice. “Go down the road.”

He went up the fen road with the leg in his left and a scar at the base of his left thumb that will be there in four years, and there is no line anywhere in this city that pays a man for a hand and none that pays a man for having a leg, and by the time he got to the ninth bridge the under-deck watch had gone off at the change and one man had taken a reading alone in the dark, and Oren Sedge's crew list has had one name on it since the winter and the span has taken a load at every change since the third of the second month and the pale permanent set has not moved.

And four miles down a fen road, a bench of four has a book in a room over a pump in which there are now two debts and one of them is a debt to a bridge that has not asked to be a creditor, and a clerk of thirty-four sat down afterwards at the table and copied out, in his own hand, the six things a man had entered against his own name, because Corva Silt asked him to and he said yes without asking what it was for.

She said, “It is for the four years.”

“Then I would like to know what happens in them,” he said.

“You do not,” said Corva Silt, and put her pen in the drawer, “and that is the second time this month I have been the second head about a thing you have not asked me for, and one of these days one of us is going to be wrong and it is not going to be me.”

# Chapter 65: A Burden of His Own

The accumulation reached the sixth of the month in a way that he could no longer work around, and the way it reached it was not dramatic, which is what made it frightening: he got up out of a chair in a stone room and could not stand up out of the chair, and then he could stand, and then at the fourth bell on the bank four miles down the fen road he put his weight on his left leg to get down a step and his body went over to the other side of it without asking him.

He went down the road anyway, at the second bell, with a leg that came and went, and it is true and it is not a cure and it is not a rule: the leg was his own in a room and was not his own within about a mile of the low water, and the boundary was the smell, and he had known it since the nineteenth and had not tested it, and he tested it on the sixth of the month and it held.

Tarin Keel was not there and did not come. That is worth saying, because a man who has been carried off a step once by a neutral boundary can be certain of getting a second one, and Tarin did not come, and the reason he did not come is that Tarin had been asked by a route-arbiter's clerk whether he would stand at the bank on the sixth of the month, and had said no, in writing, in eleven words, and the eleven words were that a neutral boundary is for a field and there was no field on the sixth of the month and a man on a bank is a man on a bank.

Nobody was on the bank except the gate hand, who was thirty years into a posture, and the reader of the district, who had brought a barrow and was going to make two runs before the water came, and a man with a slate who sat down on a barrel lid at the gate side at the second bell and could not get up off it.

“I am going to count your hours,” Ilyan said, to the gate hand, and then had to say it again, because she had not turned round and he had not known whether she had heard him the first time.

“No,” said Bryn Sallow.

“I know. I have read the third term and it is a copy of a bridge's, and a pay line with no hours on it is a line with a name on it and nothing else, and in four months somebody is going to take the name off it and call it an office again.”

“You have got about two hours before the water comes,” said Wenna Tace, without stopping, “and you are white, and you are sitting on a lid, and I am not going to be the reason a man falls over on my bank.”

“Then hold the clock for her and go and do your two runs,” said Ilyan, “because you are the only other person here who can do either and I cannot do both.”

“There is no way to do it,” said Wenna Tace, “and it is in a book in her handwriting and she told a field at the sixth bell that she has never counted them and is not going to start now for a page, and she is right, and I have been on her side of it for eleven years and I have never once been in a room where anybody else was.

“If she gives you a number then it is a wage, and a wage wants a rate, and a rate wants a body, and there is no body, and the number goes on a page and the page wants a payer, and the only thing left in the gap is her. That is how it works and that is how it has worked for thirty years and she worked it out at about twenty-six, and nobody in this city has ever once been on her side of it, including the man who wrote the wage line into a charter he did not have to write.”

The gate hand had not moved and had not looked round, and she said:

“She has told it right.”

“I know she has told it right,” said Ilyan. “That is why I have come down a hill to say the other thing to you and not to her, and I would like to say it once and then count from the fifth bell to the ninth and not put a mark in it.”

The gate hand turned round, which she does about four times in a day and had not done in eleven days.

“Say it.”

“Keep the hours a shape,” Ilyan said, and he said the next four sentences in the words of a guild's count book and not in the words of a room, and he knew he was borrowing them and he said so afterwards in a lane and nobody entered it. “Then the ward goes on standing there and this goes on for ever, and the next gate hand is a girl of twenty-two and she stands in that place for thirty years and the ward pays her the same nothing it has paid you, and there is nobody to ask. A duty is not a body. A place is not a body. A word in a guild's book is not a body. A woman on a gate is.

“You are the only person in this district that anybody can produce. If your hours stay a shape then you are the ward, for ever, and you are the one the ward is doing the work of, and nobody can be brought up in front of you, because you are not the payer, you are the one doing it.

“The whole point of a number is that it gives a person something to be angry at somebody specific about.”

Bryn Sallow looked at him for about four seconds, in the position she has been in for thirty years, one foot on the sill-stone and one on the bank.

“Say the last part again,” she said.

“You have never been able to be angry at anybody,” Ilyan said, “because nobody is on the other side of it. There is a book four miles up the road with a rate in it and no name against it, and a duty on a page and no body, and you have been standing inside that for thirty years and you have not been angry, because there is nothing to be angry at. Four hours a day is a number. Four hours a day at the district's own rate is money. Money has an author. And in this district the four people who set that rate are two of whom sleep on Weir Lane, and you will be owed by people you know, and you do not know that yet, and I would rather you had it from me at six in the morning on a bank than out of a clerk in a room in a fortnight.”

Nobody said anything on the bank for about eight seconds, and the water was still four hundred yards off and coming.

“All right,” said Bryn Sallow. “From the first light on the rise to the dark on the fall. That is what I wrote on the schedule and I wrote it in my own hand and the court entered it and said the hours are not a number. Fine. I will call the hours and Miss Tace will hold the clock and I will not stop for anything except the water, and you will write what the clock says, and if you are wrong by four minutes on any hour of it then I will know and I will tell you, because I have watched you be wrong by forty loads.”

“You will not stop me,” said Ilyan, “and if I have to be taken off this bank I will be taken off it, and you are not going to do it, and neither is a boy of nine. I am asking you for the work and not for the stopping. I have a man who does the stopping and he is good at it and he is not here because I did not ask him, which is the first sensible thing I have done in a month.”

---

He counted from the fifth bell to the ninth bell, and it is four hours, and it is the first four-hour count of a gate hand's working tide that has been taken by anybody in this city, and it is on a slate in a guild's hand on a barrel lid, and it is wrong.

That is the honest account of what he is for. A person is not a measuring instrument. He could not read a clock and the gate hand read a clock and called the hours, and he wrote them and the writing is in a load-carrier's figures because his guild terms do not have a word for *a quarter past* and he has a word for a half-load of sand and a word for a wet plank. The slate is not readable. Corva Silt will have to enter it in the district's own hand from his figures, and she has said so, and she said it kindly, and he said he understood and it went in anyway.

What the slate does have is this: the gate hand called her hours, and he wrote them down, and somewhere in the fourth hour the leg went off.

Not the first one. The fourth, maybe, or the sixth — it did not go at a moment. It thinned. His left leg went from being a foreign object with somebody's knee in it to being something that was simply tired, in the way a leg is tired, and the weight in it changed, and he understood in about nine seconds what had happened and then had to put his hand on the gate leaf to keep standing up, which is the leaf of an oak gate, forty feet above a low water, at the ninth bell, with the water coming.

He had it for about three more hours.

He sat down on the sill-stone at the foot of the gate and the low water came up in his nose in three layers, the day over the night, the flat over the water, and under both of them the chain, and he had believed since the nineteenth that the chain was a thing that was happening to him.

It was not happening to him. He had been carrying it the way a man carries a bag he has not noticed he is carrying, and the reason he had not put it down was that he had been trying to.

The leg did not clear when he rested. That was the discovery, and it took him four days to be certain of it and about nine minutes to suspect, and it was not a discovery about power. It was a discovery about a job.

It cleared when he was inside a burden of his own. The counting was his. Four hours on a bank in a smell with a slate, work he had chosen and could have walked away from at any point in the last four hours, and it was the most tiring thing he had done since he landed on a bridge five weeks ago, and for as long as he was doing it the leg was a leg and the weight in it was his own and the thing in his nose was four layers of a flat that he had stood on and counted.

When he stopped, it came back in about a minute, and it came back worse, and he understood that he had it exactly backwards: he had spent eleven days treating the afterimage as a thing to be rested out of, and it was a thing to be worked out of, and every time he had sat down and slept and gone to a bank hoping it would be quieter he had been doing the one thing that made it sit on him.

It is not a cure. It is a practice with a four-hour limit in it, discovered on a bank, at the ninth bell, by a man who was told to go and sleep by a woman with a barrow and went and counted instead.

---

The token was in his hand at the ninth bell and he did not remember putting it there.

It was warm, to about the temperature of a hand that has been on a chain, and it has been warm before and it was warm on the nineteenth and it was warm on the twenty-first and it has never once been warm in a room. He put his thumb on it on the sill-stone at the foot of an oak gate with the water coming up under him, and the low water went out of his nose.

For about fifty minutes he could not smell the flat, or the bank, or the mud, or the culvert, or his own coat. It stopped. It did not go far away; it stopped, the way a note stops, and the world was clean and quiet and about nine notes out of tune, and he understood in the middle of it, in the body, that the thing in his nose had never been a memory of the low water and had never been a smell at all, and that on the nineteenth he had got halfway to that and had gone out and bought a lamp at the end of it instead.

It was a *rendering*. It was made, and it was made for a person, and it was not made by a person who had stood on a bank.

The third layer, the one that came back under both the others on the nineteenth and had never been identified, stopped being a smell as well, and in the fifty minutes it showed itself as what it was, and it was a picture.

It was a gate. It was an oak gate on a frame, seen from above and behind, at a distance of about two hundred feet, in a light with no source in it. It was not the gate he was sitting at — wrong hinge, wrong leaf, wrong proportion, and a sky behind it that did not go anywhere. And it was the same picture. That was the part that took the whole of the fifty minutes: not that he could see it, but that he recognised it. He had seen it before, in the place he now understood he had seen it, which was the eighteen seconds on the edge of the flat at the first field, before the two shadows, before the hip went cold, on the nineteenth, at the fifth bell, and had thought it was the low sun on the mud.

It was the same picture every time, from the same height, and it did not move, because it was not a memory of a gate and it was not a vision of a gate. It was a reception. Something had put a picture of a gate into the part of him that had no reason to have ever been in a room, at the moment something else put the low water into his nose, and both of those had arrived in the same four seconds, and he had been in this city for a month and had put a token in his pocket and had understood for most of that month that the passage that brought him and the thing in his leg were one operation.

He had it in front of him now, in the plainest form there is, and the form is this: a picture that is made for somebody, by something, has a vantage. It was from above and behind, at two hundred feet, every time, and a vantage is a place, and the place was not on this flat and was not in this city and was not any of the nine bridges, and he had been looking at it since the nineteenth without knowing that looking at a picture from the same height every time is the same as standing in a room and never turning round.

He did not know where it was. That is the whole of what he got, and it cost him the only thing he had, which was the smell, and which was the reason he had come.

It came back at the fiftieth minute and it came back wrong, and it came back with a layer under the three he had, and the fourth layer was not the low water and not the flat and not the chain.

The fourth layer was a door. Not a gate: a door, in a wall, at a distance, and he could not see whether it was shut. He had been walking about this city for a month inside a rendering of a gate, and the thing that had been underneath all of it the whole time was a picture of a door, and he was standing in a lane with hay in it looking at a thing he had never seen, and the door was in the picture at the same height and the same distance every time and the vantage had not moved.

He put the token away and pressed both hands flat on the sill-stone until the stone came up through his fingers, and the gate hand was thirty feet off at the gate with her back to him, and the water was under the bank, and the leg was not his.

He had traded four days of smell for one question and he could not put the question down afterwards, which is what a trade is.

---

He did not go to the basalt court that night. He slept on the bank in a room over the pump that Corva Silt had given him the key to on the second of the month and had said nothing about, and he woke on the seventh with the leg his own until the fourth bell, and it was his own on a bank, which was new, and it was not his own in a lane with hay in it, which was also new, and by the eighth of the month it was neither, and it did not clear for four days, and it did not come back clear afterwards.

The gate hand let him count again on the seventh and the eighth and the ninth, and by the ninth she was calling the hours herself and he was writing them, and by the tenth she had made him put a line down the middle of the slate and write the day on it, and on the eleventh she asked him, in front of two people, whether it was going to stop if he stopped, and he said that he did not know, and she said:

“Then I have got a number on a slate that only exists when a man from a bridge is standing on this bank, and you have just made my thirty years into a job for a man who has a leg, and there is no line anywhere in this city that pays a man for having a leg.”

“No,” Ilyan said.

“Then say that to whoever writes the charter,” said Bryn Sallow, from the gate, in the past tense, about a machine, in the voice she used for a machine, and she went on working the gate, because the water was coming and there were about two hundred people on a flat and the foot-gate was where the foot-gate had always been, and it was the ninth bell and a person was coming up the bank with a barrow and she was not going to be late for it.

He said it to Sera Quill at the fourth bell on the eighth of the month, on a step, in the open air, in about eleven words, and she wrote it in her own record in her own hand, and then said:

“Then I am going to write the fifth term and the hole in it is going to have a line under it as well, and the line is going to say that a page is not held by a person and that a number on a slate is not a record, and I do not know how to write the rest of it, and I have got a stove that does not draw and nine sheets and a review in forty-nine days, and I would like to be somebody else for about a day, and I am not going to be, so I am going to go and be very good at this instead.”

The ninth bridge's west seat came out of its fortnight on the third of this month, four miles up the fen road, with nobody watching except a keeper and a counter and a bell rung at every change with two hands under the deck, and the span took its first load that day, thirty-four days after the ruling, and Ilyan was four miles away counting a gate hand's tide and found out from a cart, and the count came back at mark on a light load, and the pale permanent set did not move, and the fourth bridge's four props were still on a cart that had not gone, and nobody in that district knew, because nobody in that district had asked.

Four miles down the road, on a slate with a line down the middle of it, in a hand that is not a clerk's hand and is not a record, there were six days of a gate hand's tide written down in a load-carrier's figures, and a name at the top of it, and a barrow's handle against the wall, and a man standing on a bank with a leg that is his own for as long as he is counting and not one minute after.

# Chapter 87: The Man Who Took His Name Out of a Rate

Garvin Slade did not come to the room over the pump. He came to the booth, which is at the top of Weir Lane and is a plank shed with a counter and a scale and forty-four years of a chalk board with prices on it, and he is thirty-eight and has been selling salt out of it for eleven years, and in the second week of the fourth month two women who have bought salt off him for a decade asked him whether the water was going up.

He told them he did not know, and one of them said *you are one of the four*, and he said he was, and she said *then you know*, and he said he did not, and she went away, and he has been at that counter since and it is a nine-foot counter and you cannot be nine feet away from a person who has just asked you a question you cannot answer.

On the sixteenth of the fourth month he shut the booth at the fourth bell, which is the first time in eleven years he has shut before the fifth, and walked down the lane to the room over a pump, and put a piece of paper on the barrel lid.

It was a slip off his own board with the day's prices on the back.

“Read that,” he said, “and then I am going to tell you what I want and I am not going to argue about it, and if anybody in this room tries to make me argue about it I shall go home and shut the booth and we can do it another day.”

Corva Silt turned it round. It was in his own hand and it was a bench's entry, copied out, in the small hand of a man who writes prices all day and does not write sentences.

> **THE RATE FOR A HAUL IN THIS DISTRICT IS THE DISTRICT'S OWN RATE, WHICH IS A THIRD OF A HAUL RATE, SO A HAUL HERE IS PAID AT THREE TIMES THE DISTRICT'S OWN RATE, AND A HAUL HERE IS TWO THIRDS SHORT OF A HAUL. SET BY THE BENCH OF FOUR ON THE TWENTY-EIGHTH OF LAST MONTH. ASKED FOR BY THE HOLDER OF THE FIFTH TERM, WHO CANNOT APPEAL FROM IT. TO BE READ ALOUD AT THE SIXTH BELL ON THE FIRST OF A QUARTER BY THE SAME PERSON.**
>
> **N. AMBREY. C. RUNDLE. G. SLADE. O. —**

“They have not let me put a clerk on it,” said Slade. “That is on purpose. I asked. I said put the clerk on it so that a man can see whose hand the line is, and Corva Silt said no, and she was right, and it is in the book that she was right. I have copied that out and I have not put a date on my copy and I do not want a date on it.”

“Go on,” said Corva Silt.

“I am not withdrawing from the bench,” said Garvin Slade. “I want that said first because everybody is going to say it after. I will be one of the four when the next rate comes and I will put my hand to it whatever it is. I am withdrawing from *that*.”

---

It took from the fourth bell to the seventh and there were three things in it and none of them was an argument.

The first was Corva Silt's.

“A bench of four may withdraw its consent to a rate it has set, in writing, in its own book, with a date, in its own hand,” she said. “That is not a thing anybody has ever done in this district and I am not going to tell you it is law, because I do not know that it is, and I have looked for four days.”

“Then read it as what it is,” said Garvin Slade. “Read it as a man putting a line in a book. That is all I am asking for. I am not asking for it to stop the water. I have had customers at my counter ask me if the water is going up and I have said I don't know, and I want there to be a page in this district that says a man did not know, with his name on it and the date, so that when they ask me again I can say *I did not know, and here*, and hand them the book, and they can go and read it themselves and they will find my name and the date and the thing I did not know, and that is a different thing from a man at a counter with a hole in him.”

She wrote it. She read it back twice. She asked him whether he wanted the reason in his own words or in hers and he said his own words and then dictated about two sentences, slowly, in the voice of a man who has spent eleven years saying *that'll be a pound of the coarse and I can't do better than that for the money.*

> **ENTERED IN THE BOOK OF THE BENCH OF FOUR OF THE DISTRICT OF HALLOWAY, THE SIXTEENTH OF THE FOURTH MONTH, AT THE FIFTH BELL, IN THE DISTRICT'S OWN HAND. I AM ONE OF THE FOUR THAT SET THE RATE FOR A HAUL ON THE TWENTY-EIGHTH OF LAST MONTH AND I WITHDRAW MY CONSENT TO IT. I DO NOT TAKE MYSELF OFF THE BENCH. I STILL SET RATES. I AM SELLING SALT AND A CUSTOMER ASKED ME IF THE WATER WAS GOING UP AND I SAID I DID NOT KNOW, AND I DID NOT KNOW, AND A MAN WHO SELLS SALT AND CANNOT SAY WHETHER THE WATER IS GOING UP HAS HAD THE ONE THING HE SELLS AND I HAVE HAD IT TWICE THIS MONTH. G. SLADE.**

Nobody improved it. Nell Ambrey read it and Cael Rundle read it and Corva Silt read it and Slade watched all three of them read it and said nothing, and when it was read the fourth time he said, “That is right,” and then said, “I have been a man on a bench for a fortnight and about a half and I did not know I was one and now every person on this lane who buys salt off me is going to know inside a month, and I have a counter nine foot long and I cannot be nine foot away from it.”

The second thing was the rate, and it took an hour and it is the shape of the whole of this case.

It cannot be undone. That is what they established, and it was established in about nine minutes, and the person who established it was Oakes and he established it the way a clerk establishes things, by reading a document out.

“The rate is on a barrel lid nailed to a gate-side board and it was chalked there on the seventeenth of the second month and it was read out at the sixth bell on the first of this month to about two hundred people,” he said. “A rate that has been read aloud to a district has been told to that district. You may withdraw from a thing. You may not take a thing back off two hundred people, because they have heard it and they have carried buckets with it in them and one of them is a girl of fourteen who walks to that pump every morning and she has not asked me a single question about the water and I have been looking for one since the seventeenth of the second month.

“So the rate stands. And I am not going to rub your name off the entry of the twenty-eighth, because a clerk does not rub out a name, and because you said you wanted to be in it twice.”

“I did,” said Slade. “I have thought about that for two days.”

“And the entry of the twenty-eighth of last month keeps the four names and the entry of today has one name on it under the four, and the barrel lid does not change, and at the next quarter the woman with the barrow reads the entry of today out as well as the lid, because she reads the book and not only the lid, and that is how it works and I have checked it twice with her.”

“Then my name is going to be read out at a pump four times a year,” said Garvin Slade, “by the woman I have been in a room with twice in a fortnight.”

“Yes.”

“For how long.”

“Until the rate is replaced or the bench is dissolved or I am struck off, and none of those three has happened and the second one would take years and the third one I have looked at for four days and cannot do in any form I could defend,” said Corva Silt.

“Right,” said Slade, and got up, and then sat down again, and the room waited for him, and nobody prompted him, because by then all four of them had worked out that he was not finished.

---

The third thing took a while and it was not addressed to the bench.

He turned round on the stool and looked at Cael Rundle, who is fifty-eight, who pays half a farthing a bucket on his own lane, who has said *this is the right rate* four times out loud in a room in a city, who has a bad chest in the wet and a daughter of fourteen who walks to that pump every morning, and who is the other of the two of the four who live on Weir Lane.

“Rundle,” he said. “You are the other one. You live up there and I live up there and there is nobody else on that lane on that bench. And you are going to be standing up there on its own with a half a farthing a bucket and my name under it, and you are going to be asked, by people who will not ask me, why a man who sells salt in your lane does not agree with the water in your lane, and I will not be able to stand next to you and I have thought about that for two days and I am doing it anyway.”

“I know,” said Cael Rundle.

“You do know.”

“I have known since the seventh of March,” said Rundle. He did not take his hand off his chest. “I have watched you not ask for two days, in the way I watch a man not open a sack. And I have been sitting here for three hours being one of the four that sets things and I have not been able to make myself say one word about any of it, because I am one of the two of us that lives on that lane and the day I say it, the lane says it for me.”

“Does it,” said Slade.

“It has since I asked you at the counter,” said Cael Rundle, “and you gave me a quarter of a pound of the coarse and did not put it on the board.”

---

And then there was the part nobody had thought of, and it took forty minutes, and it is the only thing in that room anybody had to be talked into.

“A rate has been read out in this district once,” said Oakes, “on the first of the month, and a man has withdrawn from it, and the next reading is the first day of the next quarter, which is the first of the seventh, and that is better than two months away, and in all that time about two hundred and forty people will carry water at a rate that one of the four who set it does not agree with, and only one person in this district knows that, and she is the woman with the barrow, and she cannot say it to them because she is not a party to anything and if she did they would ask her a question she cannot answer.”

“Then she reads it out again,” said Cael Rundle.

“She reads it out again when the bench says so, and there is nothing in this district, or in nine districts, or in this city as far as I know of, that says she reads it out again.”

“Then the bench says so,” said Nell Ambrey.

“There is a quarter to a day between that and a bench of four inventing a rule, and the inventing is going to be done by a clerk of thirty-one in a room over a pump at about the ninth bell tonight — ”

Nobody looked up. “Not that day,” said Corva Silt. “Tomorrow. I am not doing it at the ninth bell at the end of a day like this and I will tell you why, and it is not modesty, it is that I have been in this room since the first bell and I have read out four things today and two of them were a man withdrawing and a man selling salt and if I write another rule tonight I shall write one that nobody would have written in the morning.”

They did it on the seventeenth, at the second bell, in about twenty minutes, and it is the shortest instrument this case has produced and it is the only one that was made because a man put his own name under a rate.

> **ENTERED THE SEVENTEENTH OF THE FOURTH MONTH. WHERE ANY PERSON WHO HAS SET A RATE IN THIS DISTRICT WITHDRAWS HIS CONSENT TO IT IN WRITING AFTER THE RATE HAS BEEN READ ALOUD, THE RATE SHALL BE READ ALOUD AGAIN INSIDE THE QUARTER, AT THE SIXTH BELL, AT THE STANDPIPE, BY THE HOLDER, TO WHATEVER PEOPLE COME, AND THE WITHDRAWAL SHALL BE READ WITH IT.**
>
> **THE COST OF A READING IS ABOUT TWO HUNDRED PEOPLE GOING UP A BANK IN THE COLD, AND IT IS PAID BY THE PEOPLE WHO GO UP IT, AND THE BENCH HAS NO MONEY AND THIS BENCH KNOWS IT.**
>
> **THIS IS NOT A RIGHT. A RIGHT IS TOWARDS A PERSON. THIS IS TOWARDS A BARREL LID.**

“The third line is not mine,” said Nell Ambrey. “That is Oakes, and it is correct, and I am not going to take it out.”

And Garvin Slade read it standing up, and said that it was the first time in eleven years that anybody in his trade had made a note in a book about anything he had said, and that he was going to think about the word *towards* for a while, and that he had a booth to open at the fifth bell.

---

It cost him two days later, at the counter, in about four minutes, and it was the price of the thing and he had priced it wrong.

He had told the bench that he wanted the page so that a person could come to his counter and he could hand them the book and they could go and read it themselves. What happened at about the third bell on the eighteenth of the fourth month is that a woman of about thirty who buys a quarter of a pound of the coarse every week came to the counter and asked him, exactly as she had asked him twice before, whether the water was going up.

“It's not going up,” said Garvin Slade. “It's the same half a farthing it's been since the seventeenth of the second month, and a man on our bench has withdrawn from it, and it's being read out at the pump at the sixth bell tomorrow with his name on it and mine under it, and if you come up you'll hear it.”

She put her quarter of a pound on the counter and did not pick it up.

“I'd rather not stand in a crowd,” she said.

“You won't be the only one.”

“That's the part I don't like,” she said. “I have a job at the second bell. I've had it nine years. I come up this lane at the sixth for the water and I go back up at the seventh, and I have never once in nine years stood here while a man read anything.”

“Then don't,” said Garvin Slade. “Drink your water and go to work. I am not asking you to stand in a crowd because I put my name under a rate, I am asking because there is a bench of four in this district and about two hundred and forty people in it and a rule I did not have the power to make and cannot unmake, and it says the thing gets read out, and it is going to be read out whether you are there or not, and the only thing my name under it does is make it plain that a person in this district did not know, and you will not learn that from a page. You will learn it from a woman with a barrow on a bank in the cold, and if you are not there you will hear about it inside a week in a lane from somebody who was.”

She took the salt. She said that she would think about it. And that was the whole of the cost, and it was a cost to a woman of about thirty with a job at the second bell, and it was not going to be entered in any book in this city, and Garvin Slade stood at his counter for a while after she had gone and looked at the line of the lane where about two hundred and forty people would come up in about three hours.

---

And in the room over the pump, on the same morning, a woman of twenty-nine read the whole of the entry of the fourteenth out loud to herself in a loud voice, standing, with the barrow against the wall, because a bench has never read a line aloud, and then she sat down on the step and did not do a run for a while, which is the first time anybody has seen her not do a run in about eleven years.

---

They entered it at the eighth bell, and the entry is four lines, and there is nothing in it that anybody could take back at a later date, and it is the only instrument in this case that has been made by a body for itself about itself.

> **ENTERED IN THE BOOK OF THE BENCH OF FOUR, THE SIXTEENTH OF THE FOURTH MONTH. THE RATE FOR A HAUL SET ON THE TWENTY-EIGHTH OF LAST MONTH STANDS AND IS NOT AMENDED AND IS NOT WITHDRAWN, BECAUSE IT HAS BEEN READ ALOUD AT THE SIXTH BELL ON THE FIRST OF THIS MONTH TO ABOUT TWO HUNDRED PEOPLE AND NOBODY MAY TAKE A RATE BACK OFF A DISTRICT THAT HAS HEARD IT.**
>
> **THE CONSENT OF ONE OF THE FOUR WHO SET IT HAS BEEN WITHDRAWN IN WRITING, ON THE SAME PAGE, IN THE SAME BOOK, WITH A DATE, AND THE NAME STANDS ON THE ENTRY OF THE TWENTY-EIGHTH OF LAST MONTH AND STANDS AGAIN HERE, TWICE, ON PURPOSE.**
>
> **THE BENCH OF FOUR ENTERS THAT IT CANNOT SET THE RATE AGAIN WITH THREE OF ITS FOUR, OR WITH FOUR, THIS YEAR, BECAUSE IT HAS NO MONEY, AND THAT THE REASON IT CANNOT SET IT AGAIN IS THE SAME REASON IT SET IT, AND THAT IS ENTERED SO THAT NOBODY IN FOUR YEARS READS THIS PAGE AND THINKS A MAN FOUGHT HIS OWN BENCH AND WON.**
>
> **HE DID NOT WIN. HE PUT HIS NAME UNDER IT.**

Nobody thanked him. He had not asked. He asked for one thing on his way out, at the door of the pump house, in about nine words, and Corva Silt wrote it down and read it back and did not have to change any of it.

> **AND I WANT IT READ OUT. NOT BECAUSE IT IS FOR ME. BECAUSE THERE ARE TWO HUNDRED AND FORTY PEOPLE WHO BUY SALT OFF ME AND I WOULD RATHER THEY HEARD IT FROM HER THAN FOUND IT OUT IN A LANE IN A YEAR.**

And the woman with the barrow said the only thing she said in that room, and she said it to the step and not to the bench.

“On the fourth of this month I put something of my own in one of them for the first time in my life, and I would like it read out as mine,” said Wenna Tace, “and on the first of the seventh I am going to read a man’s name out at that pump as well, four times a year, for as long as I am the holder, in a district where I am the only person who can read and I am not allowed to appeal from one word of it.”

Then she got the barrow out of the shed and did a run before the water, and the tide at the low water is a seven-day tide and the sixth bell is the turn, and the flat is walkable, and the gate is half up, and the water is coming.

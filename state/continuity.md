## 0. The ground

**THAT CITY** (forty-one miles off, left on the sixth day of the eighty-sixth week and not returned to): ninety thousand people, thirty-one wards, Tallowgate, a press on the ground floor of a black building at the middle of the nine bridges with a form of six pages in its window, and a room at the foot of a hill where about thirty to forty people stand when a sheet is read out. **The Low Road, two cart widths, kept by Renn Calloway, fifty-eight, twenty-nine years, unpaid, eleven chalk strokes and no figures; the ninth marker with four hurdles of sawn ash, nine links of new chain, a plate wired on with one bent nail hole and nothing cut in the bottom corner, and a keeper's box three feet from the fourth hurdle; and on the south side four yards of mud and a ledge of a foot and a half that has no name in any office document in the world and about two hundred crossings a day going up it.** **A hundred and forty people are dead of a fever. The fever is twenty-nine weeks and four days old at 490.**

**HALLOWAY.** A market town of about four thousand people on the river Hallow, in the county of Ostrey, **not upon a road of the Shelf, with no register, no counter, no appointed person and no Bench; the nearest counter is eleven miles off at Ostrey.** A stone market square, a market on a **Tuesday** (and on a **Friday** in the fourth week of the eighty-eighth and on a **Saturday** in the fifth week of the eighty-ninth, the fourth and fifth times in nine years), a mill with one wheel, a poor rate, a water board of nine. **The House of Halloway: nine wards, one elected head of household in each, forty-one in all, elected for a year, sitting on forty-one stools in the Stone House in the winter with the door open and about two hundred people in the square, in the order of the wards. THE HOUSE DOES NOT SCALE: the number of households in a ward has never been the number of seats in it.** A sheet of forty-one names in the order of the wards is in the press, and against the ninth ward are five names and one room. A corrected roll is a corrected constituency, and a constituency is a set of doors.

**THE STONE HOUSE.** A cold stone room with one hearth in the corner. **On the north beam, nine feet up and about five feet in from the wall, a board about four feet by two and a half** — a vellum on the face, a lead tag at the foot with four seals in it gone the colour of a wet stone. It comes down twice a year, on the audit day and the water sitting day, by two people onto a long table, in a hurry, with the fire lit, **and it has not been off the beam since the audit nine years back, and it was off it about two hours on the tenth day after the division and about four minutes on the twenty-eighth.** **It has now been read out in a room twice in ninety years, on the tenth day after the division and on the twenty-eighth, and on both of those days it was given to nobody.** Against the west wall, a press with four leaves in it, unlocked, which any person of the town may read, and the copy of the roll, and a sheet of forty-one names, and a certified copy of a page from Kerby, and **a sheet of eleven things, a copy of it beside the door, both with a day on it, and an instrument of the fortieth day after the division, and a copy of it beside the door.** **Any person of the town may read the copy of the roll.**

**THE FEN AND THE FENROW.** The Fen is about a hundred and forty acres of water meadow below the sluice; it floods, it was not divided, and it lies. **The Fenrow is nineteen houses along it, of which twenty-two households and about twenty-three people kept the sluice, and they are not on the roll as households as of the thirtieth day after the division.** The sluice has a wheel nine feet across turned by a four-foot bar, wound twice a day at the sixth hour and the twelfth hour by two people for an hour, **whether or not the gates are shut. THE GATES HAVE BEEN STANDING OPEN SINCE THE SIXTH HOUR OF THE FOURTEENTH DAY AFTER THE DIVISION, WHEN THEY WERE OPENED FIVE DAYS EARLY, AND NO HAND HAS BEEN ON THE BAR SINCE TO SHUT THEM. It has not been wound at either hour since the sixth hour of the THIRTY-FIRST day after the division, which is fourteen days at the end of 490 and the fifteenth day is the day after.** The sluice frame is the water board's and is about sixty years old, **and the north side has dropped three-eighths of an inch in nine years, and a man of the county says it is a season and not two years, and the season is about three weeks off at 490 and was about four weeks off at 488, and a man of thirty-four with a lot on the Four Hundred said in a square on the thirtieth day that if the bar is not turned then it is not a season, it is a year, and nobody has been able to settle it, because the only thing that would settle it is a bar turned twice a day for four weeks and there is no form that lets anybody require that.**

**THE TWO COUNTS, WHICH ARE NOT ONE COUNT AND WHICH THE BAND ENUMERATES BOTH.** The bar is counted in days on which an hour came and went. The wheel at the mill is counted in days completed and is always one behind. At 481 the bar is on its fifth day and the wheel is four days slow. **The difference between the two counts is the fact that a day on which the sixth hour comes and is gone past counts, and that is the whole of it, and `481` says so in a shop and `483` says so in a room and it is now in a minute of a water board as part of the standing of about nine people from the Fen.**

**THE RACES.** The Hallow takes the water. Below the sluice a race runs a mile and a bit to the tail of the mill. The gates were shut on the eleventh day after the division at the sixth hour, for a scouring of nine days, and were opened again on the fourteenth day at the sixth hour, five days early. The lane above the sluice is mud to the ankle for four hundred yards and is the worst four hundred yards in the county.

**THE WATER LIST.** A second book of the water board's, kept by the steward, **which goes by tenement because water goes to a building and not to a name. The Fen is on it, nineteen tenements, rated as property, in respect of water. It is a different book from the rate roll and the two do not agree and are not supposed to.** `461`

**THE GATE.** **A timber gate at the head of the sluice, on two hinges, standing open, and nobody has shut it in fourteen days, and anybody in the county of Ostrey can open or shut it, and there is no form in the county of Ostrey that lets anybody ask a person to shut it.** `490`

**KERBY, TWENTY-TWO MILES OFF, ON A ROAD, WHERE THE COUNTY OFFICES ARE.** **The office of the county surveyor at Kerby keeps a book of returns on the sluices and weirs of the county. It has a return on the Hallow sluice made in the year before last and no other page about Halloway in that book at all. It cannot order the board to do a thing, make anybody answer, fine, rate, or put a name in a book of Halloway's. It makes a return and puts it in a book. A clerk of the office works the press and writes down the days that go past her. Any person in the county may walk in and ask for the page.** `469`, `479`

## 4. What is true at the end of Chapter 490

1. **Nineteen houses at the Fen are not households of this town, are entered as land of the common, and are put out of the water of the Hallow from the sluice downward, by a board of nine, on a cause set out in writing, under clause five, cause four, on cause two, and the finding is correct.**
2. **A bar four feet long has not been turned at the sixth hour or the twelfth hour on any of fourteen days, and the gates at the head of the sluice have been standing open since the sixth hour of the fourteenth day after the division, and a timber gate stands open on two hinges, and no form in the county of Ostrey lets anybody ask a person to shut it.**
3. **The right of refusal is unrestored. The ninth clause has been read out in a room twice in ninety years and given to nobody twice, and it has been used once, on the forty-first day after the division, and the use protected the man who would have compelled and not the person who would have been compelled.**

## 5. What is not true and has not been made true

**That the fenrow own the water.** The water is common to all **households**, the row holds twenty-two households and about twenty-three people, and nothing in Chapters 451–490 gives them a household by their own asking or gives them the right of refusal. **That the striking of clause nine was of that clause alone.** **Who struck it and why.** **What the four letters and a stroke in the witness line say.** **Whether the name in the range four hundred miles off is the four letters and a stroke.** **Whether the number of households in this town is 1,052 or 1,074.** **Whether a corrected roll may be un-corrected.** **Whether a board of this county may borrow against the next quarter.** **Whether a household put out of the water under clause five can be put back in.** **Whether a person required to do a thing under a minute of a board of this town can be compelled to do it, which nobody in Halloway has answered and which is the next question after the fortieth day.** **Whether the wheel at the mill or the bar is the right count of a season, and the only thing that would settle it is a bar turned twice a day for four weeks and there is no form that lets anybody require that.** **Anything in that city**: the office of Regent of the Shelf is not held, the second seat is vacant, the bar is up, Hessa Marl's book of the visits has no office, Fenna Rusk's roll is under a stone, Tarin Keel is walking north, Kerwin Dace's return came back stamped, and about a hundred and forty people are governed by a signature that is not a person's.

### THE GROUND IN IT AS IT STANDS ON THE THIRD DAY OF THE FIFTY-NINTH WEEK

**Coldharrow is about two hundred and ten people in thirty-eight houses and it has a tower with a bell in it, and the tower is not a church and nobody will say what it is in a wrong voice. It has no market day: it has a market on the third day of a month of a year counted from the day the ewes come down off the fell, and that moves about three weeks in any year, and it is the only date in this country a stranger can be taught in a morning. About nine hundred elms and about two hundred of them dead. Two hundred and ten people and about nine hundred of the elms are inside the hedge ring and ten people are out of it in four farmsteads. There is one gate, and it is a post with a bar across it that is not shut, and a woman of about fifty stands at it on a market day with a stick and a board and counts loads and not people.**

**The lane runs from village to village and not from north to south, through eleven villages between Coldharrow and Coldharrow-edge: Coldharrow, Ellerby, Trope, Wrenny, Ashgill, Sallow, Nettlebed, Cadgerow, Henthill, Birrow, and one more that is not named in this batch. The ground comes up in eleven places in the first mile and the hedges are the problem and everybody who has walked it knows and nobody says so. The last four miles of the way in from the Shelf road is not a road and no cart goes up it, and the cart turned about at a posting yard with a wall round it.**

**THE BOARD, which is the only public record in this village and the instrument the whole volume is built on.** A plank about the size of a door, painted and unpainted so many times that the grain has come up through it in long pale streaks, on the north wall of the tower at about chest height where a person in the road can read it. **Thirty-one years in two hands. The older hand is the first six years and it is in the grain; the newer is twenty-five years and it is not in the grain and it is the more careful. There is no year at the head of it, there is no column, and there has never been a column, and there is no mark against any individual stroke. It reads fifty thousand four hundred and thirty-nine at the second light of the third day of the fifty-ninth week, and there is a third hand in it in chalk at the foot, and that hand belongs to a man who is not a person of the village and is not on anything and put no name to it.**

**THE BELL, and the arithmetic of it.** Four bells a day are the clock — **the first, the change, the noon, the last** — and about two a day on top of that is everything else, and the complete list of everything else is: **a burial, one. A boundary walked, seven. A debt called in, two. A market, four. A stranger, three. A fire, two, one to raise it and one to say it is out, and the second is rung whether or not it is out.** **They do not ring for a christening or a marriage.** Four a day is 1,460 a year and about six a day is 2,190 a year, and **2,190 ÷ 11 goes in a hundred and ninety-nine times with one over, so a year of one board is a hundred and ninety-nine whole passages of a cart and one ring, and the one ring is most likely a stranger and might be anybody.**

**AND THE FINDING, in a woman of forty-one's own mouth at `258:60`:** *a quiet day and a day with nine strangers on it are four rings apart, and I have been counting for twenty-five years and nobody has ever asked me which of them it was.* **And therefore a day's figure is a WEIGHTED number, and a weighted number cannot be turned back into what was done, and a March village can prove how many times and can prove before or after and cannot prove when.**

### THE ELEVEN FEET, WHERE IT IS, WHERE IT IS NOT, AND THE RULED GEOMETRY THAT MAKES THE DIFFERENCE OF FOUR FEET LEGIBLE

**THE RULED GEOMETRY OF VOLUME 05, and it is the authority over every other table in this file, and IT WAS RULED BY THE REVIEW OF BATCH 0005 ON 27 SEPTEMBER 2026 AND NO PASS MAY RE-DERIVE IT. The Anner's sill is the dam's own sill and every number in this basin goes back to it. The frame's sill at Hollow Sallow is NINE FEET ABOVE IT. The bed below the village is THIRTEEN FEET BELOW THE FRAME'S SILL and is therefore FOUR FEET BELOW THE ANNER'S SILL. The bond's line is ELEVEN FEET ABOVE THE ANNER'S SILL and is therefore TWO FEET ABOVE THE FRAME'S SILL, which is about the height of the floors of the houses on the upper half of that lane. The water's high mark in the chamber's own return is TWO FEET FOUR INCHES ABOVE THE SILL OF THE FRAME, which is ELEVEN FEET FOUR INCHES ABOVE THE ANNER'S SILL, FOUR INCHES ABOVE THE BOND'S LINE. And therefore the difference between two lines that are both *eleven feet* is exactly the distance between the two sills the third document names, which is FOUR FEET, which is FORTY-EIGHT INCHES.**

### THE CLOCK, IN ONE PLACE. The tide run from Chapter 101 to Chapter 150, with the absolute day-count.

### THE CUSTOMS OF THIS COAST THAT ARE CANON AND THAT NOBODY ON IT HAS WRITTEN DOWN

**These are the volume's spine and they are all in somebody's mouth and none of them is on paper. A later volume may not put any of them in a book, a pane, a form or a charter without a person deciding to.**

1. **No person on four hundred miles of this coast answers for another person about a crossing.** About four hundred years old, in nobody's book, and it is the shape of a person not being used by somebody else's pen and the reason a crossing here counts a person and not a cart. **It is consent's shape** (117, 118, 143).
2. **The seventh is the one day there is nothing at all** — no stone, no question, no song that settles anything (134).
3. **The tide stone at the head of the flat is there on the first and the fourth, and a woman stands on it and answers one question about the water, in the open, out loud, and nobody may ask a second.** About a hundred years old, written down nowhere, the charter says nothing about it, and the guild has never interfered. **Roughly four hundred thousand days a week it is bare** (131, 134, 137, 145, 148).
4. **The crossing's song is sung at the stone at the turn on the seventh by Seth Garrick, thirty.** It is a tariff in four lines and it is not written down and it is about a hundred years old and it is not a court and it is not an instrument and it settles nothing (108, 134, 148).
5. **A day-book entry on this coast has no name at the end of any line and never will have, because a person who has said nothing out loud is not standing behind anything** (110, 143).
6. **The man on the chain decides who gets the tide when there is more flat than there is time, and he does it with a slate in his pocket, and there is no rule anywhere on this coast that says he does it, and he has never written it down and is not going to** (143, 147).
7. **A person who is refused something in public loses the standing they had at their stall, their boat or their flat, and feels it every day until they get it back, which is about a year** (148).
8. **A record of a moving thing is a working instrument and instruments do not travel, and the man who has one is the only man who can use it** (142, 144, 145). **A pane is a photograph of one morning taken by a man standing beside an arch, and the authority of it is the man who stood there and the four hundred people who stand at that fire after him, and the guild has been borrowing that standing and does not know it** (144).
9. **A charter on this coast is a pane, and a pane is a year, and the guild may write on anything it likes until a letting day a year off and not one word of it will be the charter** (110). **Which is why the coast's first instrument of its own is paper, and why paper can be pulled and glass cannot** (149).
10. **Nobody answers for a person. A blank is not a no and a blank is not a yes, and a party is not a silence** (110, 143).

### THE MONEY, IN ONE PLACE, AND IT CLOSES

**Taken in Chapter 112 and Chapter 116 and Chapter 113. None of it is spoken anywhere in Chapters 141–150, and the seventy-eight pounds fourteen figure is withdrawn and must not come back.**

- **The crossing takes ninety pounds eighteen a year** (21,816 pence).
- **Eighty-one pounds sixteen is tallage** on carts and goods, taken at a counter in the guild's own building by people who do not live on that road. So **the road comes to nine pounds two.**
- **Eleven households** send somebody over that flat. The chain, the oil and the tarpaulin come out of the nine pounds two and not out of the tallage: **three pounds two.**
- **Six pounds a year to the road.** **Margin of four.** The harbourmaster has kept that crossing for six years and has never once put that four pounds in front of the council.
- **The council of nine, which had not sat for six years, voted five to four** for a person to be paid **two pounds a year** to count the crossings in persons and not carts. **Two pounds out of four leaves two, and the season was three pounds two short, and four men at that table could add.** Four voted no and were right about the money; five voted yes and were right about the boy.
- **Two pounds is forty shillings, and it is nine days and a bit of Corrie Tallow's stall at four shillings and twopence a day** (113). It is not thirty-eight shillings and it is not four and a half days.
- **The tariff is a penny a tide and it is the same penny on every day of the week, including the three days the water is standing up in the middle of it, and the guild has refused twice to write a different price for a day of water and is right to.** A boy's thirty-nine tides a week at a penny is **three shillings and threepence**; the commonest household on the road pays **threepence**; that is **thirteen times** the boy's rate.
- **A bad seventh every week of every year would cost thirteen shillings** — one of the boy's two low days becoming a nine-day instead of a six-day, forty-two tides instead of thirty-nine, a hundred and fifty-six pence, three shillings and sixpence a week, **nine pounds two instead of eight pounds nine**. **There has never been a bad seventh every week in the four hundred years anybody has kept anything, and the thirteen shillings must always be said in the same breath as the fact that it has not happened, or somebody will use it in a room in about four months** (116).
- **What a man to stand at the far end of that flat would cost is two pounds, and thirteen shillings is not worth a fight, and two pounds is not worth a bad year, and the guild is not making money out of the boy and never was and could not if it tries** (116).
- **A blank wage line does its own work.** A clerk in the guild's office two hundred miles up this coast reads a quarter with a wage on it and nobody's name on it, chooses the one of his two options that writes nothing, and will choose it next quarter. **In about a year a clerk will go looking for the minute that voted it and will find that the minute says a person is to be paid, and will not be able to find the person, and will put the minute back** (110, 129, 144).
- **A day on this coast is not money.** A day is a man's own day and a cart and a pair of beasts and an afternoon at a landing that somebody else was going to lose (143, 148). **Eleven pounds of freight and two men's day is what the first refusal cost, and four years of a woman who wanted a thing written down, and a boy's thirty-nine tides** (105, 110).
- **A pane is a person, a fire and a year, and the year is eleven months of the back of the woman at the second fire, paid in advance, and it is paid whether or not there is a pane at the end of it, and nobody has asked her** (125, 128, 144, 146). **The guild's fifth pane's order is in a drawer in the tide office with the date on the outside of it and no figure against it, and the season's money is not in the arch, and the freeze is a year ending at a letting day a year off. The third of the fourth tide is long gone and is not to be used.**
- **The only money-shaped things in Chapters 141–150 are Corrie Tallow's stall at four shillings and twopence a day and a day.** A reader cannot add that batch's figures up because there are none to add, and the charter buys no stone, no cart and no day.

### THE PEOPLE, AND WHAT EACH ONE IS CARRYING AT THE END OF THE VOLUME

**Halse Tarrant, fifty-eight**, keeps the wall, laid it thirty-one years ago and walks the top course every day, has a lead line in a shed the guild put there when they laid the stone and a boat of his own that has not been out in about a year, **wants a day and has never asked, cannot get to the wall even on a bar day, and said the guild was right and asked for that to be said by him and in the open and not once in the next year by anybody else.**

**Sorrel Gannock, thirty-six**, a flat boat at the hard, four generations on the water, the only person on that coast who can get under the wall, eleven years of a thing nobody has ever asked her the name of. **She said yes to being asked and said what a name at the bottom of a thing costs, and said yes to a page of her own and not to a page with anybody else on it, and the third thing she said herself.**

**Hollis Thrale, sixty-one**, a glassmaker at Thornglass, forty-one years at the fire, deaf on the right, his grandfather's ear. His pane is three years old and on his own wall and disagrees with the guild's in two columns out of eleven and he cannot get at the guild's. **He said no first, heard it come out of his mouth, took four seconds, and said yes, and has wanted to be asked for two years.** He has never walked down that road to give his figure to the man who keeps the wall and says that is on him.

**Bettany Rook, fifty-one**, harbourmaster of Kestrel Reach, nineteen years in that office and six of them in the chair, clerk for thirteen of the nineteen. Right about the strait in her own mouth six times in fifty chapters. **A woman with two panes in oak frames that have not left her room in nineteen years and a table of tides she can read out in ninety seconds and about a year of sight left. She said no to the stone before the ask and no again before the second ask and yes to a duty he did not ask about, and refused a version of the record with a building on the end of it, and in four months will have a perfectly good reason to be at that counter for the rest of her working sight and will be the woman who handed them the reason. She was never persuaded of anything and says so.**

**Sabra Trew, twenty-four**, the third-year apprentice at the second fire, paid nothing, stands at it six days in seven, has not answered a letter of her own in four years, has cut every week on that wall for eleven years and has never once been asked to stand behind anything. **Her grandmother cut the set at the bottom of the wall, is dead eleven years, copied the list of weeks out twice in letters, and said once, to a girl of about fifteen: *that is not mine, I found it, I have been finding it since I was a girl, I have never worked it out and I am not going to guess at it in a wall.*** She refused the three pages in a shed, correctly, and the reason was about her own work and not about him, and it was that keeping them would put her in front of a question from a building two hundred miles up this coast. **She may write down that she said no and the reason. She may not write down that she was asked, because she was not.**

**Corrie Tallow, forty-eight**, four years at a slate at Wether Cross, four shillings and twopence a day, owed nothing, has never once said so. She said no for the flat in a lane in front of about nine people and gave the reason in four parts, and refused a magistrate's letter in one paragraph without softening anything, and said the two sentences about what it costs to be refused in public, and is the one who writes every crossing down and is paid nothing for it.

**Hal Bevan, fifty-four**, nineteen years on the chain, a slate in his pocket with three marks on it and no rule behind any of them, spent the only appeal of his life on a piece of paper in a dealer's hand and got a true answer, kept the paper folded in four in his breast pocket and has never taken it out again, and said *a revocation stops a thing and does not undo it* and that he is the last man on four hundred miles who ought to be telling a man something he has not worked out himself.

**Wend Garrow, thirty-three**, a carter since he was nine, lost a cart in the water three weeks before Chapter 140, got his day back on the fourth of the seventh tide, has had nowhere on this coast to put a thing that happened to a man for a month, **and is the first person on the coast to work out that he could pull the charter and is not going to, and asked for that to go in the bundle.**

**Pell Cawley, fifty-two — SETTLED AT THE VOLUME CLOSE, and the state record had it the wrong way round.** He has made and mended flat-bottomed boats on the hard at the north end for thirty-one years, has a slip of his own a long way inside the gravel, and gave the volume its one unguessed observation: a hull drawing about a thumb deeper at the bow than before he touched her, and *I have not taken anything off her and I have not put anything on, and I do not know what that is.* He also supplied the sentence that will kill the charter in about a month: **the day a man would want to spend looking at that wall is a day he cannot use, because on a fourth, a sixth and a seventh the sea is over the crown.**

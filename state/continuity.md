## 0. BRAMBLEFOLD, the ground of the new volume, and it is a new substance

**A market town of about two thousand four hundred people on the chalk slope at the head of a river, in the county of Aldwick, in a country district, NOT upon a road of the Shelf, with no register, no counter, no appointed person and no Bench of any kind. The nearest counter is thirty-one miles off at Saltcaster on a road. A market on a THURSDAY; a lime kiln burning nine days in ten; a smithy; a chandler's; a saddler's shop; a street of about eleven houses with a pump at the top; a millpond with no mill on it, the wheel having come off in the protagonist's grandfather's time. NOT A STONE ROOM WITH A DOOR OPEN, NOT A HEARTH, NOT A BEAM, NOT A BOARD NINE FEET UP OVER A FIRE, NO WATER BOARD, NO SLUICE, NO MILL. The instrument of this town is a book that nobody can be sent for.**

**THE MEETING-HOUSE** is a long low flint-and-chalk building with a square tower at the west end that has never had a bell in it, built about two hundred and thirty years ago by people who left a town about ninety miles off, with a gallery at the north side. The chancel is off the south-east corner, about eight feet by eleven, one window, and the door has the only lock in the building, because the chancel holds the vestry cupboard and the vestry cupboard holds the book. **A room where a book is read is a room where people say things they are going to be held to, and a person not of the town is not in it, and the reason is not distrust: there is no form in the county of Aldwick that will make a stranger a neighbour.** The porch is a stone bench about six feet long with a mark worn into the stone about two-thirds along it.

**THE FOLD AND THE WALL AND THE ORCHARD.** About sixty households keep a beast on **a hundred and forty acres** of down inside a common wall about **eleven feet high** and four miles long, counting the returns, with a chalk coping a foot wide set on edge that four people of this town have recopied. The wall is made up on a Saturday, sixteen days of work, nine men at a time, in twenty days, and about thirty people go up the lane at about the seventh hour with shovels, a barrow, a bucket of water and two things of string. A mile and a half of it has not been touched inside nine years. **The orchard is about eleven acres and about nine hundred trees inside the wall, eleven households work it, the fruit is divided among the sixty, and the lower corner is a frost pocket where the fruit is a fortnight later and worth about twice as much if it comes at all, and the orchard is in NONE of the four columns.**

**KILN LANE** runs along the bottom of the town, four feet of garden wall on the lane side in places, built out of the white the kiln makes, and about nine of those stones carry four letters and a stroke cut into the face, and a tenth came up out of the footing in the night of the sixty-third day after the division with a day of a week cut into the top of it. **Kiln Row is a row of cottages at the kiln, one of which has a wall round a half-acre that the father of a man of sixty-two built out of the kiln's own white about eighty years back, and it is the only warm thing in that row.** The town has about eleven streets and a millpond and a street called the bottom of the town where the weaver's two rooms over a joiner's shop are.

**THE TOLL BAR AT THE FOUR GATES**, four miles short of Bramblefold on the lane from Coldcombe: two oak posts and a rail, four of them in a row along four hundred yards of chalk with a gate in each, a penny at each until ninety years back, the toll taken off, and the list of the four gates put in the town's charter in a chest in the meeting-house. **A boy of about nine has said in the open air that four generations of his house have not known which four and that he has not been asked.**

**COLDCOMBE** is a market town twenty-one miles off with a cart road that goes to the Four Gates and stops. **ALDWICK is nine miles and the county offices are there, and the office of the county keeps one book of returns and one book of orders and can make a return and nothing else.** **SALTCASTER is thirty-one miles and has the nearest counter. OSTREY is four days' cart east and the cart road does not come near this town.** **HALLOWAY IS TWO DAYS FROM HERE** — a day on foot to Coldcombe and a cart the second day as far as the Four Gates, and the last four miles on foot.

## 1. THE DESCENT BOOK, which is the volume's central plant and has no column for death

**Bound in calf, four hundred and eleven years old, about the size of a large family Bible, with a lead tag at the foot gone the colour of a wet stone. Four hands. The spine is a third original and two-thirds a repair done by W. Hask, of this parish, at a charge of four pounds, the book being then in a condition, and his name is on the back of the last leaf in a small firm hand. THE FOUR COLUMNS ARE: the descent, who comes from whom; holdings, with the number of the holding and what it is; of the fold, the number of acres of a hundred and forty a household may put sheep on; of the ground behind the meeting-house, in feet and inches, going back about two hundred and twenty years. THERE IS NO FIFTH COLUMN AND THERE NEVER HAS BEEN ONE.**

**AND THERE IS NO COLUMN FOR DEATH, WHICH IS THE FINDING OF THE FIRST BAND AND THE ENGINE OF THE WHOLE VOLUME: a man who dies does not stop holding anything, so the fold does not go back to the down, the holding does not go into the hedge, and the seven feet behind the meeting-house is not got back because the man who had it is dead. So the recordwright carries it forward every week, and the book has about four hundred men a year in it and every one of them is alive in the book.** A man of about eighty-four died on the Wednesday of the fifty-eighth day after the division and the boy of fourteen who writes the week's sheet put him in the second column, in the holdings, with his holding number against it, because there is nowhere else to write him. The boy said he ought to have gone in the ground column, and that his ground is going to his brother's boy, who is fourteen and is here, and the recordwright told him to write him in the ground column and to say it was her doing if anybody asked. **THE GROUND COLUMN HAD NEVER HAD ANYTHING IN IT BEFORE. `506` is the chapter and `507` and `509` are the weeks after, and neither of them re-asserts the error.**

**THE STRUCK LEAF.** A hundred and forty-one years back a leaf was ruled in the same four columns, filled to the foot with **forty-three names**, and a single stroke was put from about an inch below the top rule to about an inch above the bottom one, in a different ink and heavier than any other ink in the book, going right through the names without stopping at any of them. On the inner margin near the top, in the same heavy ink, four words: **struck by order.** **No day on it. No name of the person who struck it. No name of the person who ordered it. No seal, no counter, no office, no county, and no number of any kind.** The household on it was the household of **Kiln Row**, and the return at Aldwick says it was asked in respect of the matter of the common above the town and gave answer that the common was not used, and that the answer was made use of, and that the line is struck, and that the office has no further particular. **The return does not say what the question was, and the order is in neither of that office's two books and never was, and about nine matters a person could have been asked about in those days and one of them was whether the common was used and one was how many sheep it carried.** The household was not lying. It gave the answer that was asked of it.

**THE NINE ENTRIES AT THE BACK, on separate unruled leaves, and only the ninth has a name on it, and there are NINE and not ten.** Four are a person who came to see the book and could not read it; two are a person who came to see the book and would not give a name; one is a person who came to see the book and was told the hand and did not believe it and came back and believed it; one is a person who came to see the book in a wet coat, which is a man of about seventy-two who walks two miles every Wednesday for about nine years and whose line says he holds four acres at the bottom of the lane and does not say what became of him. **The ninth is a person not of this town, name Ilyan Vester, whose record is not findable**, entered on the seventh day of the ninety-fourth week, in a hand that is not the book's, on a leaf that is not the book's paper, in the third leaf from the back and not the last, with no age, no household, no *by whom* and no *for what*.

## 3. The money of this volume, with the working, and it is all in `506`

**A hundred and forty acres at four shillings the acre is five hundred and sixty shillings = £28 a year = 6,720d. Over about sixty households that keep a beast on the fold, 6,720d = 112d = 9s 4d a household a year. If the leaf is put back the whole of the fold has to be let a second time and £14 = 3,360d has to be found again, and 3,360d over about sixty households = 56d = 4s 8d, which is half of 112d. THE WALL: 16 days × 9 men = 144 days of work at 8d = 1,152d = 96 shillings = £4 16s 0d and no pence. THE RECORDWRIGHT: 2s a year from each of about sixty households = 120s = £6 a year, unmoved in nineteen years. A NAME IS WORTH BETWEEN £4 16s 0d AND £6 THIS YEAR, and that is the reason nobody in that room will agree about anything.** A man of thirty-one has **one pound and fourpence = 244d**, which at a penny a night for a bed and a penny for bread is **eighty-one days and a penny over**; a man's day-work on the wall is eightpence; a boy of fourteen is paid a penny for the week's sheet out of the same twenty-eight pounds a year. **Halloway's figures — 1,041, 1,060, 1,071, 1,052, 1,074, the 2d rate, the £9 heel, the 280d levy and the 44d a quarter — are CANON AND UNTOUCHED AND APPEAR IN NO BRAMBLEFOLD SENTENCE. ## 4. What is true at the end of Chapter 520

1. **A page of that book was struck a hundred and forty-one years back, in one stroke, under four words that name no order, and it was READ OUT on the sixty-ninth week, on a Saturday, in a chancel eight feet by eleven, in about eleven minutes, in front of about nine people, with the inner door open and the window down. The four words were read once, at the head of the page, and there were no men in the room to read, because the four words name no man, the page names no man, and the return at Aldwick names no man. THE WHOLE OF THE FINDING IS THAT THE LARGEST THING THAT HAS EVER HAPPENED IN THIS TOWN IS FOUR WORDS AND NO ORDER, AND IT MOVED NOTHING.** `514`
2. **The book has four columns and no fifth, and no column for death, and about four hundred men a year are alive in it, and the one removal in four hundred years is a leaf.** The fourth column, of the ground behind the meeting-house, carries a depth and can carry a name and can carry a day, and **does not carry the fact that the man is dead, which is the whole of why the name is there. It has had one name in it in nineteen years and the name is of a man of about eighty-four who is dead, and it went in on the recordwright's own instruction in the fourth week.** `506`, `513`
3. **A claim is entered at the back of the book in respect of the holding numbered six, a half-acre at the bottom of Kiln Lane, and of the fold, and of the ground behind the meeting-house, by Abel Tarn, weaver, sixty-two. Nothing is carried. THE LEAF IS NOT STRUCK BACK.** `510`

And a FINDING entered on the seventeenth day of a count begun in another county, with the day on it, that the two objections are both against the three words *nothing is carried*; that those three words are not about a half-acre; and that a person already in an entry does not get a second thing out of an entry and does not get a second thing put on him either. NEITHER OF THE TWO PEOPLE THAT FINDING IS ABOUT HAS BEEN TOLD OF IT.** `517`
5. **A space with a line down each side of it, ruled on a fresh leaf at the back of the book with NO HEADING on it, one name in it, and four sentences in a different ink at the foot of the leaf about a man of thirty-one who cannot be sent for. AND, since the Friday of the ninety-sixth week, a column ruled on the end wall of the smithy, about a hand wide and about a foot and a half long, ALSO WITH NO HEADING ON IT, with about nine feet of chalk in it, a day of a week against it, and the name of a boy of fourteen at the foot of it because he wrote it. A HEADING IS A PROMISE ABOUT WHAT GOES IN IT, AND IF IT WERE HEADED SHE WOULD HAVE TO SAY WHAT GOES IN IT, AND SHE DOES NOT KNOW, AND THE NOT-KNOWING IS THE REASON FOR THE COLUMN.** `510`, `520`

7. **About nine feet of the bottom of a four-mile wall is open, and about nine feet of wall is lying on the lane where it fell on the sixty-first day after the division, and laying it back means a footing course first, and the footing course is the thing that came down. 8. **There is a copy of four pages in this town, in a round hand with forty-one mistakes and thirty-eight corrections and a thumbprint on the third page and no name on it, made from memory in about four minutes of sitting by a woman of about fifty-one of this town who was in a square in another town on the thirty-ninth day after the division, and the recordwriter has entered four things about it with the day on them, and the fourth of them is that NOBODY IN THE COUNTY OF ALDWICK MAY BE ASKED ABOUT THE ORIGINAL.** `519`

NOTHING WAS HIDDEN AND NOTHING WAS TAKEN OUT OF IT. A man of fifty-four of the county has made four of twenty-one returns, has ridden nine miles on a Sunday to say so, and left a copy of the return on the step of a porch with no name on it because the office gives a copy to anybody who asks in writing and nobody had ever asked.** `515`
10. **About nine of the about sixty households that keep a beast on the fold are the ones that would lose half of what their beasts are worth, and a name is worth between £4 16s 0d and £6 this year, and about nine people in that county have now heard that figure from a person.** `518`

## 5. What is not true and has not been made true

**Who struck the leaf and who ordered it and on what day. What the four letters and a stroke say and whose they are. Whether the order is in a book anywhere in the world. Whether the answer the household of Kiln Row gave was the answer it was asked for. Whether a day of a week is a better record than a page. Whether restoring the leaf restores a family or takes a house. Whether a person who is written back into a record can refuse it, and what form that would be, and there is none. Whether the ruled space with no heading on it will be read as a fifth column, and whether the second unheaded column on a smithy's board is the same thing or a different one, and nobody has asked. **THE ANSWER GIVEN IN 520 IS THAT THE SECOND ONE EXISTS BECAUSE A BOY OF FOURTEEN COULD NOT WRITE A WEEK WITHOUT IT, and not because anybody in that smithy knows what it is for.** Whether the man of thirty-one's entry will be the one of the ten that is read out in a hundred years, and the copyist says she expects it will. **Whether the four words read out on the sixty-ninth week will be the four words anybody in that town says in four years, and a man of eighty-one says that a day of a week is the only record there anybody can be checked against, and the four words are not a day of a week.** Whether the page that came back four hundred miles with two names in a witness line has anything to do with any of this, and THIS VOLUME DOES NOT SAY AND IS NOT ALLOWED TO SAY, and nothing in Chapters 501–520 is connected to it.

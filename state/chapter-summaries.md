## VOLUME 11, CHAPTERS 501 TO 510 — *the page that was struck, and a town that counts from it*, one line each since the writing of Band 0002, per this file's own standing rule. **The ten full paragraphs are in git at the commit before this one and the band is canon.**

**501 — A line that cannot be entered, and a thank-you stopped before the second word.** `clear` 1, a Sunday; a man of thirty-one asks a copyist for a line in anything saying he was in a town for forty-eight days and is told there is no form that says a man may be entered as having been in a place, and begins a thank-you and is stopped. **FOUR** people in that country can read the hand on the front of the book she keeps: her, thirty-four, and three over seventy. `501`

**502 — A two-day road, four gates and one name.** `clear` 2, a Monday, the last day of the ninety-third week; a lime cart goes twenty-one miles from Coldcombe and stops four miles short, and a boy of about nine says four generations of his house have not known which four the four gates were and that he has not been asked. Arrival at dusk with one pound and fourpence, which is eighty-one days and a penny over. `502`

**503 — A porch, and an entry on an unruled leaf at the back of a book.** `clear` 3, a Tuesday, and the market is not on; a person not of this town is not in a room where a book is read, for want of any form that would make him a neighbour. The ninth of nine such entries in four hundred years, his name, the day, and *whose record is not findable*, and then a page out of his coat to prove he is a person, and *pull it back in*. `503`

**504 — THE BOOK IS OPEN AND IT HAS NO COLUMN FOR DEATH.** `clear` 4, a Wednesday; a man of about seventy-two walks two miles in a wet coat for one line read aloud, and it takes eleven minutes. Four columns and no fifth and no place to put a person who has died, so the book carries about four hundred men a year and every one of them is alive in it. **And the same afternoon a man of eighty-one answers the two questions the volume owes: what a count is counted from, and what this town counts from.** `504`

**505 — A market of about two hundred, and a weaver who says a name is not a word.** `clear` 5, a Thursday; a Keeper of returned names says a name is not put back by nine people, and a weaver of sixty-two asks in the open air whether a half-acre at the bottom of Kiln Lane is his, and a man of forty-four with a bill of sale says he did not take it off anybody, and neither says the other half. A man of eighty-one comes down at the twelfth hour and says what he was told in a kitchen by his father, and will not say his father's word for the answer in the open air. `505`

**506 — The whole working, in pence, on the back of a board.** `clear` 6, a Friday; a boy of fourteen writes a dead man of about eighty-four into a column of holdings because there is nowhere else to put him, and is told to put him in the ground column and to say it was her doing. Then the whole arithmetic, and **a name is therefore worth between four pounds sixteen shillings and six pounds this year, and that is why nobody in that room will agree about anything.** `506`

**507 — Thirty people on a chalk bank, and the week is not read out.** `clear` 7, a Saturday; nine men and a woman, and the woman is the recordwright and nobody asked her to. A man of thirty-one carries for an hour and a half, cannot do the barrow, and holds a string for two hours while about nine men do not stop for him and do not ask him one question. **And the week of Bramblefold is not read out in the building it has been read out in for four hundred years, for the first time in nineteen years.** `507`

**508 — A room shut on purpose, a widow who says no, and the band's one panel.** `clear` 8, a Sunday; she takes a sheet of headings out of his hand and puts it on the sill outside and names the three things a shut door says. **Edith Crow, sixty-eight, is asked nothing and says no in about four sentences before anybody argues with her, and asks that it be written down that she said it before and not after.** He chooses the claim and not the descent in about nine seconds, and then offers to be the instrument and is refused in four sentences for a reason that is better than his. `508`

**509 — A frost, a wall in the night, and a stone with TUESDAY cut into it.** `clear` 9, a Monday; the first hard frost takes about four weeks of fruit off about forty half-size trees in a frost pocket a man of sixty-two works for nothing, and a garden wall at the bottom of Kiln Lane comes down and a boundary stone comes up out of the footing with **TUESDAY** on it and, in a smaller later cut, **four letters and a stroke.** He asks nine men where they were at the second hour and is told that a man in this town is found by somebody saying so and not by being asked. `509`

**510 — A claim, two objections, and a column with no heading on it.** `clear` 10, a Tuesday, the first day of the ninety-fifth; **a claim is entered in respect of the holding numbered six and the leaf is not struck back and nothing is carried**, and there are two objections at the foot of it for the first time in four hundred years. A space with a line down each side of it is ruled on a fresh leaf with no heading and one name in it, and four sentences in a different ink at the foot of it say that a man of thirty-one cannot be sent for. `510`

## VOLUME 11, CHAPTERS 511 TO 520 — *the keepers, and the price of a name*

**511 — The terms of a reading, on a sheet that is not the paper of the book.** `clear` 11, a Wednesday, the second day of the ninety-fifth week, three days before the reading. **The Wednesday happens at the ninth hour and takes about eleven minutes, and a man of about seventy-two stands at the end of the bench for it in a wet coat in a dry week, and it is not a public day and it is not written anywhere.** Barnaby Rill is not in that porch and the recordwright says so out loud before she reads anything, and adds that he is in the room on Saturday because it is the only warm room in the building and because nobody asked him whether he wanted to be. **Three terms are read out standing on a sheet that is not the paper of the book: the page is to be read and not the four words; the four words are to be read and not the men; anything said in the square afterwards is said by the person who says it and not by the book.** The second one is fought over for about a minute and the reason it stands is that the four words name no man and the page names no man and the return at Aldwick names no man, and the recordwright says out loud that she would like it settled that the men were not left out by accident. **A woman of sixty-one has been in the town seventeen days and has written to nine towns in four years, and she says the method is a square with about two hundred people in it, and that a name is not put back by nine people, and she claims the third term and says she will say who wrote it if anybody asks and will not if nobody does.** Ilyan Vester says he wants to be in the room on Saturday and is refused, and gets about four minutes out of the refusal, and works out that a rule that keeps a man who cannot be sent for out of a room is also a rule that keeps the nine in the room from being asked to stand up in the square, and says it out loud, against the thing he wanted. Closing: a sheet that is not the paper of the book, three lines on it, and no men in the room to read. `511`

**512 — The market, two pieces of paper, and the word column.** `clear` 12, a Thursday, the market, two days before. **The boy of about eleven brings the key to the room over the smith's back at the seventh hour; the man of thirty-one does the arithmetic on the edge of the pond and a bed at the smith's was a bed at a penny, and the porch of that meeting-house is a stone bench about six feet long, and it is the only thing in that square that is free.** The market fills between the eighth and the tenth hour with about two hundred people, and about nine men of the wall are in it with their coats over their shoulders. **A woman of about forty at the wool bench has arranged for two pieces of paper to be in the same place at the same time, and one of them is carried there in a hand and the other got itself into the air, which is the only way a thing in a chancel ever gets into a square.** A woman of about fifty says she cannot tell which of the two was written by a person, and a weaver of sixty-two says a bill of sale is between two people and a page in a book is between a person and a thing that was dead before he was born, and that a half-acre is not a man and does not have a bill of sale and is not going to argue with anybody. **A man of forty-four then says the thing he has been holding for nine years: a book with four columns and no fifth cannot be argued with, only asked, because a fifth is a thing you put things in and this book has nowhere to put your answer; and nobody in this county has ever asked it anything.** About nine people in that square hear the sentence. About two hundred hear the word column, and it goes out of the square in four directions. **A man of thirty-one tries to make the square kinder to the man of forty-four and is cut off in about four seconds by *I did not say he was*, and about four people heard the whole of it.** Closing: one pound and fourpence, a stone bench, and the word column going round a square. `512`

**513 — A room nine feet by seven, and a list of five things.** `clear` 13, a Friday, one day before. **He pays a penny for a bed at the top of a joiner's shop at the bottom of the town; one pound and fourpence less that penny is one pound and threepence, which at three pence a day is eighty-one days exactly. He slept the Thursday night on the stone bench in the porch.** Barnaby Rill says the thing that makes the Friday, in a porch on the Thursday evening to about nine people and not in a room and not in a market: two people have been talking about the same half-acre for about forty years through about nine other people and have never been in a room with each other. **The recordwright sends for both of them herself and does not tell either of them who said it, and the man of thirty-one finds that out at about half past eight.** The door of the vestry room is OPEN, and the reason is given in four seconds: nobody in that room has been asked to be in it, and about nine people standing in a doorway are nine people who can leave. The book stays in the cupboard and stays there on purpose, because a book on a table turns a room into a different room. **What comes out of the room is not an agreement and is a list: descent carries a person being the child of a person and does not carry a death, a removal, or what became of anything; holdings carry a number and not the man and not what he paid; of the fold carries acres and not beasts, not what beasts are worth, and not who let them to whom; of the ground behind the meeting-house carries a depth and can carry a name and a day, and does not carry the fact that the man is dead, which is the whole of why the name is there.** A man of forty-four does the arithmetic of the fold in four sentences and it is right. **A man of eighty-one adds a fifth thing from a chair by the fire: a page cannot be read backwards, and a person can.** Nobody thanked anybody, and the two of them were not friends afterwards. Closing: four things a book does, four it does not, a fifth from a chair, and a man of thirty-one who said it would not work and said so that he was wrong before anybody else did. `513`

**514 — THE STRUCK LEAF IS READ OUT, AND FOUR PEOPLE STRIKE THEIR OWN ANCESTORS OUT OF THE LIST.** `clear` 14, a Saturday, the fifth day of the ninety-fifth week. **It is a market on a Thursday; the square on that Saturday had about a hundred and ninety people in it and about nine of them went into the room, and about a hundred and eighty-one heard nothing at all and will be told about it by somebody.** Nobody in that square had been asked to come, and that is the whole of what the third term said, tested on a Saturday morning. The nine in the room are named. **Ilyan Vester offers to read it out and is refused in about two seconds, and the reason is the one he was given on the fifty-first day after a division two days and four hundred miles away: a thing read out in a room by a person who cannot be sent for afterwards is a thing with nobody behind it. He understands it in the room, which is the difference, and the difference did him no good whatever.** **It took about eleven minutes, which is the same eleven minutes the Wednesday leaf takes, and there are forty-three names and beside every one of them a holding and a figure in the fold and a depth in the ground. The four words are read once, at the head of the page, and there is no day on them and no name on them and no seal and no number, and there are no men in the room to read.** At the fourth name the weaver of sixty-two says *that one is mine* and is told she is going on. Then the stroke: one line, heavier than any other ink in four hundred and eleven years, and no way of telling from the ink in what order it was made. **Nobody spoke. The claim was not read out, the objections were not read out, the leaf was not struck back, and not one thing was decided. The whole of the finding is that the largest thing that has ever happened in this town is four words and no order.** Then the recordwright gives the woman of sixty-one a sheet with the forty-three names written on the back of the terms in her own hand, because she is the only person in that country who can read the hand on the front of the book, and because the only thing that can be carried out of that room to a hundred and ninety people is a piece of paper. **She reads the whole of it out loud in the square, holdings and fold figures and ground included, in about nine minutes, and then four people go up the step one at a time over about nine minutes and strike out, with their own hands and a stub of chalk out of the recordwright's own slate, the four names they are each descended from. Nobody in that square is on that list; the list is a hundred and forty-one years old and every one of those forty-three belongs to somebody dead.** A recordwright of thirty-four says out loud that a name taken out in a square is a name a person has been said to have, and then says the other half of it, which is that the striking will be the part that is left in about eleven years. Closing: a sheet of paper with forty-three names on it and four of them gone out of it by the hand of the person they belonged to, in a square, in public, with a piece of chalk. `514`

**515 — The four words go round a town, and a page in a book of orders that is blank.** `clear` 15, a Sunday, the sixth day of the ninety-fifth week. **About nine people are saying the four words in ordinary talk — at the pump, in a doorway, in Kiln Lane, and about a dead man of about eighty-four who was not struck. A man of eighty-one says four words going round a town is four words going round a town, and will not tell the two women at the pump to stop, because a man of eighty-one telling nine people not to say a thing is the same thing with his name on the end of it, and he has seen it done twice by better men than him.** Ilyan Vester says Abel Tarn's sentence in a porch, and then says where he got it and that he has heard the same sentence in another shape from the man of eighty-one in his first week, and the man of eighty-one confirms the shape in four words: *a thing that stops being his and becomes the town's*. A woman of sixty-one says she has a third of it in a bundle of thirty-two letters from a town in another county and will not say which. **Then Simon Verge comes down nine miles on a Sunday and will not stay the night and has made four of twenty-one returns, and says the order is not in the book of orders because it was never written: that page has a heading in the same hand as the returns and a blank half-sheet pasted in under the heading, and the paste is as old as the binding.** A man of thirty-one asks whether somebody has it, and is told that there is no it. **The refusal is for a new reason: a man who cannot be sent for cannot ask a question, and that has been true for a fortnight — the new half is that a thing a body did not ask for is not carried by anybody, and the woman of forty at the wool bench says in a porch that she has been in a room for two hours listening to the woman of sixty-one make that exact argument and will not be the second person in three weeks to hear it. He leaves a copy of the return on the step, made in his own office, with no name on it, because the office gives a copy to anybody who asks in writing and nobody had ever asked.** Closing: nine miles off, a book of orders with a heading and a blank half-sheet, and nobody took anything out of it because there was never anything on it. `515`

**516 — The last day the ground carries a barrow, and nine feet of gap.** `clear` 16, a Monday, the seventh and last day of the ninety-fifth week, and the morrow is the first of the ninety-sixth. **The making-up is sixteen days of work in twenty days, the twenty were counted out from the fourth day of the ninety-third week, a Friday, the crew went up before the man of thirty-one was in the country, and this is the seventeenth of the twenty: thirteen days of the work are done, three are left, three of the twenty are left, and they are the same three days. This is the last of the three on which the ground will carry a barrow.** Thirteen days of work in seventeen days of window is about one day in five lost to weather. The four days of the work go to the top of the section, because the sightline is the top and the sheep have a hedge and the sightline has got nothing at all. **The bottom of it is left with a gap about nine feet long and about nine feet of wall lying on the lane where it fell on the sixty-first day after the division, because laying it back means a footing course first and the footing course is the thing that came down.** A man of forty-four says a wall with a gap in it is a wall that somebody can come through, and that no man in that county is going to shut it before the weather turns, and that from today the gap is a document, and that there is no column in that book for a gap, so that somebody a hundred years from now will read that a section of that wall was made up in a fortnight and will not know there was nine feet of nothing at the bottom end of it. **He says he cannot put it in the book for a better reason than that he cannot be sent for: he is one of the sixty households, he has been in that book since he was seventeen, and he has never once in nine years been the only person who knew where a thing was wrong.** Ilyan Vester offers to stand in the gap until somebody comes, and about nine men tell him in about four seconds that a man standing in a gap is not a wall, and he is told he is right that it is not and wrong that it is nothing, because a man in a gap is a thing about a man and the wall will be in that book in a hundred years and he will not. Closing: nine feet of gap, and at the end of it, in the last standing course, a shelf about four feet off the ground with a boundary stone in it that has nothing cut on it at all, put there by his own hand, and he said that one was worse. `516`

**517 — The two objections are against the same three words, and a woman of sixty-one says it first.** `clear` 17, a Tuesday, the first day of the ninety-sixth week, and the day before was the last of the ninety-fifth. **There has never been two objections on anything in that book and there are two now, entered on two different days by two different hands, and neither knew the other had done it, and about four people in that town had worked out what they were both against and nobody had said so, and the recordwright had written her belief in the margin on the Tuesday with the day on it and said that if she was wrong somebody could put her right in a year. Five days went.** Ilyan Vester has had it since the Sunday of the ninety-fourth week, in a vestry room with the door shut on purpose: a person written back into a record is owed the burden of the record, and the second of those is the one nobody is designing for. He has said it to himself in a bed, on a stone bench, on a wall and at a pump, and he gets as far as the first four words of it. **Odalie Fenn, sixty-one, says the whole of it in about eleven seconds and it is not his: both are against the three words *nothing is carried*. She has had five days and a bundle of thirty-two letters and four years of nothing else to do with either, and two of the nine towns she has written to had this exact thing in it and had called it something else so that it could go on being done.** The recordwright enters it in about four minutes at the back of the book on a fresh leaf with the day on it, which is the seventeenth day of a count begun in another county. **He had about nine seconds, and used them on not saying it, and said afterwards that he had had it first, and that he had noticed the other person get to it, and that all he did with the nine seconds was not talk.** **And at the foot of the leaf, in her own hand, not read out: neither of the two people this finding is about has been told of it.** Closing: three words entered with a day on them, and a woman who said them in eleven seconds, and a man who had them for five days and did not say them, and nobody thanked for either. `517`

**518 — A meeting of nine out of sixty, and the arithmetic read out in pence, and a yes in four seconds.** `clear` 18, a Wednesday, the second day of the ninety-sixth week. **A meeting of about sixty households is a thing this county has no form for, and the recordwright calls it anyway and says four things about it before she says a word about what it is: it is not a decision, it is not a court, anybody who keeps a beast may come, and she chose nine of the sixty and has not solved that. It costs a day. About nine came.** The finding of the Monday is read out first, and there is no form that says what a finding does. **Then the whole of the arithmetic of that town, in pence, out loud, for the first time to about nine people in a room with the door open: a hundred and forty acres at four shillings the acre is five hundred and sixty shillings, which is twenty-eight pounds a year, which is six thousand seven hundred and twenty pence, which over about sixty households that keep a beast is a hundred and twelve pence, which is nine shillings and fourpence a household for the year; if the leaf goes back the fold is let a second time and fourteen pounds has to be found again, which over about sixty is four shillings and eightpence, which is half of what a household's fold is worth in a year; the wall is a hundred and forty-four days of work at eightpence, which is four pounds and sixteen shillings and no pence; the recordwright is two shillings a year from each of about sixty, which is six pounds a year and has not moved in nineteen years. A NAME IS WORTH BETWEEN FOUR POUNDS SIXTEEN SHILLINGS AND SIX POUNDS THIS YEAR.** She then asks which of the nine keep a beast, and about nine of the about sixty households in that county are the ones that would lose half of what their beasts are worth, and four of the nine say so and five do not, and about four of the five give a reason and about one does not, and nobody is asked twice. **On the way out, a man of thirty-one's own age asks him to carry the finding to the fifty-one households that were not in that room, and he says yes in about four seconds, and about nine people refuse him in about nine minutes, and the reason is new: the nine were asked and the other fifty-one were not, and no form in the county of Aldwick says a person may send a man from another county away again once he is on the step, and a thing a body did not ask for is not carried by anybody.** THE PANEL, at the end of the porch: ASKED / KNOWN / NOT KNOWN / DECISION, four bold paragraphs, one decision, and nobody told what he chose because he chose nothing. Closing: four shillings and eightpence a household said out loud to nine people out of sixty, four who said and five who did not, and a refusal on a step. `518`

**519 — The number of a gap they cannot see, and the woman who wrote four pages out from memory.** `clear` 19, a Thursday, the market, the third day of the ninety-sixth week. **The wall stopped on the Wednesday night and this is the first working day since the ground went; three days of work went into the top of a section about eleven feet high, on a barrow for the first day and by hand for the two after.** About two hundred people are in the square and a figure they cannot check gets into it at about the eleventh hour. **A man of forty-four says it badly on purpose and a man of thirty at the ninth stall says that nine feet of a wall eleven foot high is a hole a man can go through, and the recordwright says it properly in four sentences, in the second column of a book four hundred and eleven years old, and tells the square that about nine people were on that wall and nobody in that square was.** A man of fifty walks three miles to look and comes back saying the figure is still a figure. **The recordwright then sees a woman of about fifty-one in that square who is not doing what a hundred and eighty people in it are doing, and finds her at about the twelfth hour in a lane behind the bottom of the town: she was in the square in a town two days off on the Thursday morning of the thirty-ninth day after the division, when a copyist read a woman's letter out loud and a man asked a question nobody answered all the way, and she wrote four pages out from memory in about four minutes of sitting, in a round hand with forty-one mistakes and thirty-eight corrections, with a thumbprint on the third page, and the copy came home in a recordwright's bundle and was lent out about a year ago and has not come back and nobody has asked for it.** She is the only person in about two hundred who has read the page. **He wants it read out in the square and is refused in four words for a reason that is about a person and not about a page, and is told that doing nothing with it is the first useful thing anybody in that county has been told to do since the leaf was read out.** She is not asked to say anything in the square. Four things are entered on a leaf out of a book, with the day on them, and the fourth is that nobody in the county of Aldwick may be asked about the original. **In the lane he says his own sentence, a book is a door, to a woman with a smudge on her copy, and she says she has never heard it and does not know who he is, and that is true, and he has about nine seconds.** Closing: a smudge on the third page of a copy, a name on nothing, and an entry with a day on it. `519`

**520 — A boy of fourteen, a gap, and a column with nothing at the top.** `clear` 20, a Friday, the fourth day of the ninety-sixth week, and the end of the second band. **The week's sheet is written in chalk on a board on the end wall of the smithy, and the dead man of about eighty-four is in the ground column again with the day against him, and nobody looks at him.** The boy of fourteen gets to the bottom of the fourth column at about the seventh hour and stops, because there is a gap in the wall and it was read out of a stall on Thursday and he does not know what to do with it. **Nobody in that smithy had told him, and the man of thirty-one, who has been on that wall and on that step and on that porch, cannot tell him either, and finds out in about four minutes that he cannot.** Three men in that smithy offer three wrong columns and the boy refuses the fourth out loud in front of about nine people: *that is seven feet of somebody. You do not put a wall in it.* The recordwright comes in at about the eighth hour, asks him how many columns the book has and how many it had when it was made, has him write those two numbers at the head of the board, and then **rules a column on the end of it with the edge of her hand, about a hand wide and about a foot and a half long, which is the length of a gap, and does not head it.** She puts the chalk in his hand and does not take it back. One line: about nine feet of the bottom of the common wall, open since the sixty-first day after the division, which is a Saturday, and a day of a week and not a number, because a day of a week comes round. **His name is at the foot of it because he wrote it, and there is nothing at the top.** Ilyan Vester asks for a heading, is given the same answer as on the Tuesday of the ninety-fourth week, and is then given the other half of it: if it were headed she would have to say what goes in it, and there is a gap in a wall in it and there may be four by Christmas and there may be a thing a man said in a porch on a Sunday, and she does not know, and a column with a heading has to be filled according to the heading, and a promise she cannot remember making in a year is not one she is going to make now. Closing: a column about a hand wide and about a foot and a half long, with nine feet of chalk in it and a day of a week against it, a boy's name at the foot, and nothing at the top. `520`

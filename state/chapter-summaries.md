**621 — THE FIRST TIME THE TWO COPIES WERE IN A ROOM, AND A LINE WENT ON BOTH OF THEM ABOUT A MAN WHO WAS NINE FEET OFF.** `clear` 121, the seventh and last day of the hundred and tenth week, a Monday, and his sixty-seventh day in the county. The man of thirty-eight had poled nine miles up the water on the Sunday morning, read the landing copy out loud twice in a yard with about nine people standing about, written what he had heard on a slip of board, and poled back down and come in at about the sixth hour on the Monday with it under oilcloth. **And about four people in that town work out on the Monday morning that the two copies had never once been in a room, and that the rule said the two are read against each other once a week in the open, and that the only way that had ever happened was that a man of thirty-eight had stood about in two yards nine miles apart and remembered it.** The two are nailed four inches apart on the inside of the gunwale above the after thwart and read line against line, and they agree in every line, and the thirtieth line — the plank, the eleventh thing of its kind in that town — is read out loud for the first time and its author says out loud that he has not read it back in five days. Then a man of sixty-eight who is not in a chair and cannot be corrected in one says a line has to go on both of them, in about nine sentences, using a sentence the woman who keeps the chairs said out loud on the sixth day of the second week and nobody wrote down. The thirty-first line is the ground before the name: a building with a bad coffee machine in a country that is not this one, a hundred and nine miles off and across a sea, where a mark of two letters and a number is signed at the bottom of about nine hundred records; the name is one nobody in that county makes; the day is the seventh and last day of the hundred and eighth week, a Monday, when a man of thirty-eight asked a man of thirty-one where he came from and got four things back and none of them was the answer. **It is written in a yard nine miles up the water by a man of forty-three who keeps that landing nineteen years, and on a board on a barrel in Hask by a boy of eleven, on the same day, and that is why the two agree.** And the man of thirty-one is nine feet off at the head of the plank with a withy knife, about nine hundred knots in a heap at his knee, and is told at about the eleventh hour by a boy of eleven who gives him four lines out of a yard in the wrong order. He is nobody's, and he says so out loud, and the woman who keeps that book is not on that bank and has not been told.

**622 — A THIRD COPY GOES UP THE WATER ON A TUESDAY MORNING, A WOMAN ANSWERS A THIRD OF A QUESTION, A NO GOES NOWHERE, AND UNDER THE RULE A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-ONE IS NOT TRUE THIS WEEK.** `clear` 122, the first day of the hundred and eleventh week, a Tuesday, and his sixty-eighth day. The man of thirty-eight cuts a sheet off the after thwart of the Gull at about the second hour, in the open, having said out loud what he was doing before he did it, and gives the reason in nine sentences: the rule a man of sixty-eight gave on Thursday does not say that a copy may not travel, and he is not going to be a sheet, and he has a child of nine. Nobody asked him. Then the woman of forty-four who keeps the book comes up that plank for the first time in nine days, and a man of thirty-one asks her, in the open, in front of about nine people, the question a woman of thirty-four asked out loud on the Sunday: whether the saying of no has to be written down the way the saying of yes is written down, and if it is not who decided that and when and on what day. **She refuses the whole of it in four sentences, because an answer said by her in the open is not an answer, it is the position of the record, and this record has never had a position, it has had a woman in it. And she answers the third of the three questions: nobody decided it.** The book has a column for a yes because a yes has to be written down or a chair is not a chair, and there is no column for a no because a no was never needed by the book — a no is needed by the person, and the book was never for the person — and there was nobody in a room deciding it, and she was nineteen and she had a book and she did not go and ask, because you do not go and ask the person you keep a book for. Then the man of thirty-one says no, out loud, in front of about nine people, about a chair nobody has offered him, having said before he said it that there was no chair and that he knew there was no chair. **Nothing whatever happened.** The book was not written in. The count did not move. And the woman of forty-four says out loud that in nineteen years there has never once been a day in that book for a thing a person said out loud and did not take, and the reason is that every no in that county has been a person standing up out of a chair, and a person standing up out of a chair is in the book already on the day they sat down. Then the thirty-second line goes on, at about the seventh hour, being what a woman of seventy-one told a man of thirty-one to say, and a man of sixty-one reads the two sheets against each other and says that under the rule a woman of seventy-one who has kept a house that is not hers for eleven years and was never asked **is not true this week**, and that it is not that it is not true, it is that there are two copies and one of them does not have it. And the man of thirty-one says out loud that he is not going to tell her.

**623 — A WOMAN OF FORTY NAMES A WOMAN OF THIRTY, AND THE NAMING IS DONE BY AN ORDINARY PERSON AND NOT BY HIM, AND THE REASON IS A BROTHER FOUR DAYS OFF.** `clear` 123, the second day of the hundred and eleventh week, a Wednesday, and his sixty-ninth day. A man of thirty-eight comes down the water in the night with a third copy under oilcloth and does not read it out. The woman of thirty came up the plank on the Tuesday before the copy went and was in the room for every word of it, and at about the seventh hour said the one thing nobody else on that deck had said, in four sentences, about the six that have not come, and then did not say another word for the rest of the day. On the Wednesday a woman of forty stands up on the gravel and names her out loud in front of about nine people — **Perdy Lound, thirty, not in a chair, no form in that county that says she may be sent for about anything** — and says the true of why she can do it and the man of thirty-one cannot: he has spent nineteen days asking people somebody can be sent for about, and not one of the nine he asked is a person somebody cannot be sent for about, and he asked her on the sixth day of the ninth week and she told him a true thing and he counted her. He is asked why and says the true of it and it is not a good answer: he has been standing in front of people who can be sent for about a thing and asking them what they think, and about nine times in nineteen days the person who is against it has been a person nobody can be sent for about, and he did not ask her a question — she told him a thing and he took it. Then a woman of forty-one with two carts, who has not come up that plank in seven days, asks her out loud whether she is against the copies and says she is not going to hold either answer against her after Thursday. **She says she is against all eleven and gives the reason in four sentences: a thing that gets read out in rooms is a thing people go looking with; her brother is one of the six that have not come and is four days off and she has not been able to say out loud in three weeks whether he is ill; when they go looking they are not asking her, they are asking about a list; she does not want him found, she wants to be the one who tells him.** And the other half of it, which is that a man of thirty-one answered a question about where he came from in four things in front of about nine people and she was in that room and has not been able to unhear one word since.

**624 — THE SPLIT IS DESCRIBED IN NINE SENTENCES BY A WOMAN WHO IS ON NEITHER SIDE OF IT, AND BOTH HALVES ARE RIGHT, AND HE IS NINE FEET OFF.** `clear` 124, the third day of the hundred and eleventh week, a Thursday, and the town sits, and his seventieth day. The sitting is over in about forty minutes because there is nothing to sit about, and about four people go down the cart road to a front room on the low side of the landing with the door open and were neither sent nor asked. About nine people are in it. **A man of thirty-one is not one of them, and is about two hundred yards off at the head of the plank on his knees with a withy knife, because a woman of twenty-nine put the knife in his hand at about the eighth hour and said nothing about it.** A woman of seventy-one describes the split in about nine sentences: the half that wants the copies to keep going says a record nobody can be sent for about is a lid, and that is true, and she has been under one for eleven years and it weighs a house; the half that is against them says a thing which can be sent for about goes where it goes and takes the people who were never asked with it, and that is true, and she has been taken four times in eleven years without a word of it. **And the two halves are arguing about her, and she is standing in her own front room and neither has asked her which one she would rather be.** She says it is not a betrayal and it is not a misunderstanding, and that she is the instrument and the instrument is not a side, and that she has said the shape of it three times in eleven days and will not say it a fourth, and that she has not been told there is a line in a book with her eleven years on it and has not asked. Then a man of sixty-one gives the count out loud: about nine voices and about four voices, about thirteen with a view, against about eleven people who come up a plank in a working week, and the difference is that he has counted the ones who have spoken and not the ones who have not, and it is a figure of what has been said in a week and not of what anybody thinks. Then three people tell the man of thirty-one about it in about eleven minutes, each a different way, and a woman of twenty-six who has been in a chair for nine years and has not stood up tells him in four sentences that he has been nine feet off with a knife for two days out of three while about nine people described him in a room he was not in, and that saying he would be careful is not being careful, and that being careful is standing in a room and being wrong in it in front of people. And then he goes down the cart road at about the seventh hour and stands outside a gate and does not knock, and stands there about as long as it takes to cut nine feet of a hedge at about two inches at a time.

**625 — HE IS NOT ALLOWED TO PUT HIS HAND ON ANY OF THE COPIES, AND THEN A MAN OF SIXTY-EIGHT ASKS THE HARDEST QUESTION IN THE BAND AND SHE IS NOT READY.** `clear` 125, the fourth day of the hundred and eleventh week, a Friday, and his seventy-first day. A man of sixty-eight says out loud that the man of thirty-one is not to write a single line of any of the copies, and gives the reason: there is no form in that county that says a person can be sent for about a thing he has written with his own hand, and that is the whole of why a sheet of deal in Hask can be sent for about and a hundred and twenty feet of a road cannot; **and if a man nobody can be sent for about writes eleven copies of it, then the record of that town has been made by a person the record cannot reach, and in about four years the record and the man are the same thing, and a woman of forty has a mother of sixty-eight who went to a fair at a crossroads four miles off and came back on the Saturday and sat in the fourth chair and did not know she had gone into a number, and there is no column in that book for where she was.** He may read the copies out, carry them, and stand nine feet off with a knife. Then the same man asks the woman of thirty, in the open, in front of about nine people, whether a copy of that sheet read in a room four days off is going to be used to go and look for the six. **She says out loud that she is not ready, and that a person saying she is not ready is not saying no and is not saying yes, and about four of you have it as a coward and about nine have not thought about it.** The reason: the copies are made on the Sunday in one room, she is the fastest hand in that county, if she is in that room they are made and if she is not they are made slowly and about four of them are made badly and one of them is made by a child, and she cannot tell you whether she will hold them until Saturday because she does not know whether she will be standing in that room on the Sunday, and she cannot know that until she knows the answer to the question he has just asked, and she cannot get the answer until Saturday. Then a man of forty-one from a town two days off in the county of Ashenfield, who put his own name against a chair there and has not taken it off, says out loud that he is going to take a copy two days down the water in his own cart on Monday, and that nobody asked him, and that if about four of them want to say no they have until Saturday and he would rather that than a yes on Monday, and that he is not doing it for the man of thirty-one. Then the man of thirty-one writes one, because he was asked to, in a hand nobody in that town can do, and it is the best one, and it is not the one that goes, and a woman of thirty says she is going to write nine more on Sunday and it will be a worse one on purpose, because a record a man of thirty-one made is a thing he can be asked about and this is not that.

**626 — BOTH HALVES INTO ONE ROOM WITHOUT RESOLVING ANYTHING, AND ABOUT NINE PEOPLE IN THAT ROOM ARE THE PERSON ON THE OTHER SIDE.** `clear` 126, the fifth day of the hundred and eleventh week, a Saturday, and his seventy-second day. About nine people walk down the cart road into a front room with the door open, neither sent nor asked, and the woman of seventy-one who keeps the house did not ask any of them why they had come and said so out loud first. A woman of forty, who wants a day written against every name, says the true thing about the half that is against the copies: every person in that half who is under sixty-five has been in that book nineteen years without being asked, and so has she, and so has the woman of forty-four who keeps it, and so has the woman of twenty-nine who cut withies off the land of the ninth and tenth lines, and so has a woman of seventy-one in that room, and her mother is the fourth chair, and **about nine people in that room are the thing that half is protecting, and they have all come down here and said they are not it.** A woman of thirty says the true thing back: about four of you want a day against a name so that the man in a chair can be asked what he said, and about four of you are the man in a chair, and one of you keeps the book for thirteen shillings and fourpence a year and has never been asked what it is for, and **about nine people in this room are the thing your half is afraid of, and a thing you are frightened of is a thing you are in.** Then a woman of thirty-four who came nine miles down the water nine days ago and has been in a chair since she was nineteen says one sentence, and it is the only thing said in that room in a fortnight that is about her. Then the man of thirty-one says the true thing about one half of the split and not one word about the other, in about nine sentences, and a woman of thirty-four catches it in about the fourth and tells him it is the fifth time in nineteen days, and that a man of thirty-one who has been standing in a room for forty minutes saying one true thing about one half of it is doing the thing he came to this town not to do, and that about four of the room are glad of it and about nine cannot tell. And a woman of seventy-one says nothing in this room has been resolved, that she has been asked twice since Thursday to say which half is right, and that the next person who says the shape of this thing is going to have to be one of them and she is going to sit on the settle and listen to it.

**627 — ELEVEN SHEETS OF DEAL IN ABOUT FOUR HOURS IN A FRONT ROOM, AND ALL ELEVEN ARE COPIES OF A THING THAT UNDER ITS OWN RULE IS NOT TRUE THIS WEEK.** `clear` 127, the sixth day of the hundred and eleventh week, a Sunday, and his seventy-third day. A sheet of that thing is about four feet by a foot and four inches, and the working is given out loud: thirty-two lines at about two inches a line is sixty-four inches, and the ground goes across four feet because a hand is about nine inches. The deal came from four off two cart beds belonging to a woman of forty-one who has not been up that plank in eight days and brought the lot herself and said she was not going to be thanked for it and would rather it was counted; two off a thwart of the Gull, given on the Saturday and not mentioned until the Monday; one off a hurdle at the head of the quarter mile, which the man of fifty-four let him take and said nothing about; three off a barn door on the low side of the landing that a woman of seventy-one is having taken off its hinges anyway, because she would rather the boards went on a thing that is being checked twice than on a fire; and one off the lid of the press the woman of forty-four keeps the book in. **Four and two and one and three and one is eleven.** A man of sixty-one says what the figure is a figure of: it is a figure of how many pieces of wood there are and not a figure of how many people have read one of them, and there is no form in that county that would let him find the second number. Then the copying, in about four hours of daylight, by about nine people who can write and a boy of eleven who had never copied a whole sheet in his life and a man of sixty-one who spent about four hours reading out loud instead of writing a line and said out loud that he chose it: **about nine people over about four hours at about four hours a sheet is about nine sheets, and it is eleven, and the difference is two, and one of them is the boy and the other is the man of sixty-one.** Then a woman of thirty says, with the working, that the copy kept at that landing has thirty-one lines on it and this one has had thirty-two since the Tuesday, so the two do not agree, and under the rule the thing is not true, and she has worked it out twice this week to be sure and it is the same both times, and every one of these eleven is a copy of a thing that is not true this week, and that is not anybody's fault. Then the arrangement for the eleven is made out loud and covers ten of them, and **the eleventh has no arrangement, and it is the best-looking sheet of deal in four counties, and it is the one a man of thirty-one wrote on a Friday evening in a hand nobody in that county can do, and he is not allowed to carry it and nobody else in that room can be sent for about it, and about four of them have worked out that a woman of thirty is going four days up the water on Monday morning.**

**628 — THE MIDPOINT. THE PUBLIC RELEASE OF HIS EARTH CONNECTION, OVER A PERSON WHO HAS BEEN A WITNESS TO HIM, AND SHE IS RIGHT AND SHE LEAVES.** `clear` 128, the seventh and last day of the hundred and eleventh week, a Monday, and his seventy-fourth day. The count is given out loud at about the second hour: about nine for and about four against, and about nine who have not said, and a man of sixty-one says he is counting the ones who were in the room when it was said. Then the woman of thirty says out loud that she is against all eleven, and that she said the reason on the Wednesday in four sentences and is not going to say it again. Then a man of sixty-eight says that the copies go over her, and why, in about nine sentences, and it is the sixth day of a rule he gave himself: **if nine people can stop a copy, then a person can be sent for about having stopped it, and the reason there is a sheet of deal in that county at all is that there is no column in it with a person's name at the top of it and no form that renews anybody and no rate on anybody, and he is not going to take that back on a Monday because a woman of thirty has turned out to be right about a brother in a county four days off.** He then says out loud, to about nine people, that about four of the nine people for are the only people anybody could be sent for about a chair, and that he is not going to pretend he has not thought about the four. **Then she goes, at about the third hour, up the cart road, with a place in a cart arranged on the Saturday of which she told about four people and not the other five, and nobody sent for her and nobody asked her for a reason and nobody said one word to her that could have moved her, and a man of thirty-one says out loud that he is not going after her and why, and that he has now done the same thing twice in six days about two different people and both times he was right and both times he said it out loud in advance, and that about nine people in a yard nine miles up that water are going to be two halves about it by the seventh hour and that is a thing he built and he is not going to say it was the weather.** Nine miles up that water, in a yard at about the second hour, a man of forty-three writes the thirty-second line on the landing original and reads the whole sheet out loud from the first line to the last, and does not skip a line and does not hurry the one that mattered, and about nine people there hear a line about a man they have never seen. A man of about fifty-two says the true of it in four sentences: that is the man who was in the weigh-house six weeks ago with the four of us. A man of thirty-eight reads the last line again, which is Wren Sayer, his brother, gone up the water about eleven years, and says he does not know whether he is saying it for him. **Then he says the four words, flat, at about the fourth hour of a Monday afternoon, in a yard where about nine people are standing, and he does not raise his voice and he says nothing else afterwards.** Then a man of forty-three who keeps that landing asks, out loud, in the open, in that yard, whether it is in that room, having said before he asked that he is the only person anybody in that yard could be sent for about, and about four seconds go past and nobody fills them and nothing says, and he says that there is no form in that county that will let anybody come back on the Tuesday and ask him what he meant by it. Then the man of thirty-eight asks for a hearing: every record there is, read in one room, the ones that disagree with each other especially, because about nine people in that yard are two halves about a sheet of deal and every one of both halves is right, and about four of the people in that yard think those four sheets should never have been read out in it and about nine think they should, and he read them himself. **Nobody in that yard was asked whether they wanted it, and it is not that man's decision and it is not his fault, and the man of thirty-one was nine miles down the water on a bank.**

**629 — THE MORNING AFTER. NOBODY WAS RELIEVED AND NOBODY WAS FORGIVEN AND NOBODY WAS THANKED, AND THE COUNT WENT TO NINE.** `clear` 129, the first day of the hundred and twelfth week, a Tuesday, and his seventy-fifth day. A man of thirty-eight comes down the water in the night and says four things out loud on that deck in about nine people's hearing, in the order they happened: the two originals agree; about nine people in a yard at a landing read four of those sheets out loud and about nine of them had never seen the thirty-first line; he said the four words at about the fourth hour and about four of the yard had heard them before and about nine had not; and he asked for a hearing about every record there is, and nobody asked him whether he would rather not and nobody voted on it. **Nobody in Hask was relieved and nobody in Hask was forgiven and nobody in Hask was thanked, and not one person in that town said a word to a man of thirty-eight about any of the four.** Then at about the fourth hour a lighter comes down the water and a man of forty-three gets out of it, and a woman of forty says out loud that nobody in that town sent for him and that she does not know whether he is allowed to be in that town and there is no form anywhere that says. He says he is not going to be thanked and not going to be forgiven and would like about four to notice that neither is anything he has come for, and he says he asked whether it was in that room and nobody answered and he got a worse answer than the one he was hoping for and is still glad he asked. **Nobody on that bank said a word to him that could have moved him.** And the count moved at about the second hour, before any of that, in the open, on a bank, and it moved down for the fifth time in about thirty days: a woman of twenty-nine sat down in the reed in front of about nine people and said out loud why. **Her reason is that four sheets went up the water and five went two days down the road, and about nine people in four counties are going to read those copies and about four of them are going to ask somebody what the reed is; and that she stood up out of this chair on the Wednesday of the tenth week and has not been able to stop thinking about that for six days; and that a no in the open is the only door out of a chair anybody in that county has, and there is no column for the no; and that she has not got a reason that will be believed in about four years, and there is not one, and a woman of twenty-six who said so last week is the reason she knows it and is still in the ash.** Then a man of thirty-one starts to say that it is not anybody's fault, gets about four words out of it, and stops, and a man of sixty-one says the same nine words and does the sum, and says that phrase has gone out of use in that town in a fortnight and that he does not know what they are going to use instead and it is not going to be his book of heads and carts. Then a question asked out loud on a Sunday is asked for the third time in a kitchen, by a woman of thirty-four, and is not answered, and she says she is going to keep asking it until somebody answers it or a woman of seventy-one says out loud that she is not going to answer it either.

**630 — A CASE STILL OPEN, A PERSON STILL DOING A THING, A FIGURE OF FOURTEEN, AND A BOY OF THIRTEEN ASKING WHO SAYS HOW MANY PEOPLE A THING HAS BEEN READ TO.** `clear` 130, the second day of the hundred and twelfth week, a Wednesday, and his seventy-sixth day. The case is open and is not a case anybody can be sent for about, and a man of thirty-eight asked for a hearing about every record there is in a yard nine miles up that water on the Monday and nobody granted it and nobody refused it and nobody has written it down. A man of sixty-one gives the number out loud with the working: eleven on the Sunday; one at the landing on the Monday afternoon, written by a man of about fifty-two who read one in that yard and asked for one of his own and was given it, and who cannot see well, and whose fifth line is his and not the man's; one in Hask on the Tuesday morning, written by a boy of thirteen, the first sheet of deal a person under fourteen in that county has written out in the whole of nineteen years, and his mother told him to say it and he is saying it because she told him to; and one at the landing on the Tuesday morning out of a spare thwart a man of forty-three has had on that wall for nineteen years for a thing that has not happened. **Eleven and one and one and one is fourteen, and it is a figure of the wood and not a figure of the people, and about nine people on that bank have read one and about four have read two and about nine in that town have read none, and there is no form in this county that would let him find the second number and he has stopped looking and the looking is the thing he would like somebody else to take up.** Then a boy of thirteen is named out loud in his mother's mouth — Wat Marshe, not in a chair, no column for him, and not a head in the book of heads and carts because the penny a head is on everybody over sixteen who comes through that landing, and there is no form in that county that says the man who takes it may. Then he asks, out loud, in front of about nine people, **who says how many people a thing has been read to.** He has read two of them out loud to about nine people each and there is one of the fourteen that nobody is going to read out loud to anybody because there is nobody in that county that is going to be in the room, and if the answer is that nobody ever says then somebody has to write it down somewhere and he does not know where, and he has been told there is a sheet of deal and he has been told there is a rule and he does not know of a third thing. A man of thirty-one opens his mouth and **a woman of twenty-six who has been in a chair for nine years and has not stood up says no in front of about nine people and tells him he may not answer it, because he is the man that question is about in the only way it is about anybody, and she says hold it, and he held it, and it was the first time in nineteen days that he had been told no by a person in that town and said nothing anyway.** Then he says the smallest thing he has said in thirty days, which is that he is going to be nine feet off at the head of that plank at about the fourth hour with a withy knife, and nobody thanked anybody for anything at all.

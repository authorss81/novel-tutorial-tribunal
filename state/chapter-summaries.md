## VOLUME 11, CHAPTERS 501 TO 510 — *the page that was struck, and a town that counts from it*

**501 — A line that cannot be entered, and a thank-you that is stopped before the second word.** `clear` 1, a Sunday, the fifty-fifth day after the division, bar twenty-five days, wheel twenty-four slow. **A man of thirty-one asks a copyist for a line in anything saying he was in this town for forty-eight days, and is told there is no form in the county of Ostrey that says a man may be entered as having been in a place, and that she has been looking since the fifteenth day after the division.** He begins *thank you* and is stopped, and there is no form that says a man may be thanked either, and the only record of him in that town is a sheet with a line through the middle of his own sentence and his name at the foot of it. **A recordwright of thirty-four from Bramblefold, two days off, says out loud in a square that she has read of a town where a book is a door and she has a book, and he walks out with her because the sentence was said and not because anybody asked him.** She has a copy of four pages in a round hand, unsigned, with a thumbprint on the third page, made by a woman in that square on the thirty-ninth day out of memory that night. Closing: no form in the county of Ostrey that says a man may be thanked. `501`

**502 — The second day of a two-day road, and four gates and one name.** `clear` 2, a Monday, the last day of the ninety-third week. **A lime cart goes twenty-one miles from Coldcombe and stops at the Four Gates, four miles short, and the last four miles are on foot. A boy of about nine in a shirt and no shoes says the man of thirty-one is the second stranger of that year, and that there were four gates and a penny at each until ninety years back, and that the four names went into the charter in a chest in the meeting-house, and that four generations of his house have not known which four and he has not been asked.** The chalk downs, planted hedges about a hundred and forty years old, a double row of elms with about half of them dead standing. Arrival at dusk at a town of about two thousand four hundred people with a lime kiln burning its ninth day out of ten. **He has one pound and fourpence, which is eighty-one days of a bed at a penny and bread at a penny with a penny over.** A woman of sixty-one who has been in the town eleven days tells him that questions in this town are asked in a room and not at a gate, and says what the town thinks, and says she came because of a lane. Closing: four gates and one name. `502`

**503 — A porch, and an entry at the back of a book on an unruled leaf.** `clear` 3, a Tuesday, the first day of the ninety-fourth week, and the market is not on. **A room where a book is read is a room where people say things they are going to be held to, and it is worth a holding, an orchard, a yard and about seven feet of ground, and a person not of this town is not in it, and the reason is not distrust but that there is no form in that county that will make a man a neighbour.** He is put in the porch. **He is entered on a separate leaf at the back of the book, in a hand that is not the book's hand: his name, the day, and the words *whose record is not findable*, and there is no column on that leaf for anything else, and the leaf goes in third from the back and not the last.** It is the fifth of nine such entries in four hundred years and the only one that says a man is somebody and cannot say what he is. **He then produces a page out of his coat to prove he is a person who can be asked about, and it is the page that came back four hundred miles with two names in a witness line, and she does not take it and does not refuse it and says to pull it back in.** Closing: a folded page he was told to pull back in, and the case that is different is a man with a case. `503`

**504 — THE BOOK IS OPEN AND IT HAS NO COLUMN FOR DEATH.** `clear` 4, a Wednesday. **A man of about seventy-two comes two miles in a wet coat in a dry week for the fourth time in about nine years to hear one line read about four acres at the bottom of the lane, and the reading takes eleven minutes.** Then the book: bound in calf, four hundred and eleven years old, four hands, rebound ninety years back by W. Hask of this parish at a charge of four pounds. **Four columns and no fifth — descent, holdings, of the fold, of the ground behind the meeting-house — and no place whatever to put a person who has died, so the book carries about four hundred men a year and every one of them is alive in it.** **A man of thirty-one says out loud in the porch, to about nine people, that a book that cannot record a death is a book that has never lost anybody; about four understand and about five do not; and a man of eighty-one from a chair by the fire says the other half: it has lost one leaf, the leaf took forty-three names and about four hundred years of what they did, and nobody has been able to say a day, and a Tuesday is a day that does not carry a number.** The leaf itself: forty-three names, a single stroke from an inch below the top rule to an inch above the bottom one in a heavier ink, and four words on the inner margin, **struck by order**, and nothing else. Closing: a bench in a porch with a mark worn into the stone. `504`

**505 — A market of about two hundred, and a weaver who says a name is not a word.** `clear` 5, a Thursday, and the market. **A Keeper of returned names, sixty-one, eleven days in the town, four years of writing to nine towns, says in the square that a page is behind her with forty-three names on it and a pen through the lot of them and no order, and that a name is not put back by nine people.** A weaver of sixty-two, Abel Tarn, stops in the market about forty feet away and says it is about Kiln Row because his mother is on it; about two hundred people turn round at once. **He asks, in a market, in front of two hundred people, whether a half-acre at the bottom of Kiln Lane is his, and says that the page has a holding in it and a figure in the fold and seven feet in the ground behind the meeting-house, and that a name is not a word.** A man of forty-four with a bill of sale in a coat says he held the next one along for twenty years and did not take it off anybody, and neither of them says the other half. **The four lines of a return from Aldwick, nine miles off, a hundred and forty-one years back: that the household of Kiln Row, being asked in respect of the matter of the common, gave answer that the common was not used; that the answer was made use of; that the line is struck; and that this office has no further particular.** A man of fifty-four of the county says the return does not say what the question was and the order is in neither of that office's two books and never was. Closing: a man holding a bill of sale in a coat who had not touched it. `505`

**506 — The whole working, in pence, on the back of a board.** `clear` 6, a Friday. **A boy of fourteen writes the week in chalk and has got a dead man of about eighty-four into a column of holdings, because there is nowhere else to write him, and the whole of the trouble in the band is in that one line.** Then the working, printed in the chapter: a hundred and forty acres at four shillings the acre is five hundred and sixty shillings, which is twenty-eight pounds a year, which is six thousand seven hundred and twenty pence, which over about sixty households is a hundred and twelve pence, which is nine shillings and fourpence a household; if the leaf is put back the fold is let a second time and fourteen pounds has to be found again, which over about sixty is four shillings and eightpence, which is half of what a household's fold is worth in a year; the wall is sixteen days of work by nine men, which is a hundred and forty-four days of work at eightpence, which is one thousand one hundred and fifty-two pence, which is four pounds and sixteen shillings and no pence; the recordwright is paid two shillings by each of about sixty households, which is six pounds a year, unmoved in nineteen years. **A NAME IS THEREFORE WORTH BETWEEN FOUR POUNDS SIXTEEN SHILLINGS AND SIX POUNDS THIS YEAR, and that is said out loud and it is the reason nobody in that room will agree about anything.** **The orchard is in none of the four columns, and nine hundred trees and eleven families have no column at all.** Ilyan says the figure is a good one and that nobody can be ruined by four pounds sixteen shillings, and is told that nobody in the room has been asked whether the forty households that lose half a fold are the same forty that gain a wall, and that he had. Closing: four pounds sixteen shillings and no pence on one side and six pounds a year on the other. `506`

**507 — Thirty people on a chalk bank, and the week is not read out.** `clear` 7, a Saturday. **Nine men and a woman carry at the bottom end, and the woman is the recordwright and nobody asked her to, and she is the only person in Bramblefold who has done a day's work on that wall inside nineteen years.** A man of fifty-four called Ordwin, no relation to anybody in another county, sets the top and will not let a man put chalk in the barrow. **The man of thirty-one carries for an hour and a half, is moved to the barrow, gets forty yards with it and is taken off it by two people, and then holds the string for two hours, and about nine men on a bank do not stop for a man who cannot and do not start for him and do not ask him one question.** A course and a half goes up and comes down again at a cold joint, and a man of forty-four says a pointing-out is not a man on a wall, and that the man who could put it in is the man whose grandfather bought the holding off the family that was leaving, and that a return takes about a year. **At the fourth hour a man of forty-four and a weaver of sixty-two are on the same side of the orchard, about sixty feet apart inside eleven rows, and neither says anything.** **And the week of Bramblefold is not read out in the building it has been read out in for four hundred years, for the first time in nineteen years, because the only person who reads it is up a bank from the seventh hour, and nobody had said that the reading might not happen and nobody had to.** Closing: the day a man of thirty-four picked up a shovel. `507`

**508 — A room shut on purpose, a widow who says no, and the band's one panel.** `clear` 8, a Sunday, and the door of that building has not been shut on purpose in the working life of the woman who shuts it. **She takes a sheet of headings out of his hand at the door and puts it on the sill outside and does not explain, and shuts the door, and names the three things a shut door says and says the third is the only one anybody in that town has ever been short of.** **Edith Crow, sixty-eight, four rooms and a yard on the upper row paid for twenty-two years out of a mangle and a drying ground and eleven weeks a year teaching children to read, is asked nothing and says so, and says no in about four sentences before anybody argues with her, and says a book is not a street, and asks that it be written down that she said it before and not after.** THE PANEL, at the level of the window sill, CLAIM / EVIDENCE in two paragraphs / BURDEN / CHOICE, five bold paragraphs and ten `**`: a person written back into a record is owed the thing the record carries **and** is owed the burden of it, and the second is the one nobody is designing for. **He chooses the claim and not the descent, out loud, in about nine seconds, and nobody is told and nobody is thanked.** Then he offers to be the instrument and is refused in four sentences by a person who has said the same refusal about two hundred miles off and does not enjoy saying it twice, and he says out loud that he understands it and that it does him no good whatever, and asks that it be noticed that he has noticed. Closing: a square sheet of paper on a sill outside a shut door, and nobody was thanked. `508`

**509 — A frost, a wall in the night, and a stone with TUESDAY cut into it.** `clear` 9, a Monday, the seventh and last day of the ninety-fourth week, and the morrow is the first of the ninety-fifth. **The first hard frost takes about four weeks of fruit off about ninety half-size trees in the lower corner of the orchard, which is the pocket a man of thirty-four works for nothing.** At about the second hour a garden wall at the bottom of Kiln Lane, four feet high, built out of the kiln's own white by a man dead about forty years, comes down, and a stone about nine inches by five comes up out of the footing with **TUESDAY** cut into the top of it in letters an inch and a half deep and, under that, in a smaller cut and a later hand, **four letters and a stroke.** **A man of thirty-four says that is a boundary stone and that a man in a hurry cuts the day of the week on a thing instead of the date, and that he has seen those four letters on about nine stones on that wall, and that the wall is the half-acre's and he did not build it and did not pay for it.** Ilyan asks nine men where they were between the second hour and the sixth and is told that there is no asking, that a man is found in this town by somebody saying so in about nine minutes in a room, and that a page with nine names and the second hour on it would be a worse record than no page. **A man of eighty-one comes down, looks at the word, and does not go down into the lane, and says that a day of a week is the only one of the five records in that town anybody can be checked against, and that he has known since he was about thirty that he cannot tell whose the four letters are and that the next four people who can read the book are younger than him and one of them will not have the trick.** The frost pocket is off and the week's reading did not happen on the Saturday. Closing: a stone in a shelf inside a wall with TUESDAY cut into the top of it. `509`

**510 — A claim, two objections, and a column with no heading on it.** `clear` 10, a Tuesday, the first day of the ninety-fifth, and the day before was the last of the ninety-fourth. **Nothing was decided, and about four people in that town had spent a week waiting for a Tuesday to be a day something settled on.** At the ninth hour, in the chancel, with the door open, a claim is entered at the back of the book: the holding numbered six, a half-acre at the bottom of Kiln Lane, the fold, the ground behind the meeting-house, by Abel Tarn, weaver, who is of the descent of a name upon a struck leaf of which the only account is four words in a margin. **The leaf is not struck back. The claim is entered. Nothing is carried.** It is read out in the porch first, and the three load-bearing words are said back by a man in the second row. **The objection is entered at the foot with a name against it, in four sentences, by the man of forty-four who holds the next one along, who says he does not want to be told to take it back and is not a man anybody is going to be able to find; and Edith Crow's is already on the leaf from Saturday, and that is two objections on one entry and there has never been two on anything in that book.** Then she rules a space with a line down each side of it on a fresh leaf, with no heading at the top of it, writes one name in it, and tells a man of thirty-one that a heading is a promise about what goes in it and that there is no agreement in that room. **Four sentences in a different ink at the foot of the leaf say that the name was entered at the asking of a person not of this town whose record is not findable, and that he came in on a cart road that ends at the Four Gates, and that no body in that county can be asked anything about him, and that there is no form that says he may be asked and none that says he may not. It is the fifth of the ten entries at the back of that book, and the copyist expects it to be the one that is read out in a hundred years.** A boy of nine from the Four Gates asks whether the gates are going to be written down as well and nobody answers him. Closing: a ruled column, no heading, one name, two objections, a date on the leaf. `510`

Closing: the frame standing outside a minute, on a list nine years old. `492`

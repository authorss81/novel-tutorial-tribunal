**681 — A MAN OF FORTY-THREE STOPS GIVING THE PAIR OF FIGURES OUT LOUD AND THE NINTH LINE IN A STONE IS COUNTED BACK TO A THURSDAY, AND A MAN OF FORTY-ONE SAYS HE IS STARTING IN THE MORNING.** The fourth day of the hundred and nineteenth week, a Friday, his four hundred and thirty-first morning, his one hundred and twenty-seventh day. **A man of forty-three gives the reading — about eight inches over the top step at the sixth hour, six days since it came off, counted from one morning to the next and not from a morning to itself — and gives it without the other half, and says why before subtractions, and says he is not going to add the two together and has not added them in nine days. **A man of thirty-one gives his own at one hundred and twenty-seven and says out loud that he is not going to say a second number about any other place in this county, and that there is a man of sixty-one on that bank who knows exactly what he is not saying and why, and that he said it out loud on and not of a cut in a stone — and then says that there is no form in this county that says a person may be sent for about a Thursday, and asks about four people to know that he looked that morning and not this week. **He gives the other figure with its predicate attached: eleven years of a book, about nine hundred heads in it and carts against about as many, and not one name in it of anybody who did anything in those eight doors, and that is eight doors and no names and it is a figure of a book and not of the doors.** Then a man of forty-one says he is going to ask about eight doors and not behind him unless a person wants to. He says he is not going down today because there is nothing to go down for. **A man of fifty-four who cannot read says the count of him stopping a man of thirty-one is five in five days and he is not going to do it a sixth, and gives the reason: a man who says five names in five days and then says none of them for a week is a man who has learned one thing, and he is not going to be the man who teaches him the other one.** And a woman of twenty-eight checks the lamp-and-the-record thing a third time and says she is still not going to be the one who says it and that it is still not because it is not true.

**682 — THE WATER COMES OFF THE TOP STEP IN A NIGHT AND A MAN WHO CANNOT READ FINDS A SPACE CUT SQUARE BETWEEN TWO HANDS WITH NOTHING IN IT, AND A TERM IS DEFINED OUT LOUD.** The fifth day of the hundred and nineteenth week, a Saturday, his four hundred and thirty-second morning, his one hundred and twenty-eighth day. **A man of forty-three gives the reading, says it came off the top step in the night and that he was on that landing at the second hour because there is nothing else to do at the second hour, refuses to say how many days that is, and then says the thing nobody expected him to say: the pair of figures and what that water does have come apart, and he is the man who gives both of them and he has stopped giving one of them, and he would like about four people to decide between them themselves.** A woman of forty-nine gives the days in the county at one hundred and twenty-eight with the subtraction and says she counts days because she counts loaves. **A man of forty-one then goes up the cart road at about the eighth hour, asks a woman of forty-four one question, and comes back down at about the eleventh hour; and she says out loud that she is not going to say she is glad and not going to say she is not glad and will not know which until about the sixth hour tomorrow, and that the half she is going to say now is that nobody in this county has the second thing and that the second thing is being asked, and that he has now used the only person in four counties who wants to be asked as a person to ask.** They go down at about the first hour of the afternoon. **And in the ninth room a man of fifty-four who cannot read stops, and says in nine sentences the thing nobody had asked him for: he has been looking at that mark since the Wednesday of the hundred and eighteenth week and has never once looked at the middle of it, because he cannot read and there has never been anything in the middle of it for him; and there is something in the middle of it — two hands cut side by side and a space between them about as wide as his thumb, cut square, cut to the same depth as the hands, and not a mark anybody makes; and it is a space somebody left for a thing that was not a hand, and he cannot tell anybody what is in it and cannot tell anybody whether anything was ever in it, and he is not going to start by looking at a space in a wall.** **And a man of forty-one says the whole of what it is in nine sentences, and the first one is the word nobody in that passage had used: a term is a job with a name on it, and he has got no better one and is not going to look for a better one.** He has done the ninth of those jobs for nineteen years and there is no name on it or on any of the other eight. He says the name is not missing off the stone, that the space is cut and cut square and cut old, and that those are two different things and he has got a space and not a name, and that he is the ninth of nine and nobody ever told him there was a place for his name. **And that is the first of the nine, and it was found as an absence and not as a document, and he is not going to say he has got it.**

**683 — NOBODY GOES DOWN AND THE COST OF THE FIRST ASKING ARRIVES ON THE ORDINARY WEEK OF A WOMAN WHO KEEPS A BAKEHOUSE, AND SHE SAYS OUT LOUD THAT SHE HAS BEEN TOLD TWICE AND ASKED ONCE.** The sixth day of the hundred and nineteenth week, a Sunday, his four hundred and thirty-third morning, his one hundred and twenty-ninth day. A boy of thirteen gives thirty days since he went down with the subtraction, and then gives a count of times beside it and says which is which: he has said a figure out loud on about nine mornings since the Tuesday of the hundred and eighteenth week, and that is a count of times and not a clock, and he says both of them because a man of thirty-one has been telling everybody for about a fortnight that a count has to say what it is a count of. **A woman of forty-four comes down that bank with nothing on her cart and gives the working: he asked her at about the eighth hour on the Saturday and it was about a quarter of an hour, her cart was standing at the head of that track with a load in it, his two carts were on the road in front of it, and a cart cannot pass a cart on that road at the eighth hour because there is a wall on the north side of it for about forty yards; so the forty loaves went up at about the tenth hour instead of about the seventh, and an hour and a half up and an hour and a quarter down is two hours and three quarters of a cart for one morning of bread, and it is the same figure it has been since the Friday of the hundred and eighteenth week and she has not improved it.** About nine houses got it a day late, about four of the nine cannot do a day without it, and the man of eighty-one has a Thursday in four days and is one of the four. **And she says the cost she is going to talk about is not the bread: being told, twice in nine days; being asked, once, and it is a question about a door nine miles under a river and not about her; and about nineteen years of neither. She says she is not going to tell anybody which of those three is the one she would have given back.** **A man of forty-one refuses to be sorry about it on a bank, and says the day is a Sunday and that is not the reason, and then says the other half: he is not going to stand there and say he did not know, because he did know, he has known since the Friday of the hundred and eighteenth week that she is the one person in four counties who wants to be asked, and he went up that road in the morning and asked her about a door because a door is what he was going — and that there is no form that says he may and no form that says he may not, and he would like both sentences left where they are.** Then a man of about fifty-two who cannot see well says the whole of what he thinks about it from about nine feet off, and gives the reason he cannot be the one who goes to her door, and says there is nobody else on that bank who can tell what being sent for about costs and he is not going to be asked.

**684 — ABOUT NINE PEOPLE STAND ON A BANK IN ABOUT EIGHT INCHES OF WATER AND DO NOTHING ON PURPOSE, AND A MAN OF SIXTY-ONE IS ASKED TO PUT A THING IN A BOOK AND GIVES THREE REASONS OUT LOUD FOR NOT.** The seventh and last day of the hundred and nineteenth week, a Monday, his four hundred and thirty-fourth morning, his one hundred and thirtieth day. **A man of forty-one says the difference between this Monday and the one at the end of the hundred and eighteenth week: last time about four of them did not know that what they were doing was doing nothing, and this time about four of them know and the rest have decided to do it anyway, and that is a different thing and he would like about four people to be able to tell the two apart. And he says he has got no idea what a bank is for on a Monday and is not going to find out today.** Then six of the about nine people on that bank say what their Monday is, one at a time, and nobody had asked them: two carts and about ten hours in ten days of a cart; sixty loaves and about forty of them up a road four miles off; about nine hours of a mill and about eleven people in four houses and one of the eleven is her mother; a reading and a stoppage; about nine hundred heads and carts against about as many and I did not put a line in it this morning, and he says beforehand that he is going to tell the bank why in about ten sentences; a drain and about nine feet of gravel that he has held for nine years. **And the six of them are not the same size as each other and were never going to be, and about four of them had not thought about their Monday until somebody asked them for it.** A man of thirty-one asks a man of sixty-one to put the nine marks and the nine spaces in the book, and says he is asking and not telling. **And a man of sixty-one gives three reasons out loud, in ten sentences measured with the instrument's own splitter, and asks about four people to hold on to the third one: he has not been down that flight in eleven years and will not be sent for about a wall; there is no name in it and a line with no name against it is not a thing his book does, and his book has about nine hundred heads in it and every one of them has a head against it; and the third one, which he asks about four people to hold on to — the day he starts writing down things he has been told about in a place he has not been, his book stops being a book of what happened and starts being a book of what somebody said happened, and he has not got eleven years left to find out which of those two he has been keeping. And the reading itself is about eight inches over the top step at the seventh hour, two days since it came off, and he says out loud that it was about four inches at this hour yesterday and that that is the working, and that the pair of figures is not his to give out loud any more.** **Then a man of fifty-four who cannot read says the longest thing he has said since the rack of boards: every one of you can be shown what was written and can go and look at the wall, and he cannot be shown anything, and if the book is written this afternoon he is the only person on that bank anybody can be sent for about, and he will not know what it says and neither will anybody who comes after him.**

**685 — THE WATER COMES OFF THE TOP STEP FOR THE SECOND TIME IN FOUR DAYS, A MAN OF SIXTY-SIX SAYS WHAT A FILLED HOLE IS, AND A MAN OF THIRTY-ONE PUTS HIS HAND ON A GOING WHERE NO GOING HAS GONE AND COMES OUT ON THE OTHER SIDE OF IT WITH A SCAR.** The first day of the hundred and twentieth week, a Tuesday, his four hundred and thirty-fifth morning, his one hundred and thirty-first day. **A man of forty-three gives the reading and says he watched it come off for the second time in four days and that he has said twice in about a week that he has nothing to say about a night, and what he is going to do is give the reading every morning and stop, and the block of limestone is still a different thing nine miles up that water with still no figure for it.** A man of thirty-eight says his shoulder is no good on a Thursday and this is not a Thursday. **And in the ninth room a man of sixty-six puts his hand flat on the wall behind the shelf, takes it off, and says the thing twice because it is the first thing he has not recognised in forty-one years: that is not a wall, that is a hole that has been filled and a sinker knows the difference in the dark; it is a course of stone put into a gap and then faced, the colour of it is wrong and the joint is wrong, the facing is about the thickness of his two fingers, and the stone behind the facing is not the stone of that wall and he has never in forty-one years seen the two stones together anywhere. And he refuses the fourth thing, which nobody asked him: when it was filled and by whom.** **Then a woman of forty-nine says the name for it and gives where she had it for nineteen years and that it was never once about a road: in a bakehouse a rack is six boards and four shelves, and about half the time one of the shelves has nothing on it at all, and we call that one the blank.** **And a man of forty-one gives it back to her in front of about nine people and then says the mechanic in eight sentences: a groove is a route, a route is a going and a coming, that nine-foot groove is about four hundred years old and the only reason anybody knows it is a route is that a stone has gone along it and come back every day of that time, and a going that does not come back is not a route — and it is a blank route.** Then a man of thirty-one says out loud, before he does it, that he is not going to say a word to it, and gives the reason: on the Monday of the hundred and eighteenth week a man who cannot see well stood on a bank and said a name to a stone and nothing whatever happened, and he is not going to be the second man to say a word to a wall. **He puts his hand on the facing and it is not stone. It goes about the length of two men and then it is not a room, and there is no floor to speak of and no second wall and it is dark the whole way, and he goes through it in about as long as it takes a man of forty-one to push a stone nine feet and comes out in the ninth room about nine feet from where he put his hand in, with the lamp where the man of thirty-eight had left it.** **And there is a cut across his right palm from the base of the thumb to about the first joint. It is cold and it is not bleeding much, and he holds the hand up in front of the lamp and looks at it and says nothing at all about it for about as long as it takes a man of forty-one to push a stone nine feet.** A man of thirty-eight puts his own hand on the groove above the shelf and then on that hand and says it is about as long, and that he is not going to round it and not going to say it is exactly that, because the groove has a notch in the end of it and there is no notch in this. **And he says it is a figure of a length in a hand and not of anything else, and there is no book in four counties that a man can look in and check what that cost. And a man of thirty-one says what it is not, and that nobody is to say it will keep or that it will not keep or that it is not much, because there is no figure for any of those three — and about nine people in that room did not say a word to him about the hand.**

**686 — ABOUT A FOOT OF DRY WALKED GRAVEL IS ABOUT THREE INCHES DEEP WITH ONE BOOT IN IT, AND A MAN OF THIRTY-ONE SAYS OUT LOUD ON THAT GRAVEL THAT HE HAS LOST THE WAY HE CAME BACK ON.** The second day of the hundred and twentieth week, a Wednesday, his four hundred and thirty-sixth morning, his one hundred and thirty-second day. **In the passage about nine feet past the last of the beams a man of thirty-eight puts his lamp on the floor and gets down on one knee in it and says there is about three inches of that gravel loose in it where there was about a foot on the Monday of the hundred and eighteenth week, and that he has been in it three times since and has put his own arm in to the shoulder; and that there is still a walked place in the middle of it about as wide as one boot and not two, where on the Monday of the hundred and eighteenth week there were about nine of them wide, and this morning there is one.** **And a man of thirty-one says it standing on that gravel with his hands behind his back, before he has gone any further up or down, and nobody had asked him anything: a groove is a route and a route is a going and a coming, and he has got the other half of it and the other half is this; and the coming came out of this, that is what a coming is, it is not a figure, it is a wet place about as wide as one boot, and it has gone — and he says he is not going to stand in a passage and pretend to the woman of forty-nine who gave us that word on the Tuesday that a rack with one blank shelf on it has anything to do with it.** Then he says the whole of what he has lost, in his own mouth, and nobody else in that passage says a word while he does it: **he has had a way back since the Wednesday of the hundred and first week, which is the evening of the day he came in by boat and is a hundred and thirty-three days ago, and he has never once been able to say what it was made of; and it is not there now, and he is not going to give anybody a figure for it, because there is no book in four counties with a figure in it and there is not one person in four counties who was keeping it, and he is not going to be the man who invents a number at about the ninth hour on a bank.** **A man of sixty-one gives both counts on the bank — one hundred and thirty-two days in the county, six hundred and eighty-six less five hundred and fifty-four — and says that neither of them is wrong and that the difference of one is the evening, and the evening is not a day he was in, and that nobody has ever argued with him about it; and that if a thing down there is a route because a person goes along it and comes back on it, then three inches of loose gravel and one boot of walked place is a mill with nobody in it; and that she is not a keeper of it.**

**687 — ABOUT NINE PEOPLE COULD GO DOWN AND DO NOT BECAUSE A MAN OF THIRTY-EIGHT TOLD THAT BANK A WEEK EARLIER WHAT HIS SHOULDER WOULD DO ON A THURSDAY, AND A WOMAN OF TWENTY-EIGHT FINDS OUT SOMETHING ABOUT A HAND THAT NOBODY ASKED HER TO LOOK FOR.** The third day of the hundred and twentieth week, a Thursday, his four hundred and thirty-seventh morning, his one hundred and thirty-third day. **A man of thirty-eight says his shoulder is no good for carrying a pole up that water on a Thursday, that he said it in a gallery with a lamp in his hand on the Tuesday of the hundred and nineteenth week, and that it is no good this morning and he is not going up that water today with a pole in his right hand — and about nine of them had not believed him in advance and about four of them had, and he said one word about it and the word was that they did not have to.** And the closing line of the chapter says only that he was right on the first Thursday, that the second one is seven days off, and that he says it will be the same and has asked nobody to take his word for that part — **and the second Thursday is `694`, which is in the next band and had not been written when that line was.** Then a woman of twenty-eight, who is not one of the nine and keeps nothing, looks at a hand for about as long as it takes a man of forty-one to push a stone nine feet, and says afterwards that she had not decided to look at it: **it is dry. Every hand on that bank is wet and every one of them has been wet every morning for about three weeks, and that hand has been in that water on two mornings of this week, once in about four feet of it on the Saturday and once in about two inches on the second step for two hours on the Wednesday, and it is as dry as the back of her own hand.** And the skin round it has not gone white and has not gone soft and the palm of it has not come up the way a hand comes up when it has been in water that long. **She says she is not going to be a keeper of it and she is not going to be asked about it, and nobody asked her.** **She says she wrote one sentence in her day book in her own hand with no other hand between, and that it is the first thing in about nine days she has put anywhere that was not a number, and that she broke her own rule and is not going to tell anybody what the rule was for — and that nobody is going to thank her for it.** A man of thirty-eight then says what his shoulder is and what a Thursday is for: he carries a board about nine inches long with thirty-two notches cut in the edge of it that nobody has ever asked him about, and on a Thursday he reads two copies nine miles apart from about the fourth hour to about the ninth, and this morning he cannot lift his right hand above his shoulder, and there is no form that says a man may be sent for about a shoulder and no form that says he may not. **A boy of thirteen gives thirty-four days since he went down and nine times this week that he decided a thing, and says which is a count of days and which is a count of times.** Then about four people ask how many days it has been there and about nine people have a different answer and every one of them is wrong in the same way, and a man of sixty-one says it is two and it is not three.

**688 — NOBODY GOES DOWN ON A BANK THAT IS ABOUT TEN INCHES OVER THE TOP STEP, AND THE QUESTION IS PUT A THIRD TIME BY A MAN WHO HAS A SCAR AND IS STILL NOBODY, AND THE ANSWER HAS CHANGED AGAIN AND **689 — SIX NINES AND ONE EIGHT ARE COUNTED OUT LOUD IN A ROOM AND ARE NOT ADDED, AND A MAN OF SIXTY-ONE PUTS A NUMBER IN HIS BOOK AND SAYS OUT LOUD WHAT HE WILL NOT WRITE BESIDE IT.** The fifth day of the hundred and twentieth week, a Saturday, his four hundred and thirty-ninth morning, his one hundred and thirty-first day. **A man of sixty-one announces three days early what he is going to do: he is going to write a number in this book this morning, he wrote a line in it eighteen days ago about a figure a man of forty-three had got wrong by a day and that was a line and not a number, and he has not written a number in that book since he took it in eleven years — and it is a Saturday and he would have preferred a Tuesday and there was not one.** **Then six nines are counted out loud, one at a time, each by a different mouth, with what each one is said before it: nine openings in a wall about a hundred and thirty feet past the last of about nine beams, counted twice by two men walking opposite ways, neither of whom has a hand that can write it down; Six nines and one eight is seven figures; he gives two of the three figures in the working — a six nines is fifty-four and an eight is eight — and refuses the third, because there is nothing in that county to add them to; and six is a figure of how many times the word nine has been said in a room that morning and one is a figure of how many times it has been said and been a different number.** Then a man of forty-one gives the only good count of the morning: seven times somebody has opened a door to him this week, six doors and one bakehouse, one name out of the seven, and that name is a man who has been dead about thirty years — and the eighth is on the Monday, in a village he has never been inside a gate of. **And a man of sixty-one writes it, on the leaf after the last head, where there had been four lines and there are now five. Nine.** He says what he is not going to write next to it: nine people, because there are eight of them, and a book with a nine in it and eight names against it is a list of what is missing, and a woman of forty-four said on the Saturday of the hundred and sixteenth week that a list is a column and a column is a thing people get put in inside about four years. **And a woman of forty-nine stops the count, because she is asked for her nine and says no: it is a figure of her own work and not of anything that is nine, and she would like it said that she has been asked for it and has said no and that nobody has asked her twice, and that a person who cannot be asked is not the same as a person who has said no, and she knows that and would like about four people to know that she knows it.**

**690 — THE BAND CLOSES ON THE PRICE AND NOT ON A RESOLUTION, AND A MAN OF THIRTY-ONE SAYS THE WHOLE OF IT OUT LOUD BEFORE ANYBODY ELSE HAS DESCRIBED IT AND THEN SAYS WHAT THE NINE ARE OWED FOR.** The sixth day of the hundred and twentieth week, a Sunday, his four hundred and fortieth morning, his one hundred and thirty-sixth day. **A man of forty-three gives the reading and counts the two people who are in a place and not in a room: a man of thirty-four in an ash on the low side of that landing for sixty days, and a woman of twenty-nine in a reed on the other side for sixty-one days, and he has not added those two together in nine days and he is not starting on a Sunday.** Then the things that have not moved are said out loud one under another: a sheet of deal with a seal on it is still lying face down on a barrel nine miles down this water, nobody has taken it inside, nobody has burned it, nobody has put a mark on it, the stone it is on is back on the counter where it came from, and that barrel has had it lying face down on it for about forty days, and forty is six hundred and ninety less six hundred and fifty, and a man of forty-three said out loud on the Wednesday of the hundred and fifteenth week that a sheet of deal does not spoil in three days; and there is no number for the local hearing and it has not moved in about nine days and is not going to move on a Sunday; and a hearing about every record in the world is declared by a Bench four hundred miles off and publishes no days and no room and no county, and it has still not settled one single thing that anybody on that bank has said out loud in about nine days. **A woman of twenty-eight checks the lamp-and-the-record thing a fifth time and does not say it, and says it is still not because it is not true. A man of fifty-four who cannot read reports the five days and says there is no figure for it and there is not going to be one.** **And then a man of thirty-one says the whole of the price out loud, in his own mouth, before anybody else has described it, as a list, and says what each item is a figure of: one cut across his right hand from the base of the thumb to about the first joint, cut on the Tuesday of this week, measured by a man of thirty-eight against a groove in a wall and not rounded, with no figure for what it cost because there is no book to check it in; one way back, which he had from the Wednesday of the hundred and first week and does not have, which was about a hundred and thirty feet of dry passage and about a foot of walked gravel and is now about three inches loose and about one boot wide; one morning of a woman of forty-four's bread and about nine houses a day late and about four of the nine who cannot do a day without it, one of the four has a Thursday and that Thursday was two days ago and he did not ask her on the Thursday and nobody on that bank asked him about the Thursday; seven times somebody opened a door to a man of forty-one this week and one name out of the seven and that name is a man dead about thirty years; one sentence in a day book that has had nothing but numbers in it for about nine days, written in a woman's own hand, and she is not going to be thanked for it and has said so; and one of the nine, and not one of them got back, which is the sixth item and the only one of the six that nobody on that bank has paid for out of pocket.** **Then he says the part that is owed to the next week and not to this one, and he does not dress it and does not apologise for it: there are nine of those and there is not one of them here, not a name, not a bit of a name, not a mark a man of sixty-six could put his hand on; there is no figure for when they will be got either; nobody is to hear him say sorry for it, nobody is to forgive him for it and nobody is to be asked to; and about nine of them have to be got, and it is not the work of this week, and he would like it recorded that a man of thirty-one said that out loud on a Sunday to about nine people and did not put a single one of them on a form.** A man of forty-one says he is going into a gate in the morning that he has never been inside, up that cart road past the bakehouse and about a mile further on, and that he is not taking anybody with him.

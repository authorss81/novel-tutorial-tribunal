# Chapter Summaries

**One paragraph per chapter for the band in progress. This block supersedes everything below it.** **Chapters 1–450 are canon and Volume 09 is closed. Volume 10, *The House That Refused*, is open; 451–460 are canon.** Earlier bands are one line each; chapters are the record.

## VOLUME 10, CHAPTERS 451 TO 460 — *the town, the board, and the first refusal*

**451 — The ask, the want said out loud, and the going.** A woman stands up at the back of a room at the foot of a hill and gives her name, her age, her office, her year and her county before anybody asks: **Wenna Crale, 34, copyist to the House of Halloway, eleven years, forty-one miles walked, nine nights in a hayloft so that nobody would have to find her a bed.** Her question is who is the hand on the ninth clause of the Hollow Book, and it is about a date: the front is four hundred years old and **the ninth clause is in a fifth hand, a different ink, and dated a hundred and thirty-eight years ago.** It is in writing, and it is read into the book. Ilyan says his want out loud in front of about thirty people — **a town where his name is not on anything** — and says the price of the sixth want is that he is not going to get it, and Cope enters it without a name on it and without reading it out, the first time in six weeks. Then, without stopping, he gives away the one fact that would have let her leave: **Halloway is not upon a road of the Shelf, so the declaration of continuity and nine weeks of documents have never touched it — and a thing that cannot govern you cannot protect you.** She says he should not have said it in a room. He names the price himself: eleven days he could have had. **He refuses her**, in the order it is actually true: four unfinished cases, no standing in her town, and a third reason he does not say until a yard at the sixth hour on Sunday. He goes on the Sunday for Hessa Marl's question — *what happens to a book when there is no office to keep it in* — and Fenna Rusk tells him what he will find. `451`

**452 — Milestones with a letter on one side and nothing on the other, and a notice read out in a square.** The last nine miles of the road have a milestone with the county's letter on the north face and nothing on the south face, and after it the driver has never been on a list in thirty-one years. Halloway comes out of a fold with the river on the left; on the Sunday evening about forty people are standing outside the Stone House in the rain because a notice went up on the Wednesday about scouring the race in nine days. He counts them — **forty-one people, nineteen houses, a girl of nineteen with a roll under her coat** — and a man of sixty asks who he is and what he will do with the number, and he says nothing and is told he is the first to say that in nine weeks. The man asks him to read the notice out. **He reads it out, two hundred words, standing on a step, to forty-one people, because a man he had met ninety seconds earlier asked him to.** The girl refuses to have her roll read by a stranger and he refuses for her, out loud, in front of everyone, and explains that reading a notice is what makes a man somebody's man in a town and there is no form in the county that says what a man who reads a thing out is. **That man is Amos Trill, steward forty years, whose name was at the foot of the notice.** He says the want out loud afterwards: he came here to be a man on no paper and made himself the reader in four hours. At the ninth hour a boy of eleven says his name at the door, because Wenna Crale got in on a Saturday cart and told nine people. `452`

**453 — The board on the beam over the fire, and the two hands.** The Stone House is the coldest room in Halloway and that is why the House sits in it. The board is about four feet by two and a half, nailed nine feet up over a hearth that has had a fire in that corner for four hundred years, with a lead tag gone the colour of a wet stone. **It comes down twice a year — the audit day and the water sitting — by two people, the steward and the clerk, onto a long table, in about a quarter of an hour, in a hurry, with the fire lit.** Wenna Crale asks how many people can read the hand; four can read the copy and **only two have ever read the Book, and one of the two has been dead nine years, and the grandmother learned it off the copy made by Isabel Tuke, twenty-four, ninety years ago** — so there are three people between the hand and anybody in that town who can use it. **The audit day came and went in the week of the division with the Book up on the beam, because an audit is an audit of a common and there was no common.** The next opening is the water sitting, on the Thursday, in three days. He asks to be one of the two hands on the board and is refused, in front of the board, and the reason is the best reason anybody gives him in nine weeks: **the House's whole power is that its men can be asked things, and he cannot be asked a single thing about the water of the Hallow.** He asks for the refusal to be written down, and argues — wrongly, and he knows it — that the only reader standing over the board is the person who writes the minutes. She makes him say it in front of the board. And she gives him Hessa Marl's answer: **a book in a town is kept by the town for as long as the town is feeling like it, and there is no line in four hundred years that says the next House has to open the press.** `453`

**454 — A figure in a market, and the same figure taken apart four hours later.** The copy of the roll is readable by anybody. **The Fen: nineteen tenements, common land, not rated, in respect of water** — and under it in an older hand, *formerly the whole of the Fen, in respect of water, common to all households of this town, discharged.* The roll is made once a year and it was made in the week the common was sold, so **a land is a thing you are charged for and a house is a thing you put a roof on, and the people living on the Fen at that moment were not anybody, in a book, at the only moment in a year that anybody looks.** He says **one thousand and forty-one plus nineteen is one thousand and sixty** in a market to about two hundred people, because he cannot leave a figure alone. By the evening the figure is one thousand and ninety-one and two men at the mill are saying the rate is going up a fifth. He goes back into the same square on the same day and takes it apart: **nineteen tenements is not nineteen households, the row holds about twenty-three people, the true number is somewhere between nineteen and twenty-three, and the number is the protection.** Amos Trill tells the market that the out-of-roll book is a one-line power he has held for forty years and has never used, and that it has never been written in in this town and the last entry in the county was a man at Ostrey four years back, and why: **because if he ever comes in with a thing, four years from now forty men will remember that the steward comes in with a thing.** Sefa Lund tells him not to use it, for that reason, and he is thanked by nobody. `454`

**455 — A room, a witness, and a fourth paragraph struck out in her own hand.** Sefa Lund asks for a room, a witness and somebody to write it down, and he offers to be the somebody before he has decided, and says the price out loud — a record is a handrail and also a rail a person can be pushed against. **He writes four hundred and ten words.** The man from the county, Garrick Nye, is right about everything he measures: the race has not been scoured in nine years, it silts up, and nine years back, in the worst flood anybody in that row can remember, it put four feet of water into nine houses. **The frame's north heel is a wet black end and a man's thumb goes into it to the first joint.** Sefa Lund reads all four hundred and ten words back and strikes out the fourth paragraph — that the fenrow ought to be recompensed — with two strokes, signs across the strike, and hands him the paper: *you have made a person with a claim out of me and I have not asked to be one.* He asks to be nine days late. **Mab Harrow, thirty-four, from a doorway, asks who it is for, and gets three answers, and the third is the one: the frame is for the town.** **£14 for a scouring out of a fund of £34 14s, which is two fifths of it, and the county does not pay for water.** The last line of the band: the objection is the only thing anybody has said in a week about the water, and what it says is that nobody has ever told us it is ours. `455`

**456 — The board comes down, and a woman turns it over, and a panel.** The Book comes off the beam in four minutes, two people, no asking. **Clause four is read out: the water of the Hallow from the sluice downward is common to all households, and no household is to be put out of it for any cause but the four at clause five, and nobody in the room has caused one of them in living memory.** The vote is **six to three, the three named in the minute, and they are not wrong** — £14 leviable on the eighty lots at three and a halfpence a lot. The four fenrow are in the doorway because they are not of the town. **The band's one panel** at `456:73`–`456:79`: a clause never read out in a room is not a rule; the difference is decided by whoever gets a table, a fire and nine men; the burden falls on one woman of thirty-four and on no line anywhere; the choice is to read it or put the board back up. **He chose nothing, and told nobody — and Wenna Crale turned the board over before he could.** Clause nine, a hundred and thirty-eight years old: *no household of this town shall be required to take water, or to be entered, or to answer, against its will* — signed, with **two names in a witness line, one of them four letters and a stroke in a hand that is not of the town's.** He says out loud that this is the right of refusal and that it was taken out of the law about a hundred and forty years ago; **Mab Harrow asks him how he knows and there is nothing in that town that backs him**; the word *unverified* goes into the minute at the steward's insistence. And the sentence the volume turns on: **somebody in a town nobody here has heard of had something they wanted to write down, two years after it was taken. `456`

**457 — The gates shut, the thumb in the heel, and what a witness is.** The gates shut on the Friday for nine days; the wheel is turned twice a day whatever the gates are doing. **The north heel has been a wet black end since the county man was here nine years ago and he told the steward then and the steward told the next steward, and the board has heard it and written nothing.** Perdy Sallow offers the out-of-roll book — nineteen households entered, the board not able to strike one off for a debt under two years — **and asks for the sluice kept for the nine days, with a winding at the sixth and the twelfth, in the minute.** Sefa Lund refuses in a lane in front of about nine people: **she said on Wednesday that she wants to be a woman who keeps a sluice who is not a household, she meant it, and putting nineteen houses on the roll in the morning makes her a person with a claim she has not asked for.** Sallow says he made an offer that was no offer and that he has been doing it wrong since the year before last. **Ilyan offers to carry it — one document, the ninth clause, the rot, the roll, three matters in three rooms — and Sefa Lund tells him what a witness is, in a lane, in front of about eleven people, using the sentence he said himself in another city a week before: a man who links accounts does not speak for a witness, he gets them to say it and keeps them apart.** He asks her to say it out loud and she does. And at the end of the lane the chair says the thing that turns the week: **the roll for next year is made in the autumn, and anything not on this year's roll and not in the book is not a household of this town — and he refuses to put it in a minute, because the minute is the only thing this town has ever used against anybody.** `457`

**458 — Water in pails, a miller's refusal, and a number refused.** Four days of no water: two tin pails, one a hand's width narrower, four hundred yards, twice a day, and about four hundred people doing it. **Ordwin Cape, sixty-one, eleven shillings a year against nineteen houses at a penny a day for nine days, is thirty-nine pence short and does not know which woman.** On the Saturday a miller of thirty-nine, **Ott Vernay, four years at the mill and nine hundred names on a book**, stops credit to a woman of thirty-four who has bought on credit there for eleven years, because he looked her up and she is not on the roll. He says why out loud in a shop with nine people in it: **he does not know her, he would have stopped anybody, and a stranger paying a bill in a mill shop is how nine hundred people end up owed by a stranger.** Ilyan offered to settle it out of his own pocket before anybody asked, and named where the money came from, in a town that has never heard of the forms it comes from. Sefa Lund notes that nobody has done anything to her. Late that Saturday evening he asks **Wenna Crale to copy his unsent return into the town's book — a number he has carried twenty days — and she refuses and tells him to send it**, and says the best thing anybody has heard in nine days: **in this town a thing in a book is a thing a person can be asked about, and you came here to unlearn that, and it is the one thing in this place worth having.** It goes back in the coat. `458`

**459 — The back of the fourth leaf, and a clause that shuts the door it is written in.** Wenna Crale had found the second margin on the Friday and said nothing for two days, because **her grandmother taught her that a strike is a strike and she has taught two apprentices that a strike is a strike.** On the back of the fourth leaf of the copy, in the hand of Isabel Tuke, twenty-four, ninety years ago, are two margins: *struck by order upon a paper brought to me to be fair copied; the paper is not of this town and I have seen the hand of one man only on it* — and, smaller, **I have not been able to learn whether the striking was of this clause alone.** So **clause nine is struck and clause four is not, and this town has been treating a struck clause as a repeal of the whole Book for ninety years.** Ilyan says it out loud, wrongly and happily, and **Mab Harrow asks whose: clause four gives the water to all *households*, and the fenrow are not households, and the thing that would make them households is the thing the House has just declined to do.** He counts his own errors out loud — five in eight days, three of them his own doing in four seconds. And the steward says the only thing he has: **a woman of twenty-four wrote on the back of a leaf that she could not learn who was struck, the paper is not of this town, and a town of four hundred years has never once turned a leaf over.** `459`

**460 — The House sits in the square, and declines, in the fenrow's own name.** Forty-one stools in the market square in the open air in summer, the order of the wards, about two hundred people at the edges, nine of the fenrow at the front. The head on the table was written by Wenna Crale: **whether the nineteen tenements at the Fen, entered as land and not as households, shall be entered as households upon the roll to be made in the autumn, or shall not.** Ilyan asks for his view in front of everybody and says out loud, first, that he is asking because the steward is the only person in the square who has been wrong and said so in a room. **Amos Trill declines — and the declining is at the request of the persons concerned, made in a lane, in the fenrow's own words, and he did it in about nine seconds.** Sefa Lund stands up in the square, asks for the rest of the minute, makes the clerk write the line about the fenrow not being before the House, and then says the sentence the volume is named for: **there is a sentence on a board a hundred and thirty-eight years old that says the water is common to all households, and I have not got a household, and I did not agree to not have one.** Ilyan says he has no standing and says nothing, and asks that the minute record it. The third line of the minute is correct and self-contradictory in the same sentence. The gates come off the water five days early. **That afternoon he writes a letter to Sera Quill and does not send it, and the last part of it is that under the ninth clause there are two names in a witness line and one of them is four letters and a stroke that nobody in that town can read and that he is not going to guess at.** `460`

**THE ARCHIVE LORDS, IN ONE PARAGRAPH, BECAUSE IT IS THE THING THE NEXT BAND HAS TO CARRY AND NOBODY ELSE WILL WRITE IT DOWN: a custody faction is people whose whole business is holding other people's records so that nobody loses them. They are not liars and not cruel and not working for something. THEY ARE RIGHT ABOUT MOST OF IT. A sheet of paper in a room over a milliner's is a sheet of paper in a room over a milliner's, and a woman of about fifty-one is the only person in a county who can say how four pages were made, and if she is hit on a head by a barrow on a Tuesday then the only record of how those four pages were made is gone. THE ERROR IS NOT VICE AND IT IS THAT A THING WHICH IS SAFE BECAUSE SOMEBODY HOLDS IT CAN BECOME SAFE BECAUSE NOBODY MAY TAKE IT, AND THAT IS A DIFFERENT THING AND IT LOOKS IDENTICAL FROM THE OUTSIDE. NOBODY IN THE FACTION WANTS A RULING CLASS; A MAN IN IT SAYS OUT LOUD, AT `531`, AT `533` AND AT `540`, THAT HE DOES NOT WANT ONE, AND IS RIGHT, AND IS ABOUT TO MAKE ONE ANYWAY. BY THE END OF THE BAND ABOUT NINE PEOPLE IN BRAMBLEFOLD CAN NAME THE DIFFERENCE OUT LOUD AND ABOUT NINE CANNOT, AND THE CHAPTERS SAY SO TWICE AND THE TWO SETS OF NINE ARE NOT THE SAME SETS.**

**THE MIDPOINT, AND WHAT IT WAS: a man of about seventy-two of this town was asked one question, in a room with the door open, in front of about nine people, whether he was asked a question in a square in a town two days off on the morning of the thirtieth day after the division, and what he said out loud was that he does not remember, and about four people in that room understood that this is the finding and about nine understood that it is a failure. Nobody was withholding anything. Both towns are remembering correctly. There is no instrument in the county of Aldwick that can hold two true accounts of one morning, and the record is the only thing that cannot hold both, and it has four columns and no fifth.**

## VOLUME 12, CHAPTERS 551 TO 560 — *the borrowed season, opened*. **A NEW COUNTY, A NEW SUBSTANCE, AND THE INSTRUMENT TURNED UPSIDE DOWN. THE RECORD IN BRAMBLEFOLD WAS IN A LOCKED CUPBOARD; THE RECORD IN WITHERMERE IS A SHEET TWO MEN LONG ON THE WALL OF A WEIGH-HOUSE AND NOBODY KEEPS IT. THE BAND DOES NOT RESOLVE ANYTHING AND IT ENDS ON A MAN WHO IS RIGHT ABOUT THE COVENANT AND RIGHT ABOUT THE DRAIN AND CANNOT BE RIGHT ABOUT BOTH.**

**551 — A letter refused four times, a question handed over, and a bed given back on the last day of the week.** `clear` 51, the seventh and last day of the hundredth week, a Monday. A sheet comes up the lane folded in four in a boy's hand and has been in a coat for three days. It is from **Hester Nunn of Withermere**, a woman of forty-one, and it is about eleven households and two hundred and ten acres and a man who came round with a purse in April. A second sheet is in a different hand and says four lines and does not ask him for anything. **He carries the letter for three days and tries once, on the Monday, to get it entered at the back of the book four hundred and eleven years old, and is refused in a porch in about four seconds**, and the reason is that in a hundred years somebody will want to know what the woman was owed and there will be no way on this earth to ask her. **A man of eighty-one asks him to carry a question to a place where nobody knows him — whether a man can be named in a place and still not be of it — and will not be told the answer is no, because he has had a silence twice and a silence is not a no.** And a man of thirty-one gives up a bed paid to the end of the week, on the last day of the week, and walks out of that town at about the sixth hour in the evening with one pound and threepence and a sheet of paper and no address in that county.

**552 — A wharf, a plank, a fare named out loud, and a boy who says the number is not the thing.** `clear` 52, the first day of the hundred and first week, a Tuesday. Twenty-one miles on foot. **He counts the loads of a three-thousandweight stone being put into a boat and a man of about nineteen stops with the bar across the plank and tells him, in front of about four people, that the number is only so that the other man knows it was heard.** A carrier of forty-four named **Orwin Slee** names his fare out loud on the stones — **three shillings and sixpence** — and makes a man of thirty-one turn his own money into days, and Ilyan says the number out loud, one pound and eighteen pence, because he has been doing that for a hundred and fifty-three days. He offers to work his passage and is refused in about four seconds: a man in his crew in the water is a man he has to answer for, and he has not had to answer for a stranger since he was thirty-four. The boat leaves at about the ninth hour. **The line about `clear` changes here and the chapter says why: the town the count was begun in is four hundred miles off and behind him.**

**553 — A day on the water, a boy of four words, and a town with a wall.** `clear` 53, the second day of the hundred and first week, a Wednesday. Eleven hours on a flat of brown water. **Reuben Searle, nineteen, tells him a season is *somebody's and not yours*, and then tells him the word the hundred uses, which is *borrowed*, and that it is on no paper anywhere and he has been in front of the sheet four hundred times.** Ilyan asks a question he had no standing to ask — whether the boy has a season — and is told no, and told about eleven tents and about sixty people and a licence until the water turns, and told to ask about the clerk instead. **They pass a block of limestone with a line cut round it and the boy names it unasked: the tally stone, and the day the water is over it is the first day of the year and every season in the hundred has gone.** The woman who keeps the inn of four beds will not let a man count money out loud in her parlour. At the ninth hour he stands in the dark under a lamp that is not lit and a man tells him he may read it in the morning, and that nobody has not asked him and both of those are the right way round.

**554 — A sheet two men long, a cart of wool, and nine sentences.** `clear` 54, the third day of the hundred and first week, a Thursday. **He stands in the way of a cart of wool and turns it twice and makes it eleven minutes late, and a carter of forty tells him he is no use apologising to, because a man who cannot be sent for is a thing a person would have to carry for a year.** The sheet is ruled in three columns — the ground, the taking, the year — and **there is no column with a person's name at the top of it and there never has been one.** A water-clerk of fifty-eight, **Iven Ashcombe**, whose name is on a board outside his own door with a promise on it that any person in the hundred may ask him anything and he will answer it, refuses to say the name of the man who took up the eleven seasons, and gives the reason: a keeper's name in a story is a thing a man's wife is told about at a pump. **Then he says the whole of it in about nine seconds and the whole of it is that a man of fifty-four paid the rate on two hundred and ten acres in one week in April, and it is legal, and there is no form that says otherwise. And he says the sentence the band is built on: a man nobody may ask is not a free man, and a man everybody may ask is not a free man either, because then the asking is the work and the man is only the thing being worked on.** The rising is twelve days.

**555 — A market on a Friday, a woman told the true in the open air, and twelve shillings and sixpence refused.** `clear` 55, the fourth day of the hundred and first week, and it is the market, and about three hundred people are in the green. The standing business of a Withermere market is not hay: a man may offer a parcel's season for sale at a stone step and the clerk writes it on a slate, and **none of the eleven is on the step, because they were taken up in April and there is no step for putting that.** **Hester Nunn asks the clerk, in front of about thirty people, whether her season is still hers, and he has to answer, and answers it, and could not have done otherwise, because of a board.** He then gives her the true of it: two shillings and a penny the acre, six acres, twelve shillings and sixpence a year, and that money is what cuts the ditch, and that he is doing it better. **And she says the worst of it out loud: he is doing it properly.** Ilyan offers to pay the rate out of his own purse and **is refused in four sentences**, the fourth of which is that a season bought with a stranger's money is still a season bought from her.

**556 — A mile of water cut in March, a gatepost, and the reason nobody said anything.** `clear` 56, the fifth day of the hundred and first week, a Saturday. He walks the fen pasture between a dyke and a low meadow and is put off nine acres of new sward in about four seconds. **A gatepost comes out of the ground and he goes into a ditch to the waist, and a man of about fifty-one, Hoare Ryley, gets him out with a crowbar.** Ryley then tells him the whole of it: the drain was cut in the first week of March, before the sowing, on a parcel its cutter holds the season on, at the top of it, running north a mile, and **the rule in that hundred governs a parcel and not a water, and a rule that governs a parcel and not a water is a rule that can be kept while somebody else drowns.** **About nine men in that district have known about the cut since the first week of March and not one of them has said it out loud, and the reason is that they all have land on that water**, and then he says the reason had not meant to come out and had only come out because a gatepost went into a ditch. A woman of about thirty-eight called Ilsa Rundle, with a barrow and a boy of about four, at a gate, gives the other half: a man cannot be stopped and can be asked, and being asked is enough to live on, and that is why it is not enough. The rising is ten days.

**557 — Eleven lines with nothing at the top of them, and one of them wrong.** `clear` 57, the sixth day of the hundred and first week, a Sunday. He writes eleven lines with nothing at the top of it and carries them to be nailed to the wall. **The clerk says before he nails them up that they will change nothing, and gives the reason, and nails them up anyway.** Three people come. **The third is a man of about forty-four from out of the district who says one line is wrong and is right: the long grazing of forty-four acres is the same holder's sixth year, taken up in the open at a re-letting with about thirty people in the building, and the weapon is the ten small ones in one week and not the seven years.** The correction goes under the notice in the clerk's hand with the day on it, and nothing is ruled and nothing is headed, and a man of about thirty-four stands in front of it with a rag at the fourth hour and does not touch it. **The band finds the cost of putting your hand on a public record: the hand stays on it.** The rising is nine days.

**558 — A camp, a paper in a sack, and a number that is the season.** `clear` 58, the seventh and last day of the hundred and first week, a Monday, and the morrow is the first of the hundred and second. He walks onto the low common and **counts a camp of tents out loud and is corrected by Bett Searle, twenty-eight, from sixty to fifty-eight, and the two that were subtracted are named**: a man of about seventy who went into Withermere on a Friday and has not come back, and a boy of nine who is in a village four miles off. **There is no form in that hundred that says how many people a licence covers, because the licence is the number, and the number is the season, and a man with a slate at a re-letting can write any number he likes and there is nothing anywhere to say he is wrong.** Her paper is one sheet in a sack under a churn with a figure and a day and no name on it and no copy anywhere; his is on a wall with a keeper who does not exist. **The water-clerk comes to the edge of the camp, stands there for eleven minutes and does not cross onto it**, and says the water off the cut will be over the low common on the tenth day from today, which is the second day after the rising, and says nothing else. The rising is eight days.

**559 — The instrument refused, and the room accepted, and a day on a dyke.** `clear` 59, the first day of the hundred and second week, a Tuesday, and the day before was the seventh and last of the hundred and first. **He offers, in the open, in front of about nine people, to be the one anybody in that hundred can be sent for about the eleven parcels and the drain and the fifty-eight people, and is refused in about four seconds, and the reason is the same reason wearing the other coat: in a chalk town a keeper is a danger, and in this town a man everybody may ask is a man who is only the thing being worked on.** And then **he offers, for the first time in a hundred and fifty-nine days, to be in the room on the rising — not behind the table, not at the step, in it — and the clerk accepts in about four seconds**, and the reason is that he is the only person in that hundred who will be in that room and cannot be paid by anybody in it, and the clerk has never in nineteen years been able to use anything like him. **The question Volume 11 was left open on is asked of him and is not answered: a man of eighty-one asked whether a man can be named in a place and still not be of it, and the clerk says it is a question for the hundred, which meets in seven days, and says he will not tell him now what will be said in it.** He works on the main dyke from the seventh hour to the fifth and cannot walk off the end of it, and **a man of fifty-one says the number out loud on a bank, unasked: nine men, one day, and a drain cut in March did all of that in an hour and a half.** The rising is seven days.

**560 — Seven hundred sheep, a drain taken out, and eleven doors.** `clear` 60, the second day of the hundred and second week, a Wednesday, and the band's only System panel. **A man of fifty-four with about seven hundred sheep comes four miles into a weigh-house because his name is on a wall**, and he is right in about nine sentences in front of about thirty people: the hay failed two years ago, the rate on the eleven was not paid, **the ditches filled in**, the low meadow went under, and he had seven hundred sheep and nowhere to put a third of them, and so in April he went round with a purse and paid the rate on all eleven in four days, and he broke nothing. **He is also wrong about one thing and says so before anybody asks him: the drain, which he cut in March, legally, on his own ground, because his own ground was under nine inches of water, and which he cannot see from where he stands.** He agrees to take it out at his own cost before the rising, and says out loud that **if he takes it out the water goes back where it was, and the low common is downhill of the place it goes back to, and the low common is on the far side of it, and there are fifty-eight people living on it, and there is no form anywhere that says the water may not be on them, because a nobody has never been able to be drowned.** A woman of twenty-eight with a daughter of six on her hip, who walked four miles to hear it, says it is the first true thing anybody has said to her about that common. **The clerk then says that he can hold the water either way and not both ways** — six boards, or three — and that a decision about the level of a fen is not taken by the people who happen to be in a room, and that he is going to say it out loud tonight at eleven doors before anybody writes anything, four of which are the stranger's and seven of which are his, and that if they do not both go then neither of them goes. **The panel comes, at the chapter where the cost is paid, and carries one choice: four of the eleven doors tonight, or all eleven to the clerk. He chose the four closes in about nine seconds and nobody thanked him for it, and the two of them agreed that if they do not both go then neither of them goes.** Nobody in that room worked out which of the two things the man of fifty-four was wrong about, and he did not ask, and the rising is in six days.

## VOLUME 11, CHAPTERS 521 TO 530 — *two towns that remember incompatibly*. **THE MIDPOINT IS SPENT AT `526`.**

**521 — The week read out in the ordinary way, and a boy of fourteen who worked a thing out on his own account.** `clear` 21, a Saturday, the first ordinary reading in three weeks, in about eleven minutes in a chancel eight feet by eleven in front of about nine people. The recordwright says the count out loud in the porch first — the sixty-first day after the division, when she was up a bank with a shovel, and the Saturday of the ninety-fifth week, when a leaf a hundred and forty-one years old was read out instead — and says she would like it said in the porch and not in the room, because a room is a place where people are held to. The week goes: a holding of four acres passes with the number, four sheep out of the fold and two in, a boy born on the upper row, and a man of about eighty-four who is dead in the ground column with the day against him. **Then she reads one line that is not in the book — about nine feet of the bottom of the common wall, open since the day it came down, with a boundary stone in a shelf at the end of it that has nothing cut on it at all — and reads it in the room under the column with no heading on it, and about four people know what it is under and about nine hear a wall.** He said out loud in the porch that it was an ordinary week, and a boy of fourteen came out of the room and asked him what the fourth column is for, and got the right answer, and then said that he had worked out before she told him that a dead man belongs in the ground column, and had told nobody, and was saying it to a man from another county who cannot be sent for because nobody else was there. Closing: four ordinary entries, and a boy who worked a thing out alone. `521`

**522 — A woman goes down a lane and asks a question nobody in that town had ever asked, and the answer is no.** `clear` 22, a Sunday, and the band that is not a mystery but a record. Four people went up a step on Saturday and struck out the name they are each descended from, and nothing has been done about it in a day and a half, and the reason is that there is no form that says a person may be asked whether they meant a thing they did in front of about a hundred and ninety people. **He gets as far as the lower row and a woman of about thirty-five with a child on her hip tells him in about four seconds that he cannot go and ask her, and gives the reason that about nine people heard: a neighbour can ask the question and a stranger cannot, not because the neighbour is better, but because the person can tell the neighbour to go away.** And a person who has been asked nothing in about a year does it — a woman of about fifty-one of this town, in two rooms over a milliner's, who has had a copy of four pages for about a year and has never mentioned it to anybody, goes down the lane herself at about the eleventh hour and asks one question: do you want it in the book, or do you want it left on a sheet of paper in a room. **The neighbour, a woman of about seventy, says no, and gives her reason, which is that a thing this town is told in about four days is a thing somebody has to carry in a hundred years, and asks that it be written down that she said that and not that she said she was not asked.** The recordwright comes down to the bottom of the town herself, which she has done four times in nineteen years, and enters it and reads it back in the voice she uses for minutes, and says out loud that **it is the first thing in four hundred and eleven years that records a person declining something**, and that it is one woman and one name and one afternoon, and that it is not a form, not a practice and not a thing anybody else may do, and that if a second person in that county says no to a thing about her own name then what happens to that is a question to be worked out by whoever is there at the time. **THE RIGHT OF REFUSAL IS NOT RESTORED AND THIS IS NOT IT.** Closing: a sheet with four gaps in it, and an entry nobody can ask about. `522`

**523 — The finding is put in front of the two people it is about, and one of them says she knew.** `clear` 23, the seventh and last day of the ninety-sixth week, and it names the morrow. The recordwright sends for both of them on the Sunday night, separately, in about nine words each, and reads the four sentences out on a Monday. **Edith Crow, sixty-eight, is told first, at her own door, in a porch about four feet deep with no bench in it, and says she knew, and says that the woman who keeps the book could have come up her row in four minutes and did not.** She asks for one thing and it is that the finding go in with the day she was told on it and not the day it was made, and gives the reason out loud before anybody asks her anything: a day of a week comes round and a count begun in another county does not, and four days of a man not knowing is worth having on a page. **Then she says no — to two thirds of a finding about her own objection — because she had four days to think about it and had not heard the question until two minutes ago, and says in the same breath that she is not going to say the other thing now and have it be the second time.** That goes in too, in the ordinary hand, with nothing ruled and nothing headed. Harl Wenlock, forty-four, is told in the meeting-house porch with a man of about fifty, says the finding is right, and says out loud that **he had spent about nine seconds being glad of a thing he had not been told**, and that there is no form that says a man may be asked what he felt about a thing he was not told of; the recordwright does not enter the nine seconds and says that this makes the twelfth case in nineteen years in which she has been the one who has not asked, and that the twelfth is a man of forty-four and not a widow. **And a man of thirty-one says out loud, in the square, that wanting a thing to be right is the whole of what he has been doing for a hundred and twenty-three days, and that it has not once in a hundred and twenty-three days made a person safer.** Closing: a finding with the day a woman was told on it. `523`

**524 — A copy of a copy comes down the lane and it says a name, and a man of thirty-one says the sentence about a door out loud in a room in a town he is not of.** `clear` 24, the first day of the ninety-seventh week, and it names the day before as the last of the ninety-sixth. A carter who keeps a cart between Coldcombe and the toll bar comes down four miles of lane with a bundle on the tail-board and gives it to a woman of sixty-one at the corner by the meeting-house door, because she is the only person anybody has heard of who writes to other towns; a woman of about thirty at a stall in the town two days off put it into his hands and said it was for the woman in the barn at Kiln Row. It is a copy of a copy: a generation further on again, in a quicker hand, about nine mistakes and none of them corrected. **At the foot of the third page, where the first copy has nothing at all, there are four lines that are not on the copy underneath it, and the fourth says the person was asked by a man of thirty-four of Bramblefold and not by the woman, and about four people heard the question and about nine heard the answer. At the foot of the fourth page, in a third hand, there is a name, and the name is Wynd Kettle.** He wants it read out in the porch and is refused, and the reason is given in full: the copy was made in about four minutes of sitting by a woman who could not sleep, and the corrections are the only part of it anybody can check, and the four lines are not on the copy underneath them, and she cannot tell from a bundle on a Monday night whether they are true. **And then he says the sentence he has been carrying since he came to this country — a book is a door — out loud in a porch, in front of about nine people, about a town he is not of, and about four of them understand the whole of it and about nine understand that it is a man from another county who cannot be sent for talking about a town he is not of, and she says she is not going to stand in a porch and argue a man out of the half he said.** A man of eighty-one makes him say it into a room instead. Closing: a copy of a copy with a name in a third hand. `524`

**525 — Two accounts of one morning go side by side and they do not fit, and about four of nine can say exactly where.** `clear` 25, a Wednesday, which is the day a man of about seventy-two walks two miles to hear one line read, and he comes at the ninth hour in a wet coat in a dry week and stands at the end of the bench and is told *same as ever* and says *aye*, and asks again to go and look at the stake and is refused for the fourth time. **526 — THE MIDPOINT. A man of about seventy-two is asked one question in a room with the door open, and he says that he does not remember.** `clear` 26, a Thursday, which is the market, and about two hundred people are in the square and hear none of it. The asking was arranged by a woman of sixty-one in a barn at Kiln Row in about nine sentences and she says plainly that there is no form in the county of Aldwick that says a person may be asked what they remember, that she has been told no four times in four years and not told anything at all once, and that she is not going to ask him and is going to bed. **The recordwright says out loud, before she asks it, that she will not ask him what the question was and will not ask him what he said, and the reason is that a man of about seventy-two asked to remember a thing in front of about nine people is a different room from the one this town has been working in for a fortnight.** He came up the lane at about the half hour after the first on a Tuesday-shaped errand on a Thursday, and nobody told him why, and she says out loud that that is wrong. About nine people are in the chancel and he is given a chair. **He says he was in a porch here at about the ninth hour on that morning and was asked nothing, and then he says THAT HE DOES NOT REMEMBER, and asks that it be written down that he said that and not that he said he was not asked.** A man near the door says twice that the page is wrong and is told by a man of seventy-two that a page is a thing in a hand, that a name on a page is a thing somebody wrote and may be right or wrong, and that if his not-remembering is turned into a page being wrong then in a hundred years there will be a person in that town who was told that his name was taken off a thing and never was. **He then says out loud that he is not a thing that is got back, that he has come up a lane on a Thursday nobody asked him to come up, and that the man of thirty-one had made a page into an instrument and had made it into a man in a chair.** He is entered at the back of the book and the entry says the name stands and is neither withdrawn nor confirmed and that there is no column in that book for a person who does not remember and none has been ruled. And the recordwright says out loud that this town remembers a Tuesday a hundred and forty-one years back with four words on it and no order, and that the other town remembers a Wednesday fifty days back with a woman's letter in it and about two hundred people who did not sleep, and that both are correct and neither is about the other, and that there is no instrument in the county of Aldwick that can hold two correct accounts of the same morning. **Nobody thanked anybody and nobody was relieved.** Closing: a name that stands, and no instrument that can hold two. `526`

**527 — Two accounts go round one town in the same mouths, and a woman of thirty-four says nobody has ever asked her what she remembers.** `clear` 27, a Friday. The four words and the four sentences are going round the same town in the same mouths and about nine people can give you both and about nine can give you only the one they heard first, and **the number of people who can give you both is the number of people who can be asked about the other one.** The sentence about a thing said in a room being a thing about nine people turns out to be three people's: a weaver of sixty-two said it to the man of thirty-one in two rooms over a joiner's shop on the fifty-ninth day after the division, a woman of sixty-one has said it thirty-one times in four years in nine towns, and a letter of four lines from a town four hundred miles off says it too and adds that the people there think the people here ought to be asked whether they want it. **She reads that letter out in a porch, to about nine people, and nobody asks her where it is from, and it also says that they do not know what to do with a person who says no, that they have not found out in about nine years, and that there has not been a week of those nine years without one.** She says out loud that a person who has been asked nothing is the one person in that town who knows what it is like. **And then the recordwright, in a smithy, in front of about nine people, says that she is the only person in that county who can be sent for about either account, that she has been that for nineteen years, and that nobody has ever asked her what she remembers, and that there is no form that says a person may be asked what they remember, and that in about nine rooms in a hundred and twenty-seven days she would have said something and has not said it, and that there is a difference between a thing you will say when somebody asks and a thing you say because you have not been asked in nineteen years.** He asks. She says stand there and be quiet, and that is the only thing she is asking anybody for, and he stands there for about a minute. A man of eighty-one says from the chair, unasked, that a thing a person remembers is not a copy and is not a copy of a copy, and that he has been that person twice in this town and has said nothing both times. He says the two accounts will settle in about eleven years and is told that is what a book does and not what a town does. Closing: the only one anybody can ask, and nobody has asked. `527`

**528 — Two accounts are read out in full in a square and the square decides nothing, and about five of nine cannot remember which they heard first.** `clear` 28, a Saturday, and it is not a market, and the chapter says so in its third line. About two hundred people are in the square and nobody arranged it. **The first account is read by a weaver of sixty-two with his hand flat on the wool bench, who then says he is not going to say anything about them because there is nothing to say and he is tired of being a man who says things about four words; **529 — A man of fifty-four rides nine miles and makes a return that records two answers on the back of a form, and signs the back and not the front.** `clear` 29, a Sunday. He comes down with a form and not a copy of a return, and says out loud on a step, in front of about nine people, that **a return is made by an office and not asked for by a person**, that the difference between a return and an application is about eleven years, that this county has no applications and has never had any and there is no form for one, and that he rode nine miles to say it in about nine sentences and would like it entered that he said it out loud. **And that if nothing is made, then in about a year there will be a return in that office with four lines on it and the answer that the household of Kiln Row gave a hundred and forty-one years ago, and somebody will have copied it, and a person will be told that a man of about seventy-two was asked a question in a square in another town, and nobody will be able to say that both of those are in the same four lines, because the four lines do not say what the question was.** At about the second hour of the afternoon he makes the return in a room eight feet by eleven with the door open. He reads the ordinary front, and stops before the fourth line, and says out loud that the fourth line — *this office has no further particular in the matter* — is a lie of convenience and that he is not going to write it on a return about a disagreement between two towns. **He writes six lines on the BACK, because the form has no line for two, and the back of a return is not part of a return, and he signs the back and does not sign the front, because the front is a hundred and forty-one years old in its wording and he is not going to put a man's name on it about a morning he has not been in.** The sixth line says that this office has been asked by no person to make a return upon the present matter, and that this office has made one, and that this office records that it made one. **And the man of thirty-one is in the lane and cannot be in the room, because there is a form in that county that says who may be in a room when a return is made and it says the officer and the person the return is about and he is neither, and he is told that twice, out loud, in a lane, in front of about nine people, and he says he understands and that it does him no good whatever.** The back is in a coat and will go in a drawer nine miles from here and there is no form that says it may be asked for. Closing: a return on the back of a form, and a man in a lane. `529`

**530 — A boy of fourteen is asked a question in a smithy that nobody in that smithy can answer, and a third column is ruled and not headed.** `clear` 30, the seventh and last day of the ninety-seventh week, and it names the morrow as the first of the ninety-eighth. Closing: two chalk lines, a boy's name under both of them, a dead man in the ground column, and a town that has not had a dated thing to count from since a leaf was struck a hundred and forty-one years back in a week that not one person in that county can name. `530`

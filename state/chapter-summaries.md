**THE ARCHIVE LORDS, IN ONE PARAGRAPH, BECAUSE IT IS THE THING THE NEXT BAND HAS TO CARRY AND NOBODY ELSE WILL WRITE IT DOWN: a custody faction is people whose whole business is holding other people's records so that nobody loses them. They are not liars and not cruel and not working for something. THEY ARE RIGHT ABOUT MOST OF IT. A sheet of paper in a room over a milliner's is a sheet of paper in a room over a milliner's, and a woman of about fifty-one is the only person in a county who can say how four pages were made, and if she is hit on a head by a barrow on a Tuesday then the only record of how those four pages were made is gone. THE ERROR IS NOT VICE AND IT IS THAT A THING WHICH IS SAFE BECAUSE SOMEBODY HOLDS IT CAN BECOME SAFE BECAUSE NOBODY MAY TAKE IT, AND THAT IS A DIFFERENT THING AND IT LOOKS IDENTICAL FROM THE OUTSIDE. NOBODY IN THE FACTION WANTS A RULING CLASS; A MAN IN IT SAYS OUT LOUD, AT `531`, AT `533` AND AT `540`, THAT HE DOES NOT WANT ONE, AND IS RIGHT, AND IS ABOUT TO MAKE ONE ANYWAY. BY THE END OF THE BAND ABOUT NINE PEOPLE IN BRAMBLEFOLD CAN NAME THE DIFFERENCE OUT LOUD AND ABOUT NINE CANNOT, AND THE CHAPTERS SAY SO TWICE AND THE TWO SETS OF NINE ARE NOT THE SAME SETS.**

**THE MIDPOINT, AND WHAT IT WAS: a man of about seventy-two of this town was asked one question, in a room with the door open, in front of about nine people, whether he was asked a question in a square in a town two days off on the morning of the thirtieth day after the division, and what he said out loud was that he does not remember, and about four people in that room understood that this is the finding and about nine understood that it is a failure. Nobody was withholding anything. Both towns are remembering correctly. There is no instrument in the county of Aldwick that can hold two true accounts of one morning, and the record is the only thing that cannot hold both, and it has four columns and no fifth.**

## VOLUME 12, CHAPTERS 561 TO 570 — *the hundred meets, and a rule that can be obeyed by nobody*. **THE BAND SPENDS THE ELEVENTY DOORS, THE MARKET THAT WAS NOT ON, THE FIRST RECORD IN THAT COUNTY MADE BY A CHALLENGE, A PROHIBITION ENTERED BY A MAN AGAINST HIMSELF, THE RISING, A NUMBER OF FEET ON A WALL, A FOOT OF WATER ON A COMMON THAT BELONGS TO NOBODY, A REMEDY PUT TO A MARKET, AND A NAME REFUSED AND FIVE DAYS GIVEN INSTEAD. **561 — Four doorways in the dark, three agreements, one refusal, and one number that was a day wrong.** `clear` 61, the third day of the hundred and second week, a Thursday. A man of thirty-one goes out after dark to the four closes at the bottom of the district, which are a mile and a half apart because a mile and a half is what four closes at the bottom of a fen are. **At the second doorway he says eight days, which was the clerk's figure of the Wednesday before, and a man of about forty-four makes him work it out loud with the subtraction and gets seven, and he is right on the bridge seven days later.** At the third, a woman of about sixty-two sets a time for him to come back and will not be hurried. **At the fourth, a woman of forty-one refuses him before he has finished the second sentence, and the reason is that a number said in a doorway is a thing her neighbours will have by the seventh hour, four people will have said it four ways by then, and on Wednesday morning there will be thirty men in a room and not one of the numbers on the wall will be the number she heard in her own doorway.** She says she will say the thing she has to say at the north wall in the open and not in a doorway. A man of fifty-eight comes up the lane with a slate, says six of seven are done, and says the whole of why the two of them agreed to go in eleven doors: **in this hundred anybody may ask anybody, and so there is nobody to go and check on a stranger at the seventh hour in the morning.** Nothing is on any wall.

**562 — A market called off, and eleven chairs in a room that one man fills.** `clear` 62, the fourth day of the hundred and second week, a Friday, and it is the market day. **About three hundred people come onto a green with nothing on it and go home again, because a man of fifty-eight who may not call off a market called it off at the sixth hour and put it on a board and on three doorsteps first, and said out loud at the stone step that no form in the county of Kell lets him do it or stops him doing it.** He gives the reason: on Sunday the green will not carry forty carts, and he would rather be a man who stood on an empty step on Friday than a man who watched a boy of nine go down in it. **Then a man of thirty-one counts out loud in front of about nine people how many men fill the chairs in a room that meets in four days, and finds that the hundred is about thirty men, one for every holding with a season on it, and that on eleven lines of the thirty the name in the first column is the same name, and that there is no column anywhere on that sheet with a person's name at the top of it and never has been one.** He puts four lines on the wall with nothing at the top of them. **Closing object: a man who owns eleven seasons and has no seat in the room where they are dealt with, and no rule that says he has one.** His hand is on a public wall for the second time in four days.

**563 — A sheet read aloud by a man who is not the clerk, and six challenges.** `clear` 63, the fifth day of the hundred and second week, a Saturday. A district reads its wall on a Saturday morning at the sixth hour, out loud, by whoever holds the slate, with the door open. **The clerk will not read it, and gives the reason twice: if he reads it then it is the water-clerk reading a sheet he wrote, and any man in the street may say the water-clerk read it wrong; and in nineteen years nobody has ever asked whether that sheet was right, and he has wanted it every Saturday and has not asked, because asking him is asking the only person he cannot get away from.** A man of about sixty-two called **Perrin Voss** reads it and is corrected twice by two different men in the street. **About six people say out loud that the reading was wrong about their own ground, and the clerk writes all six down with the day on it, because there is a board outside his door that says he will answer anything. It is the first record in that county ever made by a challenge instead of by a keeper.** One challenge names a parcel; the other five are attached to nothing, because the men who said them did not know which parcels. No names are read out. A man of thirty-one finds that the first thing anybody in this district has put on that wall for is a fifth of it that cannot be checked by anybody including the man who said it.

**564 — Nine lines on a board outside a man's own door, and a boy of fourteen.** `clear` 64, the sixth day of the hundred and second week, a Sunday. **A man of fifty-eight who has answered everything for nineteen years puts nine lines on the board outside his own door: that no person in this hundred is to be asked what they remember**, in a building, a yard, a lane, a market, at a door, on a boat and at a fire, of anybody including himself and including a stranger and a man with a slate, on the rising and every day after it; that a man asked may answer what he knows now and not what he remembers; that the rule does not stop a true thing that can be checked against, or a number off the wall; that he is not to be asked by anybody including his wife; and that it has no day on it and no form suspends it. **He gives the reason against it — a hundred will lose things — and then the reason for it, and says the reason for it is worse: his own promise produced a woman of forty-one being told the whole of the true of her life in a market, and then produced six people at his door asking what he remembers in March, and he remembers three versions of one week of April and one of them is not his.** About nine people ask him about it over about an hour and about nine of them are for the board and about nine are against it and the two nines do not ask each other. **A boy of fourteen writes the nine lines out in chalk, understands all nine, and asks about the one he does not: his father holds nine acres, was on that water in the first week of April three years running, has said nothing about it to anybody, and a boy of fourteen has worked out inside about a second that he has just obeyed the first rule he has ever written down in his life.**

**565 — The eve, a trench of black mud, and one season given to a room.** `clear` 65, the seventh and last day of the hundred and second week, a Monday, and the morrow is the first of the hundred and third. **The drain is a trench of black mud four feet wide with a foot of standing water in the old line, because a man of fifty-four took it out on Saturday at his own cost, and a man of fifty-one who has cut ditch for about thirty years stands at the top end of it at the seventh hour and says the water is going down the meadow and the meadow ends in about eight hundred acres that belong to nobody.** Then it is the last day a season can be given up by the person who holds it. **A man of about thirty-three with six acres of the sally bed, twelve shillings and sixpence a year, held four years, comes to the step with his purse and gives the season up to a room and not to a person.** He does not give it to a woman of forty-one, because she refused a stranger's money in a market ten days ago and was right and he was in the market. He does not give it to a stranger with a bad leg who was at his door on Thursday and did not ask him for anything. **His reason is that there is no column on that sheet with a person's name at the top of it and he will not be the first that somebody adds.** He puts the purse on the bar and then takes it off it, because there is no form that lets the clerk hold twelve shillings and sixpence or give it to anybody, and the six acres will lapse in the morning with the other hundred and thirty and the square at the end of the line will have nothing in it for the first time in about two hundred and thirty years. **A man of thirty-one derives it in a mouth: twelve shillings and sixpence is one hundred and fifty pence, and a hundred and fifty pence of ditch goes out of it, and a ditch fills in in about two years, and no person in that county can say that is wrong.**

**566 — The rising, the hundred, and a question nobody answers.** `clear` 66, the first day of the hundred and third week, a Tuesday, and the day before was the seventh and last of the hundred and second. **The water comes over a block of limestone with a line cut round it at about the seventh hour and there is no bell for it; the clerk puts his hand flat on the stone for the length of a count of five and then writes the day at the foot of a sheet of parchment two men long.** About thirty men meet in a weigh-house sixty feet long at midday, there are forty-one in it by the second hour, and about three hundred people are in a green in the same hour. **A man of thirty-one says one thing and it is not the argument**: three boards are in a six-board gate, the other three are in a yard at Coldcombe because there is no money for them, and he had that off a man with a wagon and a team of four who has hauled stone for eleven years and has never once been asked a question at a gate. Then he sits down on a bench and stays there. **A man of fifty-four with about seven hundred sheep comes four miles on his own legs, stands at the back in a room with no seat in it, says he has been wrong about the drain and is right about the common, and would rather the hundred knew both at once than either on its own, and gets into a cart himself.** Then the question. A man of about fifty-four asks it wrong — whether the stranger is going to be one of us — and the clerk refuses the wrong question, says the one that was put, **and about four people answer a different question in about four seconds and are told so in the open.** Nobody in that room answers it. **Then a number is written against the low common and it is two hundred and twenty feet: about nine hundred yards of usable edge, about twenty feet of frontage a tent measured with his own feet against two gate-posts, times eleven tents. It is arithmetically defensible and it is about a width of a common and not about people, and the clerk says out loud before he writes it that he is writing a true number he knows will be useless in the same breath.**

**567 — The first act of the new year, done to a woman rather than by her.** `clear` 67, the second day of the hundred and third week, a Wednesday. **A woman of twenty-eight with a daughter of six walks four miles over a plank bridge in about nine inches of water to ask for a number to be taken off the wall, and asks instead for fifty-eight to be written in the taking column of the low common's line**, with the day on it, and no name on it, because there is no column for a person and fifty-eight cannot go in a square. The clerk writes it in about nine seconds. **She then says in front of about thirty people that in four days about nine people will be able to say the true of that common and she will be one of the nine, and she will have got it off a number a man wrote on a wall and not off anything she has seen with her own eyes.** And she says that a sack under a churn is worse than a wall, and a wall is worse, and a woman of about seventy asked her at a pump what feet a common has. **Then a man of thirty-one says, in front of about thirty people, that he said a number out loud in the open on Friday to be useful and had a person be the worse for it, that it is not the first time he has done it, that he did it a good many hundreds of mornings ago in a market four hundred miles from here, to a woman who told him a number said out loud would be a thing her neighbours had by morning, that he has not stopped doing it since, and that there is no column anywhere in this county for the difference.** He does not say her name and about nine people look at him and about four look at the number.

**568 — A foot of water on a common that belongs to nobody, and two numbers added together.** `clear` 68, the third day of the hundred and third week, a Thursday, the tenth day from a Monday and the second day after the rising. **There is no hill in that country and the reason a man can be drowned four miles from where he can walk is not a hill: the water comes across the flat and the wind at the seventh hour pushes it over the top of a swell about eleven feet wide.** Two of eleven tents are still standing. Nobody is drowned. **Two numbers are said out loud on the plank bridge in front of about nine people and added together: ten days off the Monday and eight days off the Wednesday, and both of them come out at the same morning**, and one of the men who says them says in four seconds that he carried the wrong one forward and will not be the man who takes a number off a bridge. **The water came from the low meadow, about thirty-one acres, which had been under since Friday because the rate on eleven parcels was not paid for two years and the ditch that takes the meadow off the pasture has a foot of sallow in it — and not from the drain, which is a trench.** The four closes take a foot too and nobody in Withermere is standing at them. A man of about thirty-three stands in eleven inches of water on six acres that had a season on them the day before and nothing on them now. **A woman of twenty-eight walks four miles back over the bridge to ask for the number to be taken off the wall, and is refused, and is told the reason in four sentences: it is in the clerk's hand with the day on it, about thirty people have read it and been somewhere on the strength of it, and if he took it off on Wednesday then about nine people on Saturday would believe a stranger who had written it off as a mistake.**

**569 — The market, four sentences, and a paper under an oak bar.** `clear` 69, the fourth day of the hundred and third week, a Friday, and it is the market day, and the first one held in three days. **About three hundred people come onto a green with a foot of standing water on the low side of it, and about nine stones have been moved up out of the water and about nine have not, and nobody had said the standing water out loud.** A man of thirty-one has written three hundred words with nothing at the top of them and reads **four sentences** of it to about thirty people: the water was not the fault of any person in this hundred; eleven households lost eleven seasons in one purse in four days and nobody has broken a rule; a hundred of about thirty men is a room and not a district; and a number on a wall about a piece of ground that belongs to nobody is not a number of people. **He then says out loud that a notice on a wall is not a covenant, a covenant is not a remedy, and a remedy nobody was asked about is not a settlement — and that the same four sentences in the weigh-house would have been worth about nine repetitions and about four listeners, and at this step they are worth about nine repetitions and about thirty listeners, and no form in this county says which of those two places is the right place to say a thing.** A woman of about thirty-eight with a barrow says nine words in the middle of the market that she said at a gate thirteen days ago and does not know anybody ever heard them. **The paper goes under the oak bar, where anybody may read it, take it away and write on the back of it, and there is no form in the county of Kell that says a person may not.** About nine of about three hundred people could repeat it afterwards.

**570 — About thirty names in chalk, a name refused, and five days given instead.** `clear` 70, the fifth day of the hundred and third week, a Saturday. **A boy of fourteen writes the week's sheet in chalk and has put about thirty names on it that nobody asked for, and a stroke beside the ones that are not of this hundred, and he has known for two days what the stroke means: they came.** A remedy of three hundred words lies under the oak bar and is announced in the clerk's hand with the day on it. **A man of thirty-one asks a woman of forty-one to put her name at the top of a paper with nothing at the top of it, and is refused in about four seconds in front of about nine people**, and the reason is that a paper with a name at the top of it in a town where about nine people read that wall every morning is a paper nine people can be argued with, and she would rather somebody argued with a figure than with a woman of forty-one who lost six acres in a market. **And then she gives him a day instead of a name: the ninth day from the rising, five days off, the third day of the hundred and fourth week, a Thursday.** She says what she will have done by then, and refuses him a second time, and he then says out loud in front of about nine people the question he came to ask, which is whether there is a thing in this hundred that can be asked of anybody at all. She tells him he has just asked it and about four of them would have said it if he had not, and he says he had sixteen days to find a way of saying it that nobody could repeat and has not got one. It is repeated by about nine people before the end of the day and nobody answers it. **Closing object: a harvest coming on ground a man of fifty-four has been in season on for six years, and a man who has not worked out yet what the question is.**

**523 — The finding is put in front of the two people it is about, and one of them says she knew.** `clear` 23, the seventh and last day of the ninety-sixth week, and it names the morrow. The recordwright sends for both of them on the Sunday night, separately, in about nine words each, and reads the four sentences out on a Monday. **Edith Crow, sixty-eight, is told first, at her own door, in a porch about four feet deep with no bench in it, and says she knew, and says that the woman who keeps the book could have come up her row in four minutes and did not.** She asks for one thing and it is that the finding go in with the day she was told on it and not the day it was made, and gives the reason out loud before anybody asks her anything: a day of a week comes round and a count begun in another county does not, and four days of a man not knowing is worth having on a page. **Then she says no — to two thirds of a finding about her own objection — because she had four days to think about it and had not heard the question until two minutes ago, and says in the same breath that she is not going to say the other thing now and have it be the second time.** That goes in too, in the ordinary hand, with nothing ruled and nothing headed. Harl Wenlock, forty-four, is told in the meeting-house porch with a man of about fifty, says the finding is right, and says out loud that **he had spent about nine seconds being glad of a thing he had not been told**, and that there is no form that says a man may be asked what he felt about a thing he was not told of; the recordwright does not enter the nine seconds and says that this makes the twelfth case in nineteen years in which she has been the one who has not asked, and that the twelfth is a man of forty-four and not a widow. **And a man of thirty-one says out loud, in the square, that wanting a thing to be right is the whole of what he has been doing for a hundred and twenty-three days, and that it has not once in a hundred and twenty-three days made a person safer.** Closing: a finding with the day a woman was told on it. `523`

**524 — A copy of a copy comes down the lane and it says a name, and a man of thirty-one says the sentence about a door out loud in a room in a town he is not of.** `clear` 24, the first day of the ninety-seventh week, and it names the day before as the last of the ninety-sixth. A carter who keeps a cart between Coldcombe and the toll bar comes down four miles of lane with a bundle on the tail-board and gives it to a woman of sixty-one at the corner by the meeting-house door, because she is the only person anybody has heard of who writes to other towns; a woman of about thirty at a stall in the town two days off put it into his hands and said it was for the woman in the barn at Kiln Row. It is a copy of a copy: a generation further on again, in a quicker hand, about nine mistakes and none of them corrected. **At the foot of the third page, where the first copy has nothing at all, there are four lines that are not on the copy underneath it, and the fourth says the person was asked by a man of thirty-four of Bramblefold and not by the woman, and about four people heard the question and about nine heard the answer. Closing: a copy of a copy with a name in a third hand. `524`

**525 — Two accounts of one morning go side by side and they do not fit, and about four of nine can say exactly where.** `clear` 25, a Wednesday, which is the day a man of about seventy-two walks two miles to hear one line read, and he comes at the ninth hour in a wet coat in a dry week and stands at the end of the bench and is told *same as ever* and says *aye*, and asks again to go and look at the stake and is refused for the fourth time. **526 — THE MIDPOINT. A man of about seventy-two is asked one question in a room with the door open, and he says that he does not remember.** `clear` 26, a Thursday, which is the market, and about two hundred people are in the square and hear none of it. The asking was arranged by a woman of sixty-one in a barn at Kiln Row in about nine sentences and she says plainly that there is no form in the county of Aldwick that says a person may be asked what they remember, that she has been told no four times in four years and not told anything at all once, and that she is not going to ask him and is going to bed. **The recordwright says out loud, before she asks it, that she will not ask him what the question was and will not ask him what he said, and the reason is that a man of about seventy-two asked to remember a thing in front of about nine people is a different room from the one this town has been working in for a fortnight.** He came up the lane at about the half hour after the first on a Tuesday-shaped errand on a Thursday, and nobody told him why, and she says out loud that that is wrong. About nine people are in the chancel and he is given a chair. **He says he was in a porch here at about the ninth hour on that morning and was asked nothing, and then he says THAT HE DOES NOT REMEMBER, and asks that it be written down that he said that and not that he said he was not asked.** A man near the door says twice that the page is wrong and is told by a man of seventy-two that a page is a thing in a hand, that a name on a page is a thing somebody wrote and may be right or wrong, and that if his not-remembering is turned into a page being wrong then in a hundred years there will be a person in that town who was told that his name was taken off a thing and never was. **He then says out loud that he is not a thing that is got back, that he has come up a lane on a Thursday nobody asked him to come up, and that the man of thirty-one had made a page into an instrument and had made it into a man in a chair.** He is entered at the back of the book and the entry says the name stands and is neither withdrawn nor confirmed and that there is no column in that book for a person who does not remember and none has been ruled. And the recordwright says out loud that this town remembers a Tuesday a hundred and forty-one years back with four words on it and no order, and that the other town remembers a Wednesday fifty days back with a woman's letter in it and about two hundred people who did not sleep, and that both are correct and neither is about the other, and that there is no instrument in the county of Aldwick that can hold two correct accounts of the same morning. **Nobody thanked anybody and nobody was relieved.** Closing: a name that stands, and no instrument that can hold two. `526`

**527 — Two accounts go round one town in the same mouths, and a woman of thirty-four says nobody has ever asked her what she remembers.** `clear` 27, a Friday. The four words and the four sentences are going round the same town in the same mouths and about nine people can give you both and about nine can give you only the one they heard first, and **the number of people who can give you both is the number of people who can be asked about the other one.** The sentence about a thing said in a room being a thing about nine people turns out to be three people's: a weaver of sixty-two said it to the man of thirty-one in two rooms over a joiner's shop on the fifty-ninth day after the division, a woman of sixty-one has said it thirty-one times in four years in nine towns, and a letter of four lines from a town four hundred miles off says it too and adds that the people there think the people here ought to be asked whether they want it. **She reads that letter out in a porch, to about nine people, and nobody asks her where it is from, and it also says that they do not know what to do with a person who says no, that they have not found out in about nine years, and that there has not been a week of those nine years without one.** She says out loud that a person who has been asked nothing is the one person in that town who knows what it is like. **And then the recordwright, in a smithy, in front of about nine people, says that she is the only person in that county who can be sent for about either account, that she has been that for nineteen years, and that nobody has ever asked her what she remembers, and that there is no form that says a person may be asked what they remember, and that in about nine rooms in a hundred and twenty-seven days she would have said something and has not said it, and that there is a difference between a thing you will say when somebody asks and a thing you say because you have not been asked in nineteen years.** He asks. She says stand there and be quiet, and that is the only thing she is asking anybody for, and he stands there for about a minute. A man of eighty-one says from the chair, unasked, that a thing a person remembers is not a copy and is not a copy of a copy, and that he has been that person twice in this town and has said nothing both times. **528 — Two accounts are read out in full in a square and the square decides nothing, and about five of nine cannot remember which they heard first.** `clear` 28, a Saturday, and it is not a market, and the chapter says so in its third line. **529 — A man of fifty-four rides nine miles and makes a return that records two answers on the back of a form, and signs the back and not the front.** `clear` 29, a Sunday. **530 — A boy of fourteen is asked a question in a smithy that nobody in that smithy can answer, and a third column is ruled and not headed.** `clear` 30, the seventh and last day of the ninety-seventh week, and it names the morrow as the first of the ninety-eighth. Closing: two chalk lines, a boy's name under both of them, a dead man in the ground column, and a town that has not had a dated thing to count from since a leaf was struck a hundred and forty-one years back in a week that not one person in that county can name. `530`

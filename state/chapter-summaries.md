**611 — THE SHAPE THE TOWN CARRYING IS WRONG IN ONE WAY, AND NAMING IT DOES NOT PUT IT RIGHT.** `clear` 111, the fourth day of the hundred and ninth week, a Friday, and his fifty-seventh day in the county. Eleven days is not long and a thing has gone round a town of about eleven hundred people and about four miles of cart road in both directions, and it has not gone as a sentence. It has gone as a shape, and the shape is ten dead men, and a woman of forty called Wenna Callow names it and says it is two dead men and two dead women, one gone up the water about eleven years ago, six that have not come, and one at the fair. A man of thirty-one gets to the second hour and puts the shape himself: he said ten dead men on a bank before he knew the town had it, and had said it about four times on that deck in ten days, and every one of the first four was his, and a woman of about sixty-eight tells him it is not his and tells him not to go taking it. Her mother is Sefa Callow, sixty-eight, the fourth chair from the after end, and she went to the fair at a crossroads four miles off, which comes twice a year, on the Wednesday morning, and that is two days. A man of sixty-one called Nevin Trask comes up the plank with the wrong book on purpose and counts: ten standing up, two dead, one away, six not come, one at the fair. And a second count appears, and it is nine, and it is a count of people this town has not been able to ask and not a count of chairs, and both are true and he has kept the penny a head for eleven years and has never had to know which a number meant.

**612 — A SECOND MOUTH REFUSES THE INSTRUMENT, AND IT IS NOT THE SAME REFUSAL, AND A NAME IS BEING TOLD IN A HOUSE.** `clear` 112, the fifth day of the hundred and ninth week, a Saturday. A woman of sixty-eight comes up the plank with what she has not sold and walks eleven feet of it without stopping and sits in the fourth chair, and the count comes down from ten to nine while she does it, and about four people on that deck know why and about nine do not. Then a man of thirty-one goes down to the low side of the landing to ask a woman of seventy-one a question that is not about a chair, which is how many people have been through her house since Monday, and he says the words *I am not asking you for a chair* at the door before it is open a hand’s width, and she refuses him the instrument in four sentences anyway. Her reason is not the reason a woman of twenty-nine gave at `605`. Hers is that he has come down here to be refused, and that a person who has not asked for a thing is the only kind of person who cannot be made to say yes to it. And then it turns out she has held that house for a man who is not in it and has not been in it for nine years and is not dead, for eleven years, and was never asked once, and there is no column in that county for what she gave up, and she asked him not to put it in a book. He does not carry it about. And on the Thursday a man said a name, four words, in her front room at the middle of the afternoon, and nine people came through that door that day, and it is now a thing a man says in a room, which anybody who has been told it can say again.

**613 — NINE PEOPLE, NINE REASONS, AND A WORD THAT WAS NOT THEIRS.** `clear` 113, the sixth day of the hundred and ninth week, a Sunday. It is fair week and about nine of the people who would be on that road are selling on somebody else’s ground, and there are more people on the Gull than there have been on any day of the eleven. He asks nine people one at a time, in front of the other eight, why they come up a plank, and each says a true thing: the water and a child of nine, a mother in the fourth chair, the penny, two carts and a load in the wet, a quarter mile and twenty-two years of it, a chair got nine years ago and never regretted, a brother four days off, a bench, and a chair that would otherwise be one of the ten. About four have a reason about money and about nine have a reason about themselves and not one of the nine reasons is about any of the other eight. A man of thirty-one gives them a word for it in the open: coalition. A man of sixty-one says it is his word and not theirs, with the arithmetic, and a coalition is nine people who have agreed on a thing, and about four of them have not spoken since fair week last year. And the man of thirty-one says out loud in front of all nine of them that the word was his before it was theirs and that he said a thing in this county that was not true when he said it, and that the cost of asking nine people one at a time in front of the other eight is not the questions, it is the room.

**614 — A MAN SAYS YES TO A CHAIR FOR GROUND HE HAS NEVER WALKED, AND IT COSTS A MAN WHO IS NOT IN ONE.** `clear` 114, the seventh and last day of the hundred and ninth week, a Monday, and the town sits. A man of fifty-four called Barnaby Crove, who has held the gravel at the head of the drain for nine years, says yes to a second chair, for the plank, in the open, and tells the deck he has not walked that quarter mile in about nine years and does not know what is on it. He says one thing in it, about gravel going down at about four inches a year and about nine where a man has cut a ditch, and not one word about the quarter mile, and about nine people in that town have it as being about the road by the Monday. A man of thirty-one says the word right out loud, and it is the fourth time in fourteen days. A man of sixty-eight called Pell Vearn, who has kept that quarter mile for twenty-two years, says what it cost, and it is not the man of fifty-four’s, and it is that he cannot say so, because he is not in a chair and there is no form in that county that lets a man ask to be corrected in one. And a man of sixty-one does the arithmetic out loud, with the working: the gravel is eight hundred and sixty-four pence and the whole landing for a year takes a thousand and forty, and the difference is not anybody’s fault, and he says it as arithmetic and not as a comfort.

**615 — FOUR DAYS, NINE PEOPLE, NOBODY PAID, AND NO FORM THAT SAYS THEY MAY BE.** `clear` 115, the first day of the hundred and tenth week, a Tuesday, and the last day of fair week. About thirty feet of withy hurdles are laid edge to edge at the head of the plank, pegged at the four corners and every third hurdle, with a barrow and a half of gravel out of the head of the drain trodden in and sand out of a barrel in the two low places. It took four days and nine people and nobody was paid, and there is no form in the county of Kell that says they may be. The gravel was the near party’s and the man of fifty-four gave it on the Saturday and said nothing until the Monday. A man of sixty-eight and a man of fifty-four argue for about nine minutes about whether the sand goes in the middle of the hurdles or on the top, both are right, and they settle it by splitting the difference. A man of thirty-one counts it out loud, in eleven sentences, with the working, and a woman of forty-one called Ibb Marshe stops him after the ninth and says the cost: a bed and a crust is two pence a day, thirty-six days of work is seventy-two pence, which is six shillings, which is a little under half a year of the woman who keeps the chairs, who is paid a hundred and sixty pence and keeps a book every day of it. And the cost is not the six shillings. The cost is that a count of nine people and four days cannot tell a gift from a day’s work, and no column in that county can. A man of thirty-one says out loud how much money he has and is told no about a sum.

**616 — A WOMAN TAKES HER YES BACK IN THE OPEN AND IT FAILS IN THE OPEN, AND IT IS NOBODY’S FAULT AND IS NOT SETTLED.** `clear` 116, the second day of the hundred and tenth week, a Wednesday. Sefa Callow came back on the Saturday and sat in the fourth chair and the count came down to nine, and about four people knew why and about nine did not, and the woman who keeps the book was not on the plank and was not told until the Wednesday. Then a woman of twenty-nine stands up out of the reed in the open, in about nine seconds, and says she is taking it back, and nobody on that deck could stop her and nobody in that town could either, and the count comes back up to ten. A man of thirty-one asks for a column for a reason, and a woman of twenty-six who has been in the ash for nine years says no in about nine seconds and then says the better half: a column for a reason is a column for a reason, and the woman who stands up has not got one that will be believed in about four years, and about eight of the people who have been in that book could not say why. And a woman of forty says out loud, in the same breath, that nobody is at fault and that it is not settled.

**617 — A SHEET OF DEAL, TWENTY-NINE LINES, TWO COPIES, AND ONE NAME NOBODY CAN BE CHECKED AGAINST.** `clear` 117, the third day of the hundred and tenth week, a Thursday. A man of sixty-eight who cannot correct himself in a chair gives about nine people a rule: write it down, in two places, where about nine people can read both, and if they agree the thing is true, and if they do not agree then nobody is in the chair who is not in it. A man of thirty-one writes both halves and a boy of eleven copies one on a board on a barrel, because there is no other table in Hask. One copy is nailed inside the after thwart of the Gull and one goes to a landing nine miles up the water, and they are read against each other once a week in the open. The twenty-nine lines are read out loud with the ground said before the name. Ten have a name against them and nobody in them. A man of sixty-eight asks for one more line, for the last quarter mile and the plank, because the plank has no chair and the plank is why nine of them came. And the last line is a man called Wren Sayer, gone up the water about eleven years ago, the brother of the man who held the reed, and he is the only one of the twenty-nine that nobody in that town can be checked against, and a man of thirty-eight says out loud that he is the only one of the four in that weigh-house who is not nine miles up this water, and asks to be left alone about it for a day.

**618 — A SECOND SPLIT, INSIDE THE NINE, AND A WOMAN WHO STOPPED COMING, AND IT IS NEITHER A BETRAYAL NOR A MISUNDERSTANDING.** `clear` 118, the fourth day of the hundred and tenth week, a Friday. The split is not the split from the sixth day. This one is between about nine people who think a count is a fact and about four who think a count is a thing that happens to you, and it is about a day written against a name: a woman of forty asks for one, a woman of forty-one with two carts says that if the day went on a sheet she would have to say what the load was in the same morning, and the man of fifty-four would have had to say his barrow and a half on the Saturday instead of the Monday. A man of sixty-one says no to the penny in the open and gives the true reason, which is that he does not want a room, and about four people in that town now know that the man who takes the money would rather nobody counted it, and he said so himself before anybody else could. A man of thirty-one says his own half, that the penny should go on the sheet, and says he would rather be told no than have not said it. A woman of forty-one does not come up the plank, nobody sends for her, and the man of thirty-one says out loud that he is not going to go and ask her why, because if he does there will be a form in that county that says a person does not come up a plank because a stranger came to the door about it, and he put her on it. A woman of thirty-four comes down the water at the second hour with a man of forty-one from a town two days off.

**619 — A WOMAN COMES NINE MILES TO BE ASKED, AND WHAT SHE ASKS FOR IS NEITHER A CHAIR NOR OUT OF ONE, AND A STRANGER SAYS A NAME NINETEEN YEARS OVERDUE.** `clear` 119, the fifth day of the hundred and tenth week, a Saturday. A woman of thirty-four called Dorcas Slee, who has been in a chair since she was nineteen and has wanted to be asked for about eleven years whether she wanted it, comes up the plank and asks for the boat. About nine people in that town have been talking in front of her about the one thing she came about for two days without knowing. She says out loud that she is not asking for the chair to be got out of, that she is asking to be asked, and that saying no is the only way out and it is a one-way door, and that she found out in a kitchen from a woman of seventy-one who was never asked either that in this town a person can get out of a chair by saying no out loud, and that it has taken her fifteen years and nine miles to find that out. A man of forty-one called Heston Brake says the thing about the year out loud a second time, in about nine people’s hearing, and is not sorry about a chair he put his own name against and has not taken off. A man of thirty-eight says his brother’s name out loud for the first time in nineteen years, to her and not to the man of thirty-one, and does not know whether saying it was a thing he was doing for his brother or a thing he was doing for himself. And a man of thirty-one walks up a plank to ask the woman who keeps the book to write a line for a woman from another county, and is stopped on the plank by the woman from another county, who tells him that asking her the question is the whole of it, and he says out loud in front of about nine people that he is not going to.

**620 — A QUESTION ASKED ONCE ON A SUNDAY, ABOUT WHETHER A NO HAS TO BE WRITTEN DOWN THE WAY A YES IS, AND NOBODY ANSWERS IT, AND THE WOMAN WHO COULD IS FORTY FEET OFF.** `clear` 120, the sixth day of the hundred and tenth week, a Sunday, and there is no sitting and the chairs are stood up along the starboard side with nobody in any of them. The woman who keeps the book has not come up that plank since the sixth day of the hundred and ninth week, and that is a week. A woman of thirty-four asks, out loud, once, in about nine people’s hearing, whether the saying of no in the open has to be written down the way the saying of yes is written down, and if it is not, who decided that, and when, and on what day, and out loud in front of about nine people the way everything in that town is done. Nobody in that town answered it. A man of thirty-one says somebody ought to go and get the woman who keeps the book, and she is forty feet off at a landing, and nobody went. A woman of thirty-four says not on a Sunday, not out of a stranger’s mouth, and not on the day a boat is going up the water, and that if he asks her on a Tuesday she will stand there and be asked it herself, and then there are two of them and one can be sent for. And the count is said out loud again with the days in it: eleven, ten, nine from the Saturday, ten since the Wednesday, and it is ten, and one of the ten is standing nine feet off the boat with a knife in her apron. And the man of thirty-one says he will be on that deck on the Tuesday, because the town sits on a Monday and a Thursday and he has been there sixteen days and has not once sat in one.

**601 — THE FIRST PLACE, AND IT IS A SET OF CHAIRS, AND A MAN WHO CANNOT LEAVE IS STANDING IN IT.** `clear` 101, the first day of the hundred and eighth week, a Tuesday, and his forty-seventh day in the county of Kell. A town of about eleven hundred people called Hask, nine miles down the water from a weigh-house, on a cart road that is under water for six weeks of the year, and the last quarter mile of it is an eleven-foot plank. A lighter called the Gull, about thirty feet, lying against the bank, with twenty-nine chairs stood up along her side in two rows and nobody in any of them, and one of them mended with a withy by a woman of twenty-nine in her own time. A woman of forty-four called Orla Fennimore keeps the book, has kept it nineteen years, and is the only person anybody in that town can be sent for about a chair. A man of thirty-eight called Garrin Tolley poled the boat, cut the reed since he was nine, and said out loud that a chair is a person who says something for ground he is not standing on. Eleven of the twenty-nine have a name against them and nobody in them: two dead, one away, eight people who have not come, and the book will not write the word for that. He came it in two days with a bad leg, paid four shillings for a cart at a crossroads to a man who did not ask his name, and has a pound and fifteen shillings and eightpence of under two pounds. He could not leave the county and he was standing in that room, and about nine people within nine feet of the fact had not been told it. He is given a true thing he cannot pass on: the woman who keeps the book has sat in one of the empty chairs twice and got out of it both times and written nothing down either time, and there is no day anywhere in that book against those two afternoons, and she asked him not to say it out loud.

**602 — THE FIRST CONCRETE CASE OUT OF THE SUCCESSION DISPUTE, AND IT IS NOT A REFUSAL.** `clear` 102, the second day of the hundred and eighth week, a Wednesday, and his forty-eighth day. Oriel Sayer, forty-nine when he stopped, held the reed and has been dead since before the last flood came up, and nobody wrote the day down because there is no form in that town that asks anybody to write a day down. No wife, no children. His people are in a yard at Sallowcross four days off and there is a brother there, Wren Sayer, gone up the water in a lighter about eleven years ago and not heard of since, with a chair at the other end of the same book. A woman of seventy-one called Eliza Lark, who keeps a house on the low side of the landing that is not his and has not been for eleven years and who is not of his family, came up the plank with wet sleeves and said out loud in about nine people's hearing that this is not the right of refusal: a refusal is a person saying no, and a person who cannot be asked is a smaller thing and an uglier thing and is not anybody's no, and she will not put her no on his line either. There are three ways a chair gets filled and no fourth: a son, the man who does the work, and anybody who says yes in front of about nine people, which has happened about nine times in nineteen years and twice by somebody who was not a son. And he is told in the same hour that he is the only person in that room nobody can be sent for, and he says nothing about what that is. The keeper of the book prices her own year out loud with the working: a penny a head and a penny a cart, about eight hundred heads and about two hundred and forty carts, a thousand and forty pence, the book takes three pounds, what is left is three hundred and twenty pence, and a hundred and sixty of that is hers, which is thirteen shillings and fourpence, against a bed and bread of two pence a day and seven hundred and thirty pence in three hundred and sixty-five days, which is three pounds and tenpence.

**603 — SOMEBODY SAYS YES IN THE OPEN AND THE DAY GOES IN A BOOK AND THE WHOLE OF IT BINDS NOBODY.** `clear` 103, the third day of the hundred and eighth week, a Thursday, and his forty-ninth day. A woman of twenty-nine called Nell Prince, who cuts withies above the landing, comes up the plank with a hoop over her arm and says yes to the reed in about nine seconds, and gives her reason in a mouth: because there is nobody holding the reed and the cutting of it is nobody's business, and she is twenty-nine and is not going anywhere in a hurry, and that is the only reason she has offered. He does not ask her whether she wants it, and she tells him so in about four seconds, and asks for it to be written down somewhere that is not his book. He then asks the question nobody has asked in nineteen years, which is who in a town of about eleven hundred people has not heard what was said on that deck, and is refused a figure in a mouth: two hundred and ninety-one is a figure off a market nine miles up the water and does not travel, and she will not invent a third number out of a number that belongs to another place, because a book with that figure in it is a book that is about counting instead of about people. A man of thirty-eight then says out loud that a lighter has no door, no wall and no roof, and that about four of them were on the deck that morning and about nine were on the bank, and that every one of the nine heard a name said that they had not been given. And the count is eleven in the morning and ten in the book, and the count is spoken.

**604 — THE SECOND MOUTH, THE SAME TEN CHAIRS, AND A DIFFERENT SHAPE, AND NOBODY SETTLES IT.** `clear` 104, the fourth day of the hundred and eighth week, a Friday. There is no market in Hask and no reading and no hundred; a fair at a crossroads four miles off twice a year, and it is not fair week. The woman of twenty-nine comes back with a second version of the same case, days after her yes, and says the ten is not ten things, it is one thing, and that the trouble is that a yes in the open will put a person in a chair and a no in the open will put a person out of one, and there is nothing whatever to stop it and no form in that county that says a man may not be put out of a chair, and the reason there is no form is that in two hundred and thirty years it has never once been done in the open. She proposes to strike all ten in one morning on that deck and count the objections, and about nine people in the room have the first shape of the case and about nine have the second, and the two nines have not asked each other. He tries to hold both in one hand and is told by her that they are not one question with more of it. She counts twenty-nine chairs twice with her hand in about nine seconds and stops at the ninth both times, and the ninth and tenth are the ones she cuts withies off the land of, and she is in neither and did not know that on Wednesday.

**605 — THE CHAPTER THE BAND EXISTS FOR: HE OFFERS TO BE THE INSTRUMENT AND AN ORDINARY PERSON REFUSES HIM FOR A REASON ABOUT HIM.** `clear` 105, the fifth day of the hundred and eighth week, a Saturday, and his fifty-first day. There is no reading in Hask on a Saturday. He offers in nine sentences, with the working, to sit in all ten chairs, and gives his reason in a mouth: a thing that cannot be sent for is the only instrument nobody can be leaned on, and he has offered in nine counties and about four people in each of them said yes. The woman of twenty-nine refuses him in four sentences, and says first that she is not refusing him because he is a stranger, and then gives the reason, and the reason is about him and not about the case: every one of those ten needs a person who can be sent for, and he is the only person on that deck who cannot be, and in about four years somebody in that town will be stood up on that plank against his name and there will be nobody to stand up, and a person who cannot be asked is not a clean thing to put in a book, because a book is a list of people who can be found and she has been for nineteen years on thirteen shillings and fourpence. Then the standing cost comes out of his own mouth, because it is the only figure about him anybody in that town can be checked against, and it turns out to be the truest thing anybody said about him there. At about the sixth hour a man of thirty-eight gives him a boat-hook, five feet of ash with an iron crook polished on one side only, because he is standing on a deck with his hands doing nothing, and he holds it and does not put it down.

**606 — THE ANCHORING IS TESTED BY SOMEBODY WHO IS NOT HIM, AND IT IS NOT A POWER.** `clear` 106, the sixth day of the hundred and eighth week, a Sunday, and his fifty-second day. The Gull is brought up onto the gravel because the water has fallen about a foot, and there is a green oak post at the top of the landing with about eleven years of notches in it. A man of thirty-eight sets a test and says out loud before he sets it that he is testing him and that about four people knew he would and about nine did not. He asks what the water is doing at a block of limestone nine miles up the river, and the man of thirty-one answers in about four seconds without looking at anything: a thumbnail's width under a cut, standing, not over it since before the light on the Friday, and about a quarter of an inch is about a day of falling. He then says the second half out loud, that he has been counting from that water since a Thursday morning in a room over a stable, that he did not choose it and cannot stop it, that it is one water and not any other, and that there is no figure for it and nobody can check it. Two women say out loud, in different words, that a thing that can be asked about goes in a book, and that it is not a power and anybody who says it is a power is wrong about it in a way that will cost somebody, and the second half of it is worse than the first. And she puts the cost where it can be seen: there is a lighter drawing her after end in about two inches a minute and he has not seen it, and he has been asked about a river nine miles off and answered in four seconds, and he has lost a thing happening four feet in front of him for about a quarter of an hour and cannot be told when it is happening. And a notch is cut in the post, and the man of thirty-eight says he would rather have the post, because a post is here on the Tuesday and he is not.

**607 — THE FIRST TIME SOMEBODY ASKS HIM WHERE HE CAME FROM, AND HE ANSWERS IN FOUR CHECKABLE THINGS AND NONE OF THEM IS AN ANSWER.** `clear` 107, the seventh and last day of the hundred and eighth week, a Monday, and his fifty-third day. At about the fourth hour with the lamp lit and about nine people on the deck putting the Gull back into the reach, a man of thirty-eight asks him, without any preparation, and says he has been not asking since Saturday. He answers in four things and says in advance that he will not be able to answer it properly: the cart and the pound and the day; the year against a block of limestone nine miles up the water and the not knowing; the counting from that one water; and a settlement of about nine hundred words on a wall that he put four things into, and that he is the only person alive who has been told that a thing was said in that room in about nine people's hearing on a Monday morning and nobody in that room could answer what had been asked of it. He says two of the four are about a stone and two are about a thing he did nine days ago, and that none of them is the answer. Then the woman of twenty-nine says out loud that nobody in that room asked the question, that she was holding the book and did not stop him and a man of forty-four did not stop him, that there was no vote and no method and no third way of getting a thing said in the open, and that this is not a trick and she is not blaming him. It goes out into a bank of about nine people who have a face lit from below in the dark and not the words.

**608 — THE SPLIT, AND IT IS NOT A BETRAYAL AND NOT A MISUNDERSTANDING AND IT IS NOT SETTLED BY A SPEECH.** `clear` 108, the first day of the hundred and ninth week, a Tuesday, and his fifty-fourth day. The woman of forty-four who keeps the book does not come up the plank, and about four people on that landing notice and about nine do not. A man of thirty-eight then says out loud that he was in a weigh-house nine miles up the water on the last Monday of a week with a load of boards that never came, and that a thing that was not a person said four words in the air and the four words were its own name, that about four of the people in that room heard it and about nine heard nothing at all and thought for about nine seconds that it was the wind in a crane-beam, and that he is the only one of those four in Hask and nine miles up the water from the other three. He has been six days late in saying it, and says it now because a thing he heard in that room has been in that town since Monday night. Then the woman who keeps the book says why she is not coming, from a second boat alongside, and her reason is a book: a book is a list of people who can be found, and if she comes up that plank then the one thing that town can be sent for is a woman who stands in a place with about nine people behind her, and in about four years there will be a line in a book with her name at the top of it, and she has spent nineteen years being the other half of that line for other people. She then says the true thing about the split, and nobody in that room can answer it: the split is not between two opinions, it is between people who were on that deck and people who were not, and every one of the first is right about their half and every one of the second is right about theirs, and there is no way of putting them in one room again without one of the two being asked something twice. He says one sentence in his own defence and it goes to a bank, and there is nothing to add to it.

**609 — A DOCUMENT THAT BINDS NOBODY IS QUOTED BACK AT HIM BY A PERSON WHO HAS ACTUALLY READ IT.** `clear` 109, the second day of the hundred and ninth week, a Wednesday, and his fifty-fifth day. A man of forty-one called Heston Brake, of a town two days off in the county of Ashenfield, comes up the water in the Gull with eleven hundredweight of iron in her, gets off at the landing, does not go up into the town, and asks to sit on the deck for an hour. He says he stood under a lamp on the north wall of a weigh-house nine miles down this water on a Saturday morning about six weeks ago with a load of boards that never came, and that a boy of about fourteen held a slate under it with about nine people under the lamp and about four in the street, and read two men long of parchment and then read a thing off the front of it that was about nine hundred words, and that he did not understand one word of it and has been carrying it for six weeks, and that he went back on purpose about four weeks ago and had it read at him a second time. He quotes the line about the year back at the man who put four things into the settlement, and uses the settlement's own statement that no person in the county of Kell is bound by one word of it to say that there is therefore nothing in that document which stops him putting his own name against a chair in his own town in another county and never coming back to take it off. He asks whether he may leave it there, and the man of thirty-one says he did not write the line, and that is not a correction, and everybody works that out in about four seconds and nobody says so for about nine minutes, and then he says he cannot put the sentence right without saying more, and will not say more. A man of forty-one is then asked what the boy looked like, and says fourteen and thin, and that he turned the slate over at the end of every run of the small figures so he could see what was coming, and that he counted about nine of them himself while the boy read and they were all the same size and none of them meant anything. The one System panel of the band is here and is four sentences and is a record of a citation and not a choice.

**610 — THE BAND CLOSES WITH A PERSON STILL DOING A THING, A CASE STILL OPEN, A FIGURE IN A MOUTH, AND A QUESTION ASKED ONCE AND NOT ANSWERED.** `clear` 110, the third day of the hundred and ninth week, a Thursday, and his fifty-sixth day.

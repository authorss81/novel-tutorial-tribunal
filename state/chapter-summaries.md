# Chapter Summaries

**One paragraph per chapter for the band in progress. This block supersedes everything below it.** **Chapters 1–450 are canon and Volume 09 is closed. Volume 10, *The House That Refused*, is open; 451–470 are canon.** Earlier bands are one line each; chapters are the record.

## VOLUME 10, CHAPTERS 461 TO 470 — *the out-of-roll book and the man who would use it*

**461 — Eighteen dashes and one blank, on a counter, in the rain.** The band opens on the thing nobody is looking at: **the miller stopped credit to one woman and not to the other eighteen, and the eighteen are fine because the rate roll and the water list are two books and do not agree and are not supposed to.** The roll counts households and is a year old before anybody has finished arguing with it; the water list goes by tenement because water goes to a building and not to a name; **the Fen is on the water list as nineteen tenements rated as property, in respect of water, at the old rate of the tenement and not of the person.** Vernay keeps a second book — about nine hundred names and a mark against each, a cross, a dash, or a blank — **written four times in four years, never shown to anybody**, and the Fen is a page of its own: **eighteen dashes and one blank, done in one go on the Saturday after last with a pen that went blunt twice, and under the blank the word *Harrow*.** **The band's one panel** at `461:79`–`461:89`: a household that is not on the roll is not a household that has gone away, it is a household that was never counted, and the counting is done by a man who counts houses. He chooses nothing and tells nobody. Ilyan asks, in nine seconds, in a shop with a woman and two men buying nails, for the book to be copied into the press of the Stone House inside a day. **Vernay refuses and says the better reason: *you want it in your book, not mine* — and then, at the counter, with his hand still on the book: *you have got it in your mouth and it dies with you.*** `461`

**462 — Nine men asked a question they had never been asked, and a day put on it.** The water board walks four hundred yards of lane into a room over a taproom — about nine of the row there, about twenty from the town on a stair the width of one man — **and the chair says out loud, before anything else, that he has brought nine men down a lane to be asked a question he could answer in nine seconds in his own kitchen.** Ordwin Cape asks for the out-of-roll book. **Sallow says yes in four words**, then refuses to write the line, and gives nine sentences that are not about the water: **the year before last he was the man who came in with a thing, a rating at Ostrey, entered in his own hand with the day on it, and about two years on that man has paid everything else and has still not paid his rate, and the minute of this board is the only thing this town has ever used against anybody.** Every way of making it small is a way of making it somebody else's: a thing of the clerk (**refused: if the line is in the press the clerk is the officer of this board for it, and there is no form that says so, and in four years the steward will say she did it on her own**), a thing of the autumn roll, a thing done in a room in the town instead of a room over a taproom. **And a stranger cannot be entered: not in the roll, not in the book, not in the minute, not in the ward list.** He tries the one shape that would work — *write it as the board doing a thing it may do at any time* — and Sallow says that is the best argument anybody has made to him in nine years and the exact argument he made to himself on the Friday, and the one he was afraid of. **What Sallow writes is not the line. It is four lines: the board has been asked, the board has not answered, and the board will consider it on the twenty-fifth day after the division. Nine days off, and he says out loud that he wrote it to be a courtesy and that on the ninth day somebody is going to come and use him, and he does not answer when a stranger asks him which of the two it is.** `462`

**463 — A straight edge and a nail in the rain at the eleventh hour, and a fact on the wrong side of a leaf.** The sluice is wound twice a day whether the gates are shut or open, and on the third night of the rain a woman of thirty-eight with a bad hip and a man of sixty-one are on the head of it with a rule laid across it — **a rule a man of the county has carried three miles, which is not a thing a man of the county carries, and he says he would like to be asked a question and nobody asks him one.** A nail head in the joint is **three-eighths of an inch proud of the timber it was driven into nine years ago, and the timber has gone down under it**, and the north side has dropped that much and the south side has not moved, because the south is bedded on stone and the north on wood in the Fen. **Two years is a season, and he will not un-say a number in front of twenty people to say so.** The clerk of the House is paid two pounds a year and does not want it, the board does not take paper from the county, and nobody can get a thing into a minute — **and when Ilyan asks her to have it entered she says no in front of about twenty people, and gives her rule: *a thing said in a square belongs to whoever is standing in the square*, and she has watched three of her own sentences go out of that square without her mouth on them.** The man of the county gives her the other half: **a lane is a room with the roof off, and the reason a thing said in a lane is yours is not that nobody stands in it, it is that nobody stands in it who can be asked about it afterwards, and that is a difference about the book and not about the lane.** The fact goes **on the back of the statement of the ninth day — the four hundred and ten words with the fourth paragraph struck out and signed across — written in a lane at midnight with a blanket over his coat, and attested as to the measuring by a woman who is not a household.** **In four hundred years of that building nobody had ever read the back of anything.** `463`

**464 — Four days of rain, and a town that has finished with a subject.** The market is on a Tuesday and about a fifth of it stood in the rain, and the carts that could not get into the square came on the Friday, so **there was a market in Halloway on a Friday for the fourth time in nine years.** The nine days of buckets have become **not a hardship but an argument about mud**, and the lane above the sluice is the worst four hundred yards in the county. Nothing happens to him at all, which is new: a man with a bundle of wool asks him to hold it, and then says the other thing — **that he is not a household of here, and if he were he would be in a ward and be one of the forty-one, and there is not a thing in this town he could not ask anybody about, and he cannot, so he is a man I would have to carry my wool with and swear for.** He goes round the market for four hours asking three questions and gets nothing. **Coll Sarn, 22, has never been asked what he is and has had an answer ready since he was fourteen.** Mab Harrow comes out of the rain to tell him that **the row has begun to say the word *stranger* about him, and that a stranger is not a word a town gives a man it has had an event with — it is a word a town gives a man it was always going to have nothing to do with** — and that the row has been doing to him with its mouth what the miller did to her with a book, and it has not cost anybody a penny, and she does not know which of the two she minds. Then Sefa Lund asks him to read the fourth clause to a woman of eighty-one in the second house who has not been out since the water came over the Fen nine years ago. **He says no, and the reason is a language one and not a standing one:** if he reads *common to all households of this town* in a kitchen, there is nobody in the kitchen who can be asked afterwards whether he read it right, and he could not be asked about it himself, **and every time he has said that sentence in three rooms in twelve days he has enjoyed saying it, and it is the one thing he owns.** Both are true and neither is the whole of it. `464`

**465 — Nine to nothing in about eleven seconds, four of the nine saying the same three sentences, and then a man goes down a lane.** The board sits on a Saturday, **six days before the day it wrote down**, and the chair says out loud, before he says anything else, that nobody in the room asked for this question. The three sentences come from a man of fifty-four who has a lot on the Four Hundred and was the second of the three against the scouring: **we did not lose the water, we sold it, and we were paid; we are asked now to carry nineteen houses in a roll we did not make; and the fenrow have not asked** — and the third is the one that cannot be argued with, because if it goes the other two go with it inside a year and this room is not a water board, it is nine men deciding what a row of nineteen are. **Nine against, none for, unanimous, and nobody surprised.** Then the chair does the thing, and he is wrong, and he is the only man in the room not entitled to be: **he has not been able to sleep on the third line of the House's minute — *the declining is at the request of the persons concerned, and the persons concerned are not households of this town, have no standing in this House, and are not before it* — and the only way he has found of not being the man who comes in with a thing is to make the row say it themselves.** Ilyan has four hundred and thirty words ready in ninety seconds and Sallow gives them back on the bottom stair: *you have written a thing the row will say no to, in words so careful that it will take a man of thirty-four half a morning to find the one that is wrong.* The row says no for an hour — Sefa in four sentences, Cullen Skay in eleven words, Mab Harrow with her reason — **and then Ordwin Cape, sixty-one, eleven shillings a year, says yes**, and cannot hand the shillings over because there is no form that lets him, cannot pay because a household on the roll can be charged, and cannot be in the room where the minute is written, and asks for it to be in a book before he is sixty-two. **A girl of nineteen writes it and Wenna Crale enters it: five lines, in the minute of a water board, on a Saturday evening, of nineteen houses that are not of the town.** `465`

**466 — A roll corrected in four minutes, and a corrected roll is a corrected constituency.** Sunday morning. The steward takes out a form he has had in a drawer for forty years and told a stranger about in a market square nine days earlier and not opened: **a roll may be corrected in respect of a household entered as land, and the correction is the steward's and not the board's, and the reason and the day and the hand shall be entered.** He writes four lines in about four minutes: **the nineteen tenements at the Fen, entered in this year's roll as land of the common and not as households, are households of this town from this day.** The fund goes from **£34 14s to £35 6s 8d** and about forty people work that out for themselves by the tenth hour. Then the seat arithmetic: **nineteen households in nine wards is about two a ward, so the House has about two more seats** — and Wenna Crale says no, because **the number of households in a ward has never been the number of seats in it, there is no form that says a ward has a number of seats, and the sheet of forty-one names has said five for the ninth ward for longer than anybody can account for.** **The sentence the volume turns on:** *a corrected roll is a corrected constituency. Not a corrected count. A constituency. And a constituency is the whole of who is in the room where the minute is written. And a body of forty-one men elected for a year by the households of nine wards is not a number, it is a set of doors.* — and **nothing should be done about it this morning, because the fifth head of the ninth ward is not elected until the autumn.** The steward comes out of his office and admits, in a square, that he has not slept on it. Ilyan says the true sentence out loud, in nine seconds, to about a hundred people, and about forty of them repeat it wrongly by the evening. `466`

**467 — Nineteen doors, twenty-two households, and eleven other rows that are land with people asleep behind them.** Monday, the seventh day of a wet week, the lane the worst four hundred yards in the county. **Mab Harrow has been knocking since the sixth hour asking one question at each door — who sleeps there — because the roll was corrected on Sunday morning and it says there are nineteen households, and she rents a room in one of them and is not in the roll, and she said in a lane on Saturday that there are nineteen of us.** Ilyan offers to take the names and is refused: *a man who is not of this town comes round nineteen doors with a bill, then nineteen doors have been asked by a stranger and not by the row.* He holds the bill for an hour and eleven minutes and says nothing. **The fifteenth door is the second house and it is two: Wat Priddy, about thirty-four, has paid Iven Cape a shilling a week in coin for the back room for two years, and she is her own person and is on no roll, and there is a form in this county for a woman being her own person and he has not seen it.** **He says twenty, having been to six doors.** **The fourth house is three** — Ordwin Cape, Sefa Lund, and Mab Harrow, and not one of the three is the other's. **Twenty-two.** Sefa Lund refuses to let the corrected number go on the paper, and gives her rule as the reason, and is wrong: *a thing said in a lane is mine, and a thing said in a square belongs to whoever is standing in the square, and I will not hand this row two numbers about itself to a market in one week* — and then says out loud, in a lane, with eleven people in it, that she said nineteen on Saturday and was wrong, and that the reason she was wrong in a lane is that she would not say it in a square. Mab Harrow settles it with the miller's nine blanks: **eight of them are rows in this town entered as land with people asleep in them, and the ninth is a man called Pyle Garraw who died two years ago and whose four shillings have been carried ever since.** Three more rows are found off the west wall. **The roll is wrong by 112 pence a year, the number of households in this town is a figure that has been found rather than a count, and about two hundred people stand in a square at the eighth hour and nobody is glad.** `467`

**468 — A letter goes four hundred miles with a witness line in it, and the answer is in a hand he asked her not to use.** He reads the letter from the top for the first time since he wrote it and gets to the middle of it, **where there are two paragraphs that are not about the ninth clause: one asking her to write her answer in the small upright hand, on the ground that a hand is a thing a person can be asked about, and one, crossed out and written again, in which he tells her he has signed a return in respect of a defect in a town of ninety thousand people, has not sent it, and there is a figure in the fourth line of its margin that is the only one of its kind in ninety thousand people.** He notices, in the room over the nail shop, **that nobody says the thing this town says ten times a day, because there is nobody in the room to say it to.** The carrier takes it to the counter at Ostrey, which puts a day on it and a place, and says he has never in thirty-one years sent a letter out of that town that anybody came back for. **There was no version of that letter without the witness line, and the whole of what he had built in sixteen days came down to whether that one person existed.** Four hundred miles off, in a room with nine hundred books in it, **Sera Quill does not go to the press, and the reason is not the letter, it is the shelf: the refusals and the returns are in two ranges eleven inches apart, and a refusal is entered by the name of the person who witnessed it, and a name in that range is a name a clerk will see has been looked at.** She writes in a day book of her own that she has not answered his question. **Then she answers him in the round hand of the counters, which he asked her not to use, and signs it with that hand and not with her own name** — and she is right: every exit she has read in six years is a form, a form has a day on it, a form has to be in before another day, and the days between the two are the days in which a person who needed to leave was still there; **a steward opened a drawer he had had for forty years, a man of sixty-one asked in a room, and the roll was corrected in four minutes by nine words, and that is the whole of the machinery; a right you have to knock on nineteen doors for is a favour.** `468`

**469 — A man who was due back on a Thursday, a day early, with a bill at nine pounds and a page twenty-two miles off.** Wednesday, the twenty-third day after the division. The steward lets a man of the county into the Stone House at eight in the morning and says out loud, before the door is finished opening, that in about four years the only thing anybody will remember is that he did it. **Garrick Nye was due back on the Thursday and has come on the Wednesday, and says why: a page in a coat is a thing, and he carried it here instead of going home.** The bill is the back of a form of query he answered two years ago, and the county's return on the other side of it is **eleven lines: the frame of oak reported at sixty years, the north heel sounded to a depth of nine inches, the timber gone at the bottom and not to be relied upon, to be re-bedded on stone on the north side, and a new heel at the county figures, nine pounds.** Ordwin Cape does the sum out loud, **corrects himself twice in front of a man from the county** — the fourteen pounds of the scouring was on the lots and not out of the water — and gets to it: **the board's money is the quarter's, £8 16s 8d, and the heel is £9, and the nineteen houses the roll was corrected on Sunday bring in twelve shillings and eightpence a year between them.** Then the page, after eleven minutes and a speech about the right order, and a certified copy with the printed head of **the office of the county surveyor at Kerby, twenty-two miles off, on a road, where any person in this county may walk in and ask for that page** — and which **can do one single thing about the frame except be asked.** And the thing the chapter is for, in his own mouth: **he had the man from the county in front of him twice and asked him for a minute both times, and never once asked him what the county had, because asking a man with a county what his county has is asking him to be a second thing as well as the first.** `469`

**470 — The second of ten things this band did not fix, and a man of thirty-one eleven miles up a road with his own form in his hand.** Thursday, the twenty-fourth day after the division, and a board of nine in a room over a saddler's shop with about twenty people in the square outside. **A request of four lines, made on the Wednesday afternoon after the page came out of Kerby and entered in the minute by a copyist who was not asked to enter it:** mend the north heel at nine pounds, the cost to be levied on the eighty lots in the same manner as the fourteen pounds of the scouring, the work to be done before the water rises. **Sefa Lund asks the four words that kill it — *ask him where the fourteen pounds went*** — because the fourteen pounds was charged in the roll of this year, and the roll of this year was made in the week the common was sold, and **there is no form in the county of Ostrey that lets anybody put a cost on a roll that has been made.** **And Ordwin Cape says the second line of it is the line he copied**, out of the minute of this board's own sitting, because it is the only form of a levy he has ever seen in this town. The board declines, and **the chair says the refusal in the words of the request, on purpose**, and then says the half that is not in them: the frame is on a list, it is the third thing on it, the other two are the cleaning of the tail of the race and a second winding bar, and all three have been on that list every year for nine years. He has a sheet with **ten things this town has had in front of it in three weeks and put down again**, and will not read them out with the door shut, and gives the sheet to the copyist for the press; he names the first — the ninth clause, and that nobody in this town has ever been given it and nobody is going to be given it this year — and the second, which is the north heel. **The out-of-roll book was not written in and never was offered to anybody but one woman in a lane. The twenty-fifth day after the division is tomorrow and there is nothing set down on it.** The roll stands corrected at 1,060 and is wrong by 112 pence a year. A fact about the north heel is on the wrong side of a leaf. **The four letters and a stroke have not been read by anybody and they were on a road.** And then he does it himself: three lines at the foot of the margin of a return that is thirty-two days old — **his two hundred and twentieth morning, written at Halloway, in the county of Ostrey, which is not upon a road of the Shelf** — and eleven miles on a bad leg to a counter at Ostrey, and a clerk of about thirty who asks him the only question a counter asks, and he says **enter no person**, and she writes *no answer* in a column and shuts the book. `470`

## VOLUME 10, CHAPTERS 451 TO 460 — *the town, the board, and the first refusal*

(one line each; the full paragraphs for this band are in git at `df269b3`)

**451 — The ask, the want said out loud, and the going. Wenna Crale, 34, copyist, eleven years, gives her name before anybody asks and asks who the hand on the ninth clause is; it is a hundred and thirty-eight years old. He says out loud that he wants a town where his name is not on anything and that he is not going to get it, then gives away the fact that Halloway is not on a road of the Shelf. He refuses her. She goes home. `451`**

**452 — Nine miles of milestone with the county letter on one side and nothing on the other, and a notice read out in a square by a stranger nobody asked. Forty-one people in the wet, nineteen houses, a girl of nineteen with a roll under her coat. Amos Trill, steward forty years, is the man who asks him; he refuses for the girl, out loud, and says reading a notice is what makes a man somebody’s man. `452`**

**453 — The board comes down twice a year, by two people, in a hurry, with the fire lit, and the audit on the Monday the common was signed away found no common and did not bring it down. Four people can read the copy; two ever read the Book and one is nine years dead. He asks to be one of the two hands and is refused with the best reason anybody gives him in nine weeks. She gives him Hessa Marl’s answer: a book is kept as long as the town is feeling like it. `453`**

**454 — The Fen is on the roll as land and not as households. He says 1,041 + 19 = 1,060 in a market to about two hundred people and has to take it apart the same day; the true figure is somewhere between nineteen and twenty-three and the number is the protection. The steward names the out-of-roll book, a one-line power nobody has ever claimed, and says why he has not used it. `454`**

**455 — A room, a witness and somebody to write it down. He writes four hundred and ten words and a woman of thirty-eight strikes the fourth paragraph out in her own hand and signs across the strike. Garrick Nye is right about the race and the frame. Mab Harrow, from a doorway, asks who it is for and gets three answers. £14 out of £34 14s, two fifths, and the county does not pay for water. `455`**

**456 — The Book comes off the beam for about two hours, clause four is read out, the vote is six to three and the three are named, and Wenna Crale turns the board over unasked. The band’s one panel. Clause nine, a hundred and thirty-eight years old, with a witness line carrying two names and one of them four letters and a stroke. He says out loud that it is a right of refusal taken out of the law, and the word *unverified* goes into the minute. `456`**

**457 — The gates shut for nine days and the thumb goes into the north heel. Sallow offers the out-of-roll book, is refused in a lane, and admits he made an offer that was no offer. Ilyan offers to carry the objection as one document and Sefa Lund tells him what a witness is, in the words he said himself in another city. The roll for next year is made in the autumn, and he will not put that in a minute. `457`**

**458 — Water in pails for four days, four hundred yards, twice a day. Ordwin Cape is thirty-nine pence short. Ott Vernay stops credit to Mab Harrow in a shop with nine people in it and says why, and Ilyan offers to pay and is refused: *you want it in your book, not mine.* Wenna Crale refuses his unsent number and tells him to send it to a book that has a head on it. `458`**

**459 — The back of the fourth leaf: a strike by order upon a paper not of this town, and a woman of twenty-four writing that she could not learn whether the striking was of that clause alone. Clause nine is struck and clause four is not, and the word in clause four is *household*, and that is the word that shuts the door. Ilyan is right, loudly, and it does not help. The steward enters both margins. `459`**

**460 — The House sits on forty-one stools with the door open and declines to enter the nineteen tenements, in the fenrow’s own name, in about nine seconds, and the minute is correct and self-contradictory in the same sentence. Sefa Lund says out loud that she did not agree to not have a household. Ilyan says he has no standing and says nothing. The gates come off five days early and he writes a letter he does not send. `460`**

# Chapter Summaries

**One paragraph per chapter for the closing band. This block supersedes everything below it.** **Chapters 1–680 are canon. THIRTEEN VOLUMES ARE CLOSED AND THE FOURTEENTH IS OPEN AND IS WRITTEN TO CHAPTER 680. VOLUME 14, *The World Below the Loom*, IS WRITTEN TO CHAPTER 680 AND ITS THIRD BAND IS 671–680 AND IT CARRIES THE VOLUME'S MIDPOINT AT `677` AND `678`.**

**671 — THE PLAINEST QUESTION ANYBODY PUTS IN THAT COUNTY GETS AN ANSWER NOBODY WANTED, AND A MAN OF FORTY-THREE GIVES A FIGURE FOR THE FIFTH TIME AND SAYS OUT LOUD THAT HE HAS GOT IT WRONG BY A DAY.** The first day of the hundred and eighteenth week, a Tuesday, his four hundred and twenty-first morning, his one hundred and seventeenth day. **A man of forty-three gives the reading and the pair of figures — the bank empties in about three days and fills in about four, and he counts from one morning to the next and not from a morning to itself — and then owns his own standing saying out loud: the next time those steps can be walked is about four days after that bank is full, it was full on the Saturday, four days is the Wednesday, the water came off on the Tuesday night, that is a day early, and he would like a man of sixty-one to say so on that bank rather than him, because a figure a man of forty-three has got wrong five times ought to be got wrong by somebody who keeps a book. A man of sixty-one says he will write that one down and gives the reason: there is a difference between a figure a man gets wrong and a figure about a person.** **A man of thirty-one puts the whole of a question in one sentence and says he is not going to dress it up: what has to be done on a bank on a Monday by people who are alive, not by the nine and not by a coalition and not by a person anybody can be sent for about.** **And a man of forty-one answers it in two sentences: on the Monday morning the bank was full and there were about nine of them on it and nobody walked down anything, and that is what has to be done on a bank on a Monday this week, and about four of them did not know that until a man of thirty-one said it on a Tuesday.** He adds that a load of stone went up that track on the Tuesday and not on the Monday, that he told the bank on the Friday that it was one day late and it is one day late, that he does not know whose Monday it was, that he said so on the Friday and said it again on the Tuesday and did not look in between, and that there is no form in that county that says a man may be sent for about a Monday. A boy of thirteen gives his own count at eighteen days and says he has got a figure now and does not know what a figure of eighteen days is for. A man of forty-three counts the man in the ash at forty-one and the woman in the reed at forty-two and says he walks past both of those mouths about four times a day for nineteen years and has never once stood at either, and that it is not modesty, it is that there is a thing about a man in an ash and he does not have a figure for it.

**672 — THE DESCENT GOES PAST THE LAST OF THE BEAMS AND PAST THE PLACE WHERE THE WATER STOPS AND FINDS NINE OPENINGS IN A WALL, AND A MAN OF THIRTY-ONE SAYS A FOURTH NAME AND IS STOPPED BY A MAN WHO CANNOT READ.** The second day of the hundred and eighteenth week, a Wednesday, his four hundred and twenty-second morning, his one hundred and eighteenth day. A man of forty-one bails for about two hours and says the other half out loud: about two hours is enough for nine people to stand in and is not a figure of those nine steps. **The man of thirty-one goes past the place about nine feet past the last of the beams and past that place as well, which nobody had done, and the passage goes on dry at the same fall for about a hundred and thirty feet, given two ways and neither rounded: about sixty-five paces of a man of thirty-eight at about two feet a pace, sixty-five times two being a hundred and thirty; and about fourteen of the nine-foot grooves a man of forty-one pushes a stone along, which is fourteen and he is not going to round it. **The two figures do not agree and nobody on that bank made them agree, and the man of thirty-eight says it is a figure of his pace and not of the passage and that he would like about four people to have two figures that do not agree rather than one that has been rounded.**** Then a step up, then a wall, and nine openings with the same two hands cut over every one of them and no bar and no numeral. **A man of sixty-six says the guild mark is a different mark for the third time and gives the reason: a guild mark has a bar under it and a numeral on the bar, and a mark with no numeral on it is a mark a person puts on a thing they have to come back to.** They are counted twice, by a man of thirty-eight going along and a man of fifty-four going back, and both got nine, and neither has a hand that can write it down. **A man of thirty-one says a fourth name, in one sentence, in a passage with a lamp in it, and a man of fifty-four who cannot read stops him and says the count of him stopping a man of thirty-one is four in four days and that he would like about four people to have that figure instead of the name, and that a row is a thing you put things into and he has not seen anybody put anything into one of them and has not been in one.** A woman of forty-nine counts the days in the county at one hundred and eighteen and says she counts days because she counts loaves, and that she counted nine openings going along and nine coming back and neither of those is a number she would put on a door, because she does not know what a copy is and does not know what you would copy.

**673 — THEY GO INTO THE NINTH OF NINE OPENINGS AND FIND THAT THE KEEPING IN IT HAS BEEN ONE PERSON’S FOR NINETEEN YEARS AND WAS NEVER ANYBODY’S JOB, AND A MAN OF FORTY-ONE SAYS SO OUT LOUD AND NOBODY TELLS HIM HE IS RIGHT.** The third day of the hundred and eighteenth week, a Thursday, his four hundred and twenty-third morning, his one hundred and nineteenth day. **The ninth room is about as long as two men and about as wide as one, with a hook cut in the roof about the height of a man’s hand, a shelf cut in the wall about the height of a hand which is empty, and above the shelf a groove about nine inches long with a notch in the end of it. A man of thirty-eight says a notch is a count, that he has thirty-two notches cut in the edge of a board he carries in a coat and nobody has ever asked him what the thirty-two is for, and that he counted them again in a room with nine people in it and got the same number.** **And over eight of the nine doors it is two hands and over the ninth it is one hand, and a woman of thirty-eight says that one is not a smaller version of two and that if it is then about eight of those nine doors is a door that gets opened once instead of twice and she does not know what is on the other side of any of them.** **A man of forty-one then says, in ten sentences, standing in a doorway about as wide as himself, that he has been in and out of that ninth door for nineteen years, twice a day when the mill was working and once when it was not, and that it is not his work, and that nobody asked him, and that there is no form in this county that says a man may be asked to do a thing twice a day for nineteen years and no form that says he may not, and that the reason he is saying it is not that he wants a day counted for it — he has not wanted one once in nineteen years — and that he is saying it because it is somebody’s and it has been somebody’s this whole time and he did not know whose.** **A man of thirty-one tries to make it into a list and is stopped in seven sentences by a woman of thirty-eight: nine openings is not the nine jobs and do not put it in the same line, the nine jobs are how we get there and this is what is on the other end of it.** A man of sixty-six is asked what one hand is for and gives the same four things and then says the fifth thing, which is that a hole is a thing a person comes back to and he will not be the man who tells you.

**674 — NOBODY GOES DOWN BECAUSE A BANK IS AT ABOUT ELEVEN INCHES, AND A BAKER FOUR MILES UP A CART ROAD IS TOLD WHAT IT HAS COST HER AND WAS NEVER ASKED.** The fourth day of the hundred and eighteenth week, a Friday, his four hundred and twenty-fourth morning, his one hundred and twentieth day. A man of forty-three gives the reading and says he is glad about a day off and is not going to pretend he is not, and that there is no form in that county that says a man may not be glad about a day off. **A man of forty-one says out loud that he takes a cart up that road four miles to a village and has taken bread up it since he was about twenty-two and did not know until that morning that the bread on it was hers, and that on the Wednesday when he went into four feet of water from about the fifth hour to about the ninth hour her cart was standing at the head of that track with him not on it.** A boy of thirteen gives the days in the county at one hundred and twenty and says the nine have gone down five times since the Tuesday of last week, which is nine days, and that four miles twice a day is four hours and there is a third four miles on two of the mornings and six hours out of eight is three-quarters of a working day, and that it is a figure of his legs and not of a man of thirteen. **Hester Ollerton is forty-four and keeps a bakehouse four miles up that cart road and about nine houses in that village take her bread, and about sixty loaves in a night and about forty of them go up that track, an hour and a half up with the cart loaded and an hour and a quarter down, which is two hours and three quarters of a cart for one morning of bread, and on the mornings a man of forty-one is in four feet of water his two carts stand at the head of that track from about the fourth hour and mine is the one that stands there.** About nine houses get it a day late and about four of the nine are people who cannot do a day without it, and one of those four is a man of eighty-one who does not eat on a Thursday unless it is there in the morning. **She asks for it to be said that she was not asked and that she would have said yes to a man coming to her door, and that saying yes to being told is not the same as being asked, and that nobody in that county has the second one and she has wanted it for about nineteen years.** A man of forty-one says he will leave the carts at the head of the track and that it is about ten hours in ten days of his cart and not of her bread, and that he is not going to be thanked on that bank and not going to be forgiven on it either because there is nothing to forgive him for, and that if a person wants him sorry they can come and find him on a Wednesday. **And she says, true and not kind, that he will do it twice and then stop, and that by then he will have a reason and it will not be her.** A man of thirty-one says what he got wrong in about eleven sentences: a list read aloud once is a list nobody can be sent for about, and that is also the reason a baker is a day late on five mornings in ten and nobody in that county could have told her on the Wednesday.

**675 — THE BANK IS FULL AND NOBODY GOES DOWN, AND AN ORDINARY WOMAN WHO KEEPS A BAKEHOUSE SAYS THE WORD OUT LOUD FOR THE FIRST TIME IN THAT COUNTY, AND IT COSTS HER THE ONLY THING SHE CANNOT CHECK.** The fifth day of the hundred and eighteenth week, a Saturday, his four hundred and twenty-fifth morning, his one hundred and twenty-first day, and the fever fifty-six weeks old. A man of forty-three gives the reading and says nobody is going down today and that there is no form in that county that says a person may be sent for about a day nobody can be sent for about, and that he is not going to make one to be able to go down on a Saturday. **In the middle of an argument about a road, a woman of forty-four says that nine doors and nine jobs have all been talked about as though it were a house somebody built and left, and that it is not a house and nobody built it and there is nothing in it to be left — it is a rack. Six boards across and four shelves. Sixty wet trays on at about the eleventh hour at night and sixty off at about the fourth hour in the morning. The trays must not touch or they go to slime. There is no nail and no glue and no heat in that rack. It is six boards and a gap. It works while somebody is putting them on and taking them off. It has never once worked on its own in nineteen years and would not last a morning. And then she says the word, once, in a plain voice, and says she is not going to improve on it.** Nothing came out of that bank for about four seconds and nothing whatever happened. **A man of sixty-six says the word is older than he is, that he is not going to say it again and not going to say where he got it and not going to be the man who says it a second time on a bank, and asks for it to be noticed that a man of thirty-eight carried a lamp down about ninety steps four times last week and could have said it in the dark to about nine people and did not.** **And a man of fifty-four who cannot read says the part that was his in four sentences: she cannot read either, there is no form in that county that says a person may be asked what they said, and he knows exactly what happens next and it is that somebody with a hand and a book writes it down — and two people who cannot read are the only two people anybody can be sent for about in that county who can be sent for about what they said, and neither of them will ever know what is written down.** And a man of thirty-one says in the first sentence that it has not gone to him and is not his, and that this is the second time in eight days a person has handed a name to a bank in that county, and both times it went to a person who was not asked and not a keeper, and that this one did not go by accident.

**676 — A BANK CAME OFF THE TOP STEP IN ONE NIGHT AND A MAN OF FORTY-THREE HAS NO FIGURE FOR IT, AND A MAN OF THIRTY-ONE IS TOLD IN ABOUT NINE SENTENCES AT THE HEAD OF THE FLIGHT THAT HE IS NOT THE INSTRUMENT OF THIS EITHER.** The sixth day of the hundred and eighteenth week, a Sunday, his four hundred and twenty-sixth morning, his one hundred and twenty-second day. **A man of forty-three gives the reading and says out loud twice that he has no figure for a night in which about twelve inches of a bank came off, and that a man of forty-three who invents a figure about a night in front of about nine people is a man who will be quoted.** A boy of thirteen and a man of thirty-eight give their counts out loud with the subtractions, and the man of thirty-eight says he counts days in Thursdays. **A man of thirty-one offers to be the thing that is sent for a third time, in about nine words, at the head of the flight with his hands empty, and a man of about fifty-two who cannot see well refuses him in about nine sentences, out loud, without raising his voice, and says he is not going to give a new reason because the only reason he gives has never once worn out in eleven years: a thing that is sent for is a thing people are sent for about, there are nine doors down there and every one of them is kept by somebody nobody can be sent for about, you are the ninth of nine and the only one of the nine who has offered, and a man who offers four times in about nine days is a man who has started counting.** **He then says out loud that he has never been down that flight, that it is wet and about as tall as a man and he cannot see well, that he has not tried and is not going to, and that he is standing at the top of it on a Sunday morning because he has stood at the top of things for eleven years, and asks for it to be said that he said it first and that nobody is to make a figure of him out of it.** Nine go down and the man of forty-one bails for about two hours and a quarter. **The eighth opening is the same room and the same mark and he could not tell you which of the two he was in if somebody asked him, and the notch in it is deeper than the notch in the ninth, and a woman of thirty-eight says the shelf and the hook are both worn round the front and not the back, which is a person who takes things off and puts them on the near side standing facing the wall, and that this is about as much as the room is going to tell anybody.**

**677 — THE MIDPOINT. A LAMP IS BURNING ON A HOOK IN A ROOM NOBODY LIT, A STONE ON THE WALL HAS NINE LINES ON IT IN NINE HANDS THAT CANNOT WRITE, AND A MAN OF ABOUT FIFTY-TWO WHO CANNOT SEE WELL SAYS THE WHOLE OF IT OUT LOUD AND THEN SAYS A NAME TO THE GROUND.** The seventh and last day of the hundred and eighteenth week, a Monday, his four hundred and twenty-seventh morning, his one hundred and twenty-third day. **A man of fifty-four who cannot read stops in the doorway of the ninth room and says one word nobody asked him for: that room is lighter than the passage is, he cannot see very far, he knows a lit room when he is stood in one, and he has been in two of those eight times in nine days and none of them were lit.** Then a man of thirty-eight puts his lamp on the floor and there are two lights in the room: there is a lamp on the hook, burning, about a quarter full of oil, and the flame is the size his gets at about the second hour and he knows that because he trims wicks and has for nineteen years, and there are two lamps on that bank and both are accounted for, and he is not going to say who lit it. **And on the wall at about the height of a man’s chest is a stone about the size of two hands laid flat with nine lines cut in it, one under another, counted twice by lamp, in nine different weathers with the first nearly gone and the ninth about four days old, and in nine hands that cannot write — and a man of fifty-four who cannot read says how he knows, and the reason is that a hand that cannot write cuts hard on the first stroke of a line and light on the last stroke of the same line, because the hand is guessing where the line ends, and they are nine different hands because the amount they guess by is nine different amounts.** **The finding is put on the bank in about nine sentences because that is the only thing anybody in that county can do with a thing, and then in about eleven more, and it is not a machine and it was not made by one man and it is nine jobs and eight of them want two hands and one of them wants one, and every one of them is kept by somebody who cannot be sent for about it, and the speaker says he is not saying it as a complaint.** **And a man of about fifty-two who cannot see well puts it together in fourteen sentences, out of the sentence he has given before: nine lines, nine weathers, nine hands that cannot write and every one of them a hand that has had to get it right by itself, and eight of those nine lines were cut on a morning when somebody could not find the two hands the lintel says are wanted. It is what nine people agreed to do, written down in a stone in the ninth room, on nine different mornings, for the mornings they are not there. It is not a person. It is not a machine. It has got no opinion, and nobody put a face on it this morning. And it is the only thing in four counties that anybody can be sent for about, because there is no form anywhere that says a person may be sent for about a line of nine that was cut by somebody who cannot write, and he is that, in two places, on two sheets of deal, for eleven years, and he has never once been asked what his line was for.** **He addresses it, flat, once, to the ground and not to anybody in the bank, and says he was saying it to the stone and that he is not going to shout it and is not going to say it again. Nothing whatever happened. And he says what it cost: there is no form in that county that says a person may be asked what they said to a stone, and no form that says a person may not.** And a man of thirty-one puts the two halves side by side and joins neither: the lamp is burning and nothing has failed in about four hundred years and he is not going to be the man who calls that the wrong part; the ninth line is about four days old and nobody knows what it says and it was cut on a morning somebody could not be found and he is not going to be the man who calls that the right part either. **And a woman of twenty-eight who has been standing about nine feet off checks the thing nobody has said, twice, in front of about nine people, and says she is not going to be the one who says it and that it is not because it is not true.**

**678 — A BEAM ABOUT NINE FEET LONG GOES ALONG ABOUT TWO INCHES AND COMES BACK WHILE THERE IS A PERSON ON THE STEPS AND DOES NOT GO AT ALL WHILE THERE IS A PERSON IN THE ROOM, AND NOBODY IN THAT GALLERY SAYS WHAT THAT MEANS.** The first day of the hundred and nineteenth week, a Tuesday, his four hundred and twenty-eighth morning, his one hundred and twenty-fourth day. **A man of forty-three gives the reading and says the pair of figures has not moved once in eleven mornings.** A woman of twenty-eight gives two counts out loud and says she keeps a day book and that a day book is a thing people lie to, and that she is going to go on doing it and nobody is going to thank her for it. **About four people in that gallery said the word about the beam out loud, the way they had said it on four mornings in about eleven days, and nobody argued with them. Then a man of thirty-eight stood up under the ninth beam with his lamp in his hand at the height of it, said he was going to be there about a quarter of an hour and would have to put it down about four times, and asked for the four to be counted and not the quarter of an hour because the quarter of an hour is his and the four are not.** And while he was up the ninety steps the beam did not move for about four minutes, and it went along about two inches and came back when he came down about nine steps at a time and was not quiet about it; and it did not move while a man of forty-one was up either; and when nobody went up at all, about nine people stood in that gallery and looked at it for about as long as it takes a man of thirty-eight to pole a quarter mile and it did not move. **A man of thirty-one said the one sentence he was going to say about it and then said out loud that he was not going to supply the other half of it, and that he had worked out the half of it that has two trials behind it and had not got one person in four counties entitled to hear the other half from him. And he said the one sentence he has had since the Monday of the hundred and fifteenth week, and it was not about the beam: he has spent one hundred and twenty-four days in this county counting what he knows and has never once counted what he does not know, and that is the only figure anybody has ever wanted out of him.** A man of thirty-eight puts his lamp down and says his shoulder is going to be no good for carrying a pole up that water on a Thursday and that is a figure of his shoulder and not of anybody else, and says he is not going to say what he was doing up there for four minutes. **A man of forty-one says two sentences: in nineteen years he has never once seen anything come out of that roof, not a stone and not a lump and not a piece of the wall at the far end; and he had assumed like about nine people standing in there that somebody was holding it up, and he has got to thinking about that on the Wednesday night and has not put it down and is not going to put it down in a passage with a lamp in it.** **A man of thirty-one says nobody in that county is to be forgiven for anything that happened the week before or that week, that he is including himself in it first, that being right is not a thing anybody is thanked for and not a thing anybody is forgiven for, and that he does not want anybody in that gallery to go up that bank and say that man was right.** **And a woman of thirty-eight says, in ten sentences, that none of it is a way for anybody to say no: a thing that a person wrote down on a morning they were not there does not give anybody a way to be told they are not wanted, it means that if a person stops the line is still there in the stone and nobody has to be told; and she turns a wheel once a day and if she stops the peg goes into a plain hole on the seventh morning and about four years from now nobody will ever know that chamber was a week, and she has already had that and is not having it again for a stone with nine lines in it.** And a man of sixty-one says there is no form that says a person may be asked whether they will still come and he is not going to cut one.

**679 — WHAT ABOUT NINE PEOPLE CAN HOLD IS ASKED AGAIN AND THE ANSWER HAS CHANGED, AND THE MAN WHO FOUND OUT SAYS OUT LOUD THAT HE IS NOT GOING TO BE A KEEPER OF ANY OF IT.** The second day of the hundred and nineteenth week, a Wednesday, his four hundred and twenty-ninth morning, his one hundred and twenty-fifth day. **Nobody goes down and a man of forty-one bails the flight anyway, and says why: if he does not, nobody can stand on the bottom nine this afternoon and there is nobody coming this afternoon, and a man bailing a flight nobody is going down is not doing a job for anybody, and he has been doing it on Wednesdays for about three weeks and did not do it on the Saturday because the bank was full and he was not going to stand in it.** A man of forty-three gives the reading and says he is going to stop saying the pair of figures, and why: a figure said out loud twelve times and not written down is a figure about nine people have and about four hundred miles off nobody has. **A woman of forty-four stops a man of thirty-one from saying his own question a third time and says out loud that he has not learned it, he has learned that a woman of forty-four will stop him, and asks about four people to be able to tell the difference. And he asks a different one and says he checked it twice: not who is holding it up. What can about nine people hold.** **The answer, in thirteen sentences, from the man who works: about nine people can hold about nine doors, one each, and that is not the nine jobs; and the answer is not no now, it is nine doors and nine jobs and about nine people and one each, and about four of those nine said they would not first and came anyway; and he has had the ninth one for nineteen years and did not know it was the ninth one, and he is not going to give it back and not going to be thanked for it, and he is the only one of these nine who can say what it costs and is not going to be asked.** A man of sixty-one counts his own book out loud: about nine hundred heads in eleven years and not one name in it of anybody who did anything in those eight doors, and that is eight doors and no names and a figure of a book and not of the doors. **And a man of about fifty-two who cannot see well says in ten sentences that he is not going to be a keeper of any of it — not the ninth line, not the lamps, not the man who goes and looks at the eight doors nobody has been in — and gives the reason: the day he is a keeper of this he is a thing that is sent for and he will have said so himself. And he asks for it to be noted that a man of sixty-one has a book and a man of thirty-eight has a lamp and a man of forty-one has a door, and none of the three can be sent for about the other two.** **And a man of thirty-one says the cost of it in the first sentence: about nine people now have to be told something, one at a time, by somebody, and he is not going to be the somebody for any of them, and it is not about nobody’s sake, and nobody is to forgive him for it.** A man of forty-one says he is going to ask about eight doors and not one, on foot, one at a time, and that it will take him about a week, and that he is not going to do it on a bank with nine people standing about. And a man of thirty-one gives the days in the county at one hundred and twenty-five and says he is not going to say a hearing is a number today.

**680 — THE BAND SAYS OUT LOUD WHETHER THERE ARE EIGHT OF THEM OR NINE AND DOES NOT SMOOTH IT, AND A MAN OF FORTY-ONE ADDS UP EIGHT MORNINGS OF HIS OWN STANDING IN WATER AND NAMES THE TWO THAT CARRY NO FIGURE, AND NOBODY IN FOUR COUNTIES HAS READ THE NINTH LINE.** The third day of the hundred and nineteenth week, a Thursday, his four hundred and thirtieth morning, his one hundred and twenty-sixth day. **A man of forty-three gives the reading for the last time in the band and says the other thing once, because it is the last time anybody has asked him about it in about nine days and because he would like it written nowhere at all: the next time those steps can be walked is about four days after that bank is full, the block of limestone is a different thing nine miles up that water, and there is still no figure in four counties for when it comes out.** A man of sixty-one gives three counts with the subtractions — one hundred and twenty-six days in the county, twenty-seven days since the boy of thirteen went down, fifty days in the ash and fifty-one in the reed and he is not going to add them — and says out loud that these are the last three he is going to do on a bank in about nine days and gives his reason before the numbers. **And he says there is no number for the hearing, that he gave the last of it on the Friday of the hundred and seventeenth week and stopped, that he has been asked twice this week whether he has started again and he has not, and that he is not going to start on a Thursday because it is the last morning of a week and a man of thirty-one would like it.** A man of forty-one adds up his own eight mornings out loud in six figures and gives the total at about twelve hours and a half, and says the two mornings with nothing in them are the Friday and the Saturday — nobody went down on either, the bank being at eleven inches over the top step and then full — and that he is not going to leave those two out of it, and says the convention out loud, that he counts from one morning to the next and not from a morning to itself, and that that is eight days, and then says what it is: a figure of a man’s legs in cold water and not of the drain and not of the ninth door, and he is not going to put it against his name in anybody’s book and there is nowhere to put it. **And a man of thirty-one says the number of them out loud in the plainest form he could find: it is eight, it has been eight for nine days, the name is still the nine because a man of sixty-one said so and nobody has changed it and nobody is going to, and he has been the ninth of the nine for nine days and he is nobody’s and there is no form in that county that says he is.** He names the four who said they would not first and does not give the reasons again, because a mouth is not a figure of anything, and says one of the four is him. A woman of forty-nine says she wants it said that she does not know what eight means, and that she knows what nine loaves is and what about nine houses is and that is all she has got. **Then the four things that had not moved are said out loud: the sheet of deal with a seal on it is still face down on a barrel nine miles down that water; nine words are in the heads of about nine people and he is not going to say them again in front of a bank; there is a stone about a hundred and thirty feet past the last of about nine beams with nine lines cut in it and the ninth was cut about nine days ago and nobody in four counties has read one word of it and there is no form in that county that says a person may be asked to read it out; and there is a lamp burning on a hook in the ninth of nine rooms about a quarter full of oil and nobody lit it and nobody is going to put it out.** And a man of thirty-one says he came into that county wanting a figure for something and has left it with nine of them and has not put a single one of them on a form, and that a woman of twenty-eight has handed him a copy of two numbers in her own hand and he has put it in a coat and he is not going to say what a coat is for.

## 6. The four things this repository has learned in thirteen volumes, that a writer needs before the first sentence

**THE HANDLING OF NUMBERS.** A name and a number in the same breath must be put back together. **THE HANDLING OF A REPAIR.** This repository has been wrong about its own repairs fifteen times and the seventh was wrong in the place a reader is told to trust most. The defence has three parts and **one of the three is not a gate at all**: re-open the file and grep the finding's own figure; check a figure in a state file back against the chapter it describes; and **read the closing lines of two consecutive chapters against each other, once per band, before the instrument runs.**

**THE HANDLING OF A DOCUMENT.** Twelve volumes have learned that a record is either locked in a cupboard and can be sent for about by nine people, or nailed to a wall in the open and can be rubbed out in about four seconds by anybody, and that **neither of those is a settlement and a settlement is neither of them.** Volume 12's settlement binds nobody. **Band 0001 made a third thing: a record that is twenty-nine chairs, where a person holds one by sitting in it and a chair with nobody in it cannot answer a question about a person. A document that binds nobody cannot answer who speaks for somebody who is dead, and a chair that nobody is in cannot either, and the difference between those two failures is the shape of the next three bands.** That tension is the volume. It is not a defect to be resolved and not a mystery to be opened.

## 7. What is on the ground at the end of `610`, in one paragraph, for a writer who has not read Chapters 601–610

A town of about eleven hundred people called Hask, in the county of Kell, nine miles down the water from a weigh-house, on a cart road that is under water for six weeks of the year, keeps its record in twenty-nine chairs stood up along the side of a lighter called the Gull, about thirty feet, with a pole under her fore thwart and a stone on it. A chair is named for the ground and not for the person. Eleven of the twenty-nine had a name against them and nobody in them on the first two days of that week and ten from the third, because a woman of twenty-nine said yes to the reed in the open air in about nine seconds and nobody asked her whether she wanted it, and she said so out loud and asked for it to be written down somewhere that is not his book. Of the ten, two are dead and one is away and seven are people who have not come, and the book will not write the word for that. A woman of forty-four keeps the book, has kept it nineteen years, is paid thirteen shillings and fourpence a year out of a landing that takes four pounds six shillings and eightpence, and is the only person anybody in that town can be sent for about a chair, and on the ninth day of that week she stopped coming up the plank because a thing had gone out into a bank of about nine people and she was the only person in that town who could be sent for, and she was right, and nobody in that room said a word that could have moved her, and she is not sorry and has not been forgiven. A man of thirty-eight has poled the boat since he was about nine, cut the reed since he was nine, set a test out loud on a Sunday, asked where a man came from on a Monday, and disclosed on a Tuesday that he was standing in a weigh-house nine miles off when a thing that was not a person said four words in the air and the four words were its own name, and he is the only one of about four who heard it who is in Hask. A woman of seventy-one keeps a house that is not hers and has not been for eleven years, said out loud that a person who cannot be asked is not a refusal, has not come up the plank since, and has not offered a second thing. A man of forty-one from a town two days off in another county has put his own name on a chair at the head of a drain and has not taken it off, and is the first person in twelve volumes to hold a document of Ilyan's up as a precedent after actually reading it, and Ilyan cannot put the sentence right without saying more. A man of thirty-one is in that town and cannot leave the county, and counts from one river every morning, and can tell anybody the state of that river to a quarter of an inch and cannot tell anybody when the water will go over the cut, and is nobody's, and has a boat-hook he has not put down, and a true thing he was told and cannot pass on, and a settlement of about nine hundred words that binds nobody and has now been used once.

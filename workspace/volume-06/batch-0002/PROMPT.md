## WHAT YOU ARE CONTINUING FROM, IN ONE PARAGRAPH

A village of about two hundred and ten people in thirty-eight houses called Coldharrow, in the Briar March, has a tower, a bell, a gate that is a post with a bar across it that is not shut, and a board on the tower wall that has been going thirty-one years in two hands and reads fifty thousand four hundred and thirty-nine, with a third hand in it in chalk belonging to a man of thirty-one who is not a person of the village and is not on anything and put no name to it. **The board counts rings and is not a count of anything, because nobody ever wrote down what any ring was for, and a day's figure is a WEIGHTED number — four bells plus a burial, or seven for a boundary, or two for a debt, or four for a market, or three for a stranger — and a weighted number cannot be turned back into what was done, and a March village can prove how many times and can prove before or after and cannot prove when.** The bell-keeper is Ottily Roke, forty-one, twenty-five years, ninepence a day for a hundred and forty days. The gate takes threepence a load at nine loads a day and three thousand seven hundred and eighty pence a year and does nothing, because there is no road for it to be the gate of. Eleven villages' whole margin is fifty-seven pounds fifteen shillings, and that is exactly the eleven bell-keepers' wages. A Bench date costs a pound three shillings and sixpence and fifteen days of the man who walks the road, and Nell Callow sells about forty a year for forty-seven pounds, and she cannot make a date for a boundary, because a man can walk one twice and the second time is the one a room counts. **A sealed copy of four lines about a river four hundred miles off is under the seat of a timber cart and is going to be under that seat on the day it goes. It was copied at Sallowby for threepence and carried by a sawyer of fifty-one who was given five shillings and four days and does not know what it is. Its second line says a schedule is not a river. Its third line says the height is eleven feet and no sill is named in it and a height with no sill named in it is a height above whatever the reader is standing on. Nobody in two hundred and ten people has done anything about it, and that is not inertia and not a decision anybody took, and there is no bell in this country anybody could have rung about it.**

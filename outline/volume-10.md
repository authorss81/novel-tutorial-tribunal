## 2. The place, and why it is here

**HALLOWAY.** A market town of about four thousand people on the river Hallow. A stone market square, a wool and hide market on a **Tuesday**, a mill with one wheel, a poor rate, a water board of nine, and **the House of Halloway — nine wards, one elected head of household in each, forty-one of them, elected for a year. The House meets in the Stone House, because the House meets in the winter and the Stone House is the only room in Halloway with a hearth in it, and the door is open, and about two hundred people cannot get in and stand in the square.**

**Halloway is not upon a road of the Shelf. It has no register, no counter, no appointed person, and no Bench of any kind; the nearest counter is eleven miles off at Ostrey and it has never had anything to do with this town.** That is the volume's first fact and the reason the volume exists. **The declaration of continuity of the eighty-sixth week governs every person entered in a register of the Shelf in every town upon a road of the Shelf, and Halloway is not upon a road of the Shelf, and therefore it is not governed by it and has never been protected by it either.** The whole of the last nine volumes arrives at a town that has never been touched by any of it, and the town has been managing on its own.

**The Stone House and the Hollow Book.** The House meets in a stone building with a hearth, because the House meets in the winter. Nailed high on the north beam is a board about four feet by two and a half, with a vellum on its face and a lead tag at the foot, and it is the oldest covenant in this part of the country. **The board is taken down twice a year, on the day of the audit and the day of the water sitting, by two people at a table, in a hurry, with the fire lit, because that is the only light in the building.**

**The Hollow Book, and what is on it.** One vellum, eleven clauses, in four hands over four hundred years, and the seals at the foot in a lead tag that has gone white. **Clause one: the common of the Four Hundred acres, upland, and of the Fen below the sluice, water meadow, is common to all households of this town.** Clause four: the water of the Hallow from the sluice downward is common to all households of this town. Clause nine, at the foot, in a fifth hand and a different ink: **no household of this town shall be required to take water, or to be entered, or to answer, against its will.**

**The removal.** In the week of `hall = 0` a commission of survey came, and the steward, Amos Trill, caused clause one to be struck, on the ground that the common had not been used in ninety years and a covenant nobody uses is not a covenant and binds nobody. **He was right.** The Four Hundred was divided into eighty lots of five acres and sold. The Fen floods and was not divided and lies. **The water went with the eighty lots, because the sale said the Four Hundred and the water of the Hallow from the sluice downward, and nobody wrote a line saying the water did not go with the land.**

**The minority: the Fenrow.** Nineteen houses along the Fen, below the sluice. They kept the sluice — it has to be wound twice a day and it takes two people and an hour — and it was common work and a common is not a service, so they have never been paid. **They were on the common, so they bought none of the eighty lots. They are the only people in Halloway who own nothing and are still in the town.** About twenty-three of them live there, so the number of households in that row is somewhere between nineteen and twenty-three and cannot be got.

**The out-of-roll book.** The water board may at any time enter a household that is not on the roll. It is one line, it takes a minute, and **it has never been written in in this town. The last entry anywhere in this county was a man at Ostrey with no roof on him, entered by that board four years back.** A one-line power nobody has ever claimed, held by a board whose chair has a reason for never using it and has said the reason out loud.

**The roll.** The rate roll of Halloway for the year is made in the autumn. This year's was made in the week of `hall = 0`, the week of the division. **On it, the nineteen houses of the Fenrow are entered as land of the common and not as households.** A common is not rated. The land changed and the roll did not follow it, because the roll is made once and the land is not.

**The money, with the working, and the working is the argument.** 1,041 rated households, unmoved in eleven years. 80 lots at £2 = **£160** in all. The water rate is **twopence a quarter** on 1,041 rated households = 1,041 × 8d = **8,328d = £34 14s** a year, and that is the whole fund. A sluice keeper would be **£6 a year, which is £6 of £34 14s and not nothing.** The scouring of the race is **£14**, and **£14 of £34 14s is two fifths**, and it is leviable on the eighty lots at **3½d a lot** (280d ÷ 80), charged in the roll of this year and not in next. Rating nineteen tenements would add 19 × 8d = **152d = 12s 8d** to the rate and would take the roll to 1,060 — **and the House of Halloway is not sixty seats. It is nine wards, one elected head of household in each, forty-one in all, and the number of households in a ward has never been the number of seats in it and there is no form that says it is. No card and no later plan may build on *seats scale with households*, because the House does not scale.**

**A RECORDED CONVENTION, and it is not a calendar.** The chapters say *six weeks* and *nine weeks* in that city about eighteen times, and they mean **the run of the matter, not elapsed days** — 202 mornings is a very great deal more than nine weeks, and it always has been. Volumes 09 and 08 use the same idiom in the same breath, it is spoken by the characters rather than printed in a date line, and **no band may convert it to a day-count or a month and no band may "fix" it.** `hall`, the date line, and the closing fever are the clocks; the six weeks and the nine weeks are a person talking.

## Central pressure

**CANON, verbatim:** *The Briar March's unmapped communities must decide whether to use, expose, or destroy routes outside the Bench.*

**PLANNED, in the March's own words, and it is the sentence every chapter in this volume has to be able to survive: there is a lane in this country that everybody walks and no map has ever had, and the argument is not about the lane.**

**PLANNED, the mechanism, and it is mechanical and not mysterious. The March keeps time in rings, and a ring has no year in it.** A bell-right village has one bell. Four of its uses are the clock — **the first, the change, the noon, the last** — and every other use is added to the same bell: a burial, a boundary walked, a debt called in, a market, a stranger, a fire alarm and the putting out of a fire. The village keeps the count on a **tally board** in two hands, and neither hand ever wrote down what any of the rings was for.

**So a March village can prove how many times and can prove before or after, and cannot prove when.** That is the volume's engine, and it is arithmetic and not politics, and it is one notch bigger than Volume 05's two-calendar problem in the only direction that is available: **Volume 05's instrument named one day two ways; Volume 06's instrument counts everything and names nothing.**

**THE SENTENCE THE WHOLE VOLUME RESTS ON, and it is said out loud by a woman of forty-one in her own mouth in Chapter 258 and not by him:** *a quiet day and a day with nine strangers on it are four rings apart, and I have been counting for twenty-five years and nobody has ever asked me which of them it was.*

**PLANNED, the opposition, and there isn't one and there must not be.** The thing that keeps this volume's disputes unsettled is **a price and a distance and a habit**: a Bench date costs **a pound, three shillings and sixpence** and two days off a road that earns **one shilling and sixpence a day**, and a village that wants to be heard pays for the hearing out of the road, because the road is the only money it has. **The people who keep this volume's disputes from settling are a bell-founder's agent of sixty-three who quotes the same price twice a year and has watched it go up twice, a woman of twenty-nine who keeps the only press in eleven villages and is paid by the day she sells, and about thirty clerks in a building with a yard at Orison who did their jobs properly.** **No Volume 06 chapter may turn that into a conspiracy, and no Volume 06 chapter may introduce a person who wants to harm anybody.**

**Chapter 256 — A Woman Who Sells Days.** **Goal:** find out what a date costs in the only money a March village has. **Resistance:** she is efficient and she is not ashamed and she says the price first; and she cannot make a date for a boundary, only for an event, and nobody has ever asked her why not. **Major turn:** **a date is a pound, three shillings and sixpence, and fifteen days of the man who walks the road and one shilling, and four dates is a year of the woman who keeps the count less eleven shillings** — and that is a village paying for being heard out of its road. **Consequence:** a March village is not poor because it is ungoverned. It is poor because the price of a date is set by how far away somebody else's count agrees with yours. **Ending hook:** Tarin Keel wants the whole of it in one figure and there is a cart yard to do it in.

**Chapter 257 — The Whole Of The Margin, With The Working Printed.** **Goal:** get the whole of the route's margin into one sum. **Resistance:** nine men in a yard price things the only way anybody in a yard can, and the only figure anybody can name is what a day of walking is worth, and Tarin Keel prints the working and gets it wrong once out loud and corrects it. **Major turn:** the levy is a pound fifteen shillings and fifteen pence a village, the walking is ten pounds ten shillings, and **the excess of the one over the other is five pounds five shillings, and the bell-keeper's wage is five pounds five shillings, to the penny** — and eleven villages make fifty-seven pounds fifteen shillings, and that is the entire margin of a lane eleven villages own. **Consequence:** a village that wants to be left alone has one lever and it is the wages of one woman, and nobody in eleven villages has ever noticed. **Ending hook:** a carter of thirty-eight says he wants it to be a road, and gives the reason, and the reason is a gate.

**Chapter 258 — The Day That Added Four. THE PANEL.** **Goal:** find out what a quiet day is. **Resistance:** a woman of forty-one goes back through her father's hand in the board and finds a day four years back that added four in the same week, and a day in the same week the year before that added thirteen, and she can say which is which and cannot say which was which. **Major turn:** the panel, in two plain lines, about a height with no sill named; and then her finding, in her own mouth: **a quiet day and a day with nine strangers on it are four rings apart, and I have been counting for twenty-five years and nobody has ever asked me which of them it was.** **Consequence:** a boundary walked in the thirty-second year of the board, over ground about eleven families have grazed for nine years and paid nothing for and were never asked about, **cannot be proved, and the man who walked it is her father and he is four years dead.** **The first day of the fifty-ninth week, and the day's rings go to four.** **Ending hook:** the next day's rings go to thirteen.

**Chapter 259 — A Woman With A Board, Second Time.** **Goal:** work out what is in the March that is not a village. **Resistance:** Sable Venn is not a stranger in this country and the room knows it, and she does not get to be an interesting stranger, and Ilyan needs her and does not trust her and both of those are visible. **Major turn:** she says, in about nine words and without being asked, that she put four lines into the March, and that she has never read a record in her life, and that she had it copied by somebody who does. **Consequence:** the woman who has said for nine years that she does not read other people's records has put an unread record four hundred miles into a country that has never had one, and it is not a lie and it is not a mistake and it is the volume's whole engine. **Ending hook:** a satchel of letters comes into the yard at Coldharrow with a cart, and a man of about fifty-one in it has been in four counties buying paper.

**Chapter 260 — The Copy Arrives.** **Goal:** find out what four lines about a river four hundred miles off are doing in a county where a bell is a schedule. **Resistance:** nobody in Coldharrow can read it except two people, and one of those two is a woman of twenty-nine who will not read it in front of the village, and a man of about fifty-one bought it at Sallowby at threepence because a woman with a board told him it was a thing you could hold. **Major turn:** the second line of the four pages is a schedule and not a river, and a bell is a schedule, and **the first bell of a Coldharrow day is a height above nothing**, and a copy of an order that names a height without naming a sill is load-bearing here and not a curiosity, because of a chamber's own nine-year-old printed rule that is on a wall four hundred miles off. **Consequence:** nothing is decided and nobody is asked to decide anything, and the volume ends its first ten chapters with a piece of paper in a village that has never had a piece of paper, and he asks for it to be read out the way he has asked for everything to be read out for some time, and a woman of forty-one who has kept a board for twenty-five years does not remark on it. **Ending hook, and it is the volume's question asked once and not answered:** **a carter of thirty-eight asks in a yard, in nineteen words, to nobody, why a lane that runs from village to village was laid out that way, and nobody answers him, and the System says nothing about it.**

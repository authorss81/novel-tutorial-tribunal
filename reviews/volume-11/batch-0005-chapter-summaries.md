## VOLUME 11, CHAPTERS 541 TO 550 — *the first removed witness, and the list of what is missing*. **THE VOLUME'S CLIMAX AND RESOLUTION. ILYAN IS ENTERED IN A CLASS CALLED AN EXTERNAL WITNESS, A NAME GOES IN AT THE BACK OF THE BOOK, THE FORTY-THREE GO OUT INTO THE PUBLIC RECORD, AND THE QUESTION THE VOLUME EXISTED FOR IS PUT IN A ROOM AND NOT ANSWERED.**

**541 — A form opened in a porch, and a heading over a man.** `clear` 41, the fourth day of the ninety-ninth week, a Friday. A man of fifty-four of the county rides nine miles down a lane with a form folded in four in the inside of his coat and refuses to open it in a lane, and is told by the keeper of the book that she will not have it opened in a lane and will not have it kept, and it is opened in a porch in front of about nine people. The form is an entry out of the book of orders at Aldwick putting a man of thirty-one into a class by himself with a name over the class, and the name is an external witness, and a person at that office may be asked about him and make a return in respect of him in the ordinary form. **He asks what it would take to be a person of this county and is told, at nine miles a ride, that there is no form in the county of Aldwick that says what a person must do to become a person of it.** He says out loud that an external witness is a man who is not a person of anywhere, and is told that an external witness is a person of the record and not of the place, and that the difference between those is the whole of what has happened to a man of thirty-one in a hundred and forty-one days. A recordwright says the sentence the band is built on: a heading is a promise about what goes in it.

**542 — A boy of fourteen with a fourth thing to write, and a fifth place with a name over it.** `clear` 42, the fifth day of the ninety-ninth week, the ordinary reading, a Saturday. The week is read out in a chancel eight feet by eleven in about eleven minutes and about nine people go in and the man of thirty-one is on the step. In the smithy the boy has a fourth thing to write and the fourth is that a person of another county has been entered in a book nine miles off in a class with a name over it, and the class is not a person, and he cannot work out what to do with a class. **The man of thirty-one offers to write the boy's line for him in front of about nine people and is refused in about four seconds, because a line a man who cannot be sent for writes has nobody behind it and a line a boy of fourteen writes has a boy behind it.** She rules nothing and heads nothing and the boy writes it with his own name at the foot. Four ruled things in this county have no heading on them and there were three a fortnight ago; a fifth place nine miles off has a name over it and what is under the name is a man.

**543 — The prohibition is broken, and the reason is that she wrote a county and not a person.** `clear` 43, the sixth day of the ninety-ninth week, a Sunday. A room with the door open and nothing on the table, which is the first thing of its kind in that county in nineteen years. A man of thirty-four from a town four days off asks the entry be broken, out loud, in front of about nine people, having carried her no from a lane for nine days. **She says yes, and the reason is not that anybody has convinced her of anything: the entry says nobody in the county of Aldwick may be asked, and not that nobody may be asked, and the person asking was not of that county, and a rule a person can break because they came from somewhere else is a rule about a place.** The man of thirty-one is told out loud in the same room that he is the reason it happened and that wanting it was right and wanting it that way was wrong. The entry is not struck out, and four lines go in beneath it, and what that entry had been keeping out turns out to have been a question and not a person, because nobody in this county has the original and nobody has seen it.

**544 — Nobody asks him, and a man of thirty-one says he is not going to say the sentence.** `clear` 44, the seventh and last day of the ninety-ninth week, and the morrow is the first of the hundredth. A man of about seventy-two is in a room he was not sent to and is told beforehand what it is about. The four lines at the foot of the third page of a copy of a copy are read out in full by a man of thirty-four from four days off, and the name at the foot of the fourth page is read as it is written. **The reading stops where it stops, at a man of thirty-four of Bramblefold who asked a question in a square in another county, with no name for him on that page or on any page.** A name is entered at the foot of that page with the day on it, as the name of a person asked a question in a square, and the person named is told and is asked nothing and says nothing, and four words go in under it: that he said nothing. **And a man of thirty-one is asked by the keeper of the book whether he is going to say the sentence he has been carrying for about forty days, and says that he is not, and gives a reason, and the sentence is not in that room and is not in that book and is not in a square.**

**545 — Four people are asked, separately, in four rooms, and four answers come out.** `clear` 45, the first day of the hundredth week, and the day before was the last of the ninety-ninth. The four people who struck their own names out of a list in a square thirty-one days ago are asked separately in four rooms what the household of Kiln Row is owed, by the one person who can be sent for, with the same terms said at four doors. **The answers are nothing; a page with four gaps in it; not known and not guessed; and a thing said out loud once in a square.** Nothing is entered, nothing is ruled, nothing is headed. Then a man of thirty-one asks a weaver of sixty-two in a lane in front of about nine people for the four of them to agree, and is refused in about four seconds: **a room in which four people agree is a room in which one of them has been persuaded, and the only person anybody could send for in this county is the recordwright and she is not going to send for four people to agree about a household that is dead.**

**546 — A man of seventy-two is asked, and says no, and it is entered.** `clear` 46, the second day of the hundredth week, a Wednesday, which is the day he walks two miles to hear one line read out about four acres. The keeper asks him the question he asked to be asked about fourteen days ago, with his three terms read out to the porch first, and he says no — no to the question, no to the answer, no to the four lines, no to the name. **It is entered at the back of a book four hundred and eleven years old with the day on it and it is the second thing in four hundred and eleven years that records a person declining something, and she says out loud in front of about nine people that it is two people and two afternoons and not a form and not a practice and not a thing anybody else may do, and that if a third person in this county says no to a thing about their own name then what happens to that is a question that will have to be worked out by whoever is there at the time — which is the sentence she said in a lane twenty-four days ago about the first one and is now true of herself.** A man of thirty-one asks whether the man would mind him being in the porch, and is told, out loud, that a man who cannot be sent for standing in a place is a man who cannot be told to leave it.

**547 — Nine things read out in a market square, one line each, by about nine people.** `clear` 47, the third day of the hundredth week, the market, a Thursday, about two hundred people. The keeper refuses, in about four seconds and before anything happens, to enter the list before it is read out, on the ground that a list entered before it is read out is a finding and a finding goes into a book without anybody asking the people it is about. Then about nine people read one line each in about eleven minutes, and each says where the line came from and in how many words: a boy of fourteen who worked out a thing about a dead man on his own; a widow of sixty-eight who said it off her own door; a man who mends ploughs who asked whether six pounds a year is enough; the keeper herself; a man of eighty-one in a chair somebody carried into the square; a weaver of sixty-two who is tired of being a man who says things about four words; a woman of about fifty-one who made four pages out of her own memory; a man of thirty-four from a town four days off; and a woman of forty at a wool bench. **About nine of the about two hundred can repeat one of the nine lines this afternoon and about a hundred and ninety-one heard a woman say nine things they had never been told, and not one of them is in a book.** A man of thirty-one asks for the list to be entered first and is refused again, and is told that a hundred and ninety-one people heard it and there is nothing anywhere that says they heard it. **The chapter closes on that refusal and not on the ninth line, which is the beat its title promises.**

**548 — THE FIRST REMOVED WITNESS IS RESTORED, AND THE LEAF IS NOT STRUCK BACK.** `clear` 48, the fourth day of the hundredth week, a Friday. A name goes in at the back of a book four hundred and eleven years old, on a fresh leaf of the same paper as the leaf the copy of the four pages is entered on, in the ordinary hand, with the day on it, ruling nothing and heading nothing. It says that a household of this town was asked a question a hundred and forty-one years back by an office nine miles off and gave an answer and the answer was made use of, that the leaf is not struck back and is not to be struck back, that the person descended from the first name has been told, and that nothing is carried. **A weaver of sixty-two says the first name on that leaf out loud in a room for the first time in this county's knowledge — it is his grandfather's, and a leaf of descent is filled from the eldest at the top — and she asks him for it before she writes it.** When a man of thirty-one asks for the leaf to be struck back, in front of about nine people, **he is refused in about four sentences, and the fourth is that he is the last of a name and has been for nine years and can stand it another nine.** A man of eighty-one says from the chair that a name can be in that book with a line through it and on a page without one, and both are the same name and neither is the person.

**549 — Forty-three names with four gaps in them, and a woman of seventy says no a second time.** `clear` 49, the fifth day of the hundredth week, the ordinary reading, a Saturday, about a hundred and ninety people in the square. The week is read in the room and then the leaf is carried out and read in the open air in the order it is ruled, in about eleven minutes, and the four gaps are read as gaps. **The keeper says out loud that a square is not a book: a book is where a thing goes so that one person can be sent for about it, and a square is where a thing goes so that about a hundred and ninety people can be told about it and nobody can.** A man of thirty-one asks for the four names to be read as struck. **The woman of about seventy is asked first, in a porch, before anybody argues, and says no, and gives the same reason she gave in a lane twenty-seven days ago, and it is entered, and she is still one of two people in four hundred and eleven years and it is still not a form.** He asks a second time in the square and is refused in about four words. Nobody is struck out. Seventy-three days back from that Saturday is the morning of the thirtieth day after the division, and about a hundred and ninety people now have an account of it in their mouths that this town has never resolved.

**550 — A list read as a list, a class named out loud, and the question put in the room and not answered.** `clear` 50, the sixth and last day of the hundredth week, a Sunday, a three hundredth morning. The whole of what has happened in this town in a hundred and forty-one days is read out in a room with the door open as a list and not as a finding, in about eleven minutes, with the nine lines at the end of it. **The keeper then names his class out loud — an external witness is a person of the record and not of the place — and says that a heading is a promise about what goes in it.** A man of eighty-one, not sent for, asks out loud whether a person of the record and not of the place can become a person of the place, and the keeper says it out loud so that about nine people know it has been asked, and **nobody answers it and nobody is going to.** Then the man of thirty-one says the four words he came to this country carrying, out loud, in that room, in front of about nine people; about four of them know which sentence it is, where he got it and who first said it to him, and about nine do not, and nobody stops him. **She writes nine lines at the back of that book on a fresh leaf in the ordinary hand with the day on it, ruling nothing and heading nothing, and reads them back to him in the doorway before she shuts the cupboard. It is the first time in a hundred and forty-one days that anything he has said has been entered in that book.** Nobody thanked anybody and nobody was sorry for anybody.

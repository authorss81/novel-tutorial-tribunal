## VOLUME 11, CHAPTERS 531 TO 540 — *custody*. **THE ARCHIVE LORDS ARE NAMED, NO SINGLE PERSON CAN HOLD IT, AND THE STAGE DID NOT ADVANCE.**

**531 — A man of thirty-four from a town four days off stands in a porch with his hands empty, says the word *shelf*, names his own trade as the Archive Lords, and says out loud that he does not want a person in this county who answers for other people's papers; a man of thirty-one offers a room and is refused in about nine seconds in four sentences, the first of which is that a man who cannot be sent for cannot be asked whether he still has it, and the last of which is that a thing nobody can be asked about is a thing nobody can check, and that this is not a protection but the opposite of one. A woman of about fifty-one then tells him, in a lane behind the bottom of the town, not to offer it again, and says that the name at the foot of the fourth page of the copy of a copy belongs to a man who cannot be sent for and was written by a woman who cannot be sent for either, and that between those two facts there is a gap nobody in this county can close. `clear` 31, a Tuesday, the first day of the ninety-eighth week. The keeper of the book, told that she is the only person anybody can be sent for, said out loud that she had never had to think about the word *keeper* and that nobody has ever asked her whether the one was enough.**

**532 — A Wednesday, which is the day a man of about seventy-two walks two miles to hear one line read out about four acres, in about eleven minutes, in a wet coat in a week in which it has not rained for about eleven days. After the eleven minutes he says out loud in the porch the one thing he has not said in nine years, which is that he would like to be asked about it again, and he gives the terms: once, not to be told what he said last time, not to have anybody told what he says, and not to have it read out in a square on a Saturday. About four of the nine in that porch understood what it costs him. The keeper of the book answers with a sentence that is neither yes nor no, and gives her reason: there is no form in this county that says a person may be asked what they remember, and she has had nineteen years to find one. A man of thirty-four then asks her, at the corner of the meeting-house wall, what a shelf is, and is told it is a shelf with the name of the keeper on it and the number scratched out, and that she has known that since about the fourth hour the day before and has not told him. And at about the fourth hour a woman of about fifty-one says, in a lane, in about ten words, *a man who says he does not remember has been asked*, which is the sentence a man of thirty-one has been carrying since the Sunday a woman of sixty-eight said no in a room with the door shut, twenty-four days back, and which he did not say, in a porch, in front of about nine people, an hour earlier. `clear` 32, a Wednesday.**

**533 — The market, on a Thursday, about two hundred people in a square. A man of thirty-four says out loud that a shelf is a good thing and that he is not here to take anybody's, and tries to explain the difference between a held paper and a copied one, which is about four minutes of sitting. A man of thirty-one then says out loud, in the open air, that a shelf in a room in Bramblefold is the only thing in this county that would make the copy of the four pages safe, and a woman of about forty at the wool bench says the other half in about four seconds: a shelf is a thing a person is responsible for, there is no form in this county that releases a person from being the one, and the copyist of that book has been the one for nineteen years and nobody has asked her whether it is enough and she has wanted somebody to ask her that for nineteen years. The keeper of the book says out loud, at the edge of the square, that about nine people in this town could hold that room today and about nine could be asked about it in a year, and that those are the same nine, and that this is a trouble with a county of about two thousand four hundred people in which about nine is the largest number that can be got into a room, and that it is not a trouble with the man from four days off. A woman of about fifty-one is in that square for an hour and is asked nothing, and tells a man of thirty-one that his refusal on the Tuesday was the fastest anybody had ever said anything to him in this country. The band does not resolve it. `clear` 33, the market Thursday.**

**534 — A Friday, and a woman of about fifty-one asks, in a lane behind the bottom of the town, for her two rooms to be looked at by about nine people one at a time, and gives the reason: if they come in together it is nine people who have seen a thing, and in about eleven years there will be a person in this town who says nine people saw her four pages, and she has not got nine people in this county who can be sent for. The first to go up is a man of thirty-four, alone, and he says out loud that he can tell how the four pages were made — forty-one mistakes and thirty-eight corrections, and the corrections are the only part anybody can check, because a correction is a person disagreeing with themselves in writing — and that he cannot tell whether they are true, and that about four people in his country can answer the second question and not one of them is in this county. A man of thirty-one goes up second and is the wrong kind of eyes, and is told in about four seconds, in a lane, in front of about nine people, that a man who cannot be sent for can look at a thing and cannot be asked whether he looked, and that what he can do is nothing, and that this is the first useful thing anybody in this county has been able to tell him about himself. The same lane then holds the band one step short of breaking a prohibition: a man of thirty-four asks who it is permissible to ask about the four pages they came from, and is told that there is an entry in the back of a book four hundred and eleven years old saying that nobody in the county of Aldwick may be asked about the original, and that it may be broken in a room with the door open with about nine people in it and only if one of the nine asks her first, and that she has said in front of nine people that the answer would be no so that she cannot be asked privately in a fortnight and say something else. The prohibition was not broken. `clear` 34, a Friday.**

**535 — A Saturday, and the first week since a third column was ruled on a board in a smithy with nothing at the top of it. The week is read out in a chancel in about eleven minutes in front of about nine people, and then the keeper reads out of the doorway one line from the board: the gap in the wall, the two accounts of one morning, fifty-nine days back, and a person of this town asking to have four pages kept in this town. A boy of fourteen tells the smithy that a person asking is a different kind of thing from a thing that happened, and gets no help. A man of thirty-one asks, for the third time in fifteen days, for a third unheaded thing to be headed, and is refused, and then is not given the reason, and says out loud that she gave it to him the other two times, and is told that a reason given once is a reason and a reason given twice is a habit, and that in about eleven years there will be a person in this town who was told that a column with nothing at the top of it is a good thing and who will not be able to tell you that she thought it in a room in her nineteenth year. She rules nothing and heads nothing, writes one line, and says out loud that there are now four ruled things in this county with no heading on them and that two of them are on a board that any person in that town may rub out with a rag. `clear` 35, a Saturday.**

**536 — A Sunday, and a woman of sixty-one who has written to nine towns in four years asking for pages says at the door of a barn at Kiln Row, and then in a lane, that she is going to stop, and gives the reason in about four sentences: the first of the nine towns to be asked a question about a person is going to be a town where somebody has heard her say a sentence she was told, and she would not know the difference and neither would they. A man of thirty-one asks her in a porch in front of about nine people to keep going, on the grounds that the two towns that remember that morning differently are still remembering it differently, and she tells him that this is the reason and that a person who is the reason a thing keeps going is a person who cannot stop, and that in every one of her nine towns she was the reason, and that the last of them was a market town with a bar in the mud where a woman of thirty-four would not let a man read a line out of a room, and that she sat on a bench for an hour afterwards and worked out that she would not have given him those four sentences in the first year. Then a man of eighty-one comes down on a stick, unsent for, and asks her the question four counties of people have never asked her, which is who said the sentence to her first, and she gives three answers and says she does not know which was first, and he says that this is the same thing as four words walking round a town from a pump and that it has a worse end on it. She will not hand the bundle of thirty-two letters to anybody. `clear` 36, a Sunday.**

**537 — A Monday, the seventh and last day of the ninety-eighth week, with the morrow named as the first of the ninety-ninth, and a room eight feet by eleven with the door open and the window down and no book out of the cupboard. A recordwright reads out as a list what the four columns carry — descent, holdings, the fold, the ground behind the meeting-house — and what they do not carry, which is a day, a year, a death, and a person who does not want to be in it. Then a fifth thing said from a chair by a fire, which was a thing a person remembers and is not a copy and is not a copy of a copy. Then three things said out loud in a lane in one week, named and dated by their weekdays. Then six lines on the back of a form nine miles off, which she refuses to read out and gives the reason. Then the whole of it is declared to be a list, and a list is not a finding, and a finding is not a decision, and nothing has been carried in four weeks. A man of thirty-four then names, in about nine words, the finding of the band: there are two kinds of safety, a thing is safe because somebody holds it and a thing is safe because nobody may take it, and those look the same from the outside, and about nine people in that room can say the difference afterwards and about nine cannot, and the two sets are not the same sets. A man of thirty-one asks her to enter the list as a finding and is refused in about four sentences, on the ground that a finding goes into a book without anybody asking the people it is about, and she says out loud that the thirteenth case in nineteen years of the one who has not asked is going to be a man of thirty-one who is not a person of this county. **THE LEDGER SHE READS OUT IS THE tenth, a widow of sixty-eight who came because a man in a chair asked her to come; the eleventh, a boy of fourteen who writes the week; and the twelfth, a man of forty-four on a Monday morning, and not one of the three is a person she asked — which is what `508`, `521` and `523` each say in their own words, and the first write of `537` got all three wrong and was repaired.** A woman of about fifty at the door asks to write the list down and is told she will get the lanes wrong. `clear` 37, a Monday.**

**538 — A Tuesday, the first day of the ninety-ninth week, with the day before named as the last of the ninety-eighth. A recordwright is paid two shillings a year by each of about sixty households, which is six pounds a year and has not moved in nineteen years, and a man called Joe Drury, thirty-four, who mends ploughs and has worked in that smithy eleven years, asks her in front of about nine people whether that is enough, and says out loud that he had thought about it for about four days and was going to ask it in a room and not in a kitchen. She answers in about four sentences, and it is not a complaint and it is not a thank you: it is enough because she has a house and keeps no beast, and a thing nobody has asked her about for nineteen years is not a thing she has been underpaid for but a thing she has not been asked about, and those are two different things. She tells him not to thank her, because if he thanks her then it becomes a thing that happened to her instead of a thing she answered. A man of thirty-one stood at the end of that smithy for about four minutes with the question ready and did not ask it, and about four people noticed, and Joe Drury then said out loud to the room why he could and the other could not, which is that she can send him away. A woman of sixty-one then says her four sentences in that smithy for the last time in this county and says that it is a room, and that rooms are where people are held to, and that she would like somebody in about eleven years to be able to tell which of the two of them arranged that. A man of thirty-four finishes the day by saying out loud that on a shelf the keeper is paid, and that sixty households can stop it in about four days and one person cannot stop it at all, and that this is not a fair arrangement but it is a checkable one. `clear` 38, a Tuesday.**

**539 — A Wednesday, which is the day a man of about seventy-two walks two miles to hear one line read. He is read his line, he says that was right, he declines to go and look at the stake for the fifth time, and nobody asks him anything else, and the not-asking is said out loud in the porch and about four of the nine understand that it is an arrangement. Then the room is opened at about the eleventh hour and a woman of about fifty-one says out loud that a man of thirty-four told that room on Monday that there are two kinds of safe, and that she has had it for two days and is not going to argue with him about it, and is going to use it. She asks eight people one at a time, in about four seconds each, the same four sentences: will you hold one page of these four in your own house and be a person who can be sent for about it. Four say yes and about four say no, and Edith Crow, sixty-eight, gives a reason for the second no of her life in this volume. The keeper of the book is not one of the eight and had not offered, and says out loud that she noticed at the fourth asking and said nothing at the fourth asking, and that this is the fourteenth time in nineteen years, and that she is not going to rule a line about it. A man of eighty-one says from the chair that if one of those four houses is emptied, or the woman at the wool bench dies, or the man at the kiln goes to a town four days off, then in about four weeks the arrangement is three houses and in about a year it is a sentence in a smithy. Then there is one thing left on the table and one person she has not asked. The thing is a fifth sheet and it is not one of the four: it is a list she wrote out that morning with four names on it and four houses under the names. A man of thirty-one says yes in about four seconds, and a woman of about fifty-one says no on his behalf in about nine words before he has finished: a thing a man who cannot be sent for says yes to is a thing that has a man who cannot be sent for in it. She puts the list down beside the four and does not pick it up again, and nobody in that room is holding it, and the whole of the difference between the four pages and the list is that the four are four custody acts and the list is one document. `clear` 39, a Wednesday.**

**540 — A Thursday, the market, and the eleventh day of a man of thirty-four down a lane. He says out loud in a market square in front of about two hundred people what he came for, and a man of thirty-one says out loud, before he can, that the man came for the four pages, and is corrected in about four seconds by the man himself, in the voice of a man who has said the same thing to about nine people in four counties. It is not the four pages. It is that a woman of thirty-four in this town is the only person in this county who can be sent for about anything, and that in about eleven years she will be sixty, and that there is no form in the county of Aldwick that says what happens to a book when the person who keeps it cannot come in on a Wednesday, and that he has kept a shelf for about eleven years and has watched two of them stop being kept and that neither time was anybody's fault, and that he is not here to take anybody's, and that nobody in this county has believed him. About four people in that square understood the whole of it and about two hundred heard the word *shelf*. The keeper of the book says three things at the edge of the square, the third of which is that she is not going to tell about two hundred people what she is going to do about it, and that she will not tell a room either until a room has asked her first, and that there is a man in a room on Monday who has to ask her. Then a man of about thirty asks whether it is true that four people in this town are each holding one of four pages, and is told yes and then told that nothing happens if one of them loses it, and that this is not a shelf, because a shelf has a keeper and a number on a board and a person who is paid. A woman of about forty at the wool bench says that none of it is in a book. Nobody in that square believed a word of it. `clear` 40, a Thursday.**

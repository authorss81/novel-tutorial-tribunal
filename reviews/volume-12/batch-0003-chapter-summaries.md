## VOLUME 12, CHAPTERS 561 TO 570 — *the hundred meets, and a rule that can be obeyed by nobody*. **THE BAND SPENDS THE ELEVENTY DOORS, THE MARKET THAT WAS NOT ON, THE FIRST RECORD IN THAT COUNTY MADE BY A CHALLENGE, A PROHIBITION ENTERED BY A MAN AGAINST HIMSELF, THE RISING, A NUMBER OF FEET ON A WALL, A FOOT OF WATER ON A COMMON THAT BELONGS TO NOBODY, A REMEDY PUT TO A MARKET, AND A NAME REFUSED AND FIVE DAYS GIVEN INSTEAD. IT RESOLVES NOTHING.**

**561 — Four doorways in the dark, three agreements, one refusal, and one number that was a day wrong.** `clear` 61, the third day of the hundred and second week, a Thursday. A man of thirty-one goes out after dark to the four closes at the bottom of the district, which are a mile and a half apart because a mile and a half is what four closes at the bottom of a fen are. **At the second doorway he says eight days, which was the clerk's figure of the Wednesday before, and a man of about forty-four makes him work it out loud with the subtraction and gets seven, and he is right on the bridge seven days later.** At the third, a woman of about sixty-two sets a time for him to come back and will not be hurried. **At the fourth, a woman of forty-one refuses him before he has finished the second sentence, and the reason is that a number said in a doorway is a thing her neighbours will have by the seventh hour, four people will have said it four ways by then, and on Wednesday morning there will be thirty men in a room and not one of the numbers on the wall will be the number she heard in her own doorway.** She says she will say the thing she has to say at the north wall in the open and not in a doorway. A man of fifty-eight comes up the lane with a slate, says six of seven are done, and says the whole of why the two of them agreed to go in eleven doors: **in this hundred anybody may ask anybody, and so there is nobody to go and check on a stranger at the seventh hour in the morning.** Nothing is on any wall.

**562 — A market called off, and eleven chairs in a room that one man fills.** `clear` 62, the fourth day of the hundred and second week, a Friday, and it is the market day. **About three hundred people come onto a green with nothing on it and go home again, because a man of fifty-eight who may not call off a market called it off at the sixth hour and put it on a board and on three doorsteps first, and said out loud at the stone step that no form in the county of Kell lets him do it or stops him doing it.** He gives the reason: on Sunday the green will not carry forty carts, and he would rather be a man who stood on an empty step on Friday than a man who watched a boy of nine go down in it. **Then a man of thirty-one counts out loud in front of about nine people how many men fill the chairs in a room that meets in four days, and finds that the hundred is about thirty men, one for every holding with a season on it, and that on eleven lines of the thirty the name in the first column is the same name, and that there is no column anywhere on that sheet with a person's name at the top of it and never has been one.** He puts four lines on the wall with nothing at the top of them. **Closing object: a man who owns eleven seasons and has no seat in the room where they are dealt with, and no rule that says he has one.** His hand is on a public wall for the second time in four days.

**563 — A sheet read aloud by a man who is not the clerk, and six challenges.** `clear` 63, the fifth day of the hundred and second week, a Saturday. A district reads its wall on a Saturday morning at the sixth hour, out loud, by whoever holds the slate, with the door open. **The clerk will not read it, and gives the reason twice: if he reads it then it is the water-clerk reading a sheet he wrote, and any man in the street may say the water-clerk read it wrong; and in nineteen years nobody has ever asked whether that sheet was right, and he has wanted it every Saturday and has not asked, because asking him is asking the only person he cannot get away from.** A man of about sixty-two called **Perrin Voss** reads it and is corrected twice by two different men in the street. **About six people say out loud that the reading was wrong about their own ground, and the clerk writes all six down with the day on it, because there is a board outside his door that says he will answer anything. It is the first record in that county ever made by a challenge instead of by a keeper.** One challenge names a parcel; the other five are attached to nothing, because the men who said them did not know which parcels. No names are read out. A man of thirty-one finds that the first thing anybody in this district has put on that wall for is a fifth of it that cannot be checked by anybody including the man who said it.

**564 — Nine lines on a board outside a man's own door, and a boy of fourteen.** `clear` 64, the sixth day of the hundred and second week, a Sunday. **A man of fifty-eight who has answered everything for nineteen years puts nine lines on the board outside his own door: that no person in this hundred is to be asked what they remember**, in a building, a yard, a lane, a market, at a door, on a boat and at a fire, of anybody including himself and including a stranger and a man with a slate, on the rising and every day after it; that a man asked may answer what he knows now and not what he remembers; that the rule does not stop a true thing that can be checked against, or a number off the wall; that he is not to be asked by anybody including his wife; and that it has no day on it and no form suspends it. **He gives the reason against it — a hundred will lose things — and then the reason for it, and says the reason for it is worse: his own promise produced a woman of forty-one being told the whole of the true of her life in a market, and then produced six people at his door asking what he remembers in March, and he remembers three versions of one week of April and one of them is not his.** About nine people ask him about it over about an hour and about nine of them are for the board and about nine are against it and the two nines do not ask each other. **A boy of fourteen writes the nine lines out in chalk, understands all nine, and asks about the one he does not: his father holds nine acres, was on that water in the first week of April three years running, has said nothing about it to anybody, and a boy of fourteen has worked out inside about a second that he has just obeyed the first rule he has ever written down in his life.**

**565 — The eve, a trench of black mud, and one season given to a room.** `clear` 65, the seventh and last day of the hundred and second week, a Monday, and the morrow is the first of the hundred and third. **The drain is a trench of black mud four feet wide with a foot of standing water in the old line, because a man of fifty-four took it out on Saturday at his own cost, and a man of fifty-one who has cut ditch for about thirty years stands at the top end of it at the seventh hour and says the water is going down the meadow and the meadow ends in about eight hundred acres that belong to nobody.** Then it is the last day a season can be given up by the person who holds it. **A man of about thirty-three with six acres of the sally bed, twelve shillings and sixpence a year, held four years, comes to the step with his purse and gives the season up to a room and not to a person.** He does not give it to a woman of forty-one, because she refused a stranger's money in a market ten days ago and was right and he was in the market. He does not give it to a stranger with a bad leg who was at his door on Thursday and did not ask him for anything. **His reason is that there is no column on that sheet with a person's name at the top of it and he will not be the first that somebody adds.** He puts the purse on the bar and then takes it off it, because there is no form that lets the clerk hold twelve shillings and sixpence or give it to anybody, and the six acres will lapse in the morning with the other hundred and thirty and the square at the end of the line will have nothing in it for the first time in about two hundred and thirty years. **A man of thirty-one derives it in a mouth: twelve shillings and sixpence is one hundred and fifty pence, and a hundred and fifty pence of ditch goes out of it, and a ditch fills in in about two years, and no person in that county can say that is wrong.**

**566 — The rising, the hundred, and a question nobody answers.** `clear` 66, the first day of the hundred and third week, a Tuesday, and the day before was the seventh and last of the hundred and second. **The water comes over a block of limestone with a line cut round it at about the seventh hour and there is no bell for it; the clerk puts his hand flat on the stone for the length of a count of five and then writes the day at the foot of a sheet of parchment two men long.** About thirty men meet in a weigh-house sixty feet long at midday, there are forty-one in it by the second hour, and about three hundred people are in a green in the same hour. **A man of thirty-one says one thing and it is not the argument**: three boards are in a six-board gate, the other three are in a yard at Coldcombe because there is no money for them, and he had that off a man with a wagon and a team of four who has hauled stone for eleven years and has never once been asked a question at a gate. Then he sits down on a bench and stays there. **A man of fifty-four with about seven hundred sheep comes four miles on his own legs, stands at the back in a room with no seat in it, says he has been wrong about the drain and is right about the common, and would rather the hundred knew both at once than either on its own, and gets into a cart himself.** Then the question, and it was asked from the bottom of the room by a man who is not the man of fifty-four. It is asked wrong — whether the stranger is going to be one of us or four miles south in the water-season, in twelve days, which is `chapter − 554` and not the age of the notice on the board — and the clerk refuses the wrong question, says the one that was put, **which is the question a man of eighty-one asked in a room with the door open on the sixth day of the hundredth week, a Sunday, and gave to a stranger at a porch step on the last Monday of that week, and which was not answered then either**, **and about four people answer a different question in about four seconds and are told so in the open.** Nobody in that room answers it. **Then a number is written against the low common and it is two hundred and twenty feet: about nine hundred yards of usable edge, about twenty feet of frontage a tent measured with his own feet against two gate-posts, times eleven tents. It is arithmetically defensible and it is about a width of a common and not about people, and the clerk says out loud before he writes it that he is writing a true number he knows will be useless in the same breath.**

**567 — The first act of the new year, done to a woman rather than by her.** `clear` 67, the second day of the hundred and third week, a Wednesday. **A woman of twenty-eight with a daughter of six walks four miles over a plank bridge in about nine inches of water to ask for a number to be taken off the wall, and asks instead for fifty-eight to be written in the taking column of the low common's line**, with the day on it, and no name on it, because there is no column for a person and fifty-eight cannot go in a square. The clerk writes it in about nine seconds. **She then says in front of about thirty people that in a day, when the water is on that common, about nine people will be able to say the true of it and she will be one of the nine, and she will have got it off a number a man wrote on a wall and not off anything she has seen with her own eyes.** And she says that a sack under a churn is worse than a wall, and a wall is worse, and a woman of about seventy asked her at a pump what feet a common has. **Then a man of thirty-one says, in front of about thirty people, that he said a number out loud in the open on Friday to be useful and had a person be the worse for it, that it is not the first time he has done it, that he did it a good many hundreds of mornings ago in a market four hundred miles from here, to a woman who told him a number said out loud would be a thing her neighbours had by morning, and that there is no column anywhere in this county for the difference.** He does not say her name and about nine people look at him and about four look at the number.

**568 — A foot of water on a common that belongs to nobody, and two numbers added together.** `clear` 68, the third day of the hundred and third week, a Thursday, the tenth day from a Monday and the second day after the rising. **There is no hill in that country and the reason a man can be drowned four miles from where he can walk is not a hill: the water comes across the flat and the wind at the seventh hour pushes it over the top of a swell about eleven feet wide.** Two of eleven tents are still standing. Nobody is drowned. **Two numbers are said out loud on the plank bridge in front of about nine people and added together: ten days off the Monday and eight days off the Wednesday, and both of them come out at the same morning**, and the man who carried one of them a day wrong says so himself in about four seconds, on a bridge, in the open, in front of about nine people. **The water came from the low meadow, about thirty-one acres, which had been under since Friday because the rate on eleven parcels was not paid for two years and the ditch that takes the meadow off the pasture has a foot of sallow in it — and not from the drain, which is a trench.** The four closes take a foot too and nobody in Withermere is standing at them. A man of about thirty-three stands in eleven inches of water on six acres that had a season on them at the sixth hour on Monday morning and nothing on them now, having given them to a room on the last day a season can be given up, and asks for a line about him to be taken off a wall, and is refused. **A woman of twenty-eight walks four miles back over the bridge to ask for the number to be taken off the wall, and is refused, and is told the reason in four sentences: it is in the clerk's hand with the day on it, about thirty people have read it and been somewhere on the strength of it, and if he took it off that afternoon then about nine people on Saturday would believe a stranger who had written it off as a mistake.**

**569 — The market, four sentences, and a paper under an oak bar.** `clear` 69, the fourth day of the hundred and third week, a Friday, and it is the market day, and the first one held in three days. **About three hundred people come onto a green with a foot of standing water on the low side of it, and about nine stones have been moved up out of the water and about nine have not, and nobody had said the standing water out loud.** A man of thirty-one has written three hundred words with nothing at the top of them and reads **four sentences** of it to about thirty people: the water was not the fault of any person in this hundred; eleven households lost eleven seasons in one purse in four days and nobody has broken a rule; a hundred of about thirty men is a room and not a district; and a number on a wall about a piece of ground that belongs to nobody is not a number of people. **He then says out loud that a notice on a wall is not a covenant, a covenant is not a remedy, and a remedy nobody was asked about is not a settlement — and that the same four sentences in a room over a stable would have been worth about nine repetitions and about four listeners, and at this step they are worth about nine repetitions and about thirty listeners, and no form in this county says which of those two places is the right place to say a thing.** A woman of about thirty-eight with a barrow says nine words in the middle of the market that she said at a gate thirteen days ago and does not know anybody ever heard them. **The paper goes under the oak bar, where anybody may read it, take it away and write on the back of it, and there is no form in the county of Kell that says a person may not.** About nine of about three hundred people could repeat it afterwards.

**570 — About thirty names in chalk, a name refused, and five days given instead.** `clear` 70, the fifth day of the hundred and third week, a Saturday. **A boy of fourteen writes the week's sheet in chalk and has put about thirty names on it that nobody asked for, and a stroke beside the ones that are not of this hundred, and he has known for two days what the stroke means: they came.** A remedy of three hundred words lies under the oak bar and is announced in the clerk's hand with the day on it. **A man of thirty-one asks a woman of forty-one to put her name at the top of a paper with nothing at the top of it, and is refused in about four seconds in front of about nine people**, and the reason is that a paper with a name at the top of it in a town where about nine people read that wall every morning is a paper nine people can be argued with, and she would rather somebody argued with a figure than with a woman of forty-one who lost six acres in a market. **And then she gives him a day instead of a name: the ninth day from the rising, five days off, the third day of the hundred and fourth week, a Thursday.** She says what she will have done by then, and refuses him a second time, and he then says out loud in front of about nine people the question he came to ask, which is whether there is a thing in this hundred that can be asked of anybody at all. She tells him he has just asked it and about four of them would have said it if he had not, and he says he had sixteen days to find a way of saying it that nobody could repeat and has not got one. It is repeated by about nine people before the end of the day and nobody answers it. **Closing object: a harvest coming on ground a man of fifty-four has been in season on for six years, and a man who has not worked out yet what the question is.**

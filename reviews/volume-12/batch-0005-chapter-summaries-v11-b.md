**521 — The week read out in the ordinary way, and a boy of fourteen who worked a thing out on his own account.** `clear` 21, a Saturday, the first ordinary reading in three weeks, in about eleven minutes in a chancel eight feet by eleven in front of about nine people. The recordwright says the count out loud in the porch first — the sixty-first day after the division, when she was up a bank with a shovel, and the Saturday of the ninety-fifth week, when a leaf a hundred and forty-one years old was read out instead — and says she would like it said in the porch and not in the room, because a room is a place where people are held to. The week goes: a holding of four acres passes with the number, four sheep out of the fold and two in, a boy born on the upper row, and a man of about eighty-four who is dead in the ground column with the day against him. **Then she reads one line that is not in the book — about nine feet of the bottom of the common wall, open since the day it came down, with a boundary stone in a shelf at the end of it that has nothing cut on it at all — and reads it in the room under the column with no heading on it, and about four people know what it is under and about nine hear a wall.** He said out loud in the porch that it was an ordinary week, and a boy of fourteen came out of the room and asked him what the fourth column is for, and got the right answer, and then said that he had worked out before she told him that a dead man belongs in the ground column, and had told nobody, and was saying it to a man from another county who cannot be sent for because nobody else was there. Closing: four ordinary entries, and a boy who worked a thing out alone. `521`

**522 — A woman goes down a lane and asks a question nobody in that town had ever asked, and the answer is no.** `clear` 22, a Sunday, and the band that is not a mystery but a record. Four people went up a step on Saturday and struck out the name they are each descended from, and nothing has been done about it in a day and a half, and the reason is that there is no form that says a person may be asked whether they meant a thing they did in front of about a hundred and ninety people. **He gets as far as the lower row and a woman of about thirty-five with a child on her hip tells him in about four seconds that he cannot go and ask her, and gives the reason that about nine people heard: a neighbour can ask the question and a stranger cannot, not because the neighbour is better, but because the person can tell the neighbour to go away.** And a person who has been asked nothing in about a year does it — a woman of about fifty-one of this town, in two rooms over a milliner's, who has had a copy of four pages for about a year and has never mentioned it to anybody, goes down the lane herself at about the eleventh hour and asks one question: do you want it in the book, or do you want it left on a sheet of paper in a room. **The neighbour, a woman of about seventy, says no, and gives her reason, which is that a thing this town is told in about four days is a thing somebody has to carry in a hundred years, and asks that it be written down that she said that and not that she said she was not asked.** The recordwright comes down to the bottom of the town herself, which she has done four times in nineteen years, and enters it and reads it back in the voice she uses for minutes, and says out loud that **it is the first thing in four hundred and eleven years that records a person declining something**, and that it is one woman and one name and one afternoon, and that it is not a form, not a practice and not a thing anybody else may do, and that if a second person in that county says no to a thing about her own name then what happens to that is a question to be worked out by whoever is there at the time. **THE RIGHT OF REFUSAL IS NOT RESTORED AND THIS IS NOT IT.** Closing: a sheet with four gaps in it, and an entry nobody can ask about. `522`

**523 — The finding is put in front of the two people it is about, and one of them says she knew.** `clear` 23, the seventh and last day of the ninety-sixth week, and it names the morrow. The recordwright sends for both of them on the Sunday night, separately, in about nine words each, and reads the four sentences out on a Monday. **Edith Crow, sixty-eight, is told first, at her own door, in a porch about four feet deep with no bench in it, and says she knew, and says that the woman who keeps the book could have come up her row in four minutes and did not.** She asks for one thing and it is that the finding go in with the day she was told on it and not the day it was made, and gives the reason out loud before anybody asks her anything: a day of a week comes round and a count begun in another county does not, and four days of a man not knowing is worth having on a page. **Then she says no — to two thirds of a finding about her own objection — because she had four days to think about it and had not heard the question until two minutes ago, and says in the same breath that she is not going to say the other thing now and have it be the second time.** That goes in too, in the ordinary hand, with nothing ruled and nothing headed. Harl Wenlock, forty-four, is told in the meeting-house porch with a man of about fifty, says the finding is right, and says out loud that **he had spent about nine seconds being glad of a thing he had not been told**, and that there is no form that says a man may be asked what he felt about a thing he was not told of; the recordwright does not enter the nine seconds and says that this makes the twelfth case in nineteen years in which she has been the one who has not asked, and that the twelfth is a man of forty-four and not a widow. **And a man of thirty-one says out loud, in the square, that wanting a thing to be right is the whole of what he has been doing for a hundred and twenty-three days, and that it has not once in a hundred and twenty-three days made a person safer.** Closing: a finding with the day a woman was told on it. `523`

**524 — A copy of a copy comes down the lane and it says a name, and a man of thirty-one says the sentence about a door out loud in a room in a town he is not of.** `clear` 24, the first day of the ninety-seventh week, and it names the day before as the last of the ninety-sixth. A carter who keeps a cart between Coldcombe and the toll bar comes down four miles of lane with a bundle on the tail-board and gives it to a woman of sixty-one at the corner by the meeting-house door, because she is the only person anybody has heard of who writes to other towns; a woman of about thirty at a stall in the town two days off put it into his hands and said it was for the woman in the barn at Kiln Row. It is a copy of a copy: a generation further on again, in a quicker hand, about nine mistakes and none of them corrected. **At the foot of the third page, where the first copy has nothing at all, there are four lines that are not on the copy underneath it, and the fourth says the person was asked by a man of thirty-four of Bramblefold and not by the woman, and about four people heard the question and about nine heard the answer. At the foot of the fourth page, in a third hand, there is a name, and the name is Wynd Kettle.** He wants it read out in the porch and is refused, and the reason is given in full: the copy was made in about four minutes of sitting by a woman who could not sleep, and the corrections are the only part of it anybody can check, and the four lines are not on the copy underneath them, and she cannot tell from a bundle on a Monday night whether they are true. **And then he says the sentence he has been carrying since he came to this country — a book is a door — out loud in a porch, in front of about nine people, about a town he is not of, and about four of them understand the whole of it and about nine understand that it is a man from another county who cannot be sent for talking about a town he is not of, and she says she is not going to stand in a porch and argue a man out of the half he said.** A man of eighty-one makes him say it into a room instead. Closing: a copy of a copy with a name in a third hand. `524`

**525 — Two accounts of one morning go side by side and they do not fit, and about four of nine can say exactly where.** `clear` 25, a Wednesday, which is the day a man of about seventy-two walks two miles to hear one line read, and he comes at the ninth hour in a wet coat in a dry week and stands at the end of the bench and is told *same as ever* and says *aye*, and asks again to go and look at the stake and is refused for the fourth time. Then the room is opened up and it is not opened up for a book: two sheets side by side on a table, the copy of four pages and the copy of a copy, and about nine people are told that the meeting is not a meeting and produces nothing and that anybody may go out of the door and come back in. **The place where they stop fitting is at a hundred and forty-one years.** A man of about seventy-two was asked a question in a square in a town two days off on the thirtieth day after the division, which is a Wednesday, and gave an answer, and a household of this town was asked a question by an office nine miles off a hundred and forty-one years back and gave an answer, and both answers were that the common was not used, and both were made use of, and neither of them is wrong, and the return at Aldwick does not say what the question was. **The recordwright puts a sheet down flat with her hand beside it — the first time in a hundred and twenty-five days that anybody in that county has put a document down in front of about nine people rather than read it out — and says that a thing written out from memory by a person who could not sleep is not a copy and is not a copy of a copy either, that it is a third thing, and that there is no column for a third thing in that book or in the book of returns at Aldwick or in any instrument in that county, and that she has had a day to look for a word for the different thing and has not found one and will not invent one at the fourth hour of a Wednesday in front of nine people.** He then tries to make them fit by saying the one that is less useful is an error of copying, and is refused, and the reason he is given is that he is doing it about a man who cannot be in that room and has not been asked anything. **He works the interval out loud in the room: forty-nine days, being the seventy-ninth day after the division less the thirtieth, and says there is nobody in that county who can check it but him.** Closing: two sheets that stop fitting at a hundred and forty-one years, and neither of them is wrong. `525`

**526 — THE MIDPOINT. A man of about seventy-two is asked one question in a room with the door open, and he says that he does not remember.** `clear` 26, a Thursday, which is the market, and about two hundred people are in the square and hear none of it. The asking was arranged by a woman of sixty-one in a barn at Kiln Row in about nine sentences and she says plainly that there is no form in the county of Aldwick that says a person may be asked what they remember, that she has been told no four times in four years and not told anything at all once, and that she is not going to ask him and is going to bed. **The recordwright says out loud, before she asks it, that she will not ask him what the question was and will not ask him what he said, and the reason is that a man of about seventy-two asked to remember a thing in front of about nine people is a different room from the one this town has been working in for a fortnight.** He came up the lane at about the half hour after the first on a Tuesday-shaped errand on a Thursday, and nobody told him why, and she says out loud that that is wrong. About nine people are in the chancel and he is given a chair. **He says he was in a porch here at about the ninth hour on that morning and was asked nothing, and then he says THAT HE DOES NOT REMEMBER, and asks that it be written down that he said that and not that he said he was not asked.** A man near the door says twice that the page is wrong and is told by a man of seventy-two that a page is a thing in a hand, that a name on a page is a thing somebody wrote and may be right or wrong, and that if his not-remembering is turned into a page being wrong then in a hundred years there will be a person in that town who was told that his name was taken off a thing and never was. **He then says out loud that he is not a thing that is got back, that he has come up a lane on a Thursday nobody asked him to come up, and that the man of thirty-one had made a page into an instrument and had made it into a man in a chair.** He is entered at the back of the book and the entry says the name stands and is neither withdrawn nor confirmed and that there is no column in that book for a person who does not remember and none has been ruled. And the recordwright says out loud that this town remembers a Tuesday a hundred and forty-one years back with four words on it and no order, and that the other town remembers a Wednesday fifty days back with a woman's letter in it and about two hundred people who did not sleep, and that both are correct and neither is about the other, and that there is no instrument in the county of Aldwick that can hold two correct accounts of the same morning. **Nobody thanked anybody and nobody was relieved.** Closing: a name that stands, and no instrument that can hold two. `526`

**527 — Two accounts go round one town in the same mouths, and a woman of thirty-four says nobody has ever asked her what she remembers.** `clear` 27, a Friday. The four words and the four sentences are going round the same town in the same mouths and about nine people can give you both and about nine can give you only the one they heard first, and **the number of people who can give you both is the number of people who can be asked about the other one.** The sentence about a thing said in a room being a thing about nine people turns out to be three people's: a weaver of sixty-two said it to the man of thirty-one in two rooms over a joiner's shop on the fifty-ninth day after the division, a woman of sixty-one has said it thirty-one times in four years in nine towns, and a letter of four lines from a town four hundred miles off says it too and adds that the people there think the people here ought to be asked whether they want it. **She reads that letter out in a porch, to about nine people, and nobody asks her where it is from, and it also says that they do not know what to do with a person who says no, that they have not found out in about nine years, and that there has not been a week of those nine years without one.** She says out loud that a person who has been asked nothing is the one person in that town who knows what it is like. **And then the recordwright, in a smithy, in front of about nine people, says that she is the only person in that county who can be sent for about either account, that she has been that for nineteen years, and that nobody has ever asked her what she remembers, and that there is no form that says a person may be asked what they remember, and that in about nine rooms in a hundred and twenty-seven days she would have said something and has not said it, and that there is a difference between a thing you will say when somebody asks and a thing you say because you have not been asked in nineteen years.** He asks. She says stand there and be quiet, and that is the only thing she is asking anybody for, and he stands there for about a minute. A man of eighty-one says from the chair, unasked, that a thing a person remembers is not a copy and is not a copy of a copy, and that he has been that person twice in this town and has said nothing both times. He says the two accounts will settle in about eleven years and is told that is what a book does and not what a town does. Closing: the only one anybody can ask, and nobody has asked. `527`

**528 — Two accounts are read out in full in a square and the square decides nothing, and about five of nine cannot remember which they heard first.** `clear` 28, a Saturday, and it is not a market, and the chapter says so in its third line. About two hundred people are in the square and nobody arranged it. **The first account is read by a weaver of sixty-two with his hand flat on the wool bench, who then says he is not going to say anything about them because there is nothing to say and he is tired of being a man who says things about four words; the second is read by the woman of about fifty-one of this town, who has never read anything out loud in her life, out of a sheet written in the ordinary hand by the recordwright with her own thumbprint described in brackets, because there is no form that says a document may be read out of a hand anybody else can read.** Then about nine of them are asked, in the open air, which of the two they had heard first, and about four say the first one, about four say the second, about one says the first and cannot say where he heard it, and **about five cannot remember, and that is the largest number in that square and it is a number about five people and not a number about a finding.** He says out loud that the square cannot settle it, in front of about two hundred people, and the weaver tells him the square was not asked to settle it and that the number of people who can be asked about the other account has gone up by about a hundred and ninety in about eleven minutes and that this is the most a market in a country district has ever done for anybody in that county in four hundred years, and then says the other half, which is that a man of thirty-one has not been in a room in this country that could not. **And the recordwright says out loud in the square that about nine out of about two hundred is the whole of the finding and that it is not a decision, and that there is no form in the county of Aldwick that says a thing anybody has heard has to be heard by anybody else, and that a thing that is not wrong is a thing somebody has to carry, and about two hundred people went home carrying one or both of them.** She has four sentences for what the point of a square is and says they are not good ones and that she has not got the fifth. Closing: about nine out of about two hundred. `528`

**529 — A man of fifty-four rides nine miles and makes a return that records two answers on the back of a form, and signs the back and not the front.** `clear` 29, a Sunday. He comes down with a form and not a copy of a return, and says out loud on a step, in front of about nine people, that **a return is made by an office and not asked for by a person**, that the difference between a return and an application is about eleven years, that this county has no applications and has never had any and there is no form for one, and that he rode nine miles to say it in about nine sentences and would like it entered that he said it out loud. **And that if nothing is made, then in about a year there will be a return in that office with four lines on it and the answer that the household of Kiln Row gave a hundred and forty-one years ago, and somebody will have copied it, and a person will be told that a man of about seventy-two was asked a question in a square in another town, and nobody will be able to say that both of those are in the same four lines, because the four lines do not say what the question was.** At about the second hour of the afternoon he makes the return in a room eight feet by eleven with the door open. He reads the ordinary front, and stops before the fourth line, and says out loud that the fourth line — *this office has no further particular in the matter* — is a lie of convenience and that he is not going to write it on a return about a disagreement between two towns. **He writes six lines on the BACK, because the form has no line for two, and the back of a return is not part of a return, and he signs the back and does not sign the front, because the front is a hundred and forty-one years old in its wording and he is not going to put a man's name on it about a morning he has not been in.** The sixth line says that this office has been asked by no person to make a return upon the present matter, and that this office has made one, and that this office records that it made one. **And the man of thirty-one is in the lane and cannot be in the room, because there is a form in that county that says who may be in a room when a return is made and it says the officer and the person the return is about and he is neither, and he is told that twice, out loud, in a lane, in front of about nine people, and he says he understands and that it does him no good whatever.** The back is in a coat and will go in a drawer nine miles from here and there is no form that says it may be asked for. Closing: a return on the back of a form, and a man in a lane. `529`

**530 — A boy of fourteen is asked a question in a smithy that nobody in that smithy can answer, and a third column is ruled and not headed.** `clear` 30, the seventh and last day of the ninety-seventh week, and it names the morrow as the first of the ninety-eighth. The board is still up with Friday's week on it and the dead man of about eighty-four is still in the ground column with the day against him, and the boy comes in at about the seventh hour with the chalk and the rag and does not start. **He says there is a dead man in the ground column and a gap in a wall in the next column with nothing at the top of it, and now two accounts of one morning read out in a market square, and that he does not know what to do with it, and that there are two of them now and not one.** Nobody in that smithy had asked him for any of it. When a man at the fire says put it in the ground behind the meeting-house he does not answer out loud, and about four of the nine notice that he did not answer it and about nine notice that he did not say no to it this time. **The man of thirty-one asks for the third one to be headed and is given the same answer for the fourth time and then the half that is new: if the other two were headed, a person in a hundred years would fill them in according to the heading, and would fill them in wrong, and reasonably, and out of the best intentions, and with a good hand, and the one filled in wrong is the one somebody in a hundred and forty years would go and act on. A NOT-KNOWING IN A COLUMN IS BETTER THAN A KNOWING THAT IS NOT TRUE.** The boy asks the best question anybody asked in that smithy, which is what a heading is for if it is not for telling somebody what goes in it, and she says she will answer it in a room and not in a smithy and will tell him in about four years if she has got one. She rules the column about two feet long, puts the chalk in his hand and does not take it back for the third time in nineteen years, and he writes one line about two accounts of one morning in two towns fifty-four days back. **And then she says out loud that a book with four columns and no fifth is now a book with three columns that have no heading on them, and names a fourth one nine miles off on the back of a form that she cannot see and has not asked to.** Nobody thanked the boy. Closing: two chalk lines, a boy's name under both of them, a dead man in the ground column, and a town that has not had a dated thing to count from since a leaf was struck a hundred and forty-one years back in a week that not one person in that county can name. `530`

## 0F. WHAT CHAPTERS 601–610 ADDED TO THE GROUND, AND EVERY FIGURE IN IT IS ONE A PERSON SAID OUT LOUD

**THE PLACE IS NOT A LOCKED CUPBOARD AND IT IS NOT A WALL. IT IS TWENTY-NINE CHAIRS, AND THE RECORD IS A SET OF CHAIRS.** A town of **about eleven hundred people** called **Hask**, in the **county of Kell**, **nine miles down the water from a weigh-house where a man of thirty-one stood on the last Monday of the hundred and seventh week**, on a cart road **that is under water for six weeks of the year**. **THE TOWN IS IN THE COUNTY, WHICH IS THE WHOLE OF WHAT IS TRUE ABOUT WHY A MAN WHO CANNOT LEAVE THE COUNTY IS STANDING IN IT.** Gravel bank, silt, osier and withy beds, reed. **No chalk, no hill, no stone building, no meeting-house, no chancel, no market, no weekly reading, and no such thing as a hundred of about thirty men.** The last quarter mile of the road is a plank **eleven feet long**. `601`

**A LIGHTER LIES AGAINST THE BANK AT THE END OF THE PLANK. SHE IS CALLED THE GULL, ABOUT THIRTY FEET FROM HER STEM TO HER RUDDER, AND SHE LIES LOW IN THE WATER.** A pole under her fore thwart with a stone on it so it will not walk. **There are twenty-nine chairs stood up along her starboard side in two rows, and one of them has a split in the seat mended with a piece of withy gone round and twisted twice, by a woman of twenty-nine, in her own time, unasked.** `601`

**A CHAIR IS NAMED FOR THE GROUND AND NOT FOR THE PERSON, AND THE NAMES OF THE GROUND ARE ON THE INSIDE OF THE LIDS IN A HAND OLDER THAN THE BOOK.** Eleven of the twenty-nine have a person against them in a column called the taking, because the person taking a thing on ground is the person who may speak for it. **A person holds a chair by sitting in it when the town sits, and what he says in it is said for ground he has never walked. When he is not in it the chair is stood up against the side of a lighter and it is nobody's.** `601`, `602`

**THE COUNT IS TWENTY-NINE AND IT HAS BEEN TWENTY-NINE FOR NINETEEN YEARS AND WAS TWENTY-NINE THE YEAR BEFORE, AND THE WOMAN WHO LEARNED IT OFF SAID THERE HAD BEEN THIRTY-ONE AND COULD NOT SAY WHICH TWO HAD GONE.** It has never been found in nineteen years and is not going to be invented now. `601`

**ELEVEN OF THE TWENTY-NINE HAD A NAME AGAINST THEM AND NOBODY IN THEM ON THE TUESDAY OF `601`. TWO OF THE ELEVEN ARE DEAD, ONE IS AWAY, AND EIGHT ARE PEOPLE WHO HAVE NOT COME, AND THE BOOK WILL NOT WRITE THE WORD FOR THAT, BECAUSE IF IT DOES, IN ABOUT FOUR YEARS EVERY PERSON WHO GOES TO THE FAIR FOR A WEEK WILL HAVE IT WRITTEN AGAINST THEM.** On `603` the reed was filled and it is TEN. `601`, `602`, `603`, `604`, `610`

**THE CASE, AND IT IS THE FIRST CONCRETE CASE OUT OF THE SUCCESSION DISPUTE: ORIEL SAYER, FORTY-NINE WHEN HE STOPPED, HELD THE REED AND HAS BEEN DEAD SINCE BEFORE THE LAST FLOOD CAME UP, AND NOBODY WROTE THE DAY DOWN BECAUSE THERE IS NO FORM IN THAT TOWN THAT ASKS ANYBODY TO WRITE A DAY DOWN.** No wife, no children. His people are in a yard at **Sallowcross four days off** and there is a brother there, **WREN SAYER**, who went away in a lighter **about eleven years ago** and has not been heard of since and has a chair at the other end of the same book. The two of them are the two of the eleven who are not in that town and not dead. `602`

**AND A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-ONE SAID OUT LOUD, IN ABOUT NINE PEOPLE'S HEARING, THAT THIS IS NOT THE RIGHT OF REFUSAL.** *A refusal is a person saying no.* **Oriel Sayer is not in a position to say no, and if that is written down in a book as a refusal then in about four years there will be a form in this county that says a man who does not answer has refused.** **A PERSON WHO CANNOT BE ASKED IS A SMALLER THING AND AN UGLIER THING AND IT IS NOT ANYBODY'S NO**, and she would not stand in a chair and say no for a man who is dead and did not ask her. `602`

**THERE ARE THREE WAYS A CHAIR GETS FILLED AND THERE IS NO FOURTH.** A son takes his father's chair, which is what about nine people have done about nine times. The man who does the work sits in the chair of the ground he works on, which has happened about four times and every one of the four times he was somebody's son. **And anybody who stands on that deck and says yes in front of about nine people has the chair until somebody says no — which has happened about nine times in nineteen years, and two of the two that were not a son are the only two lines in that book anybody can tell you were not a son.** `602`

**THE APPOINTMENT, AND IT BINDS NOBODY, AND THE REASON IS NOT THE PLACE.** **There is no form in the county of Kell that could make any person in that town do anything about it, and the same is true of the refusal to be put out of a chair, which has never once been done in the open in two hundred and thirty years.** A **woman of twenty-nine said yes on the Thursday of `603`** in the open air, in about nine people's hearing, and the day went into a taking column with a pencil and nothing at the top of the page, and **about four of the ten on the deck were on the deck and about nine were on the bank and every one of the nine heard a name said that they had not been given.** `603`

**THE THIRD WAY OPEN TO ANYBODY IS ALSO THE THIRD WAY OPEN AGAINST ANYBODY, AND A WOMAN OF TWENTY-NINE PROPOSED ON `604` TO STRIKE ALL TEN IN ONE MORNING IN THE OPEN AND COUNT THE OBJECTIONS, AND NOBODY SETTLED IT.** `604`

**THE MONEY, AND IT IS DERIVED IN A MOUTH AND IT IS CHECKABLE.** **A penny a head on that landing and a penny a cart · about eight hundred heads and about two hundred and forty carts in a year, which is a thousand and forty pence, which is four pounds six shillings and eightpence · the book takes three pounds of that, which is ruling, binding, lamp oil and copying it out · what is left is three hundred and twenty pence, which is one pound six shillings and eightpence, between two people · a hundred and sixty pence, which is THIRTEEN SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE, and that is a year of the woman who keeps the book.** A bed and bread in that town is a penny and a penny, which is two pence a day, which is seven hundred and thirty pence in three hundred and sixty-five days, which is **three pounds and tenpence**, and the year of the woman who keeps the book costs a person less than a year of being bedded and fed. A lighter passage is three shillings and sixpence. `602`

**AND THE ONLY FIGURE ANY PERSON IN THAT TOWN CAN CHECK HIM AGAINST IS ONE HE SAID OUT LOUD TWICE.** A cart at a crossroads nine miles off is four shillings; two nights of a bed and bread at a penny and a penny is four pence; under two pounds on Monday morning of the last week is a pound and fifteen shillings and eightpence on the Tuesday, and that is the whole of what he has spent. `601`

**THE ANCHORING, AND IT IS HALF PAID AND IT IS UNCHANGED.** A year of his own future from the crossing of that block of limestone to the crossing after it; he cannot leave the county until the water goes over that cut; he does not know when; **he can tell anybody what the water is doing at that cut to about a quarter of an inch without looking at anything, and he cannot tell them when the water will be over it, and there is no figure for the counting and nobody can check it.** It is one water. `605`, `606`, `607`, `610`

**A MAN OF THIRTY-EIGHT ASKED HIM WHERE HE CAME FROM AT ABOUT THE FOURTH HOUR ON THE SEVENTH DAY OF THAT WEEK, IN ABOUT NINE PEOPLE'S HEARING AND WITH NO PREPARATION, AND HE ANSWERED IN FOUR CHECKABLE THINGS, AND A WOMAN OF TWENTY-NINE SAID OUT LOUD THAT NONE OF THEM WAS AN ANSWER AND THAT NOBODY ON THAT DECK HAD ASKED THE QUESTION.** The four: the cart and the pound and the day; the stone and the year; the counting; and a settlement of about nine hundred words on a wall nine miles up the water that he put four things into. **It went out into a bank of about nine people, and about nine of the people in a town of about eleven hundred had it by the fourth hour and about nine of them have it a different way.** `607`

**THE NAME, AND IT IS THE ONE SPOKEN AT `600`, AND IT HAS NOW TRAVELLED THIRTY-ONE MILES OF TOWNSHIP AND NINE MILES OF WATER.** A man of thirty-eight says at `608` that he was in that weigh-house on the last Monday of the hundred and seventh week with a load of boards that never came, and that a thing that was not a person said four words in the air and the four words were its own name, and that **about four of the people in that room heard it and about nine heard nothing at all and thought for about nine seconds that it was the wind in a crane-beam.** He is the only one of those four in Hask and he is nine miles up this water from the other three. **IT HAS NOT BEEN ADDRESSED AND IT HAS NOT BEEN ANSWED AND IT IS NOT ADDRESSED OR ANSWED IN ANY OF THE TEN CHAPTERS.** `608`

**AND A WOMAN OF FORTY-FOUR WHO KEEPS THE BOOK STOPPED COMING UP THE PLANK, AND SHE WAS RIGHT, AND THE SPLIT IS NOT BETWEEN TWO OPINIONS.** *The split is between people who were on that deck and people who were not.* **Every one of the first is right about their half and every one of the second is right about their half, and there is no way of putting them in one room again without one of the two being asked something they have already been asked once in the dark.** `608`

**AND A DOCUMENT THAT BINDS NOBODY HAS BEEN USED ONCE, BY A PERSON WHO HAS ACTUALLY READ IT.** **HESTON BRAKE, FORTY-ONE, OF A TOWN TWO DAYS OFF IN THE COUNTY OF ASHENFIELD, STOOD UNDER THAT LAMP ON A SATURDAY MORNING ABOUT SIX WEEKS AGO WITH A LOAD OF BOARDS THAT NEVER CAME AND HAD ABOUT NINE HUNDRED WORDS READ AT HIM BY A BOY OF FOURTEEN, AND WENT BACK ON PURPOSE FOUR WEEKS AGO AND HAD IT READ AT HIM AGAIN.** He quoted the line about the year back at the man who put four things into the settlement, and used the settlement's own statement that **no person in the county of Kell is bound by one word of it** to say that there is therefore nothing in that document which stops him putting his own name against a chair in Ashenfield and never coming back to take it off. He carried a copy of one page two days in a bag with nothing else in it. **It binds nobody and it has now been used once, and nobody asked it to be used, and the man whose line it is cannot put the sentence right without saying more.** `609`

**THE QUESTION ASKED ONCE AT `610` AND NOT ANSWERED, AND IT IS NOT THE RIGHT OF REFUSAL AND IT IS NOT THE NEW QUESTION THE VOLUME OWES AT ITS END:** *whether a person who has said yes to a chair may take the yes back when they find out what is on the chair.* A woman of twenty-nine asked it in about nine people's hearing. A man of thirty-one got about four sentences out of an answer and then stopped out loud and said why. `610`

**A SECRET THAT IS A THING HE WAS TOLD AND CANNOT PASS ON.** The woman who keeps the book told him, at the after end of a lighter, with her back to the two of them, that **she has sat in one of the empty chairs twice, on a Tuesday afternoon in front of about nine people, and got out of it both times before the sitting was done, and has written nothing down either time, and there is no day anywhere in that book against those two afternoons.** She asked him not to say it out loud, because if it is said it is a thing a person can do, and then any of the ten can be done by anybody who says they are minding it. `601`

**NEW DISTANCES, ENTERED ON THE DAY THEY WERE INVENTED, AND EVERY ONE IS SPOKEN IN A MOUTH:** **Hask is nine miles down the water from the weigh-house at Withermere and the last quarter mile of the cart road is a plank eleven feet long · a yard at Sallowcross is four days off · a fair at a crossroads four miles off, twice a year, and it is not fair week · the county of Ashenfield is two days off, in another county · the hedge above that landing is where a woman of twenty-nine cuts withies.

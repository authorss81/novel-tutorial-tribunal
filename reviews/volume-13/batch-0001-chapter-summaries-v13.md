**601 — THE FIRST PLACE, AND IT IS A SET OF CHAIRS, AND A MAN WHO CANNOT LEAVE IS STANDING IN IT.** `clear` 101, the first day of the hundred and eighth week, a Tuesday, and his forty-seventh day in the county of Kell. A town of about eleven hundred people called Hask, nine miles down the water from a weigh-house, on a cart road that is under water for six weeks of the year, and the last quarter mile of it is an eleven-foot plank. A lighter called the Gull, about thirty feet, lying against the bank, with twenty-nine chairs stood up along her side in two rows and nobody in any of them, and one of them mended with a withy by a woman of twenty-nine in her own time. A woman of forty-four called Orla Fennimore keeps the book, has kept it nineteen years, and is the only person anybody in that town can be sent for about a chair. A man of thirty-eight called Garrin Tolley poled the boat, cut the reed since he was nine, and said out loud that a chair is a person who says something for ground he is not standing on. Eleven of the twenty-nine have a name against them and nobody in them: two dead, one away, eight people who have not come, and the book will not write the word for that. He came it in two days with a bad leg, paid four shillings for a cart at a crossroads to a man who did not ask his name, and has a pound and fifteen shillings and eightpence of under two pounds. He could not leave the county and he was standing in that room, and about nine people within nine feet of the fact had not been told it. He is given a true thing he cannot pass on: the woman who keeps the book has sat in one of the empty chairs twice and got out of it both times and written nothing down either time, and there is no day anywhere in that book against those two afternoons, and she asked him not to say it out loud.

**602 — THE FIRST CONCRETE CASE OUT OF THE SUCCESSION DISPUTE, AND IT IS NOT A REFUSAL.** `clear` 102, the second day of the hundred and eighth week, a Wednesday, and his forty-eighth day. Oriel Sayer, forty-nine when he stopped, held the reed and has been dead since before the last flood came up, and nobody wrote the day down because there is no form in that town that asks anybody to write a day down. No wife, no children. His people are in a yard at Sallowcross four days off and there is a brother there, Wren Sayer, gone up the water in a lighter about eleven years ago and not heard of since, with a chair at the other end of the same book. A woman of seventy-one called Eliza Lark, who keeps a house on the low side of the landing that is not his and has not been for eleven years and who is not of his family, came up the plank with wet sleeves and said out loud in about nine people's hearing that this is not the right of refusal: a refusal is a person saying no, and a person who cannot be asked is a smaller thing and an uglier thing and is not anybody's no, and she will not put her no on his line either. There are three ways a chair gets filled and no fourth: a son, the man who does the work, and anybody who says yes in front of about nine people, which has happened about nine times in nineteen years and twice by somebody who was not a son. And he is told in the same hour that he is the only person in that room nobody can be sent for, and he says nothing about what that is. The keeper of the book prices her own year out loud with the working: a penny a head and a penny a cart, about eight hundred heads and about two hundred and forty carts, a thousand and forty pence, the book takes three pounds, what is left is three hundred and twenty pence, and a hundred and sixty of that is hers, which is thirteen shillings and fourpence, against a bed and bread of two pence a day and seven hundred and thirty pence in three hundred and sixty-five days, which is three pounds and tenpence.

**603 — SOMEBODY SAYS YES IN THE OPEN AND THE DAY GOES IN A BOOK AND THE WHOLE OF IT BINDS NOBODY.** `clear` 103, the third day of the hundred and eighth week, a Thursday, and his forty-ninth day. A woman of twenty-nine called Nell Prince, who cuts withies above the landing, comes up the plank with a hoop over her arm and says yes to the reed in about nine seconds, and gives her reason in a mouth: because there is nobody holding the reed and the cutting of it is nobody's business, and she is twenty-nine and is not going anywhere in a hurry, and that is the only reason she has offered. He does not ask her whether she wants it, and she tells him so in about four seconds, and asks for it to be written down somewhere that is not his book. He then asks the question nobody has asked in nineteen years, which is who in a town of about eleven hundred people has not heard what was said on that deck, and is refused a figure in a mouth: two hundred and ninety-one is a figure off a market nine miles up the water and does not travel, and she will not invent a third number out of a number that belongs to another place, because a book with that figure in it is a book that is about counting instead of about people. A man of thirty-eight then says out loud that a lighter has no door, no wall and no roof, and that about four of them were on the deck that morning and about nine were on the bank, and that every one of the nine heard a name said that they had not been given. And the count is eleven in the morning and ten in the book, and the count is spoken.

**604 — THE SECOND MOUTH, THE SAME TEN CHAIRS, AND A DIFFERENT SHAPE, AND NOBODY SETTLES IT.** `clear` 104, the fourth day of the hundred and eighth week, a Friday. There is no market in Hask and no reading and no hundred; a fair at a crossroads four miles off twice a year, and it is not fair week. The woman of twenty-nine comes back with a second version of the same case, days after her yes, and says the ten is not ten things, it is one thing, and that the trouble is that a yes in the open will put a person in a chair and a no in the open will put a person out of one, and there is nothing whatever to stop it and no form in that county that says a man may not be put out of a chair, and the reason there is no form is that in two hundred and thirty years it has never once been done in the open. She proposes to strike all ten in one morning on that deck and count the objections, and about nine people in the room have the first shape of the case and about nine have the second, and the two nines have not asked each other. He tries to hold both in one hand and is told by her that they are not one question with more of it. She counts twenty-nine chairs twice with her hand in about nine seconds and stops at the ninth both times, and the ninth and tenth are the ones she cuts withies off the land of, and she is in neither and did not know that on Wednesday.

**605 — THE CHAPTER THE BAND EXISTS FOR: HE OFFERS TO BE THE INSTRUMENT AND AN ORDINARY PERSON REFUSES HIM FOR A REASON ABOUT HIM.** `clear` 105, the fifth day of the hundred and eighth week, a Saturday, and his fifty-first day. There is no reading in Hask on a Saturday. He offers in nine sentences, with the working, to sit in all ten chairs, and gives his reason in a mouth: a thing that cannot be sent for is the only instrument nobody can be leaned on, and he has offered in nine counties and about four people in each of them said yes. The woman of twenty-nine refuses him in four sentences, and says first that she is not refusing him because he is a stranger, and then gives the reason, and the reason is about him and not about the case: every one of those ten needs a person who can be sent for, and he is the only person on that deck who cannot be, and in about four years somebody in that town will be stood up on that plank against his name and there will be nobody to stand up, and a person who cannot be asked is not a clean thing to put in a book, because a book is a list of people who can be found and she has been for nineteen years on thirteen shillings and fourpence. Then the standing cost comes out of his own mouth, because it is the only figure about him anybody in that town can be checked against, and it turns out to be the truest thing anybody said about him there. At about the sixth hour a man of thirty-eight gives him a boat-hook, five feet of ash with an iron crook polished on one side only, because he is standing on a deck with his hands doing nothing, and he holds it and does not put it down.

**606 — THE ANCHORING IS TESTED BY SOMEBODY WHO IS NOT HIM, AND IT IS NOT A POWER.** `clear` 106, the sixth day of the hundred and eighth week, a Sunday, and his fifty-second day. The Gull is brought up onto the gravel because the water has fallen about a foot, and there is a green oak post at the top of the landing with about eleven years of notches in it. A man of thirty-eight sets a test and says out loud before he sets it that he is testing him and that about four people knew he would and about nine did not. He asks what the water is doing at a block of limestone nine miles up the river, and the man of thirty-one answers in about four seconds without looking at anything: a thumbnail's width under a cut, standing, not over it since before the light on the Friday, and about a quarter of an inch is about a day of falling. He then says the second half out loud, that he has been counting from that water since a Thursday morning in a room over a stable, that he did not choose it and cannot stop it, that it is one water and not any other, and that there is no figure for it and nobody can check it. Two women say out loud, in different words, that a thing that can be asked about goes in a book, and that it is not a power and anybody who says it is a power is wrong about it in a way that will cost somebody, and the second half of it is worse than the first. And she puts the cost where it can be seen: there is a lighter drawing her after end in about two inches a minute and he has not seen it, and he has been asked about a river nine miles off and answered in four seconds, and he has lost a thing happening four feet in front of him for about a quarter of an hour and cannot be told when it is happening. And a notch is cut in the post, and the man of thirty-eight says he would rather have the post, because a post is here on the Tuesday and he is not.

**607 — THE FIRST TIME SOMEBODY ASKS HIM WHERE HE CAME FROM, AND HE ANSWERS IN FOUR CHECKABLE THINGS AND NONE OF THEM IS AN ANSWER.** `clear` 107, the seventh and last day of the hundred and eighth week, a Monday, and his fifty-third day. At about the fourth hour with the lamp lit and about nine people on the deck putting the Gull back into the reach, a man of thirty-eight asks him, without any preparation, and says he has been not asking since Saturday. He answers in four things and says in advance that he will not be able to answer it properly: the cart and the pound and the day; the year against a block of limestone nine miles up the water and the not knowing; the counting from that one water; and a settlement of about nine hundred words on a wall that he put four things into, and that he is the only person alive who has been told that a thing was said in that room in about nine people's hearing on a Monday morning and nobody in that room could answer what had been asked of it. He says two of the four are about a stone and two are about a thing he did nine days ago, and that none of them is the answer. Then the woman of twenty-nine says out loud that nobody in that room asked the question, that she was holding the book and did not stop him and a man of forty-four did not stop him, that there was no vote and no method and no third way of getting a thing said in the open, and that this is not a trick and she is not blaming him. It goes out into a bank of about nine people who have a face lit from below in the dark and not the words.

**608 — THE SPLIT, AND IT IS NOT A BETRAYAL AND NOT A MISUNDERSTANDING AND IT IS NOT SETTLED BY A SPEECH.** `clear` 108, the first day of the hundred and ninth week, a Tuesday, and his fifty-fourth day. The woman of forty-four who keeps the book does not come up the plank, and about four people on that landing notice and about nine do not. A man of thirty-eight then says out loud that he was in a weigh-house nine miles up the water on the last Monday of a week with a load of boards that never came, and that a thing that was not a person said four words in the air and the four words were its own name, that about four of the people in that room heard it and about nine heard nothing at all and thought for about nine seconds that it was the wind in a crane-beam, and that he is the only one of those four in Hask and nine miles up the water from the other three. He has been six days late in saying it, and says it now because a thing he heard in that room has been in that town since Monday night. Then the woman who keeps the book says why she is not coming, from a second boat alongside, and her reason is a book: a book is a list of people who can be found, and if she comes up that plank then the one thing that town can be sent for is a woman who stands in a place with about nine people behind her, and in about four years there will be a line in a book with her name at the top of it, and she has spent nineteen years being the other half of that line for other people. She then says the true thing about the split, and nobody in that room can answer it: the split is not between two opinions, it is between people who were on that deck and people who were not, and every one of the first is right about their half and every one of the second is right about theirs, and there is no way of putting them in one room again without one of the two being asked something twice. He says one sentence in his own defence and it goes to a bank, and there is nothing to add to it.

**609 — A DOCUMENT THAT BINDS NOBODY IS QUOTED BACK AT HIM BY A PERSON WHO HAS ACTUALLY READ IT.** `clear` 109, the second day of the hundred and ninth week, a Wednesday, and his fifty-fifth day. A man of forty-one called Heston Brake, of a town two days off in the county of Ashenfield, comes up the water in the Gull with eleven hundredweight of iron in her, gets off at the landing, does not go up into the town, and asks to sit on the deck for an hour. He quotes the line about the year back at the man who put four things into the settlement, and uses the settlement's own statement that no person in the county of Kell is bound by one word of it to say that there is therefore nothing in that document which stops him putting his own name against a chair in his own town in another county and never coming back to take it off. He asks whether he may leave it there, and the man of thirty-one says he did not write the line, and that is not a correction, and everybody works that out in about four seconds and nobody says so for about nine minutes, and then he says he cannot put the sentence right without saying more, and will not say more. A man of forty-one is then asked what the boy looked like, and says fourteen and thin, and that he turned the slate over at the end of every run of the small figures so he could see what was coming, and that he counted about nine of them himself while the boy read and they were all the same size and none of them meant anything.

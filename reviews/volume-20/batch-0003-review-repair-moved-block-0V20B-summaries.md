## 0V20B. ⚠ THE CHAPTER SUMMARIES FOR VOLUME 20 BAND 1, CHAPTERS `951`–`960`, ⚠ AND ⚠ **EVERY FIGURE IN THEM TAKEN FROM THE CHAPTER IT IS IN AND NOT FROM A PLAN AND NOT FROM A PROMPT**

**⚹⚾ TEN CHAPTERS, TEN SUMMARIES, IN CHAPTER ORDER. ⚠ NOT ONE OF THEM RESOLVES ANYTHING, ⚠ NOT ONE OF THEM THANKS A PERSON, ⚠ AND NOT ONE OF THEM PRINTS THE FIGURE OF HOW MANY THINGS ARE LEFT ON THAT LANDING, ⚠ WHICH IS OWED IN BAND 5.** ⚠

**`951` — Sixteen Inches And It Holds, And A Man Who Gave The Asking Up For Ten Mornings Is On That Step Again This Morning.** The man of fifty-four comes up the cart road after ten mornings off and stops at the foot of the steps with the barrow and does not come up, and says out loud that nobody came up that road to ask him why, and gives his own figure — two hundred and thirtieth, with the subtraction and the *and then one* — and nobody asked him for it. The man of forty-three gives sixteen inches that hold, about sixty of ninety under, three days of the coming back, and refuses a fourth in front of everybody. He names the wedge and says he did not put it in, that it has not been touched, and that he wants the whole landing to hear him say both refusals before anybody thinks of them. **The man of fifty-four then says the thing the volume opens from: he is not asking for a day any more; he is asking, every morning, in the same words, whether there is one thing in that county a man who cannot read is wanted for, once, with nothing kept against it.** The man who moves chairs comes up past the wall and reports a thing on a doorstep six hundred yards up that road, says nobody put it down, and says he is not going to ask the woman whether she took it. The man of thirty-one says the one thing nobody can take off him is that he can walk down a bank and leave, proposes to go and look at the thing on the doorstep, and then refuses his own proposal out loud. The man of forty-three sweeps the top step, nobody answers anybody, nothing that had been put down is touched, and the man of thirty-one walks up the road past the green gate and does not stop at the step with the corner chipped off the top left.

**`952` — Sixteen Inches And About Sixty Of Those Ninety Steps Under, And A Woman Has Come Half A Mile Up To Say She Is Not Going To Be Asked Whether She Took It.** A woman of forty-five comes up the low side along the edge of that drain with her sleeves wet, puts her mat on the bottom step, says there is a thing on the stone her tub stands on and that she did not put it there, and says in one breath that she is not going to be asked whether she took it and that there is no giver in it and that nobody put that down. **She says she does not need nine people to carry it and that she is not going to say how many people it would be.** The man of forty-three gives sixteen inches that hold, about sixty under, two days, and says the thing on her stone was not put down by whoever works at her tub because the only person who works at her tub is standing on his step with her sleeves wet, and says it has not been touched and is not going to be turned over or weighed in the hand or read out loud or taken inside or put back. The boy of thirteen gives his own count and says he is not going to ask her either and that he noticed everybody being careful. **The man of thirty-one proposes that somebody write the places down and put them on a wall, and the woman who reads the ninth run refuses him in a sentence with the working given: she is refusing him the day, and the day is the part that turns a place into something a person can be asked about.** She tells him to write every stone he can walk to and then to look at six of them and work out which was the important one, and says it is the one he did not write. He says he will not write it down anywhere either. Nobody is thanked; nothing is touched.

**`953` — Sixteen Inches And One Day Of The Coming Back, And A Man Of Fifty-Eight Has Come A Mile Up The High Side To Say There Is A Thing On A Hand-Rail.** A man of fifty-eight comes up the high side with coal dust in both palms, says he wants to be careful about the order, and says first the nothing thing: there is something on his hand-rail, he did not put it there, and it has not been touched. Then the other thing: he is not going to ask the woman at that yard whether she took it, and he wants it noticed that he has said the whole of it before anybody works out that they want to ask her. **He says a rail cut for a man before he gets there is not a kindness and not a gift and not a favour and nobody has to be thanked for it, because the person who cut that rail did not do it for him.** The man of forty-three gives three figures, says a rail cut for a man before he gets to it has no giver in it that the man who cut it can use, and then refuses to say it twice in one morning and refuses to let anybody call the wedge a rail, because nothing asked for it and the step was doing its work without it. The man of fifty-four asks his question and is refused. **The man of thirty-one asks to be allowed to stand his weight on the fortieth step and tell him what it does, and the man with the broom refuses with the working given: the only experiment that would answer the question anybody wants answered needs a person to come along and decide they are the one who does it, and there is nobody on that landing with the standing to decide it, and if he stands on that step this morning then the man who does it is him, and it will be him for the rest of the year.** The man of thirty-one says that means there is not one thing in the morning any of them can put down, and that he has spent nineteen years wanting somebody in the county to ask him a question and the moment has been the wrong moment every morning. Nothing was pulled, turned over, weighed in the hand, read aloud, carried inside, put back where it was, or used.

**`954` — The Water Came Off That Bank At About The Sixth Hour And All Ninety Of Those Steps Were Walkable By About The Seventh, And A Man Said Out Loud That There Is No Figure Of Inches.** The water comes off, all ninety steps are walkable by the seventh hour, and that is the thirty-sixth time. **The man of forty-three says the absence out loud before anybody asks him for it — there is no figure of inches, not one, and no figure of steps under, and no figure of days, because when all ninety are out there is no coming back to count out from — and says he is not going to round it and not going to hedge it and not going to give a feeling of it instead, because a feeling of it is still a figure.** He says he has said it before eleven because a man who is honest walks out early with his mouth shut and everybody decides he came out to look at the water, and there is no water. He sweeps eighty-nine steps, goes round the fortieth without slowing and without putting the broom on it, and says why: he is not going to get a figure for how long that step has been steady, because the only way is to count back, and counting back is a figure, and the day he has one, somebody has a figure to put beside the thing in its seam and make a sentence out of them. The man who moves chairs reports a thing on a trough a mile and a hundred yards up the high side and says he will not ask the woman about it and will not go up a second time to look. **The woman who reads the ninth run enters the morning, finds nothing in it, and says a morning with no figure on it is the worst morning there is for asking anybody anything, and refuses to spend her one question on it.** The man of thirty-one says his whole county figure out loud, that it is four hundred days and a whole figure, and says it is a figure of days, not one of his, and that he will go down the bank before anybody finishes a sentence about what it means. Nobody said anything about four hundred.

**`955` — Four Inches On The Top Step At About The Seventh Hour, About Ninety Of Those Ninety Steps Under, Seven Days Of The Coming Back, And A Man Who Cannot Read Says So Out Loud.** Four inches, about ninety of ninety under, seven days of the coming back. The man of fifty-four, who cannot read, says **ninety-six weeks** out loud himself, before anybody works it out for him, and says he wants it said by him and not by one of them, because he is the man who cannot read and they are the people who can. He says he does not know what a whole number of weeks is for and will not be the man who says he does not. A man of sixty-one who cannot come down a bank comes as far as the wall and reports a thing on the outside sill under the front window he works at with his hands, half a mile down the low side past where that drain turns, and says whoever put that thing on his sill does not work at his window, and that they are not going to put another one there tomorrow morning, because a person who gets one thing left on it every morning is being worked on. **The boy of thirteen notices that about nine of them have been standing near the step with the chip and that nobody has put anything near it, says he is not asking anybody to tell him he got it right, and says that if anybody does he is going down into the lane until they have all stopped.** The man of thirty-one is offered a whole number of weeks three times by three different people, refuses all three out loud in order, and says the plainest thing he has said in a year: there is a whole figure on that landing this morning and the man who cannot read has already said it so that nobody could help him with it, and he is not going to be the one who gives it to anybody. He then says the whole of the house out loud, in his own mouth, for nothing: a thing put down where somebody works is not a given thing, it has no giver in it, the person who put it down does not work there, and nobody on that step may say who put the last one down, and it may not be said either, and that is not a thing he worked out. Nobody thanked him for it and nobody agreed with it.

**`956` — Eight Inches On The Top Step At About The Seventh Hour, About Eighty Of Those Ninety Steps Under, Six Days Of The Coming Back, And A Man Said One Whole Thing Out Loud On Purpose.** Eight inches, about eighty under, six days. **The man of forty-three says a fourth thing out loud for the first time in nineteen years and says why: nineteen years of saying nothing about that wedge is a habit and not a rule, and a habit is a thing you do because you did it last time.** He gives the rule and counts it on his fingers — not pulled, not turned over, not weighed in the hand, not read out loud, not carried inside, not put back where it was, not used — and says it is seven things and not a figure of anybody and not a fifth figure off his stone, and says he wants it known that it is a rule and not a virtue, because a virtue is a thing you are praised for. He gives the reason and says it is not moral: pulling it out is the only way anybody would ever find out what it is holding, and that is an experiment, and an experiment has to be run by somebody with standing to run it, and there is nobody on that landing with standing to run it and there is not going to be one. **The woman who reads the ninth run has written a place down that week — one place, no day, no morning, no who — and will not say which, because the day she says a place out loud it stops being a place and becomes a thing people can walk to, and whoever put something there has then got a road to their own front door whether they wanted one or not.** She says she gave her one question up and chose badly. **The man who moves chairs then says the whole of the ordinary form out loud, and says he has been doing it since he was sixteen and did not know there was a word for it and is not after a word for it: he carries a chair in, puts it down where somebody works, is not in the house, does not work in it, is not coming back in the morning to put another one down, and the person it went in for is not going to be asked whether they took it.** Nobody thanked him. The man of thirty-one says the plainest thing said on that step in nineteen years — that asking is the only thing left in the world that costs him nothing, and that a man who asks every morning is nobody and always was — and the man with the broom does not agree with it and does not correct it, and sends him down the bank.

**`957` — Twelve Inches, About Seventy Of Those Ninety Steps Under, Five Days Of The Coming Back, And A Man Of Fifty-Four Asked Again In The Same Words And Was Refused In The Same Way.** Twelve inches, about seventy under, five days, and the man of forty-three says the last day of a week is when a keeper is most tempted to give a man something extra and that he is not going to. The man of fifty-four asks at the hour he said he would, in the words he used, and is refused, and nobody tells him the question is good or foolish or owed an answer. **The man who moves chairs says out loud, in his own mouth, that he is not thanking him for asking, and that a man asking every morning in the same words at the same hour with nothing after it is the only thing he has seen in eleven years of carrying furniture into other people's houses that came into the house and got left where it was put — and that he is not going to do anything about it and the man is not going to get anything out of him.** The man of thirty-one, who has had something ready since Friday, proposes to make one morning a week out of the two of them — his unanswered standing offer and the other man's unanswered asking, said at the same hour in front of about nine ears, neither asking the other for anything — **and the man of fifty-four refuses it in a sentence, and says he has heard a man make this offer before, that he did it with a chair once and I did it, and that what it turned into was two men who both wanted something standing in the same place and him with his hand on a barrow and nobody able to say afterwards which of them had been the reason the other was there. He says: you want me to be in it.** The woman who reads the ninth run says no one has come up that road all week, that she is not going to say the woman of forty-four is not coming because she says she is not going to be asked again, and asks that nobody go up that road to say anything about what she has just said. The week closes with a man on that landing who has been asked nothing, told nothing and given nothing, standing at the bottom of his own steps.

**`958` — First Day Of The Hundred And Fifty-Ninth Week, And A Man Described One Thing In The Same Words He Uses Every Morning, And A Man With A Broom Told Him To Stop, Then Let Him Go On.** Sixteen inches, a full bank, four days of the coming back, about sixty under, and the man of forty-three makes sure nobody goes away thinking a full bank is a kind of morning: it is four days, it is not a good morning and not the day anything gets decided on, it is the day the water has been in the same place for as long as it is going to be in the same place, and he has been waiting for it for nineteen years and has never got anything out of the waiting. **The man who moves chairs describes the wedge in the exact words the man with the broom used on the first morning of the week, and gives the argument for it: if a thing is put down and nobody touches it and nobody says who put it there, then after a while there is no way on earth for anybody to tell whether it is still being left or whether it has been there since the beginning, and when those two stop being different the whole of what happened has stopped having a shape, and a thing with no shape is a thing somebody can put a name on tomorrow.** The man with the broom calls it the cleverest thing said on that step since he came to it, says he has no answer to it, says he will not tell him he is right because he is half right, and sets one rule: if the thing is going to be described it is described by him, once a week, in his own words — and says out loud that nobody is going to thank the other man for carrying it and nobody is going to tell him he got it right. The man who cannot see well says he can tell the bank is full from the sound and has not been able to look at it since the water came in, and nobody noticed, and he would like it left that way; **nobody said what it was worth and nobody said what would stop him.** The man of thirty-one says out loud that tomorrow is ninety-nine and the morning after is a whole figure, that he is not going to think about it, that he would rather everybody kept saying the four days, and that the four days are the only figure he has been handed in four hundred and four days that nobody has asked him a question about.

**`959` — Sixteen Inches And It Holds, About Sixty Of Those Ninety Steps Under, Three Days Of The Coming Back, And A Chair On That Landing That The Man Who Moves Chairs Says He Did Not Put There.** Sixteen inches that hold, about sixty under, three days. **The man of forty-three finds a chair on the landing he has swept for nineteen years, about a yard from where he works, not in the way of anything he does, not there at six o'clock, and he does not sit on it and sweeps around it, and gives no fourth figure about it.** The man who moves chairs comes up on foot and stands at the top of the steps and looks at his own trade standing on a landing he has never stood on, and says before anybody can say it for him that he has carried about nine hundred chairs into other people's houses and has never once put one down on that landing and did not put that one there and that the man who did it does not work here — and asks the whole landing to hear him not say who. He says the rest of his trade out loud: he carries it in, puts it down where somebody works, is not in the house, does not work in that house, is not coming back the next morning to put another one down, and the person it went in for is not going to be asked whether they took it. **Then the man of forty-three asks the only question anybody on that landing had asked anybody all week — what the chair is for — and gets the answer in a sentence: it is not for anything, it is not for you and it is not for me, it is not a gift and not a kindness and not a reward for nineteen years of sweeping, because if it were a reward then somebody had to decide to give it and nobody decided anything, and a thing left beside a person is not a version of what that person has wanted.** He says he would rather be owed by nobody than be owed by a man who holds a landing. The man with the broom does not thank him for it and says why: a thing put down beside you with nothing attached to it is not a gift, because a gift comes from somebody who is there, and that is the part he has not had in nineteen years on that step. **The boy of thirteen says he is not going to tell anybody what he thinks the chair is for and that if he is asked he will say he does not know, because the first time somebody asked him what a thing was for he was ten and he answered anyway**, and says nobody has told him he had it right and that he would rather it were noticed that nobody did it while he could have heard. The chair stayed where it had been put. Nobody said who had put it there, and nobody on that landing ever said.

**`960` — Sixteen Inches And It Holds, Two Days Of The Coming Back, And A Man Walked Down Ninety Of Those Steps And Gave A Figure That Is About Steps And Not About Anything Else.** Sixteen inches that hold, about sixty under, two days. **The man of forty-three goes down his own bank at about the eighth hour with his hands empty, puts his weight on each of the ninety the way a man does who does not know what is in any seam, comes back up, and gives a fourth figure: fourteen of those ninety move under a man's weight and seventy-six do not.** He says it before he has finished the sentence that it is a figure of steps and not a figure of faults, and that in about nine days somebody on that step is going to write that word down next to his fourteen and then the two will never come apart. He gives the working — you do not get fourteen by looking, you get it by going down, on the outside of the heel first — and says he did not count them and did not write them down and did not keep a list, because the day he has anything in his coat that says which ones move is the day a man comes up with a question about it and he is a man with a list. **He then says what the figure is not: it is not how many things are on that landing, he has not counted that and neither is going to, and he would rather it stayed not come up with.** The man of thirty-one gives his four figures and says **a hundred wrong things in a hundred mornings**, says he has had it out since Monday and has been refusing it, and says he is now going to stand there and find out what happens. Nothing happens. Nobody has told him it is a lot, nobody has told him it is nothing, nobody has told him whether a hundred mornings of the county's business is a hundred mornings of his, and he says he would like it noted that he did not get an answer and is not going to be given one and is not going to be relieved about it either. **He says he is not going to be told he was right and is not going to be told he was wrong, and gives the reason: being told he was wrong would be a relief, and this landing has spent nineteen years teaching anybody who stands on it that a relief is a thing somebody hands you with a rope on it.** The man of fifty-four asks at the hour he asks at, in the words he uses, is refused, is not thanked, and says he is going to keep asking. The woman who reads the ninth run enters the hundredth morning and does not open the book in front of anybody. The man of forty-three sweeps the top step, goes round the chair and round the fortieth step, does not sit on the chair, touches nothing, and says no fifth thing.
